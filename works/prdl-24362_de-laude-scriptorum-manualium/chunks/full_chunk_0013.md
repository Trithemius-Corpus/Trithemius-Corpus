If you cannot write elegantly, write as you are able; only take care of this, that you do not corrupt the sense of the Scriptures by your negligence. For I would rather have a corrected codex than a beautiful one, a meager one than none. Do not say, therefore, “Because I do not know how to write well, I am therefore excused”; but write as you can, and nothing more is required of you.

*On the material that they should write. Chapter 10.*

But if it is asked what sorts of materials monks ought to write, we answer briefly: such as the obedience of their superiors enjoins. For it lies in the judgment of the abbot or prior what he wishes to enjoin each one to write. Yet they should take care not to assign to weak brothers for writing such materials from whose reading they may become weaker. For it is not expedient that all materials be read by everyone. But if some such thing must be written, let it be assigned to stronger and learned brothers, who know how to suck honey from the rock and oil from the hardest stone. The books of the holy fathers, however, by which they explained the divine Scriptures, are to be written by monks with particular zeal, because from knowledge of them they are more richly instructed toward understanding the mysteries of God. But also the treatises of the same fathers which are suitable for arousing devotion are to be written by our scribes with special intention. And in general all books which serve either for establishing good morals or for the integrity of the orthodox faith are to be acquired for the studies of monks. Nor do we reject codices of secular letters; indeed we value them, since knowledge of them is very necessary to us for understanding the holy Scriptures. The ancient monks, not ignorant of this, were instructed in all the disciplines of secular literature. Four things, moreover, must be known by monks, so far as their state is concerned, without knowledge of which ecclesiastical unity could not long subsist in its strength: theology, music, laws, and the art of computus. To those who understand, the reasons for each are plain; we have set them out more fully in another place.

And concerning these things too our scribes had to be admonished, lest they regard the sciences of secular letters as useless. Therefore let the providence of the abbot provide for each, according to his capacity, what he should write. But if abbots immerse themselves in the cares of secular affairs, as many do, and neglect books, the monks should nevertheless continue the pursuit of writing, nor should anyone wish to lose his own progress because of the negligence of abbots. In this case let a solicitous prior make up for the deficiency of a sluggish abbot, and assign to the brothers suitable materials for writing. For I know several distinguished monasteries whose priors, notwithstanding the abbots’ sloth, have furnished most splendid libraries. Therefore if the brothers are free to write, let each choose for himself individual teachers whose volumes they may write: one Origen, another Basil, another Augustine, another Jerome, and so on with the rest, according to each one’s inclination, because from this they very often make greater progress in sacred study. But if they are not free, let them know that obedience to the prelate is rightly to be preferred to their own choice. The brother scribes too, who wish to make progress in the study of the divine Scriptures, should make for themselves little books which they call rapiaria, so that when in writing they run through the books of the holy fathers, if they find anything worthy of memory, they may gather it after the example of the most sagacious bee; and, heaping up useful sentences like fragrant flowers in the beehive of their own volume, they may have them ready, so that at the opportune time they may know how to bring forth a suitable discourse. The venerable monk Bede is read to have done this; Rabanus the abbot, Bonaventure the cardinal, and very many other most learned men are remembered to have practiced this. For one who keeps this order of learning makes much progress toward the understanding of sacred Scripture. But let him take care not to prefer private study to the usefulness of very many; that is, by continually lingering over his own collections, let him not pass by the good of obedience. For studies that resist the laws of obedience will not prosper. Let him hold the mean in each respect, lest he fail by writing too much, or, having leisure for himself, place obedience and charity after his own advantages.
