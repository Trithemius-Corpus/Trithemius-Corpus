We read of Saint Bede, the venerable monk, that although he wrote many volumes, he nevertheless was seldom accustomed to absent himself from the divine service on account of study; but, content with the interval between the hours, he wrote the books he published, almost innumerable. Nevertheless, if the abbot judges it useful and it is necessary, a good scribe may sometimes be excused from the common services. But in this freedom the proper circumstances must necessarily be weighed. For not every spirit is to be believed. The office of a monk is to lament both his own misdeeds and those of others, and to await with fear the coming of the Lord; to devote himself often to prayer and contemplation; to flee the world; to sit in solitude. But in this our age, what monk will you give me, one out of thousands, who dwells continually on the mountain of contemplation? Since, therefore, we are sluggish for prayer and untrained for contemplation, lest we run out into vain desires, we shall be able to compensate for this deficiency by no labors better than if we devote our study to writing volumes for the edification of many. But if there is anyone who, in the hours free for prayer and contemplation, can apply himself to them properly and steadfastly, he will be excused from the burden of writing. But let the examination of this matter be placed in the discretion of the abbot, or of some other experienced man. For one must not quickly trust the impulses of young men. For often, when they think they have already arrived at the mountain of contemplation, they draw near to dangerous errors. Therefore, O abbot or prior, if you see a young monk not yet well instructed in active habits, but still seething with his own passions, even if he says marvelous things, do not permit it; but seize his feet and cast him down to the earth of subjection, so that he may learn not to savor lofty things in pride and elation of heart, but to agree with the humble. In this we are not saying that you ought to prohibit one who can act well, but that you should teach such men, if they are ours, the order of making progress, lest, if the beast touches the mountain, it perish overwhelmed with stones. But he who has crucified his flesh with its vices and desires, who knows how to rule over his passions, who has learned to live humbly under obedience, even if he is young, is fit to ascend the mountain of God. But let him who is still subject to animal passions, who is still weak and feeble, keep his foot from approach to the mountain and devote his effort to the holy exercises of the active life. Such men, therefore, are to be assigned to the office of writing, lest, being ruinously released to their own freedoms, they cast themselves headlong into the pit of error. For no one suddenly becomes supreme; rather, by fixed degrees of distinctions, a man advances to the heights. One must begin from the active life, so that one may duly come to the contemplative. But in no matter can a monk come closer to the perfection of the active life than if, out of charity, he keeps watch in copying the divine Scriptures. For from what source will he give alms, who has nothing? But a devout scribe is recognized as fulfilling works of mercy more abundantly, and his labor is proved to be of greater merit. Hence someone sang thus: The scribe preaches and studies, gives freely and prays. He afflicts himself, gives [unclear], and [unclear] a fountain for those to come. He enriches the Church, arms it, guards it, honors it. What labor is more useful than this, which is honored with so many privileges? Finally, if anyone desires to be better informed about this matter, let him read the book of the chancellor of Paris On the Praise of Scribes. For there he will see the benefits of writing already mentioned set out abundantly, and many compositions gathered together in praise of the good scribe. Therefore no labor exercised by the hands accords more with our purpose than the office of writing. Considering this in former times, monks copied books with incredible desire, knowing that this art was singularly beloved by almighty God. For God wills us to know and do his will, and carefully to keep his commandments. But his will would in no way reach our knowledge unless he had committed it to letters by this industry of the scribe. Scribes, therefore, are messengers of the will of God, who have handed it down to us through letters.
