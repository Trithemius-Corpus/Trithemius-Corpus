Great, therefore, is the usefulness of this most sacred art, which announces the will of God not only to those present, but also to those to come. Monks, moreover, who write divine books, who have the office not of teaching but of lamenting, since they are unable to preach by speech, announce by hand and pen the will of the Lord to those far in the future. And the piety of the scribe is greater than the office of the preacher, because the admonition of the one perishes with time, while the announcement of the other endures for many years. The preacher speaks only to those present; the scribe preaches also to those to come. The word of the one, once heard, is quickly reduced to nothing; the reading of the other, repeated a thousand times, is never diminished. When the preacher fails, the office ceases; the scribe, even when dead, gives instruction in morals in the volume. The office of the preacher has little value if it is not aided by the ministry of the scribe. For what will he preach, if the scribe has not written what he may read? Therefore, from the piety of the scribe the sermon of the preacher is made useful; [unclear].

Four chief advantages, moreover, the devout monk obtains in writing. The time, which is most precious, is spent fruitfully; his understanding is illuminated while he writes; his inward affection is kindled to devotion; and after this life he is rewarded with a singular recompense. How could a monk spend the time granted to him more fruitfully than to devote himself, with the love of God, to the writing of volumes? For God favors this office; the angels rejoice with it and assist it; the sincere devotion of all the faithful serves it. Finally, meanwhile, because he copies good and devout materials, he does not suffer empty or base thoughts to trouble him; he does not speak idle words; he is not stained by rumors; but, sitting quiet and alone, he feasts with joy upon the scriptures, and by good exercises provokes those who look on to glorify God. For while he writes good materials, by writing he is gradually brought into the knowledge of mysteries, and inwardly in his mind [unclear], because we who write and read treat them with some delay.

The mind also of the writer is for the most part more happily kindled to devotion, while, ruminating on the scriptures, he is often inflamed by them. But what reward in heaven is owed to the devout scribe can easily be gathered from his many labors. For we believe that the devout and sincere scribe is not deprived, in that recompense of the just, of the preacher’s aureole, since he is seen to teach so many in the faith by hand. For what the one teaches by speaking, this one teaches by the office of writing.

There was in a certain monastery of our order, not unknown to me, a certain devout brother who had great zeal in writing volumes for the adornment of the library, so that whenever he could be free from the divine office, betaking himself to the secrets of his cell, he would devote himself to this sacred labor. Hence he also copied out many little works of the saints with great devotion. When, after his death and after many years, his bones were lifted from the earth, three fingers of the right hand, with which he had written so many volumes, were found so whole and incorrupt as if they had been placed in the tomb at that same time. The rest of the body, however, as is customary, had been consumed down to the bones. From this testimony it is gathered how holy this office is judged before almighty God; for, in order to show its merit to all who live, he honored the members of scribes even in dead bodies.

O my brothers, if you knew the usefulness of this work, you would not be so sluggish and slow for the office of the scribe. Or do you think that God can be appeased by the sacrifices of fools? A humble brother who, devoutly from obedience, copies out one book has greater merit before God than he who seems, both devoutly and on his own authority, to be free for contemplation. For obedience is better than sacrifices. A monk, moreover, if he is ordered, must set aside a good work and fulfill the lawful will of the abbot, not his own. For evil must never be done for the sake of obedience. Therefore, dearest brothers, do not neglect this good; do not despise the zeal for writing. For it is a great good, and very useful to the Catholic Church and to your souls. If anyone has practiced himself in it, he will recognize how great its usefulness is. Begin with desire, and with love you will complete it. Let each one rejoice to have brought, by his labors, some usefulness to others in the house of God.
