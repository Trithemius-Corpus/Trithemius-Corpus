Concerning this industry Cassiodorus and Peter Damian produced exceptional and very distinguished treatises. But neither, brothers, ought we carelessly to pass over your Rupert of Deutz, whose depth of intellect the bright mass of so many volumes adorned. For he himself, since he was a devout man and a most ardent lover of God and of the Scriptures, deserved to be divinely illuminated through a vision, so that in expounding the Scriptures Germany in his time had no one like him. He composed many excellent volumes, which he copied out with his own hands after the example of Ambrose, and cheerfully shared with others to be copied.

Among these, Cuno, abbot of the monastery of Siegburg and later bishop of Regensburg, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures, had almost all the treatises which your Rupert had fashioned copied out, both by himself and by his brothers. Hence the greater part of Rupert's volumes is held in the monastery of Siegburg. But Rupert himself also, gathering books of various arts, established at Deutz a most richly furnished library, which the malice of the times, as is evident, has long since scattered.

In many monasteries of our order also, the diligence of the ancients in writing volumes is still seen today, where twenty or thirty volumes, often most handsomely written by the hand of one man, are shown. Finally, in the Life of Saint Martin of Tours, Sulpicius relates that in his monastery no craft was practiced except that of copyists. From this it is plainly gathered that even the most holy fathers of old preferred this happy art to all manual exercises in monasteries. Therefore let none of you, brothers, shrink from what he knows the holy fathers did with such great diligence. As they benefited us by writing, so let us prepare memorials of salvation for those who come after us. For if the ancients had not written long ago, what would we now read? Indeed, if I had not resolved to serve brevity, I could still declaim many things about the diligence of the ancient copyists. But since I am speaking to those who know the histories, I wish to shorten the discourse I have begun.

*That copying the books of the ancients is, as it were, the proper and fitting craft of monks.*

Let us attend, brothers, to what Paul the Apostle says for our purpose, and what holy Benedict too, our lawgiver, proclaims in the Rule. For they are our leaders, whose commands it is fitting for us to obey. The first says thus: He who does not work, let him not eat. The second says: Then they are true monks, when they live from the labors of their own hands. Therefore, if the man who does not wish to work is forbidden to eat, idle monks should either not eat, or should acknowledge themselves transgressors of the apostle's precepts. But if they cannot live unless they eat, they must work, so that, working and eating, they may also be able to live well. For to live and not to live well is already to begin to die. But he lives badly who lives otherwise than he ought. We all ought, however, [unclear] according to the rule of life; the end of the worst condition is the beginning [unclear], unless repentance is undertaken before the end of life.

Likewise, if those are true monks who live from the labors of their own hands, it follows that those are not true monks who, living idly, despise work. But he who is not a true monk in a monastery, what, I ask, is he? He is not what he is, who is not true. Certainly he is not a monk who is not a true one; and what is contrary to truth is not as it ought to be. But what is not as it ought to be contradicts the highest truth, which has established for all things the due order of being. And therefore it truly is not.

And so we must work; what better suits us, is more expedient, and comes closer to our profession than the office of writing? For, bound to the divine offices, we cannot dig; we cannot bear the hard and heavy labors of the fields, because if it happened that we were worn out outdoors through the day in rustic works, the divine service in choir would perish. But since service to God must be placed before all labors, it would be very unfitting for monks to immerse themselves in exterior things while interior things were neglected.
