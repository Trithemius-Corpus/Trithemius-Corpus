This indeed is the most holy knowledge. It is a lamp to our feet, lest on this road full of darkness by which we walk we stumble; it is the companion of our journey, lest we go astray. This is set before the minds of the faithful as a mirror, so that the inner face may be seen clearly. For just as we behold the blemishes of our face in a material mirror, so in sacred Scripture we see the negligences of our actions. For it corrects us when we transgress, pricks us when we grow sluggish, rouses us when we are negligent, instructs us when we are ignorant. Holy Scripture is known to confer on us a great and incredible good, if it is read attentively with humble devotion. For it enlightens our understanding for instruction, draws the mind away from the vanities of the world, and reforms it in the love of God. Often, being drawn away by familiarity with it, we are separated from the desires of the world to such a degree that we seem to ourselves not to be in the world. And the longer we abide in love of it, the less we think about the changefulness of the age. For true joy, which takes its strength from the sole truth, which is God, despises both the pleasant and the harsh things of this world as dung. For the happiness of this world, too intensely pondered, harms the servants of God. But no greater or more wholesome joy is acquired in this life than if the mind, well conscious of itself, is occupied with love in the sacred writings. It gladdens the mind of man with inestimable joy; while it is examined with piety by its lover, the eye of the heart is sweetly lulled in contemplation of heavenly things. Nothing separates a monk so much from the love of this world as if he devotes his mind to sacred readings with desire at every time. For holy blessings are contained in the divine Scriptures; when we run through them in attentive reading, we most sweetly perceive the force of divine love. I have not experienced so powerful a burning of the love of God as the loving knowledge of the holy Scriptures. Love it, and it will exalt you before God and men, and together with it you will possess all good things. For he who truly holds it possesses all things. For it is the teacher of the discipline of God, and has knowledge friendly to him. Now therefore, brothers, hear me, and cultivate this mother of beautiful love with all devotion, so that through its service you may deserve to be brought to the throne of glory of the great God. For in the court of the heavenly emperor this mother is very well known, like a queen. Let us consider, O brothers, the most reverend monks of our order, who once burned with great desire for the holy Scriptures; and let us imitate their holy studies as much as we can, so that we too may deserve to come to the glory which, by the Lord’s bounty, they attained through learning and holiness. Let no one say in his own excuse, “I cannot advance without a teacher,” since teachers can always be found for those who are disposed. Purge the [unclear] of your race, and God will cause the wheat of the Scriptures to be brought in. For the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee what is feigned, nor will it dwell in a body subject to sins. Therefore let the house of conscience be cleansed, so that it may become a worthy dwelling of the Scriptures, because the monk who wishes to learn the sacred Scriptures usefully must begin from the correction of morals. Nor ought you to abandon the monastery for the sake of study, because you can find at home the means by which you may advance. For we know that many very learned monks once shone in our order, whom we know to have studied only in monasteries. Bede, priest and monk, most skilled in every respect, was entrusted at the age of seven to a monastery, in which he drew forth whatever he learned of the Scriptures. Among others his most learned pupils were Alcuin, afterward teacher of Charles the Great; Claudius; and John Scotus, monks excelling in every doctrine, both divine and secular, instructed in Greek and Latin speech, of whom many distinguished little works survive. Rabanus, abbot and archbishop of Mainz, while still almost a boy, was entrusted to the monastery of Fulda; how much he advanced in every branch of knowledge his volumes bear witness. Yet the abbot of Fulda sent him from among his own monks to Francia for the sake of study, to Abbot Alcuin, of whom we have already made mention, under whose teaching he persevered for many years in the monastery. Strabus, monk of Fulda, is said to have been a disciple and scribe of Abbot Rabanus; he, having been given to the monastery as a youth, shone forth as an outstanding teacher. So Haymo; so Notger; so Hermannus Contractus; so other monks almost innumerable [unclear].
