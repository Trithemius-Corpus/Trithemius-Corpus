The second kind is *homilies*, which we call a “word,” and they are delivered to the people. The third kind is *tomes*, which we call books or volumes. Homilies are for the common people; tomes, that is, books, are of a more substantial disputation. A dialogue is a conference of two persons, from *dia* and *logos*, which is “the speech of two.” A treatise is the manifold exposition of a single subject. Commentaries are so called as if “comments”; for they are interpretations: comments on law, comments on the Gospel. An apologetic is a book of excuse, in which certain persons are accustomed to answer accusers. A panegyric, that is, laudatory, is a licentious and wanton kind of speaking, in praises of kings and princes; in composing it men flatter with many lies. A prologue is a preface before a book, as the prologue of blessed Jerome on Genesis. A proem is the beginning of a speech or work, and is often taken for a preface. A *simperasma* is the conclusion of some work, and in our usage it is treated as of the third declension, like *dogma*. *Biblos*, that is, a codex, is a book of many books, as the codex of the Bible, which contains many books. A book is a partial treatise divided by itself, or a single one, as one book of Augustine on penance, or four on Christian doctrine, etc. A volume is a book, so called from rolling, as volumes of the law, volumes of the prophets. Books of fasti are those in which kings and consuls are written, because they were made from the bark of those same [unclear], or because they flutter in a codex after the manner of leaves. Verses are so called from turning, because the ancients wrote as [unclear] of trees were named: for first they led the stylus to the right, then they turned back from below and again wrote toward the left. A draft-sheet, *sceda scede*, is what is still being corrected and has not yet been reduced to a fair copy; for this we say “conceptions,” as if unpolished materials. The instruments of a scribe, moreover, are the reed and the pen.

*On Establishing Scribes in Monasteries.*

*Chapter 14.*

Since, as we have said above, no craft is so fitting for the profession of monks as the office of writing, for the reason that many weak and feeble persons, not to say untrained ones, are not suited to higher exercises. For it has not been granted to all always to devote themselves to contemplation or prayer, or to instruct others by preaching. There are indeed some idle and lazy men who excuse themselves, indeed boast, saying: “I do not know how to write.” If you do not know, learn; otherwise, he who does not labor, let him not eat. Ignorance does not excuse you from the duty, when, easily conquerable as it is, you neglect to overcome it. If only good will were in you, assiduity would quickly provide the art of writing. Therefore, to remove the excuses of the lazy, it seems advisable and very profitable that abbots should have monks instructed in the art of writing, either by their own monks or by others, because from this both the material of excuse is taken away from the lazy, and very great usefulness is produced from it. For idleness is an enemy of the soul, and desires kill the lazy man. But if anyone is lazy or malicious, so that he will by no means consent to write, he must be afflicted with other labors and abstinences until he asks as a consolation for what he previously despised. Yet let the abbot be cautious in all things, and let the arduous things always be preceded by charity. The demons will hate this discipline greatly, knowing that through its ministry the salvation of many is procured. Therefore they strive with every effort to draw monks back from its office, and sometimes to bring them so far that many, resisting their prelates even to the crime of disobedience, fall into a horrible calamity. We have already known very many such men, disobedient to their superiors, who, so that they might not be able to write, at the devil’s persuasion became cruel to themselves and fell into extreme madness. But let us omit these things, lest they harm weak readers more than they profit them. No one has a legitimate excuse when the defect consists in will alone.
