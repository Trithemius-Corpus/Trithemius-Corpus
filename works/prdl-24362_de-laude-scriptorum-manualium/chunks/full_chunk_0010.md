*That, because of printing, one must not desist from writing volumes. Chapter VII.*

[unclear], brothers, let no one say: “What need is there for me to weary myself by writing, when the art of printing pours forth so many and such great books into the light that with little money we can furnish a great library?” Truly, whoever speaks in this way is trying to make darkness for his own leisure. Who does not know how great the distance is between writing and printing? For writing, if it is put on parchment, can endure for a thousand years; but printing, since it is a thing of paper, how long will it last? If it can endure in a paper volume for two hundred years, that is a great thing, although there are many who think that printing will be consumed by its own material. Posterity will judge this. Indeed, even if many volumes have been printed, they will never be printed to such an extent that you cannot always find something not printed to write. Nor [unclear], even if all the books of the whole world were printed, should the devout scribe in any way desist from his pursuit, but he should also perpetuate useful printed books by writing, since otherwise they cannot last long. For by doing this he will give firmness to writings that totter, greatness of value to cheap things, longevity of time to perishable things.

Moreover, the devout scribe will always find something in which, by applying himself to his office, he may earn merit. He does not allow himself to be constrained under the printer’s condition. He is free, and he will rejoice in the office of his liberty. For he is not inferior to the printer, so as to abandon his pursuit because of the other’s printing. But, prudently passing him by, he proceeds gladly on the path begun, knowing that his crown before God will not be diminished by any importunity of another. But he who ceases from the pursuit of writing because of printing [unclear] is not a true lover of writings, because, looking only to present things, he has no concern for the edification of posterity. But we, dearest brothers, looking to the reward of this holy labor, should not cease from its pursuit, even if we have many thousands of volumes. For printed books are never compared on equal terms with written codices. For printing very often neglects orthography and the other ornaments of books; writing, however, is a matter of greater diligence.

*Chapter VIII.*

But in order that the devout scribe may usefully fulfill his office, and may render the books that he writes adorned with beautiful grace, let him observe most diligently the precepts of orthography; without these, books, however useful, are rendered ugly and contemptible. On this matter abundant instructions are found in the old codices of the authors, over which it is not the business of the present time to linger any longer. The examples of learned men are also precepts of diligence for new scribes. Let us inspect the most ancient volumes that learned scribes wrote, and from imitation of them take the advantage of our art. Among them there were some who applied exact diligence in writing, so that they wrote not only correctly but also skillfully, distinguishing the volumes with signs, cola, and figures in beautiful variety, so that by the sight alone you seem to be invited to the reading of them. Although among the ancients unskilled scribes disfigured their codices, since they either did not know, or did not wish to understand, how to write well. But you should know that not the negligence of the unskilled, but the [unclear] of the learned, which they also [unclear], is set before you to be imitated. In antiquity they did not admit scribes indiscriminately, but only the learned and educated, who from their skill in signs were also called notaries. They wrote volumes not only beautiful but also corrected, with wondrous subtlety.
