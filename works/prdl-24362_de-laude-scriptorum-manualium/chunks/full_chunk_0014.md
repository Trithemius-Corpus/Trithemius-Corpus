*Whether it is permitted for monks to write on feast days. Chapter XI.*

Now, the occasion having been given by the preceding matters, it is asked whether it is lawful for monks to write on feast days, on which in the law they are commanded to abstain from servile works. We read that this question was once put by certain persons to John, formerly chancellor of Paris, a most learned man; and in his book *On the Praise of Scribes*, after developing it through twelve considerations, he finally concludes thus, saying: “Nothing prevents one from writing books or short treatises or little books of devotion on feast days, provided moderation is observed, such as a wise man will judge proper in this work; and this assuredly is not to be deemed servile, but sometimes tends more to the honor of the Church than the multiplication of vocal prayer, indeed than the unbecoming repetition of masses in some cases.” Thus he.

But we, adding this with humility, say that a monk placed under obedience, if at the command of his prelate he writes useful materials on feast days, should fear nothing, because the obedience of one writing is better than the sacrifices of his own will. Yet on this occasion let him not neglect those things to which he is bound by the institution of the feast, such as celebrating or hearing Mass and duly performing the canonical hours. Therefore he does well who writes books on feast days, provided the due circumstances are carefully observed. But he who can fittingly persist in divine contemplation is believed to hold a more excellent degree, unless the merit of obedience rightly intervenes. For obedience adds increase to good works, and from equal things makes better ones. Since, then, we are weak men and less fit for contemplation, it is better to make good this loss by writing than to be occupied, as commonly happens, with useless conversations. Since we also cannot always read or pray, there is nothing after these by which we may occupy ourselves more usefully than if we devote our effort, with devotion, to writing books. By this we not only instruct ourselves, but also prepare material for edification for those who will come long after us. The scribe teaches good things by writing no less than the preacher teaches holy things by preaching. And what is permitted to the latter, who forbids to the former?

And, as we have already said, studious brothers, especially on feast days, consulting their devotions, may confidently write their little commonplace books, unless, as has been said, obedience commands them to do something else. In all these matters, however, the intention of the one commanding must be made clear, and the devotion of the one writing must also be regulated. But those who write anything for payment, however useful the material may be, are excluded from this liberty and shall cease from work on feast days, because in intention they serve earthly gains; and therefore it is judged a servile work, unless perhaps some other regard of piety intervenes, which rarely happens.

*On the distinction between scribes and the names of small works. Chapter XII.*

Among the ancients scribes were known by a twofold name. The first were called antiquarii, because they wrote only old things, taking their name from their office. The second were called librarii, who wrote both new things and old. When antiquarii wrote, they used old letters as well; librarii, however, used new ones. Librarii are so called from liber, that is, the inner bark which adheres to the wood, because before the use of paper or parchment, volumes were put together from the bark of trees, just as traces are still sometimes found today in the very oldest libraries.
