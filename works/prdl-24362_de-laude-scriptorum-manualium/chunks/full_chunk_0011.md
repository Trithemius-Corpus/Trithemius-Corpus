What shall I say about certain scribes of this age, who, although for the most part they understand what they are writing, are so lazy and idle that they are unwilling to apply any diligence at all to the writings? These are corrupters of the correctness of the Scriptures, falsifiers of the bulls of the pontiff Jesus Christ, foolish and perverse messengers: for when they write the will of God, they ought to announce it with fear and concern; on the contrary, they handle it negligently and corruptly.

If, according to the statutes of the canons, he who falsifies the letters of the pope is excommunicated, with what penalty should he be punished who corrupts the Scriptures of God? Just as a devout and diligent scribe renders great fruit in the Church of God by writing, so the idle and negligent one brings very great losses upon it. For when he corrupts the Scriptures he announces false things to the Church as true; he hinders readers from holy study; he takes away understanding; he overturns the order of teaching. I have said these things against those who abuse the gift of understanding bestowed on them, and who, from laziness or negligence, are not ashamed to corrupt the holy Scriptures by writing them falsely. But against those who are less learned, and earnestly do what they can, so harsh a judgment is not to be brought. For no one sins unwillingly. Therefore, when a devout scribe has carefully copied a book, let him compare it again with the exemplar; wherever he has erred, let him correct it by careful emendation. By doing this, the scribe himself is found suitable and faithful, and the book acceptable in its proper order. If it can be done, let books be adorned with fair decoration, so that even by their appearance they may draw those who look at them to reading. For we know that the ancients took great care for this: that outward beauty should commend the inner beauty of books. For Holy Scripture is most worthy of every ornament. Yet care must be taken that the ornament itself not be over-elaborate, lest we put beauty before correctness; for where propriety is admitted, curiosity is forbidden. At the same time, let us not be marked by the saying of Jerome, with which he reproves the unskilled, who prefer to have beautiful books rather than corrected ones. And concerning a multitude of books, a similar judgment will have to be applied.
