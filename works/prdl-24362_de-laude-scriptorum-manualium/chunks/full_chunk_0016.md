For no discipline, however celebrated, is retained for long without practice. Practice, indeed, is the strength of an art. Let monks who do not wish to write read the book of blessed Augustine *On the Work of Monks*, and let him blush for his sloth who lives idly contrary to the decrees of the fathers. Scripture says: “Every idle man is in desires.” Why? Because, while the mind is not bent outwardly by exercise to useful work, being empty within it is defiled by unclean thoughts. Fullness of bread and idleness led the Sodomites to eternal destruction. Idleness is the mother and wellspring of all vices, and the destruction of all virtues. For it spends acceptable time fruitlessly and shamefully; it carries away the supports of virtues; it suggests lust; it diminishes strength; it kills the soul. Therefore flee idleness, you who desire to arrive at eternal life, which has been promised not to the idle but to those who labor.

And this sloth is especially reprehensible in monks, who are called not from labor to idleness, but from idleness to labor. But if you say to me, “I cannot write; I cannot remain in the cell through the whole day; I will gladly go to exterior labors; I will by no means refuse to dig or carry stones, provided only that I do not suffer distress in those things which are wholly contrary to me,” I shall answer briefly: it is not a good comparison which withdraws from a better progress. It is better to write than to dig, to read sacred books than to carry stones, unless obedience or piety, which has force in all things, has chosen to intervene. If you cannot write, if you cannot remain alone in the cell, why did you wish to come to the monastery, when you were not going to live as a monk? For to live in a cenobium not as a monk is to bind oneself to damnation by the sentence of one’s own mouth. For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is commanded to sit alone in the cenobium, so that through silence he may be separated from the cares of the world. Therefore, if you refuse to be what you promised, you lie to God as a transgressor of your vow. But to lie to God is to condemn oneself by one’s own mouth. You vowed the conversion of your ways according to the Rule of Saint Benedict, which commands monks to labor to such a degree that it denied that idle monks are monks.

But by the fact that you desire to be occupied with exterior labors, you show that you are empty of interior exercise. For whichever of the monks these labors delight, they must necessarily abandon the interior ones. You are being led, I say, to exterior labors by a diabolical impulse, which knows that the interior ones are contrary to itself. If you were a true monk, you would not abhor the secrecy of the cell, which is especially fitting to your honor. In our age there is not such devotion among monks that, after the example of the ancients, they can make progress in works of agriculture. These labors destroy devotion more than they build it up. For as soon as one has come to conversations, no speech of edification, or scarcely any, is brought forth; rather, mouths are opened most eagerly to the business of the world. Pleasant discussion is held about the wars of kings, the conflicts of princes, the pomps of the age; no guard at all is applied to silence.

Therefore, freed from these labors as from certain bonds of distractions, and turning the mind to interior things, let us love the secrecy of the cell, let us apply ourselves more often to prayer and reading, let us write the books of the holy fathers with devotion, let us seek foods that feed the soul, since by our labors we cannot nourish our bodies. Let him who does not know how to write learn from others who know, and let him subject his own will to obedience if he desires to escape the penalty of those who eat idly. For the devout brother will always find writers by whom he may be instructed. And because a good will deserves grace, so that it may always advance by pious increases, it is more frequently to be honored with suitable stipends. Therefore, when the abbot has seen that his brothers are ready to obey and willing and cheerful in writing, let him pursue them with greater kindness, honor them with fitting favors, and sometimes lighten the burdens of the others. And because such devout brothers, on account of obedience and love of the Scriptures, neglect themselves, let the abbot provide for them in all necessities more abundantly than for the others, who scorn to be such and to be found such. For by doing this he will both preserve the good in holy zeal and provoke others to imitate them. Yet under pretext of this opinion let him not transgress the paths [of the Rule?].
