*Whether it is commendable in monasteries to have many books. Chapter 14.*

There are some who reproach lovers of books for their multitude, as if for excess, saying that few books are sufficient for the formation of a good life: books that teach us to flee vices and love virtues. For they say to those whom they see to love books: Why do you burden yourselves with such a multitude of books, when you cannot read all those you have? For it is better to have a few books and read them than to pass by many stored up in a library without reading them.

To these we reply briefly. If it is blameworthy to have many volumes, since a few could suffice for saving knowledge, let them also admit that it was evil for the holy fathers to have written so many and almost innumerable books. Why did Origen publish six thousand books, why Augustine a thousand, why Ambrose two hundred, why Jerome three hundred, when a few suffice for the formation of a good life? Or perhaps it is permitted to write many, but not to possess them? To what purpose, then, are they written, if it is not permitted to have them? If it is evil to buy many books, why did the holy fathers in former times apply such diligence in collecting and writing them? Are you so foolish that, asserting good to be evil and evil good, while you bark without distinction against lovers of books, you do not fear also to speak against the most holy fathers, whose imitators you reproach, accusing those fathers as though they had proceeded badly? If it is evil to imitate, how will it be good to have gone before? But because you cannot justly reproach the zeal of the saints, if you are wise, cease tearing at their imitators. For if it was good for them, it will not be evil for these.

Behold, you gather gold and silver; you join field to field, and you do not judge it evil. But you say: To collect money for the present necessity of the brothers belongs to prudence, and to store it up for the future belongs to just reason. But to buy so many innumerable books belongs not to providence but to curiosity. O emperor of covetousness, O prince of avarice: what honesty does your sloth not attack? What does your savage greed not gnaw away? That you may revile lovers of sacred knowledge, you commend avarice. Out of the abundance of your heart your mouth speaks, and your speech testifies that you are an adversary of wisdom. It is not prudence, as you pretend, but avarice; not the necessity of the brothers, but desire for gold. You have hated discipline and have cast the words of the Lord behind your back, and as though you were glorying in distinguished justice. But let these things pass, to come forth in public at another time.

All books of the good arts, however, are good, especially those that conduce to the understanding of sacred Scripture. Therefore it is not evil to have good books, unless prohibition makes it unlawful. But no commandment is found that forbids a multitude of books. Why then do you insult us over an abundance of books? Why do you condemn a holy and necessary study in the Church? It is good to have dared to say steadfastly that there should be both many and good books in a monastery, by which the zeal of the brothers may be advanced, even if they are not able to read them all. Nor should the authorities of secular writers be kept out of monks’ libraries; without knowledge of them, I do not think anyone perfectly understands the divine books.

For if we examine the libraries of the ancient fathers of our order, such as Bede in England, Rabanus in Fulda, Haymo in Hirschfeld, Peter in Vivarium, Notker at Saint Gall, Guimund at Saint-Leufroy, Sigebert in Gembloux, Heriger also at Lobbes, and other most learned and venerable fathers who shone in knowledge and holiness in the Church of God, we shall find an abundance of books in every faculty, in every science, in every doctrine. This we can also easily recognize from their little works, since they show that they were skilled in all literature, both divine and human. To imitate the examples of these men is honorable and useful for monks.

Moreover, there is nothing in this life so pious, so wholesome, so holy, that an evil will cannot abuse it. Thus it will also be able to happen with a multitude of books, if the cause is not love of God and of the Scriptures, but curiosity or pride. Therefore let him who is watchful in collecting volumes take heed that his appetite not be set upon vanity. Let him strive to direct his intention to the proper end, if he does not wish to stray from the good.
