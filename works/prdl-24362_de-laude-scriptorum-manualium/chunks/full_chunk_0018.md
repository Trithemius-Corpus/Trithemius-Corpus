*On the custody and cleanliness to be maintained for books. Chapter XV.*

Since the greatest treasure of the Church is the abundance of writings, whoever neglects to guard holy books diligently does injury to the Church. For books are the weapons with which she fortifies herself against heretics; whoever allows them either to be dispersed or to perish is proved to love the Church too little. If he loved her as he ought, he would by no means permit her to be stripped of her own weapons. Therefore we earnestly exhort lovers of books to guard the library carefully, or to entrust it to such librarians as will allow nothing to be stolen from it. From libraries the discipline of monks is easily known, and from their books it can quickly be observed what they study. If I come to your house, there is no need for me to ask you what you study: your volumes will give testimony concerning your actions. Your codices are honored. If you neglect them, they will neglect you; if you despise them, they will despise you. Finally, “Those who despise me,” says the wisdom of God, which is recognized in books, “shall be without honor.” If your volumes fly about torn on the benches, if they are covered with dust, if they are stained within, if they are disordered without, they will proclaim you unlearned and a hypocrite, even if you are regarded as a doctor. How will you be able to love learning, if you despise and neglect the books that instruct you?

Just as wisdom loves and inhabits the dwelling-place of a pure heart, so the lover of wisdom loves the cleanliness and adornment of books. For whoever tears the house of another shows that he does not revere the one who dwells in it. And whoever handles books of divine or human writings negligently and filthily proves that he does not love the knowledge contained in them. Therefore let our Philobiblos devote the utmost zeal to the custody and adornment of books; let him arrange the books at the desk according to their subjects or dignities, so that studious brothers may more quickly be able to find what they seek. Let him not think that any subject is to be cast out of his library, except the dogmas of heretics, because no book is so slight that it cannot be useful for some purpose. Let the library be under secure custody, and let it not lie open indiscriminately to everyone, but only to those whom love and study of the Scriptures recommend. For we have known many losses inflicted on libraries because they lay open without discrimination to all. Nor are books to be readily sent out from their places, except under pledge and sure confidence. Inventories of all the books of libraries should be kept, so that nothing can be carried off or lost.

Moreover, let our zealous supporter of useful disciplines sometimes visit the books that he loves, and let him examine most diligently how rightly they are kept. Finally, let him not be sparing and envious in communicating volumes from the library to studious men, because a hidden treasure and concealed wisdom are proved to be as if useless. Therefore, whenever he is asked for volumes to be copied out, let him willingly lend them to students, yet under the sure caution of a pledge, so that he may both guard his own treasure for the monastery and make others rich without any diminution of it. But whoever receives books under pledge to be copied out should take diligent care that he neither soil nor tear them; but, according to the sentence, while they receive a rule for their studies, let them take care not to stain or destroy them, but, reading with devotion and purity, let them keep them unstained. But if anyone handles codices negligently and filthily, let him be punished as a sacrilegious person and a squanderer of the monastery’s property, so that his correction may become an example to the rest. But why have we lingered with many words over the custody of books? Those who love them undoubtedly care for and preserve them, even if we are silent; and we cannot persuade the unskilled of this in vain. For fools despise the studies of the learned and do not love volumes unless they conform to their own vanities. Leaving these things aside, therefore, let us proceed to an exhortation of our own men, who both love and read books.
