Hence they not only bought up the volumes of their own peoples, but also, translating into their own language the sayings or deeds of any foreign nations whatsoever, strove to lay them up in public libraries. Among these, Pisistratus was the first among the Greeks to establish a library; after it had been enlarged by the Athenians, Xerxes, when Athens was burned, carried it off to Persia, and long afterward Seleucus Nicanor brought it back again to Greece. From this there arose among kings and other cities a zeal for gathering books of various peoples and translating them into another language through interpreters. Later King Alexander, surnamed the Great, although he was continually occupied with military expeditions, devoted his mind in a wonderful way to establishing libraries of all books. What shall I say of Ptolemy Philadelphus, a man most acute in every branch of learning? Since in his zeal for a library he emulated Pisistratus, he brought into his own library, with incredible fervor, not only the writings of the nations, but also the divine writings, translated at very great expense. For in his time seventy thousand books were found at Alexandria. Plato, chief of the philosophers, is said to have had so great a love for books that he bought the three books of Philolaus the Pythagorean for ten thousand denarii. Aristotle too, of no lesser renown among the philosophers, [unclear]; [unclear] of books, whose number is immense. Let us pass on to our own men. Ambrose, disciple and friend of the great Origen, burning with a credible zeal for books, daily urged Origen to write, indeed drove him to it, so that in a certain letter he affectionately called him the taskmaster of the work. The blessed martyr Pamphilus, [unclear] presbyter of Caesarea, loved books with so great a desire that, copying with his own hand the greater part of Origen's books, he gathered a very great multitude of books at Caesarea, with Eusebius cooperating. Saint Jerome the presbyter also, withdrawing from the city, although he despised all worldly things, confesses that he could not abandon only the library which he had founded for himself. Augustine, outstanding doctor of the Catholic Church, when he was about to die, entrusted to the brothers, as though by a special testament, that they should carefully guard the ecclesiastical library.
It would be long if we wished to describe by name the foremost lovers of books whom we know, who flourished from the beginning of the Christian faith down to our own age. The almost countless volumes which they handed over to their libraries bear witness to their sacred love. We see in many monasteries, especially of our order, splendid and very ancient copies, which, written with marvelous fineness, demonstrate the diligence of the ancients. Once there was among the abbots and monks of our order an incredible zeal for books, since they knew that in them were found the adornment of the order and the remedy for infirm souls. For books are what create in us a desire for future blessedness, lessen the miseries of present exile, drive out vices, bring in virtues, bestow constancy in tribulation, and produce the fruit of allotted time. For what could we do except set our minds on earthly things, if we did not have the consolation of more pleasant writings? In short, the Catholic faith would quickly fail from human minds if the instruments of Scripture were not available, [unclear]. For among all manual exercises, none is so fitting for monks as to devote their zeal to writing sacred volumes.
