You have preserved the ordinances of the Law
which Moses once bestowed
upon the fathers of old
and upon the Hebrew peoples,
obtaining wondrous, gracious gifts
for the faithful
by pleasing entreaty
from the Son of the high Father.
You heal the sick,
and grant that hearts be chaste.
We beseech you, Father,
and the Son with the Spirit,
that you grant joyful things to us
as we sing the renowned praises
of most sweet Anne,
the kindly mother of the Virgin. Amen.

*Poem of Rutger, a venerable German regular canon of [Beynensis], on the book On the Praises of Saint Anne*

Whoever you are, reader of this excellent book,
lay aside vain fear; it contains no frivolities.
This book, pregnant with wondrous meaning and sweet with charm,
Abbot Trithemius has published by his art,
keeping watch over books, guardian of the sheepfold of Sponheim,
who cherishes the sacred writings in the threefold tongue.
Here you will find titles full of reason and art concerning Anne;
only turn them over in a devout heart.
He does not follow vain and doubtful little tales everywhere;
he teaches the clear streams of sacred eloquence.
Therefore, I pray, read the worthy praises of venerable Anne,
which the learned man has brought forth for you in love.

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, sends greeting to the reader.*

When recently, at the insistence of friends, I had resolved to write something on the praises of the most holy mother Anne, certain fathers of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel asked that I not omit a defense of the most pure conception of Mary. And so, persuaded by their prayers and otherwise by just reason, I assigned the seventh chapter of this work to the conception of the Mother of God and Virgin. Lest anyone receive it otherwise than my intention has it, I thought the reader should be warned.

For I know, and it does not escape me, that many most holy and most learned men once wrote that the Mother of God was subject to the stain of original sin, men who flourished long before the Council of Basel. I, conceding this opinion to its own time, pass it over altogether untouched, and I in no way intend to say or write anything against them. But if anything said by me is seen as rather harsh, let it be referred not to those who thought otherwise than we do, but only to the obstinate, who, prattling without learning, do not shrink from calling the defenders of the other opinion heretics. And to this my words look, for they mark the present time and not the past. For when I say, “What do the foolish bark? What do the ignorant mutter?” this belongs to the present time, not to the past, and today it rebukes the obstinate, not the ancients who held a different opinion.

When, moreover, I ask what learned man ever wrote the contrary, I do not intend this of the ancient fathers in general, but of those who hold another opinion after the determination of the aforesaid council. And so I censure only the obstinacy of certain ignorant men of this time, not the opinion of any learned men whatsoever who think otherwise, since I am not ignorant of the declaration of Pope Sixtus IV. It has seemed to me, however, in no way abhorrent from the holy Roman Church to hold the faith of the most pure conception, which she believes and venerates; and today this is shown by written documents. Finally, with the doctors of the universities of Paris, Cologne, Heidelberg, Erfurt, Vienna, Leipzig, [Thowingensis], Ingolstadt, Basel, Mainz, and of many others, and with almost all orders and Christ’s faithful spread throughout the whole Christian world, I do not fear to venerate the conception of the Mother of God and Virgin Mary as pure. Yet I do not admit obstinacy, but I wish to obey the Roman Church in all things as the mother, most reverently to be honored, of all churches.

This is the pure and sincere intention of my aforesaid chapter, in which I consider that no one who holds an opinion soberly and without obstinacy is disparaged. Farewell, reader.

This letter of the aforesaid lord Johannes, the abbot, is lacking from certain copies through carelessness and negligence; nevertheless he himself ordered that it be appended to all copies, and decreed that it be regarded by each reader as placed at the beginning.
