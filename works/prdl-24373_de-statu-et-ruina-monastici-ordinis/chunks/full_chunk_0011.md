Do you think that by the authority of the supreme pontiff you can escape the wrath of God? I dare to say: the pope’s dispensation will profit you nothing if it has been obtained by fraud, by deceit, by falsehood, or unjustly. That is not good, that is not just, which is acquired by evil arts. For a dispensation to be just, four things are required, without which a dispensation will by no means save you. What are they? Necessity, namely, congruity, usefulness, and honorableness. But you, perjured monk, who are led by mere willfulness, who despise God and your order, invent necessity, show a false congruity, lie about usefulness, and fashion honorableness by fraud. Let necessity be evident and manifest, congruity true, usefulness not private but public, honorableness not fictitious but proper; otherwise the dispensation will be unjust.

Indeed, I have nothing for which I should be angry with the supreme pontiff in this dispensation; I reprehend nothing in his actions, since I am not unaware that he was circumvented by the false narratives of monks. You are the guilty ones, you deceitful men, you who must be marked for wickedness, O perverse monks, criminal apostates, who by lying, deceit, and fraud seek what you despair of obtaining by just prayers. What necessity urges you to desert your order and pass over into the state of secular life? What congruity recommends breaking what, once promised to God, no one may revoke? What usefulness is there in laying aside the monastic habit, deserting the order, denying your purpose, transferring the monastery, and not only breaking but utterly casting away the vow made to God in the presence of all the saints? Or what honorableness is it for a monk dedicated to God to return to the world, break faith, commit perjury, and perpetrate sacrilege? Workers of evil and impious men, how will you ever be able to excuse such a wicked presumption? None of these four agrees with your transfer; none of them confirms your dispensation. For what necessity could have driven you to this rashness? What cause for this dismemberment could you have alleged before the vicar of Christ? The poverty and ruin of the monasteries. But you could have quickly restored this if you lived according to the Rule of Saint Benedict.

*For a dispensation to be just, four [things are required], etc.*

For you have been made poor by wicked morals; by holy conduct you would again be enriched. Look around you at monasteries restored to regular observance: how, after extreme poverty, with the Lord bestowing it, they now advance again to their former riches. And this, indeed, quite justly. For the Lord promised us in the Gospel, saying: First seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things shall be added unto you. But you put aside the love of God, neglect his worship, devote yourselves to games, banquets, drunkenness, and gluttony, and do not regard the work of the Lord. You care nothing for regular observance; therefore, because spiritual things have been neglected, by the just judgment of God temporal things too have failed you. False, then, is the necessity that you invent, and insufficient the dispensation that you allege. But perhaps you say: We are not sons of rustic men but of nobles, noble, delicate, and weak. We cannot keep the observance of servants, farmers, and shepherds; fasts, abstinence, vigils, disciplines, and the other exercises they have are unbearable for us, which we neither can nor ought to imitate. What then? Are you not a monk because you are noble, because delicate, because weak? There is nothing by which you can defend yourself against Saint Benedict, who in the Rule provided abundantly for every estate, every rank, every age; who with the greatest discretion came to the aid of the delicate and the weak. Are you the only ones from among the noble, tender, and delicate who have professed the monastic order? Many most noble young men, sons of emperors, kings, dukes, and princes, have shone in our order in holiness and learning under regular observance at various times; they, nobler, more delicate, and more tender than you, yet preserving cenobitic discipline inviolate, will stand against you in testimony, easily able to prove to those who love God that what you, full of [unclear], cast away as if unbearable [unclear]. Nor do I bid you imitate the observance which you despise as that of cobblers, but the Rule which you promised, which you vowed, which you pledged yourself before God to keep. If anything in our conduct displeases you, you have the Rule; go to its precepts, shape your life. No one exacts more from you; no one requires more.
