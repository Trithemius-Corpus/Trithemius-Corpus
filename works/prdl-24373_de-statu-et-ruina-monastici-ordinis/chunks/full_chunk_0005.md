Let no one despise me as I mourn; let no one blame my grief. I do not seek earthly honors; I do not aspire to ecclesiastical dignities, but I groan that learning and holiness have fallen away from my order. What have we to do with empty honors, we who seek life in heaven? Alas, the crown of our head has fallen; monastic devotion has grown lukewarm; charity has grown cold; the honor of regular discipline has for the most part failed. Where now in our order, and in its most holy men, is the devotion once so abundant? Where, in every variety of writings, is venerable learning? Where is the solicitude of abbots for the care of the Lord’s flock? We see many monasteries founded according to the Rule of Saint Benedict, in which, apart from the name and the habit, we see no trace of regular discipline. Over this, O fathers, take up lamentation; deplore the ruin of our most holy order, if perhaps God may turn to mercy and leave a blessing behind him. And you transgressors, who are the cause of these tears, return, even late at last, to your heart; return, and fear the indignation of your blessed lawgiver. Consider the desolation of the order which you bring in to your own harm; fear the wrath of the Almighty, which you provoke by your crimes. Woe to us. The city of our religion is being destroyed by enemies all around us; the house of discipline is being reduced to nothing, and no one is found who resists, no one who sets himself like a wall before the house of God. We all seek the things that are our own; we all conceal the common necessity of the order, until little by little we too shall fail. Our inheritance has been turned over to strangers, our houses to outsiders. Monasteries that were consecrated as dwellings for monks are now becoming stables for clerics and strongholds of fighting soldiers. What shall I say? I am ashamed to utter what men without conscience are not ashamed to commit in the cloisters of monks: they disturb the seclusion of religious men; they change houses of prayer into dwellings of merchants. I suffer with your calamities, O venerable religion of Benedict; I groan that you are exposed to plunder by everyone who passes by. There is none to console you from among all your dear ones, because those who could help you are unwilling, and those who wish to assist you cannot.

O with what beautiful comeliness you once flourished in the Church of God, when you brought forth men distinguished by every holiness and knowledge, and, like a youth strong and steadfast, you were accustomed to drive away from yourself every dishonorable manner of life. But now, as if worn out by old age and the long oppression of days, you have failed, and the strength of your youth has been taken away, because your beauty is no longer in you. Where now is your zeal? Where is the beauty of your face? Where is your honor? Where is the power once widespread throughout the whole world? Where are your sons around your table, learned and most holy men? Where is the study of letters, venerable to God and the angels, whose ardor once made you exalted above all orders? They have already failed, and you have been reduced almost to nothing; you have lost the beauty of your youth, and the strength of your adolescence has been scattered. To what shall I compare you, or to what shall I liken you, secluded daughter? To what shall I equal you, and console you, mother of monks? Great as the sea is your contrition; who will heal you? O if you had foreseen this ruin in any way and guarded against it, how blessed all nations would call you. But you said in your abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Plenty has made you poor, and the abundance of temporal things has stripped you of all your adornment. You have begotten perverse sons, disobedient, rebellious against holy institutions, who, hiding under the honorable cloak of your [unclear], have stripped you bare of all your beauty. Alas, mother and virgin, you have grown old in a foreign land; you have been counted with the dead; there is no appearance of your beauty. Arise now, O Benedict, most holy prince of monks, and see your order, which you founded, and your inheritance, which you planted. Look upon the monks who have forgotten your profession, immersed in earthly things, given over to pleasure, all full of the springs of vices. You once gave them the best precepts for living; you commanded them to be free from the cares of secular business; you ordered their hearts to be stretched out to the love of God alone. Rise, I say, O most blessed father, and see the desolation of your order, which is confounded within by its own sons and scattered without by enemies.
