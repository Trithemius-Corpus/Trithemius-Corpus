These things, which you judge hard and burdensome, are so proper to the Rule that without them no one deserves to be called a monk.

Tell me, I ask, transgressor of your vow and of your order, tell me: do you still wish to live blessedly? Do you still hope that you can come to eternal life? You will answer: “That question you ask is foolish. Who would not wish to live blessedly? Who does not hope to come to eternal life? Therefore I both desire to live blessedly and trust that I can come to the heavenly fatherland.” If, then, it is so, if you trust that you will receive the blessed life, why do you abandon, spurn, and despise the way that leads to it?

You are mistaken, wretched unhappy man; you are deceived. One does not come to the kingdom except through humility, nor to rest except through labor, through abstinence, through obedience, through poverty. For Scripture says: “Through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of God.” But you, unhappy apostate, who cast away the order, spurn humility, refuse obedience, hate poverty, despise patience: by what rashness do you presume to such heights? If the mouth that lies kills the soul, unless you do penance you have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God, you who lie not to man but to God, who violate the pact, who deny the faith, who retract the vow of religion made to God.

The dispensation of the Roman pontiff, which you seek by fraud, recount with falsehood, and extort by importunate petitions, will not save you, I dare to say. For the four things which we said make a dispensation due we see to have come together not at all in your frauds. For that transfer which you make from a holy order is neither useful nor necessary, and for that reason is unfitting and dishonorable. In it, indeed, will is put in place of necessity, pleasure in place of utility, carnality in place of fittingness, impiety in place of honorableness. Is this the due occasion for your dispensation?

Keep for yourself your little letters obtained by fraud; keep the instruments of denial and apostasy. It is good for me to rest under the Rule of the most pious father Benedict, in which, if I have offended in anything through frailty, I trust that I have him as a pious advocate before the strict Judge; whose order I love, venerate, and guard with such diligence as I can. But I do not know what patron you will have, you who cast away the order, offend all the saints, and publicly lie to God. For the Rule shows that he who, after profession of the order, lives otherwise than he promised is to be condemned as a mocker of almighty God. For it has this: “Therefore, if he has acted otherwise, let him know that he is to be condemned by him whom he mocks.”

Hear these things, sons of Belial; deserters of the order, attend, and if there is any sense in you, tremble. “Let him know,” it says, “that the converted monk is to be condemned by him whom he mocks.” What greater mockery, what crime more wicked than to deny the faith promised to God, to set aside the vow, to lie to him, to withdraw service from him? I wonder, and indeed greatly, whether ever, even for an hour, apostates so criminal can have true joy, because of the confusion of conscience and the remorse of synderesis. But, to confess plainly what I think, I can be induced by no persuasions to believe that they will thereafter ever live without the very grave torment of their conscience.

By probable experience we have learned that the monks of whom we speak, after the transfer of order, become almost mad: prone to every shameful work, desperate, without devotion, without affection, without piety, without conscience, without honorableness, [unclear], troubled, witless, and obstinate; so much so that they care nothing, notice nothing, wish to hear nothing about God, about his kingdom, about purgatory, about hell, about death, about virtue, about seemly conduct. And in them is fulfilled that saying of the wise man: “When the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.” And he always presumes that with a troubled and defiled conscience. Hence such apostates, as if freed from long imprisonment, boldly hurl themselves into every vice, and compensate for the shortness of time by the magnitude of their crimes.

They are offended if anyone wishes to speak before them about the love of God, the study of the Scriptures, or the honorableness of life. Nor is it any wonder if they cannot hear the words of God, to whom after so unspeakable an apostasy they prove unfaithful. They have hated the one who rebukes in the gate; they do not admit the words of salvation; they despise the sermon of penance.
