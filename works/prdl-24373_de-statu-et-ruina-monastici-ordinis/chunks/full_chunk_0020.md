O feeble zeal, which tenacity renders useless. If we will not give money for our brothers, when shall we expose our life? If we do not contribute a few expenses for the preservation of the order, when shall we give our bodies? He who is grasping in a small matter, when will he be generous in a great one?

I shall now speak to you, fathers, who pretend not to notice that the order has been placed in such great dangers and needs help. Blessed Benedict made you rich from poor men; now he asks alms from you, and, shameful to say, does not receive them. Behold how many there are among us, if I may say so with pardon, who divide their substance among frivolous persons, prepare banquets for birds and dogs, enrich their parents and kinsmen from the goods of the monastery, and squander the rest on empty pleasures. Few are those who grieve over the desolation of our order; fewer think about a remedy; very few supply assistance. Is the sacred order of Benedict, once the most excellent among all, thus not defended from the assault of enemies? Wake up, fathers, while watchfulness is of use, and do not repent too late that you have slept.

Look at the officials of other orders, how carefully, how zealously, how steadfastly they defend the rights, privileges, and monasteries of their religion, sparing neither expenses, nor labors, nor contributions, so that they may preserve their orders in vigor. Are you inferior to them? Are you poorer than the mendicants? If one wishes to consider the number of monasteries of the order, we are more numerous; if the substance of temporal goods, richer; if force and authority, undoubtedly more outstanding. What is lacking to us, except zeal and constancy, that they possess? Why then do we hesitate? Why are we ensnared in sluggish idleness? Why do we not keep watch for the defense of the order? Do you not know, fathers, that the laws assist those who are vigilant? It is not good patience by which the honor of God is neglected, the worship due to him is diminished, and the integrity of the holy order is violated.

What shall I say? You already grieve over monasteries that have been transferred, and yet you do not undertake to preserve those that are to be transferred. You put private things before common ones; for that reason your zeal bears little fruit. Each one of us, as I see, thinks himself safe if, when his neighbor’s house is burning, his own wall does not immediately grow hot. But O folly, O stupidity deservedly to be blamed. Whoever, entangled in so many dangers, is not stirred concerning his own safety, after a little while will be compelled, when he has fallen, to beat himself.

Therefore, fathers, if we desire to keep our houses unhurt by fire, let us meet the fire with water while it is still far away. For if the flame is allowed to pass as far as the neighboring places, thought is taken for a remedy much too late. Hence the poet says: Resist at the beginnings; medicine is prepared too late, when evils have grown strong through long delays. Dangers that are ignored are strengthened by time, and what in the beginning readily admits a cure gradually becomes an incurable wound. At the same time take care that an error, neglected in [unclear], does not in the course of time grow beyond measure, when no one is found who will steadfastly set himself against it on behalf of the order.

But if you say that opposition has likewise always been made to the authors of dismemberment, and that they nonetheless prevailed, I answer with that Virgilian saying: because the herdsmen came late. After the confirmation of letters, a Latin sentence is not easily recalled. For if Pilate was unwilling to change what he had once written, how will the supreme pontiff, the vicar of Christ, readily revoke a decree once made? A sentence is more easily prevented than revoked. Moreover, he who neglects his right yields to his adversary. All must keep watch in a case that touches all, because what is snatched from one is judged to have been taken from all. Whenever a monastery is seized from our order, our common inheritance is in part dissipated. If anyone should attempt to occupy your house or field, you at once accuse him before a judge, at once request an inhibition, and until you defeat your adversary you do not desist from the lawsuit. Why then do we all not do in the public causes of the order what each one does in private matters? Will that be impossible for many which one man does? Unless perhaps where there is a multitude, there is also confusion, so that what is entrusted to different persons is preserved more negligently. For we gladly embrace what each person commands to be nearest to himself. But while, with the body drawn together on every side, like a hedgehog, we turn over our own concerns within ourselves, we cast aside care for the order.
