Rise up also, O great Pope Gregory, you who forbade, under anathema, places sacred to God belonging to monks to be transferred to secular uses; and see monks driven from monasteries, and worldly clerics thrust in. You, once the prince of all churches, used to devote the greatest care to monastic honor, taking pains with immense solicitude that nothing should be presumed in the sacred order outside the Rule of Benedict. Behold now the manner of life of monks, O most reverend prelate, stripped of all honor, full of all foulness. See how monasteries dedicated to your order are handed over into the hands of clerics, and those which once could be compared to angelic choirs now become brothels of harlots.

Rise up, most glorious emperor and unconquered defender of the Christian faith, Charles, glory of the Germans, and see the calamity of the monks: you who once made the order of Father Benedict glorious, and by your zeal spread it through almost the whole world. See now the monasteries which you once founded, all exposed to the plundering of enemies. You who once revered the monastic order as the camp of God, now have compassion, O holy prince, on it as despised by all and stripped bare of its goods. You were once accustomed, with immense care, to bring lukewarm abbots and monks back to the observance of regular discipline, and, once reformed, to defend them with immense solicitude from every harassment by your own people or by any others.

Rise up also, Berno, abbot of the monastery of Cluny, together with your successors, you who in ancient times magnificently restored the holy order when it was collapsing, and brought it back wholly to its former beauty. Rise, I say, now, and see how the iniquity of monks abounds; rage rules; crime overflows; pleasure makes almost all men go mad. The beauty of reformation has been taken away. Discipline has collapsed. Zeal for holy conduct has perished; humility has breathed its last.

Rise up, William, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, most reverend father, you who restored the Cluniac renewal in Germany after its fall; you who provided abundant institutes for monastic conduct; you who by your prayers strengthened the limbs of the sick; you who illuminated the German nation with doctrine and holiness like a most brilliant star. Rise also, you most excellent fathers, authors of various reformations, and see that your labors have now perished: constitutions neglected, exhortations spurned, the solemn warnings of solicitude despised. By life and doctrine you made the monastic order glorious, beloved by God, venerable to angels, holy to yourselves, reverend to men.

Where now are those merits? Where now are those prayers of the saints? Alas, venerable mother Religion, to what great calamity you have come. Those who once glorified you now despise you; those who honored you now scorn you. You have abandoned the commandments of your lawgiver; you have cast aside the study of the Scriptures; therefore you have become contemptible. The sons whom you bore have done this evil to you. For as soon as monks began to savor earthly things, to neglect sacred readings, to put inward things after outward ones, your best color was changed into foulness. But what of it? He who falls often resolves to rise again; but you, once fallen, never rose again altogether.

What could Cluniac, what could Specuensian, what could [Ycalian?], what could Melk, what could the very recent Bursfelde reform, and any other reforms besides these, accomplish for the whole order, spread throughout the entire world? For although the Cluniac reform poured forth the light of its honor in all provinces of the world, nevertheless it left many monasteries in different kingdoms in their former deformity. For from that time when our religion first fell, there has never been any general reformation. O how easy is the descent to Avernus; how ready the will to sin; how very great, after a fall, is the difficulty of rising again to uprightness. All, so to speak, have fallen; few have risen from their deformity; many are rotting away in their pleasures.

I grieve over you, O flower of monastic beauty; I grieve, and indeed vehemently, and if I cannot aid you otherwise, I pour out tears both hot and most bitter for your contrition. But you also, O fathers most worthy of reverence, who love the honor of your order, who grieve over the idle crushing of our mystical Joseph, who desire to come to the inheritance [unclear], consider the state of our religion; look upon its desolation; think of a remedy.
