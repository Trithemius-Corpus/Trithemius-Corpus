They willingly commit what, once they have committed it, they make a crime. It is boldness wholly characteristic of revilers, and shamelessness befitting the shameless. Often the avaricious man rebukes the greedy, the lustful man the immodest, the proud man the haughty, the criminal the base. What, fathers, what do you think? Can deceivers of this kind be found even among the visitors themselves? I dare not say it; yet I have experience of it. Many say many things; few show in deeds what they speak. They cannot reform themselves and their own, and yet they strive to force others to observance. They say great things and do the smallest. Foolish presumption, which, neglecting its own affairs, is carried away to those of others. He who is not sufficient for himself, for whom will he be sufficient? He who cannot bring himself and his own to amendment of life, when will he be able to amend others? I have said these things against those who are such men, who condemn in others what they scorn to amend in themselves. But as for zealous, good, and suitable visitors, of whom I have known several, I would not wish anything to have been said as an injury. But you, most excellent fathers, who have to choose visitors, take care to appoint better men to that office, if you desire to bring monks to regular observance. For the whole of regular discipline lies with the visitors: when continued it grows; when neglected it fails. Indeed, if the visitors diligently fulfill their office, they will preserve reformed monasteries in regular observance, and will easily bring unreformed ones back to a better state. But if they are negligent, even things well begun by others they would quickly destroy. For nothing so confounds unity, nothing so disturbs peace, nothing so injures charity, as the ignorance of visitors. It grows heated not by reason but by an undiscerning will. Experience has very often taught us what great disturbances, dissensions, angers, quarrels, slanders, and hatreds unskilled visitors have stirred up in congregations of monks: men for whom everything consists in command, and nothing in knowledge and charity. But I have said enough about this, lest I seem to accuse those whom, because of the dignity of their office, we are commanded to treat with honor.

*How Dangerous the Government of Souls Is. Chapter XI.*

If we considered how dangerous a thing the government of souls is, how heavy the burden of pastoral care, how subject it is to strict examination, it would be a wonder if ever in this life our heart presumed upon security. For Scripture says that very hard judgment will be made upon those who preside. For, as blessed Benedict attests in the Rule, the abbot will have to render an account to God for the souls of all the monks entrusted to him. Therefore he says: Let the abbot who has been ordained always consider what a burden he has undertaken, and to whom he will render an account of his stewardship. Certainly it is both a heavy and dangerous burden to render an account for the souls of many, since each person can scarcely answer for himself. O what will abbots devoted to earthly things answer to the supreme shepherd, who destroy the souls of their brothers by evil example, and feed them neither by word nor by deed? What shall I say about certain abbots of our order, who neither read the Rule of Saint Benedict which they profess, nor, if they read it, understand it? I would be lying if I said that I did not know abbots established under our chapter who are wrapped in such darkness of ignorance that they do not understand even the smallest sentence from the Rule. They neither know nor love the divine Scriptures; submerged in the vanities of the world, they vanish away, serving the desires of the flesh and not of the spirit. According to the precept of our lawgiver, the abbot ought to be learned in the divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth new things and old; chaste, sober, merciful, and so forth. What will these men say, what will they teach, what exhortation will they give, who understand nothing of the holy Scriptures, nothing of regular discipline? O good God, to what misery our order has come, which once was full of most learned and most holy men. Where now is love of regular observance? Where is study of the Scriptures? Where do we find the traces of honorable conduct?
