What will you answer? How will you excuse yourself? Who is going to make a distinct plea to the judge for your deliverance? What advocate will you then be able to have, you who now are the advocate of the wicked against God? Would that you were wise and understood; would that you looked to the end, and by no means entangled yourself in the sins of others. But, inflamed by the torches of avarice, you think only of the gains of this life; you show that you have no understanding at all of future calamities. Therefore, just as birds are caught in a snare, so in your sins and in those of the men whom you made apostatize from the order, when you do not expect it, you will be caught. O how harsh a sentence they will receive by whose counsel monks have despised their order. You lied about a great advantage in the petition, you who intend only this: that you may disgracefully empty out the honor of the order. Is this how the worship of God is increased? Is this that peace promised round about to men? You will never make bad monks into good canons. But he who makes men bad, what does he deserve except the reward of the bad? Monks who, having despised the order, return to the world are bad. For unless they were bad and sinners, they would by no means have deserted the holy order. For no one hates the good except a bad man; no one withdraws from the holy purpose that he vowed except a perverse man. But he who offers himself as patron to perverse men in evil causes makes himself a participant in the sins of others. To be a good canon in the church of God is holy; but to be a monk for the love of God is of greater perfection. A canon is permitted to pass over to monks as to a higher grade, but a monk is forbidden to descend to canons. Nor will a dispensation make lawful what has been obtained by false accounts. If there is necessity in the petition, integrity in the intention of the petitioner, and honorableness in the use of the dispensation, I say boldly that the pope can dispense with a monk so that he may become a canon, yet in such a way that he is not absolved without necessity from that by which he is known to be bound to God. For I am certain that the supreme pontiff intends to absolve no one from any approved religious order without evident common advantage and manifest necessity. But the advocates of apostates recount false causes as true ones; they fabricate necessity; they lie about advantage; they pretend to honorableness, which they flee. The root and cause of this whole evil is founded on pleasure alone and hatred of discipline. But when the cause of the account is false, the dispensation does not free the one who obtained it, but condemns him. The supreme pontiff, after hearing the accounts of petitioners, is accustomed to answer cautiously: “If it is so,” he says, “as is related, let what is requested be done, in the manner and form that follows.” He answers cautiously; he grants secretly; and, once the dispensations have been heard under fixed limits, they are transgressed, while they consider not what they have obtained but what they wished to obtain. O senseless misery, O unhappy men, why do you deceive yourselves? Why do you voluntarily destroy your souls? The intention of the dispenser does not absolve you from what you promised to Saint Benedict. But you usurp more for yourselves than the pope granted; indeed you assume the whole without permission, which you unlawfully desired to obtain. It is true what a certain man, still surviving among us, said about some of us, when, with excommunication, he had stripped them of the monastic habit: “And if,” he says, “I have taken off the habit from them, nevertheless, according to the tenor of the bull, they are still monks before God.” Unhappy apostates, who will deliver you from the hand of the living God? Who will be able to defend you from the accusation of Saint Benedict? What will it profit you that here you have been made canons from ex-monks without attack, if at the last judgment of God it happens that you are numbered with the reprobate? For truly, unless Saint Benedict, prince of monks, consents, no dispensation excuses you. Your transfer does not have regard to the proper end, and for that reason it will be inexcusable. Question your consciences, and consider with what intention you withdrew from the order. If for the love of God, if from desire for the heavenly homeland, if for the salvation of souls, if for the advancement of virtues and the state of a holier life, you had put off the habit. But in all these things your intention was contrary; therefore the transfer from the order is deservedly judged alien to the will of God.
