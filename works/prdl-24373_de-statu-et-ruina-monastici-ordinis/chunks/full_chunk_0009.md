See, monks cast off the habit; monasteries are turned into dwellings of clerics; and while we attend to private advantages, while we flee labors, while we spare expenses, while we serve our own avarice, the order perishes, religion passes away, the worship of God vanishes. Many monasteries have already, through the wickedness of the times, not to say our own sloth, been transferred away from the order; and unless we keep watch otherwise, the rashness of wicked men threatens that greater losses must be inflicted upon us. Certainly, where the audacity of fools is not at once repressed, an occasion for attempting similar things is offered to the rest. What shall I say of the monks whom we still have in the order by habit and tonsure alone? And, lest we put the members before the head, what is more wretched in this unhappy time than deformed abbots? They care for nothing less than the Rule; they pursue nothing more zealously than lust, avarice, pleasure, pride, and the vanity of the world. Thus, when the head is infected, all the members of the body are corrupted. Now in monasteries where the Rule is despised, where discipline is not observed, what else is seen than a certain spring of all vices? No obedience, no poverty, no chastity is held among them out of love of virtue; among them, to live without crime is a crime. These are corrupt men, perverse monks, craftsmen of deceit, authors of fraud, ministers of iniquity, friends of wickedness; they flee discipline, now despise observance, destroy the order, confound unity. And truly, fathers, unless you quickly provide remedies for these evils, in a short time you will see the order diminished, and you will grieve for it. But perhaps you say: vigilance has never been lacking; our diligence has never ceased; we have always opposed the rashness of evil men; we have always tried to prevent the dismemberment of monasteries, and we have never accomplished anything. What do our labor, our efforts, our heavy expenses accomplish, when princes and rectors of churches oppose themselves so powerfully? I know, fathers, that what you say is true; I know the deeds, I know they have been continued. But I beg you not to take my words in a bad sense, if I dare to answer these objections. Your vigilance in the causes of the order is known to me; your labors are evident; the magnitude of the expenses is proven; perseverance in these matters has always been applied.

But I should like you to remember, O fathers, two verses of Horace in which he says: There is measure in things, and finally there are fixed limits, beyond and short of which what is right cannot stand. We have many and different adversaries; one must not fight against all in one manner, but in different ways, in varied ways, with discretion. We are monks, professors of humility; the battle must be begun by us with prayers, entreaties, and supplications; high Jupiter must not be provoked by ferocity; a headlong sentence must not be brought against the gods. Divine powers are placated by prayers, not by threats. But enough of this. For I believe, fathers, that you understand what I mean. Nor are these things to be explained indiscriminately to everyone, but only to those who have zeal for the order and the power to carry it out.

*Against those who transfer themselves from the order, or have already long since transferred themselves. Chapter 3.*

Whether I am justly moved enough against those who try to dismember themselves from our order, I do not sufficiently know; and if I did know, I would hardly dare to say, since the patience of the Almighty tolerates them so long and lashes them so late. But when I look upon the end of those who have already long since dismembered themselves from the order, I consider this long-suffering of God’s patience sufficiently avenged by strict chastisement. For, as we have learned from the report of those who knew many of them, few of those who cast off the monk died a good death. Rather, called suddenly from this life without signs of penitence and without the ecclesiastical sacraments, they gave a manifest indication of their own unhappiness. What, then, shall we say to these things? Shall we rejoice in the destruction of the dying? Far be it. It is the Lord who long tolerates sinners here so that they may be converted; it is he who condemns more harshly those who are not converted. But we, with the prophet, fear his sentence, saying: You are just, O Lord, and your judgment is right. But the just man, by whatever death he has been overtaken, will be in refreshment.
