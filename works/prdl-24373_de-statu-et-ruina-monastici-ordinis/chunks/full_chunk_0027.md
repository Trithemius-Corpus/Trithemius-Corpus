Where are those terrible oaths of all the abbots of our province, by which they bound themselves to regular observance into the hands of the same cardinal, before the altar of Saint Stephen at Würzburg? Where is the promised regular observance? Behold, fathers, you have one hundred twenty-seven abbeys under your chapter; of these, from the three observances already mentioned, scarcely seventy have remained under reform. Yet there are, I do not doubt, some who think themselves excellently reformed; but their manner of life denies it. Would that the abbots would heed the threat, often repeated, of our holy lawgiver, in which he says that the government of souls is liable to a most exact accounting. Would that they compelled the sheep entrusted to them to the pastures of heavenly life, lest they themselves perish eternally with their sheep. Look at the manner of life of abbots as well as monks, reeking all around without the law of monastic propriety; of this, once known to me, it is shameful for me to speak, and dreadful for you, most reverend fathers, to hear. For the three vows of religion, which because of their excellence are called substantial, they care for no more than if they had never promised to observe them. They confound everything; they defile everything; they presume everything.

If we look to divine worship, they perform it so confusedly, so disorderly, so dissolutely, that neither do the words sound out their sense, nor the melody its proper tone. For they are devoid of all learning and of the good arts, so that they understand absolutely nothing of the things they sing. Hence it happens that without affection, without devotion, without the savor of inward sweetness, they do not so much discharge the canonical hours as confound them. In their hands the book of divine Scripture is never seen. The practice of edifying speech is never taken up. No account is ever taken of the formation of morals. The whole day is consumed in obscene rumors. All time is enslaved to games and feasting. No mention is ever made of reform. The madness of those at odds with one another rages, and among the cowls, peace having been broken, the monastery, with the law despised, [unclear] to insane lawsuits. Spears and sword are ready there, so that you would think what you see not a monastery but a stronghold of men at war.

Witnesses to their own possession, contrary to the statutes, contrary to the laws, contrary to the Rule, they each inhabit separate houses, in which they practice not the pursuits of monks but those of clerics. They neither fear God nor love him. They think nothing of the future life, since they put the concerns of the soul after the pleasures of the flesh. They do not read the Rule of monks; they do not heed the statutes; they despise the decrees of the fathers. They spurn the vow of poverty; they know nothing of chastity; they hold obedience in contempt. And would that, since they refuse to be chaste, they would at least deign to be continent. The filth of vices fumes all around; and what everyone knows, we alone, who are commanded to correct it, do not know. Why should I use many words? They commit so many, so great, so various illicit acts against the Rule of Saint Benedict that I would think it easier to establish a monk anew than to be able to amend the life of such men. For nothing of monastic propriety remains inviolate among them; instead everything lies neglected, everything despised, everything contemned and torn. Angers, quarrels, dissensions, envies, detractions, curses, oaths, insults, and things like these are not judged among them to be sins, because what is public has begun to be lawful. A place lies open to vices and crimes; virtue alone does not deserve admission. They laugh if anyone begins to give a discourse on penitence before them; and, as Mantuan says, they despise those above and Acheron alike. There is no fear of punishment, no dread of the judge. You would see among them, as if in the spring of all vices, pride, greed, lust, gluttony, sloth, anger, hatred, and every pleasure of the flesh abound; if they can grasp it, they rejoice; if they cannot, nevertheless they eagerly desire it. The oaths have collapsed in their filth, and, as though reason had been lost, they are unwilling to be called back to penitence.

O good God, to what great calamity has an order once exalted been brought, one which could deservedly have been preferred to all orders. O holy father Benedict, who once were so solicitous for your religion, why do you abandon us at the end? Why do you not look upon your inheritance? Why do you not come to the aid of your order, now already falling? Have you cast aside care for it?
