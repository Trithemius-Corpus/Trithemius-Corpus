For in a chapter of abbots vices are to be corrected; exhortations to virtues are to be made, so that the bad may find what they should fear, and the good may hear what may make them better. Therefore various things are read for edification, so that increases may always be present to good minds. There the abbot is instructed concerning his governance; there he is warned concerning the danger of pastoral care. There what he should do is commanded; what he should avoid is forbidden for the love of God. In short, when we come together thus for chapter, many useful things result, which, if we seek the salvation of souls, are very necessary. For, to pass over the rest, mutual acquaintance in charity is conceived there; experience shows how much this conduces to love of regular observance. There, from acquaintance, fraternal charity is both preserved and increased; otherwise it would quickly grow lukewarm if it did not have the nourishment of mutual recognition. Here easy access lies open to uniformity, where, by honorable conduct, from the mutual conference of the fathers a fire is prepared; from the fellowship of holiness an example is furnished. These are the camps of God, O fathers, and the strongholds of our order, by which all the devices of our adversaries are reduced to nothing. But there is not one sincerity in all; nor does equality of conduct appear among you. Whence could so great a disparity of manners have arisen in one order, where capitular unity is seen? Who has divided between you and the Rule of Saint Benedict, O fathers, long-haired with thick-grown hair? Why do you not look to uniformity, you who celebrate an assembly of unity? If you love the holy order which you profess, why do you not love uniformity in it? What is the use to you of the shadow of a hairy head? Whom do you think the height of a curled crown adorns, O ringlet-wearers? Believe me, fathers: a face covered with hair is reckoned a disgrace, not an honor, in a monk. O how beautiful this assembly would be, if it came together, as it ought, in one discipline of regular observance. What shall I say? I praise the assembly, but I rebuke the deformity in its members. For because of the confusion of the order, the presence of unity grows cheap. The outer habit judges that the inner clothing reproves the life of many. Truly, fathers, this disparity of manners and habits offends the provincial chapter not slightly; and it is known to have been instituted for this reason, that equality of life might be preserved above all things. For outward discord is an indication of inward discord. Therefore, so that we may be of one mind in observance of regular discipline, let us strive first of all to be conformed in habit and tonsure.

*How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our province would be. Chapter 9.*

How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our province may be, you yourselves know, O fathers, who are not ignorant of the condition of the monks of this time. I have known it as known to you, with how great labors, expenses, and sweats the fathers once reformed our order; concerning them we find memorials, both few and weak, in our age. But among all the reforms of our order in our province, three most recently shone forth before the rest: namely the Castellan, taking its name from Castellum; the Melk, from Melk; the Bursfeld, from Bursfeld. All these, as they were fervent in the beginning, so, gradually cooling again, they approach their end. For the first two, as if worn out with age, have been reduced to a small number. The last, however, which is also the later, because it is still younger, also seems to be more steadfast. Yet even it nevertheless now seems to grow cool again in certain of its members, and to decline back again toward the laxity of the former life. So once the Cluniac reform, known throughout the whole world, when it carried itself far and wide, gradually failed, as though its strength had been lost through expansion. For it is said in the proverb: everything that grows old is near destruction. For a reform, however celebrated or holy it may have been, unless it is often renewed through the diligence of prelates, gradually vanishes. For the more it grows with time, the more it is diminished in fervor.
