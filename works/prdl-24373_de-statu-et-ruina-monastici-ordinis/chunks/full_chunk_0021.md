Shameful is the keeping of riches by which the commonwealth is not helped in necessity. What will an insatiable desire for gold profit you, if the desolation of your order, so holy, so venerable, so glorious, should prevail? If our predecessors had not kept watch for the order with greater care than we, the name of our religion would long ago have failed in many monasteries. Let us therefore imitate the constancy of those whose inheritance we have received. If we love God, if we love the honor of the order, if we desire to preserve it, let us think in time about a remedy. Let us contribute a little to the preservation of the order, so that we may deserve to receive much; let us spend a part, so that we may be able to preserve the whole; let us offer cheap things, so that we may be able to acquire precious things; let us give earthly things, so that we may be able to possess heavenly things. Let us expend money, and let us expend prayer also, since by the former man is appeased, by the latter God. Whatever we have belongs to the right of our holy father Benedict; when we expend something for him for the preservation of the order, we do not give him what is ours, but return to him what is his. Indeed, we are bound to him not only for the souls committed to us, but are also obliged to render account for the substance of the monastery. Never, moreover, is a safer account rendered concerning the [revenue?] than if money is spent opportunely in the causes of the Lord. But if money is spared in the Lord's necessity, suspicion is generated of the servant's faithlessness. At the beginning of this chapter we set down the title concerning the manner of resisting the dismemberments of the wicked; lest we seem to have passed it over untouched, let us explain it briefly. And although that discussion does not pertain to me, nevertheless, since I am a foster-child and lover of your chapter as well as of the same order, I think the admonition of a disciple will not be burdensome to you. It is for you, fathers, to distinguish the true from the false; let it suffice for me to have explained what I think about it. The manner of opposing the presumptions of monks is easily learned if it is turned from example into practice. Look at the other orders, and from their diligence it will be recognized what you must do. [Each?] establishes procurators of its own in the city of Rome, who manfully resist the beginnings of evils and permit nothing to gain strength against their order. And you, fathers, unless you do the like, will feel grave losses hereafter.

If therefore it can be done, if it is pleasing, let some man be chosen from the order, zealous, devout, learned, and experienced, who fights no less by favor than by zeal; who does not prefer his own advantages to public ones; who does not flee labors; who attempts nothing by his own rashness to be done contrary to the commission given him; who, established as procurator of the order, may always keep careful watch for it in the city. I believe that if such a man were had, he would forestall many dangers of the order. For it is the greatest foresight if a procurator in the city can denounce before the pope as false and fictitious the things which perverse monks lie as true for the sake of their own dismemberment. And this is not a work of great difficulty. For all the causes which they allege are full of fear and lying. But the vicar of Christ, who grants the transfers of monks more from importunity than from favor, when he has perceived that their allegations have been repelled by the diligence of our procurator, will rejoice that he has found an occasion for denying what he grants unwillingly. For he too fears the judgment of his Lord. Therefore, if we kept watch, we would have him as defender and advocate of our order. Our negligence alone is accused; our stinginess alone is condemned.

*Against those who uselessly consume the goods of monasteries and scorn to assist the order even in a little. Chapter VI.*

But now it is pleasing to turn the force of words against those who in such great necessities do not deign to assist the holy order even in a little. These are worldly abbots, useless, given over to pleasures, who, to use the words of the Mantuan, neglecting the worship of those above and spurning the command of the Thunderer, indulge Bacchus and serve Venus. They bear sacred things with gold, store coined money in gold; with gold they pour libations of wine, with gold the panelled ceilings gleam. They cover whores with gems; they set feasts for dogs; from the spoils of the needy they are adorned, rich by plunder, elated by success, and they laugh at the powers above and at Acheron. They have no care for the salvation of the soul; no love of God, no reverence for holy things, no exercise of virtues.
