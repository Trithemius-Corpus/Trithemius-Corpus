*The Mournful Book of Johannes of Trittenheim, abbot of Sponheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict, of the Bursfeld Observance, on the condition and ruin of the monastic order, no less useful than pleasing to religious and devout men.*

To the venerable and most beloved father, lord Blasius, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau: brother Johannes, called abbot in Sponheim, sends greeting and charity.

Although your request and that of your brothers, dearest father, far exceeds the powers of my talent, nevertheless I have preferred to undertake the arduous burden rather than fail, as far as I can, to satisfy your charity. For love of God, zeal for the order, and fervor for discipline make you worthy that I should deny you nothing when you ask for the honor of God. You asked me, when I was present, with words; when absent, with frequent letters; and in a certain way you compelled me by the authority of your presidency to write something to you about the calamity of our order, so that you might stir the fathers, to be assembled with you in chapter, to compassion for it.

The request is holy, but the difficulty of obedience is great. I know I shall be torn by the teeth of the envious, who will judge me presumptuous, and [unclear] the things that lie open to the eyes of all. If they do not wish these things to be written, let them grieve that they were even done. In this work only charm of speech is lacking; and for this I do not fear being blamed among religious men. By whom? Is truth therefore despised because it is not clothed in the ornament of eloquence? Far from it. Better is plain and true speech than ornate and false speech. But these matters are nothing to the point. I therefore submit this little work to your examination, so that, if you judge it worthy, it may be read to the brothers; but if unworthy, it may flee the eyes of the lords. Farewell. In the year of the Lord 1493, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of May.

*Here begins the mournful book of lord Johannes, abbot of Sponheim, on the ruin of the order of our holy father Benedict.*

When I call back to mind the former beauty of our order, I grieve vehemently over the deformity which it now suffers, so that I would rather shed tears than compose a mournful discourse. For who would not weep over its ruin, if he weighs its ancient glory? O, if only we would at some time recall to memory the once golden ages of this most sacred order, when, like a river of paradise in doctrine and holiness, it watered the whole Church throughout the world, we would easily perceive that its present condition is nothing. Indeed our observance, which we call regular, when compared with the institutions of the ancients, is found not only lesser, but in many respects more lax. How the gold has grown dim; how the finest color has changed! The splendor of extraordinary holiness, once gleaming, has been darkened by desire for earthly things; the rule of honor has yielded to the enticements of pleasures. Therefore let my eyes bring forth tears by day and by night, and let them not be silent, because the religion of my people has been crushed with a great crushing, with a very grievous wound. If I go out to the fields, behold the idle and vain; if I enter the monasteries, behold those wasted by pleasures. For I find many professors of the rule of father Benedict; I discern few walking according to his tradition. “You have multiplied the nation,” says the prophet to the Lord, “you have not increased the joy,” because, since the holy exercises of monks fail in many, corrupt hearts do not admit the true joy that is born of virtue. If only we would awaken, even late at last, after the destruction of so many souls, which, despising the yoke of regular observance, have descended into the abyss of damnation.
