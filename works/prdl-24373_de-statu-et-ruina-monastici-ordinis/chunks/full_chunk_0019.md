I have heard from many with how great bitterness of mind, at the point of death, many transgressors of the order have been constrained: those who from monks became canons, or also those who were the authors of transfers of this sort. For they said that they grieved for no sin so much as for that one, because they knew that they had given their consent, or their help, to the transfer of the order. If the transfer of monasteries from the order is good, why did those who had made it grieve when they were dying? But if it is evil, why do you make it and take it upon yourselves? Let all who occupy the monasteries of monks remember how stern an anathema they are marked with by blessed Pope Gregory: those who do not fear to turn the property of monasteries consecrated to God to alien uses. Let Thietgandus, formerly archbishop of [Trier?], be an example to them: when he had received from Pope Adrian, in the city of Rome, the monastery of Saint Andrew in [unclear], and, after that had been [unclear], had been warned by Saint Gregory in a third vision not to occupy the dwellings of monks any longer, since, when ordered to withdraw, he ignored it, he perished with all his people by a hard death. Whether those who occupy the monasteries of monks are secure before God, we leave to their consciences. But he who possesses another man’s goods without that man’s will is bound to restitution; whoever occupies the goods of some saint against his will, let him consider what he deserves. We have now used a sufficiently long discourse against the transgressors of our order; and so let us now come to the things that remain.

*Chapter V.*

*How these dismemberments of apostate monks are to be opposed.*

Although both my mind and necessity itself, having been constrained, offer an abundance of material for inveighing against the destroyers of our order, nevertheless, since those are not present who might be recalled to penance by exhortations, I think that enough has been said against them. For they are senseless, those who so wickedly oppress the honor of their order; and I judge that they must be resisted not only with words, but with deeds. For those who do not fear God will by no means acquiesce in our admonitions. Come then, most reverend fathers, who have assembled in chapter for the adornment of our order, who labor for its restoration: recall to mind its former state, and consider into how great a calamity it has fallen in this time. Behold, monasteries are snatched from the hands of Saint Benedict; our order is being destroyed all around by the enemies of God; monks return to the world; the impious tear apart our religion; strangers are endowed with our goods. What shall I say of internal wars? What shall I report of civil evils? It is no wonder, fathers, take heed, if we are confounded without, since we are torn apart within by our own people. Our abbots kick against discipline, tear apart the unity of the order, neglect the statutes of chapters, spurn visitations, and despise your censures, fathers. Unless you fight against these men with all censures [unclear] to bring back [unclear] under the order for long. Why then do you dissemble? How long do you sleep? When will you wake up? When will you fight for the inheritance of Saint Benedict? Fathers, arm yourselves with zeal for God, for order, and for discipline, because it is time; fight for your lawful rights; defend the rights of sanctity. Stand manfully against transfers of the order. Behold, from our order in the province of Mainz alone six monasteries have already been transferred in our times; and unless we keep watch otherwise, we shall suffer more grievous losses day by day. Thus, while we dissemble, not to say grow torpid, the order is gradually diminished, until at last it will be entirely scattered. Why do we not meet such great dangers? Or, while the others perish, do we wisely trust in our own security? Did not the poet truly say: your affair is at stake when the nearest wall is burning? Yesterday it was for them; tomorrow a like danger will threaten us. Each one of you, I do not doubt, loves his own monastery, and would in no way consent that it be transferred from the order; indeed, he would wish not only its present but also its future happiness to be extended for eternal years. Why, then, do you not defend in common what you fear to lose individually? I fear, fathers, that in this most evil dissembling our tenacity may be accused. We love money and lose a monastery. We fear a few expenses and lose broad dwellings. We are unwilling to lay out a modest sum of money, and we suffer most abundant monasteries to pass from the order to outsiders.
