*How disobedient and rebellious abbots are to be compelled to assist the order. Chapter 7.*

If judges lack authority to restrain rebels, legislation will be made in vain. For we always strive after what is forbidden, and unless compelled we resist commands. For where there is no fear of power, the condition of law is quickly despised. Therefore the preservation of law is the fear of power. But where the laxity of power dissembles, the vigor of law totters. For a constitution, however holy or necessary, is quickly carried into oblivion if there is no authority by which contempt may be punished. Take away power, and the commonwealth will be nothing other than a confusion full of sedition. For rustic savagery is not led by reason, nor calmed by gentleness. Among our own too, may God amend it, there are many abbots and monks, coarser than rustics, more savage than beasts, who are not bent by gentleness to the observance of the commandments of God and of the Rule, and are never persuaded by exhortations, however holy. If the authority of superiors does not oppose these men, they will never be led by any obedience of the chapter.

For this reason the supreme pontiffs, founders of this most sacred provincial chapter, established for us many suitable remedies by which we can compel the abbots subject to the chapter to assist the order. We have authority; we hesitate about power. And what is authority worth without power? If you wish, most honored fathers, that I should say more openly what I have wished to depict concerning the power of authority, you will pardon me if I have noted your pusillanimity, not to say negligence. Behold, we know that one hundred twenty-seven abbeys have been placed under our provincial chapter, but we do not receive from all of them the subsidies of the contribution owed. Why is this? We have received authority to exact it, but we do not exercise power. We receive what is freely offered; we fear to compel the disobedient. For I am certain that many abbots of our province contribute nothing to the assistance of the order; indeed, if they are asked, they contradict as if by prescription. What is it that we are doing? Why do we dissemble so long? To one running of his own accord we apply spurs; to one lying down, indeed kicking back, we do not even apply lashes. From this is born contempt of our statutes; the authority of the chapter is despised; the inheritance of the order is everywhere laid waste.

These things are to be brought against you, O fathers, who neglect to subdue rebels at the beginning. Consider what this dissimulation is at last going to bring forth, and if you love your order, think as soon as possible about a remedy. Weak and near dissolution is the body that is deprived of the support of its members. Behold, from one hundred twenty-seven abbots, how few you see in the present chapter, all of whom ought by statute to have been present. Where are the rest, with all the procurators of the absent? Few have contributed to the necessity of the order; fewer keep the statutes of the order; very few have assembled at the chapter. Where are the letters of excuse of those who did not send procurators? Where are the double expenses of the rebels? Who of the absent and rebellious has ever in many years been compelled to pay the penalty of double, and [unclear]? What use are our statutes to us; what do laws do; what help are the privileges of the supreme pontiffs, if in time of necessity they are not put into practice? Behold, Pope Benedict XII provided for us suitably in all things, when he promulgated the restraints of censure to be imposed against the contumacious and rebellious. Why then do we delay? Why do we not put into practice against the disobedient the remedies granted to us? Truly, fathers, this dissimulation of ours that we practice is not praiseworthy. For the rebellion of a few, if dissembled, will be an occasion for many to resist. But if a few are compelled to obedience by discipline, the boldness of the multitude will be restrained by fear. Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste, and every religious order disagreeing with itself is gradually dissipated. There is no fair hope for an order where the confusion of unity is in tumult. For the first road to desolation in every commonwealth is the disobedience of subjects. Therefore, venerable fathers, if you desire to continue the provincial chapter; if you wish to oppose the dismemberments of monks; if finally you choose to defend your order from the hands of enemies, take care first of all to call the contumacious back to obedience, lest what you acquire outside by industry you lose within through the division of subjects. This indeed must first be attempted by the sweetest exhortations; but if with hardened neck they have despised these, they must be [met] with harsher rebukes
