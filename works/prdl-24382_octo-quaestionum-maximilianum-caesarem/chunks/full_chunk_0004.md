*On Faith and Understanding, the First Question*

The first question of your most serene majesty was this: Why did the all-good God wish to be believed by mortals rather than to be known and understood, namely in that mode of knowledge and understanding by which he is known and understood by the holy angels?

I remember that, when this question was put before your most sacred majesty, I answered as follows. Almighty God, who is without beginning, pure, everlasting, and unchangeable intellect, existing above every position knowable to us, far more perfect than infinite perfections, by his most wise will created from nothing at the beginning two intellects, namely the angelic and the human. The angelic intellect, at the beginning of its creation, was made in act with respect to all knowable things, and it knew all things. The human intellect, however, at first consists in potency with respect to all things, but is ignorant of all things. Yet in the end, through many continual upward actions, it will become like the angelic, knowing both God and all things that pertain to the knowledge of the promised knowledge and understanding.

The angelic and human intellects differ as any two opposites do, namely act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, habit and privation, beginning and end, immobile and mobile, substance and accident, truth and likeness, nature and art, health and infirmity, whole and part. For the angelic intellect is as act, light, knowledge, habit, beginning, immobile, immediate, simple substance, truth, nature, and whole. The human intellect, however, is as potency, darkness, ignorance, privation, end, mobile, mediated, composite, accident, likeness, art, and part.

Hence whatever things belong to the angelic intellect from the beginning, these same things belong to the human intellect at the end, after it has laid aside the burden of flesh; for just as the beginning perfects the angelic intellect by nature, so the end, by art, after many exercises at last raises the human intellect upward. Therefore between these two intellects there is great inequality from the beginning; nevertheless in the end they are at last made equal, where the human intellect will be as great by art as the angelic had previously been by nature.

Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect, as soon as it was created, from the beginning, without any action or motion of its own, knew by nature, immovably and substantially, all things that it can understand, and as a creature became in an instant full and perfect with the knowledge of all things. But since the human intellect was created in ignorance and privation of all things, and was temporally destined for a body for its own exercise, it must by continual action, motion, and art come to knowledge of God and of all things. The end will at last make it equal to the angelic intellect, changing its potency into act and bringing it from the merely potential and possible into essence; from empty it will make it full, from imperfect perfect, from deprived possessing, from dark luminous, from nocturnal diurnal, from lunar solar, from earthly heavenly. This cannot happen while the mind is bound in the body, as the holy Christ-loving Paul says: As long as we are in the flesh, we are pilgrims away from the Lord.

To know God belongs to a pure, simple, and perfect intellect. But the human intellect, as long as it dwells in the body, is impure, mixed, unstable, and imperfect; for this reason it is ignorant of the knowledge of God, namely of that highest thing among things. But of both intellects, namely the human and the angelic, the object is the same: God, the supreme and single object of all things, toward which [unclear]; God, the single principle of things, toward which [unclear].
