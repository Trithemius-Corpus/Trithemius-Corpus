Through manifest invocation necromancers and the petty women whom we commonly call witches do wondrous things; they, corrupted by a likeness of will, have passed into the fellowship of demons. For just as in the Church of God the sacraments are signs of the invisible grace of the Holy Spirit, so in the school of demons certain characters and modes of invocation are marks of a pact between a man and an evil spirit. Hence, when witches display the signs of demons, the invoked evil spirits have readily been accustomed to come to them and to show forth dreadful acts of witchcraft and at the same time certain wondrous things. But witches and necromancers produce wondrous effects by invocation of demons in different ways. Witches subject themselves to demons by a certain kind of profession; by their ministry they trouble the air, darken storms, ravage crops, and make men and beasts sick. They carry on with demons the foul commerce of most filthy pleasure; by pernicious charms they call back from below, into sight, whomever they wish. The deeds of these women should not be called miracles, but rather acts of witchcraft, deservedly to be punished with the severest penalty. Necromancers, however, subject themselves to demons not by a manifest profession but by an implicit condition, when they promise to compel them to become visible in a circle, not only by conjurations and the display of characters, but also by sacrifices, fumigations, and vows. Their crimes are without number. Since, whether implicitly or explicitly, they are made partners in the fellowship [unclear], or are equal in deed, yet all are in agreement, to their own destruction, in damnable curiosity, by which they desire, with the aid of demons, to attain what they cannot discover naturally. All their works and volumes, full of superstitions and impiety, must be destroyed.

There is in the East a great multitude of enchanters and necromancers, who by their tricks miserably deceive human beings and long ago turned almost the whole of Asia Major and Asia Minor away from the faith of Christ. Tricks can be performed in three ways: by the ministry of demons, by the operation of nature, and by the deception of human art. No true Christian will doubt that the miracles of unbelievers are performed in one of these ways, once the determinate root of the agent is established. But today there are many among Christians who are devoted to the works of demons: enchanters, witches, reprobates concerning the faith, who deceive many people by excessive curiosity and promise impossibilities by their arts.

But by the implication of demons wondrous things are sometimes done; namely, whenever a man not sufficiently and rightly grounded in the faith desires or wishes for something otherwise than befits a Christian, even if he believes it to be no sin; and although this ought not to happen, nevertheless the devil, inserting himself, very often supplies the desired effect. Such is that practice which sufferers from spasms commonly use. On the sacred day of Parasceve they fashion a ring of any metal during the office, and afterwards they always wear it on the ring finger. These things are utterly vain and have no root of truth at all. Yet I cannot deny that I have seen certain people cured of spasm by that same vanity. Likewise, I know that certain men have opened very strong locks with words and herbs; I do not think this was done either by the power of God or by the violence of the things applied. I could bring forward countless similar things which I have seen and heard: what else are their manifest effects but certain miracles? Finally, there are some exceedingly curious people who, by persistently placing faith and confidence in characters, little slips, rings, figures, necklaces, and unknown words, often obtain wondrous effects. We by no means doubt that the devil has supplied these, not on account of the merit of holiness nor by the mercy granted by God, but rather through faithless credulity. For demons, having the greatest envy of the human race, in order to destroy the souls of mortals, gladly come to vain [unclear].
