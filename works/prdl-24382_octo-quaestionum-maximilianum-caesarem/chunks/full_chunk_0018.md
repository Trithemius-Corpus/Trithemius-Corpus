For the demon who gave the cause of the error could also, once the occasion had been taken, display a false miracle to those deceived. Hence, if miracles had been wholly lacking to Mahomet as he was persuading a rude people of a new religion, perhaps no one would have put faith in him. For he too showed such things, both by subtlety of intellect and by the cooperation of the devil, that he seemed to have confirmed certain things by miracles. Miracles were not lacking even to the idolatrous pagans, if we do not deny credence to histories. But the Jews, before the incarnation of the Son of God, receiving his law through Moses, were often made illustrious by divine miracles. Yet after the Lord’s resurrection, when the Mosaic law had been made void, those who do not believe in the only-begotten Son of God remain under the wrath of his indignation; and all miracles of truth have ceased among them. Hence, if they do any wondrous things today, they are works of demons and manifest signs of error. For the demons watch with the greatest zeal so that those men whom they have once made to wander from the way of truth, which is Christ, they may, by a continuation of fraud until death, make stubborn in error. Indeed, when God for just reason withdraws his grace from unbelievers, the malignant deceiver, an unknown spirit, approaches them; and when he sees that they are far removed from salvation, by false miracles he seduces the minds of the ignorant, as though the Lord were near. Therefore whatever miracles belong to unbelievers are works of demons; for just as there is no salvation for mortals outside the unity of the Christian faith, so without Christ the most holy power does not grant wonders. For now the demons, hiding in ambush, long since defeated and confounded by Christ’s trophy, fear to appear openly as they did before the Lord’s incarnation; and therefore among unbelievers they falsely present themselves as good spirits.

There are also some who add to the aforesaid three modes a fourth, saying that the mind or spirit of man himself can naturally do wondrous things, provided it knows how to recall itself from every adventitious thing into itself, above sense, in unity. For those who assert such things promise to do the wonders of nature, to predict future things, to disclose men’s secrets, to remove diseases, and suddenly to change men’s counsels. But that mode of miracles cannot be simply natural, certain, common, and infallible; for if it is true, which I have not experienced, some external power must now necessarily intervene and occupy the thinker in part, whether a good angel or a malignant spirit. But if none of the powers from without mixes itself in, the prophet must necessarily form the beginning of his speaking either from conjectures or from bold presumption, and pronounce a judgment sometimes true, sometimes false. Indeed, as long as the spirit dwells in the body, it understands nothing naturally without the ministry of sense. Hence I would not wholly resist if they grant that our confused and rude intellect is assisted by a like ministry.
