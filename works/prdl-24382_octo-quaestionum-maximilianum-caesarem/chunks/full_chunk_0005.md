But if the angelic is compared with the human, it is as nature is to art, and as intellect simply speaking is to reason, which is closely joined to disciplinary principles, whereas reason runs through principles to conclusions. Hence it is manifest that the angelic intellect apprehends its proper object immovably; toward this the human intellect, through the angelic medium, after many exercises of itself, as has been said, at last, by reasoning, prepares an ascent for itself. But the human intellect is separated, by the medium of angelic cognition, from the perfection of its natural object, and therefore necessarily becomes mobile, returning through that medium to its own principle. For every motion is made from one extreme through a middle to an extreme; but where there is no middle, the extremes are already immediately proximate, and for this reason immobile. But the human intellect, placed as it were in a dark prison so long as it is held in the body, is moved by sense and reason to understand, although confusedly and much too imperfectly; and for that reason it admits a medium between itself and God, which we have called angelic, bearing the body as a very great impediment to its destined perfection. Indeed a lover of wisdom said: The body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things. Therefore it was altogether necessary for us to believe God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible for that super-divine, uncircumscribable majesty to be comprehended by us, who understand nothing without the ministry of the senses and the discourse of reason. But God, existing far above every position, remains subject neither to the senses of mortals nor to reason, as he is read to have said to Moses: No man shall see me and live. Hence also the divine evangelist John says: No one has ever seen God. Nor is it any wonder. For since God is the first, eternal, purest, simplest, most perfect, immobile intellect; the creator, incomprehensible, highest, and far above every position, immeasurable and uncircumscribable, he could not be comprehended by the human intellect, which is impure, subject to composition, imperfect, mobile, created, comprehensible, lowest, and established under the measure of position.

But as to that question which seemed to arise from the first, whether God, since he is omnipotent, could have made himself knowable to the human intellect just as to the angelic, I say that no one of sound mind has ever denied that God can do all things. But we say that God cannot do those things which involve contradiction, because they are contrary to the order of wisdom that immutably disposes all things, just as they are not doubted to be contrary to the destruction of the whole; and if I shall say it, utterly opposed. For it neither was fitting, nor could in any way have happened, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect, since if mortal man, by the dictate of nature, comprehended immortal God, certainly he would no longer seem to have to be called man or mortal, but angel; and either man would have to be compared to God, or God to man. For as long as the angelic intellect stands between, the human cannot understand God. And it stands between until the death of the flesh, when the mind, having obtained natural liberty, escapes, wholly equal to the angel. You see now, I believe, emperor, that it is manifest that it neither was fitting nor was possible that the human intellect should comprehend the divine: the created, the creator; the temporal, the eternal; the mutable, the immutable; the weak, the omnipotent, since, as a certain philosopher says, there is no proportion of the finite to the infinite. It is therefore necessary for mortals to believe in immortal God, whom we can comprehend by no manifest reasoning.
