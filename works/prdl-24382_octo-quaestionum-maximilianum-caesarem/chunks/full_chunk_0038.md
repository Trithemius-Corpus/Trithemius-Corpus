For the most abundant influence of the first Being extends through individual things to whatever beings follow in fixed orders; and the things which act after him are everywhere moved and act by his power.

Therefore either God understands or acts upon nothing outside himself, which is false; or, by acting upon all things and each thing, he also understands those same things, which is true. For they would in no wise come to be unless first, as we speak in our own manner, they were understood by God himself, who makes them; just as those things are not made by the sun which are not looked upon by it. But because the Almighty understands all things and each thing that he makes, there is no doubt that he disposes, rules, and orders all things that he has made; from him things have not only that they exist, but also that they are certain causes of other things. And so, if things have from God that they are causes, from him also they have received the proper office of a cause: that is, to preserve according to their powers, and to care for what they have made. Wherefore, if all causes have this property from the first cause insofar as they are certain causes, then deservedly that first cause naturally has this goodness and providence, so that it cares for all things and each thing that it has made, insofar as it is most especially the cause of all and each of them. Indeed, unless individual things are preserved one by one by him who makes them, common things, which are preserved in individuals, will never be preserved. For neither will the whole, which consists of parts, be preserved if the parts themselves are neglected. Therefore from the intention of the first and most universal intelligence proceeds the universal distinction of things. But that intelligence is distinguished in itself from other things, and by the standard of its knowledge it distinguishes the rest among themselves. Hence, just as God knows all things and each thing most distinctly, so also he most distinctly rules, governs, and cares for them. Therefore, most clement Emperor Maximilian, I think it manifest and sufficiently shown that the providence of God, the supreme ruler, embraces, disposes, rules, and governs all lower things and each of them; for otherwise, without the provident disposition of one who watches over them, they would by no means maintain this most beautiful order that we perceive.

Nor does it escape me that the wise men of this world formerly held various opinions about providence. Some of them thought that this world was made not by providence but by chance; if their opinion were true, no place would be left for providence, but all things would necessarily drift at random, since the world, which was made by chance, could perish by chance. Against their false opinion others taught that the world could not have come into being without reason, nor certainly continue to exist unless it were ruled by the supreme prudence of its maker. Hence Boethius, a most Christian man, said: O you who govern the world by everlasting reason, begetter of earth and heaven, who command time to proceed from eternity, and, remaining stable, make all things move. And again he said: The generation of all things, and all the processes of mutable natures, and whatever is moved in any way, obtain their causes, order, and forms from the stability of the divine mind. But others thought that God cared only for incorruptible things, such as the heavenly bodies and separate substances, together with the species of things, but that he knew nothing else at all and utterly neglected all the rest. But these men erred shamefully, because the Almighty has provident care not only of incorruptible universals, but also of corruptible particulars. Hence the same Boethius again says: The high founder of things sits on the earth, and, ruling things, bends the reins: king, lord, source, origin, law, wise arbiter of justice. He is the continuous love for all things, and they seek again the good by the end he holds; because they cannot endure otherwise unless, turned back again by love, they flow back to the causes which he himself gave. But others, taking it ill that they themselves were neglected, exempted man from the number of corruptible things on account of the preeminence of his intellect, saying that God has care and providence for man, but despises all the other earthly creatures. Against their false opinion enough has been said before; to produce many things would be altogether superfluous. For Scripture says: Men and beasts you will save, O Lord.
