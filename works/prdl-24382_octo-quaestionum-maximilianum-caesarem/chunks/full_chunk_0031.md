The sixth kind of demons, however, is called light-fleeing, because they greatly abhor and detest the light: for they never appear by day, nor can they assume a body in any way except at night. This is the business that walks about in darkness: a kind of demons inscrutable and utterly dark, and stirred by cold passions; malicious, restless, and disturbed, which violently crushes men who meet it in the hours of night, and very often, God permitting, kills some either by its breath or by its touch. This kind of demons does not suit sorcerers, nor can it be constrained by charms, because it flees both the light and the voices of men, and likewise every tumult. The aerial kind is especially accustomed to present itself for the sorceries of women, since it is subtler than all the rest, and of most audacious rashness. Some of the terrestrial demons also, as has been said, serve sorcerers; yet these, being held to be of a lower rank, can by no means bring about those greater sorceries. It is therefore manifestly clear that the whole power of sorceresses, by which they do such astonishing things, is granted to demons by the Lord God within limits, and when he does not permit it, they accomplish nothing.

*On Divine Permission. Seventh Question.*

The seventh question of your most serene majesty was this: Why does almighty God, since he is just and the avenger of evils, permit such great sorceries to be done to the destruction of the human race, by which not only sinners, but also many innocent persons perish, grievously tormented? To this question I remember that, when I was formerly placed before your majesty, I answered in this way. Almighty God is just, upright, gracious, merciful. What he permits, he permits justly, since he is just; there is no iniquity with him, but all things that are done or that he permits are in true judgment. Therefore, when it is asked why God permits evils to be done, we answer: He permits them for this reason, because he is just. Let not the answer we have given seem absurd to your majesty, O Caesar, since it in no way prejudices the divine goodness that he permits evils to be done for a most just reason; rather, indeed, it would prejudice it if he did not permit evils to be done in the present world. For truly, if he did not permit evils to be done, man would now be unable to sin; he would certainly be impeccable, which does not belong to his nature, to which from the beginning the Creator of all gave free will. For since man is a creature and not the Creator, he is necessarily as capable of being turned toward good as toward evil; his being depends on the supreme maker of all creatures, just as everything caused depends on the cause of its own being. But to create has been left to God alone, who is incorruptible and everlasting; everything corruptible, however, necessarily fails, but is preserved by the goodness of the Creator so long as it does not lose the influence of its cause. Hence it neither could nor ought to have come to pass that man should be created impeccable by the condition of his nature, a thing which only God, the maker of all, naturally enjoys, who is immutable and everlasting. Whenever, indeed, it is conferred on man that he seems unable to sin, the freedom of choice is not removed, nor is the possibility simply taken away, but the will is confirmed in good. And as often as this gift is conferred on a creature, it is a gift of grace, not of nature. For Scripture says: God from the beginning created man, and left him in the hand of his own counsel. For, properly speaking, it cannot be that mortal man, conceived in original displeasure, should both have free will and be unable to commit sin. For the most holy bishop Augustine says: The first freedom of the will was to be able to sin; the last, in glory, will much more be to be unable to sin. Therefore, if man could not sin, he would not have mastery over his own will. Hence, although almighty God does not will sin, yet he justly permits men to sin and to do evil, so that, while freedom of choice remains, both the virtue of a good work may receive its reward, and voluntary depravity may receive the evil it has done.
