Since he is far above all things that we, or any mortal, can understand concerning him, he is far beyond and above every affirmation, as holy Dionysius says. For he is neither soul, nor sense; nor does he have imagination or opinion, or reason, or understanding; nor is he reason, nor understanding; nor is he spoken of, nor understood. Nor is he number, nor multitude, nor order, nor magnitude, nor smallness, nor equality, nor likeness, nor unlikeness; nor does he stand, nor is he moved; nor does he bring about rest, nor have power; nor is he power; nor is he light; nor does he live, nor is he life; nor is he substance, nor age, nor time; nor is there any spiritual contact with him; nor is he form, nor truth, nor kingdom, nor wisdom, nor one; nor is there denomination of son or father; nor is he any of those things that are known to us or to anyone else in the world. Nor is he any of those things that are not; nor do any of the things that are have knowledge of him, [unclear] as he himself is. Nor is there speech of him, nor error, nor truth. For the perfect and singular cause of all things is also above every affirmation. And his excellence, namely of him who is simply released from all things and is above all things, is above every negation. Therefore we say, Dionysius says, that since he is [the cause?] of all things, he is neither without substance nor without life; nor does he lack reason or mind; nor is he body, nor figure, nor species; nor does he have quality, quantity, or bulk. Nor is he in a place, nor is he perceived, nor does he have sensible touch; nor does he sense, nor is he sensible; nor is he subject to rashness or disturbance, agitated by fleshly passions. Nor is he weak, subject to sensible accidents; nor is he in need of light, nor does he admit change, corruption, division, or flux; nor is he or does he have any other thing belonging to sensibles. Hence, since he is far above every affirmation, excelling [unclear], he could not be comprehended by a creature that necessarily uses the senses for understanding; as the true Peripatetic said, that there is nothing in our intellect unless it has first been in sense. Therefore God, who is not subject to the senses of mortals, far exceeds human understanding: according to affirmative positing he is truly altogether in all things, and according to negative removal, the nearer he is approached, he is none of all the things that are subject to the knowledge of mortals. Indeed, since he is above all things, he can be none of those things that are subject to him. Therefore Empedocles beautifully gave a likeness, saying: God is a sphere whose center is everywhere, whose circumference is nowhere. Let human understanding now raise itself as much as it can; and let him who strives to comprehend God comprehend, if he rightly understands it, the center of this imagined sphere and at the same time its nowhere-ending circumference. But if he is not capable of understanding this proposition of the likeness properly, why does he strive to investigate the essence of God, which exists above every affirmation? May it not weary you, most unconquered Caesar, to hear another likeness concerning God from Cusanus, formerly cardinal priest of the Roman Church, my fellow Mosellan, a man most learned in those times, who says: God is a straight line of infinite length, which makes a circle. Now then let us see. If it is a straight line, how does it make a circle? But if it makes a circle, how is it straight? For a circle and a straight line are naturally opposed. Nevertheless, a line of infinite length, since it has neither beginning nor end, necessarily makes a circle, although it is proposed to be imagined not as curved but as straight.
