*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, formerly of Sponheim: The Book of Eight Questions to Emperor Maximilian.*

*With the privilege of the imperial majesty: that it not be printed in his kingdom, empire, and lands, nor copies printed elsewhere sold there, within ten years, under the penalties expressed in the privilege, of ten marks of gold and the loss of all those books, etc.*

*Epigram of Richard Sbrulius of Friuli.*

Who could deny that there is a God, parent of heaven and earth? No one is so savage as to deny that there is a God.

God himself is worshipped everywhere throughout the whole world; he is sought by different paths, yet he is the same.

The barbarian worships him, admonishing all things by a fixed law; he denies incense to the holy Son and to the Spirit.

The Hebrew boasts that he holds the true doctrines, and, with Moses as his leader, thinks himself to be the race of God.

But the people of Christ, who took their name from his name, steadfastly sings that God is triune.

Maximilian, greatest in empire, who presides over the realm, continually aids the cause and honor of Christ.

He asks, as it is right for an august king to ask such things, why the highest God permits there to be so many sects.

Why does he not reveal himself equally to all mortals? He is the leader and ornament of the hooded flock.

Whoever you are who used to doubt concerning the holy faith, you will read these things; soon you will be a certain foster-child of Christ.

*End.*

*To the most serene and unconquered lord of the world, Emperor Maximilian: Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of [Peapolitanus?], The Book of Eight Questions. In the name of Christ begins [the book] of the monastery of Benedictbeuern.*

I have obeyed your commands, most unconquered Emperor Maximilian, and have set down in this little book those eight questions which you formerly proposed to me, when I had been summoned to your Highness in the castle of Boppard, resolving them with what brevity I could.

You wished, however, if I remember correctly, that, so far as possible in their solution, I should follow the path of nature, on account of those who yield less to belief. Yet this did not seem possible to me in all matters, because the subject matter of the questions in many places rested upon theological rather than natural principles. For although the Christian religion, among all the sects of mortals, is held to be more closely conformed to the laws of nature, nevertheless the principal articles of our faith are not all provable according to the common path of nature. We believe that this was necessarily done so that faith might have a place, and that firm belief might not lack its own merit.

Therefore, entering upon the middle path of disputation, as far as my powers allowed and by command, I have used partly nature, and partly also the testimony of the divine Scriptures. For truly, the things which it is fitting for us to believe are not to be rashly examined by human arguments, since, as the holy Gregory says, faith has no merit when human reason supplies proof for it.
