Again, if the witch is lacking, the devil will in no way accomplish the witchcraft. For the devil uses the will of the raging witch as an artisan uses an instrument for working; without it he could not inflict witchcraft.

And just as demons differ from one another in power and in keenness of subtlety, so the power of witches is not the same in all, but differs in individuals according as demons of a subtler or grosser nature join with them. For there are many kinds of demons, and they stand apart from one another by definite degrees, according to the places into which, when they fell, they were cast down at the beginning.

The first kind of demons is called fiery. It roams about the upper air and is never plunged down to the lower regions before the day of judgment; rather, remaining continually beneath the lunar regions, they have no commerce with men on earth. That they remain beneath the moon Saint Augustine bears witness in his work On the Christian Combat, saying thus: demons do not dwell in the heaven where the sun and stars are, but all move about beneath the lunar region. Hence, as I think, Apuleius called demons fiery animals, because Aristotle affirmed that in burning furnaces certain small animals have often been seen fluttering, furnished with little wings, which spend their whole life in fire, since they arise with the fire and are extinguished with it, and, vanishing, return to their own place from which they had come. They have no partnership in acts of witchcraft, because, since they dwell in fire, because of their subtlety they cannot put on a gross or aerial body.

The second kind is called aerial, which wanders in the air near us. These can descend to lower places and, taking bodies from the thicker air, sometimes appear visibly to men. When God permits, they often disturb the air, stir up thunder and storms, and all together conspire for the destruction of the human race. In the manner of men they are moved by the affections of the passions, especially by pride and envy, and they are touched by continual disturbances; they are neither enclosed in a solid body nor fixed in one place. Nor do they all have one form, but many, and they often change these according to the variety of the affections by which they either come forth when summoned by the spells of little witches, or are driven by disturbance to do harm. For they have an altogether violent and furious nature, full of disturbances; hence, when violently disturbed by evil deeds, they very suddenly devise many snares, and while they carry out their assaults, partly they wish to lie hidden, partly they inflict violence.

Witches, sustained by the cooperation of these demons, are more powerful for working witchcraft in proportion as they have obtained the cooperation of a demon higher in their order. In the Life of Saint Anthony, Saint Athanasius said that the air is full of demons, which Mercury Trismegistus had said before him: namely, that no part of the world is without the presence of demons. The holy bishop Ambrose also says: the world is full of holy powers because it is full of wickedness. Hence the Platonists have handed down that demons flying in the air like very dense snow become visible to those who desire to see them, if for some time, while the sun is shining, they keep their gaze fixed toward heaven with steady, unblinking eyes. But I do not know whether the object is caused in the air from real consistency, or whether the restrained motion of the eyes presents false images; nevertheless, not without some slight harm to the sense, I have tested what was promised.

But the third kind we call terrestrial demons, whom we do not at all doubt to have fallen from heaven to earth according to their deserts. Concerning these, the most holy bishop and martyr of Christ, the God-bearer Ignatius, once wrote in a certain letter to the Ephesians as follows: nothing is better than to have peace, in which every weapon of the spirits of the air and of the earth alike is made void. Of these demons, some dwell in forests and groves and lay snares for hunters; others live in open fields and make travelers wander by night; some dwell in hidden places and caves; the rest, less furious and disturbed than the others, delight in dwelling obscurely with men. They are not led by one affection but by various ones, since some are less depraved than others, although all are full of evil disturbances. Of these, some rejoice in frightening men by illusions alone; others desire to draw those who hear them into admiration of themselves by predictions of future things. Some, however, strive with the greatest effort to terrify, injure, or kill men disturbed by irrational frenzy or raging with melancholy, which we have learned by experience has often happened.
