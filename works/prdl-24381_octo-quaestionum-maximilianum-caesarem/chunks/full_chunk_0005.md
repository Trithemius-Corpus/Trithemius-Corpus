It is therefore necessary for mortals to believe in the immortal God, whom we cannot comprehend by any evident reasoning. Although the order of the whole universe declares the provident author of all things, and although we understand nothing besides him to have been brought into being without a beginning, nevertheless the human mind cannot grasp what God is or in what manner he is, because, since he is far above all things that we can understand concerning him, or that any mortal can understand, he is placed far outside and above every affirmation, as holy Dionysius says.

For he is neither soul, nor sense, nor does he have imagination, or opinion, or reason, or understanding; nor is he reason, nor understanding; nor is he spoken, nor understood. He is neither number, nor multitude, nor order, nor greatness, nor smallness, nor equality, nor likeness, nor unlikeness; nor is he something that exists, nor is he moved, nor does he cause rest; nor does he have power, nor is he power, nor is he light, nor does he live, nor is he life, nor substance, nor age, nor time; nor is there any spiritual touch of him; nor is he form, nor truth, nor kingdom, nor wisdom, nor one, nor unity, nor deity or goodness, nor spirit, so far as we ourselves can know.

Nor does the designation of Son or Father belong to him, nor anything else among those things known to us or to anyone else in the world. Nor is he anything of the things that are not, nor of the things that are. Nor do the things that are know him as he himself is. Nor is there speech of him, nor name, nor knowledge; nor is he darkness, nor light, nor error, nor truth; nor is there any affirmation at all of him, nor any removal. For the perfect and singular cause of all things is above every affirmation. And his excellence is above every removal: his, that is, who is simply absolved from all things and is above all things.

Therefore, as Dionysius says, we say that, since the cause of all things is above all things, he is neither without substance nor without life, nor does he lack reason or mind; nor is he a body, nor a figure, nor a form, nor does he have quality or quantity or bulk. Nor is he in a place, nor is he seen, nor does he have sensible touch, nor does he sense, nor is he sensible; nor is he subject to rashness or disturbance, stirred by fleshly passions. Nor is he weak, subject to sensible accidents; nor is he in need of light; nor does he admit alteration or corruption or division or flux; nor is he or has he anything else belonging to sensible things.

Therefore, since he is far above every affirmation in excellence, he could not be comprehended by the creature placed beneath him, which necessarily uses the senses in order to understand, just as the true Peripatetic said: there is nothing in our intellect unless it has first been in the sense. God, then, who is not subject to the senses of mortals, far exceeds the human intellect: he who, according to affirmative affirmation, truly is wholly in all things, and, according to negative removal, the nearer he is approached, is none of all the things subjected to the knowledge of mortals. For since he is above all things, he cannot be any of those things that are subject to him.
