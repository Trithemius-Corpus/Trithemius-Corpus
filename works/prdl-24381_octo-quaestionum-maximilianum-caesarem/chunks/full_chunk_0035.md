The lower things will declare that all these things are done, preserved, disposed, and moved by divine providence, to whose command absolutely nothing is alien. For God himself, the ruler of all things, understands both all things and each thing, not stretched out, after our manner, among many things, but abiding in himself as an indivisible one; and by beholding himself as the exemplar of the whole universe and the cause of causes, and through causes as the cause of all things, he contemplates all things, and in contemplating all things he cares and provides for them, directing each thing to its end and good in everlasting order.

Come now, most wise Caesar: in order that we may understand divine providence governing all things, let us use arguments as brief as we can for our purpose. And so, if God’s action is his own intelligence, which no wise man fails to admit, as being at once most eminent and most inward, then as much as he acts, so much surely he understands, and conversely. But he acts in each thing, not only in great things but also in the smallest; therefore he understands each thing. For the most abundant influx of the first being passes through each thing that in any way exists, following in fixed orders; and the things that act after him are everywhere moved by his power and act.

Therefore either God understands or acts nothing outside himself, which is false, or, by acting upon all things and each thing, he also understands those same things, which is true. For they would nowhere come to be unless first, to speak in our manner, they were understood by God himself, who makes them, just as those things are not made by the sun which are not looked upon by it. But since the Almighty understands all things and each thing that he makes, there is no doubt that he disposes, rules, and orders all things that he has made; from him things have not only that they exist, but also that they are certain causes of other things. And so, if things have from God that they are causes, from him too they have received the office proper to a cause, that is, to preserve according to their powers and to care for what they have made. Therefore, if all causes have this property from the first cause, insofar as they are certain causes, deservedly that cause itself naturally has this goodness and providence, so that it cares for all things and each thing that it has made, insofar as it is in the highest degree the cause of all those things, both collectively and singly. And unless these are guarded individually by him who makes them, the common things that are preserved in individuals will never be guarded. For neither will the whole that consists of parts be preserved if the parts themselves are neglected.

From the intention of the first and greatest intelligence, therefore, comes the common universal distinction of things. It itself is distinguished from others through itself; the rest it distinguishes among themselves by the standard of its own conception. Hence, just as God knows all things and each thing most distinctly, so too he most distinctly rules, moderates, and cares for them. I think it is therefore clear, most clement Caesar Maximilian, and sufficiently shown, that the providence of God, the supreme ruler, embraces, disposes, rules, and governs all lower things and each thing; otherwise, without the provident disposition of one who observes them, they would in no way continue this most beautiful order that we discern.

Nor am I unaware how variously the wise men of this world once thought about providence. Some of them judged that this world was made not by providence but by chance; if their opinion were true, no place would be left for providence, but all things would necessarily drift, and the world, since it was made by chance, could perish by chance. Against their false opinion others taught that the world could not have been made without reason, nor certainly could it stand unless it were ruled by the highest providence of its maker. Hence Boethius, a most Christian man, said: “O you who govern the world by everlasting reason, maker of earth and heaven, who command time to go forth from eternity, and, remaining stable, grant that all things be moved.” And again he said: “The generation of all things, and all the developments of changeable natures, and whatever is moved in any way, receive their causes, order, and forms from the stability of the divine mind.”

Others, however, thought that God cared only for incorruptible things, such as the heavenly things and separate substances, and likewise the species of things, but that he was wholly ignorant of all the rest and utterly neglected them. But these men erred shamefully, because the Almighty has providence and care not only for universal and incorruptible things, but also for particular and corruptible things. Hence Saint Boethius again says: “The lofty founder of things sits upon the earth and, ruling, bends the reins, king and lord, fountain and origin, law, and wise judge of what is just. He is the continuous love for all things, and they seek to be held by the end of the good, because they cannot endure otherwise, unless, turned back again by love, they flow back to the causes that he himself gave.”
