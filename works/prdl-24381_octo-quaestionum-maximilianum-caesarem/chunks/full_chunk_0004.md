It was created from the beginning, without any action or motion of its own, immovably and substantially, and by nature knew all things that it can understand; and it came forth created in an instant full and perfect in the knowledge of all things. But the human intellect, since it was created in ignorance and privation of all things, and was appointed in time to a body for its own exercise, must by continual action, motion, and art arrive at the knowledge of God and of all things. At last the end will make it equal to the angelic intellect, changing its potency into act, and bringing it forth from merely potential and possible being into essence; from empty it will make it full, from imperfect perfect, from deprived possessing, from dark luminous, from nocturnal diurnal, from lunar solar, and from earthly heavenly. This cannot happen while the mind is bound together with the body, as the holy lover of Christ Paul says: “As long as we are in the flesh, we are pilgrims away from the Lord.”

To know God belongs to a pure, simple, and perfect intellect. But the human intellect, as long as it dwells in the body, is impure, mixed, unstable, and imperfect, and for this reason, without faith, is ignorant of all knowledge of God and of heavenly things. Yet the object of both intellects, both human and angelic, is the same: namely that highest immutable good, God, the one principle of all things, to which they return by the natural desire of knowledge.

But if the angelic intellect is compared with the human, it is like nature compared with art, and like intellect simply speaking compared with reason. Intellect is close to the principles of the disciplines, while reason proceeds through principles to conclusions. Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect immovably apprehends its proper object, toward which the human intellect, through the angelic medium, after many exercises of itself, as has been said, at last at some time prepares an ascent for itself by reasoning. But human intellects are separated from the angelic intellect by the perfection of knowledge of their natural object, and therefore the human intellect necessarily becomes movable, returning through that medium to its own principle. For every motion goes from an extreme through a medium to an extreme; but where there is no medium, the extremes are already immediate and near, and for that reason immovable.

The human intellect, however, placed as it were in a dark prison as long as it is held in the body, is moved by sense and reason toward understanding, although very confusedly and imperfectly; and therefore it suffers a medium between itself and God, which we have called the angelic intellect, bearing the body as an impediment to its destined perfection, as a certain lover of wisdom said: “The body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the sense that thinks many things.”

Therefore it was wholly necessary for us to believe God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible for us, who understand nothing without the ministry of the senses and the discourse of reason, to comprehend that super-divine and uncircumscribable majesty. But God, existing far above every position, remains subject neither to the senses of mortals nor to reason, as he is read to have said to Moses: “Man shall not see me and live.” Hence also the divine evangelist John says: “No one has ever seen God.” Nor is this surprising. For since God is the first and eternal intellect, most pure, most simple, most perfect, immovable, creator, incomprehensible, highest, immense far above every position, and uncircumscribable, he could not be comprehended by the human intellect, which is impure, subject to composition, imperfect, movable, created, comprehensible, lowest, and established beneath the measure of position.

As for the question that, from the first line of argument, ought to have arisen: whether God, since he is omnipotent, could have made himself knowable to the human intellect as to the angelic intellect, I say that no one of sound mind has ever denied that God can do all things. But we say that God cannot do those things that imply contradiction, because they are contrary to the order of wisdom, which immutably disposes all things, just as destruction is not doubted to be contrary to the construction of the whole at the same time, and, if I may say so, utterly opposed to it.

For it was neither fitting, nor could it in any way have happened, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect, since if mortal man, by the guidance of nature, comprehended the immortal God, surely he would already seem to have to be called not man nor mortal, but angel; and either man would become comparable to God, or God to man. For as long as the angelic intellect stands between, the human intellect cannot understand God.
