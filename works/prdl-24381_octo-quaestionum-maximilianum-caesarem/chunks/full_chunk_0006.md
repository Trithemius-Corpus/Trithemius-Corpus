Now, insofar as the human intellect can, let it rightly understand and comprehend the center of this imagined sphere, and at the same time its circumference, bounded nowhere, since it strives to comprehend God. But if it is not sufficient to understand this by the proposed likeness proper to it, why does it contend to investigate the essence of God, which exists above every position?

May it not weary you, Caesar, to hear in turn another likeness concerning God from Nicholas of Cusa, sometime priest of the Roman Church, Cardinal, my fellow Mosellan, a man most learned in those times, who says: God is a straight line of infinite length, which makes a circle. Let us therefore now consider. If it is a straight line, how does it make a circle? But if it makes a circle, how is it straight? For a circle and a straight line are naturally opposed. Yet a line of infinite length, since it has neither beginning nor end, necessarily makes a circle, although it is proposed to be imagined not as curved, but as straight.

Since, therefore, God is infinite, greatest without quantity, best without quality, all things in all without circumscription, above all things without a position that excludes, it was not possible that he should be comprehended by the circumscribed human intellect, which uses the service of the senses for understanding, and that what is promised to it when purified at last in its homeland should be granted to it while confused and impure on the way.

But afterwards, when our mind, at last having left the flesh, has become bare, simple, pure, perfect, and consummated at its center, which is God, then, made equal to the angels, we shall know God perfectly, and all things, as also Saint [unclear] says to the Corinthians: Now we see through a mirror and in a riddle, but then face to face.

Therefore we said that the angelic intellect, by the guidance of its nature, adheres immovably to its principle, because it is pure act and habit; and since by its condition it is naturally simple, it cannot be impeded by any alteration of composition. But the human intellect, as it is now sufficiently clear has been declared, since it is potency and privation, impure because of its commerce with its body, is thereby held back from perfect act until at last, freed from the flesh, it passes into the fellowship of likeness with the angels, then first to be admitted to the true knowledge of God.

But if, meanwhile, the affection of love necessary for salvation is joined to present Christian faith and to future divine knowledge, the same everlasting blessedness prepared with the holy angels for the worshippers of God will be given as a gift. But if the depraved mind, through too great commerce with its flesh, has despised the love of God, the intellect indeed, released from its prison, will rise to the measure of knowledge belonging to it by nature, but it will be so much the more miserable as, by its evil will, it is found to be more like the demons.

Therefore, to conclude the solution of the proposed question as I may, I say that it is not possible for almighty God to be perfectly understood by us so long as we mortals live in the flesh.
