For the devil is the worker of all necromantic effects and of the effects of witches; through the application to the subject matter of the means that he himself first instituted, he produces the desired work, sometimes really, sometimes only apparently. Witches, however, who, with their will now depraved and by consent, have passed through pacts into fellowship with the demon, receive from them certain marks and symbolic signs, by which, when summoned, the demons immediately come.

In these pernicious operations of demons, nothing is stronger than the foolish credulity of witches, which is increased by pacts and vows; for the firmer the faith they place either in demons or in their signs, the more swiftly, with the demons cooperating, they obtain the operation. For the exceedingly savage depravity of the witches’ will in a certain way turns them into frenzy; when this has seized their mind, the demons at once attach themselves to the frenzied women and, God permitting, furnish to those raging women the effect of their petition, strengthened by pacts and signs.

For Plato recounted something similar in his book on poetic frenzy, saying: “Just as Bacchic women, when their mind is unsound, draw honey and milk from rivers, but when their mind is sound they cannot draw them.” Their mind, carried outside itself into fellowship with an evil demon, brings this about. We have verified that witches have at times drawn butter from rushing rivers, which can in no way happen except by the ministry of a demon.

In like manner, when they have been brought into hatred of some man or beast and long for his destruction, they are driven by a most violent frenzy, thinking of nothing in their soul except vengeance. They take up things known and familiar to them for witchcraft, invoke demons, display the signs and characters instituted by them, and what they cannot accomplish by their own power they obtain through the ministry of those demons.

But another person, who is not subject to demons by pact and surrender, even if he knows both the things suitable for witchcraft and the method of working, and although he displays the signs, nevertheless rarely obtains the effect, because he is not counted among the familiars of demons. For the devil, puffed up with pride, imitates the Church of Christ to such a degree that, just as one who is not ordained cannot consecrate the body of Christ, however much he utters the words of consecration, so also the demon, however much a man invokes him and displays his signs and characters, either never or only rarely joins himself to one whom he does not know to be subject to him by pact.

For if he came indiscriminately to all who invoked him without pacts having been made, he would not possess so many witches called his own by right of ownership. Hence it is that many people devoted to profane arts, laboring with dreadful conjurations to summon demons to a circle, to a mirror, or to any other receptacles, obtain no effect, because either God’s command stands in the way, or the devil despises the curious man not dedicated to him by homage.

For three things are required for witchcraft, without which it is agreed that none is perpetrated: a witch frenzied in mind and depraved in will, the cooperation of a familiar devil, and before all else divine permission. For if one of these does not concur, witchcraft cannot be perpetrated. If God did not permit it, neither the devil nor the witch could do anything. And if the devil did not come to meet the will of the depraved woman, she could perpetrate no witchcraft [unclear].
