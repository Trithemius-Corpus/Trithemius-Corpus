For, as Saint Augustine says, he is less stirred by pleasures who is not where there is a crowd of pleasures; and he suffers less the troubles of avarice who, placed far from men, does not see riches. Therefore, since the holy Spirit of God loves the secrecy of the mind but shudders at public exposure, it belongs to you, brothers, to love the solitude of cells as the workshop of all virtues, and never to withdraw yourselves from its dwelling-place without reasonable cause. For the monk who loves secrecy, not idleness, who has learned to remain steadfastly in his cell, sometimes enjoys many holy consolations, of which the wandering and restless man is necessarily deprived. Just as a fish dies if it is kept too long out of water, so the devotion of a monk perishes if he wanders too often outside his cell. Let us imitate John the Baptist, whom Chrysostom says is the chief of monks: he, a monk, lived in the desert as soon as he was born, was nourished in the desert, and as a monk awaited Christ in solitude. For just as it is difficult for a tree placed beside a road to preserve its fruits until maturity, so it is difficult for a monk who often wanders outside his cell and is occupied in the affairs of the world to preserve the fruits of righteousness unspotted to the end [unclear]. For a mind that is run together with the cares and anxieties of the world is rarely truly rooted in the love of God. Indeed, movement of the body often alters the state of the mind, and a man who is always submerged in external things is never illumined by the fervor of the divine Spirit. The instruments of spiritual exercises in the cell are four: to read, to write, to pray, and to meditate. But a fifth is granted to few: those who, after true purity of heart, are raised to the mountain of contemplation of the Lord. Finally, these four exercises of monks who remain in the solitude of the cell are to be so moderated that they generate neither weariness nor sluggishness of mind, but that a fitting time be assigned to each activity, so that, while variety lightens the mind, the little body may not be burdened beyond its strength. Therefore let the reading of the divine Scriptures have its own suitable hour for study; and when it perceives that the desire of devotion is present, let it at once be changed into fervent prayer. Manual labor, however, which is enjoined upon the monk dwelling in the tranquility of solitude, is very useful, lest a harmful lukewarmness arise in the mind. For the reading of the holy Scriptures stirs the monk to the love of God; meditation on the Lord’s passion and on the benefits of God inflames the mind to the fervor of most ardent love; prayer strengthens the weak spirit; and manual labor, temperately continued, preserves him from sluggishness and weariness. When we pray earnestly and devoutly, we speak with our God, who is present everywhere; but when we read in the holy Scriptures, we are taught by the Lord himself what is to be sought by us or avoided. If prayer is ardent in the love of God, it purifies the mind from sins and vices; reading, however, instructs and forms it, as Saint Isidore says: "By prayers," he says, "we are cleansed; by reading we are instructed. Therefore both are good, if both are possible; if both are not possible, it is better to pray than to read." Therefore the monk who desires to spend his time in the cell usefully and fruitfully should strive continually, by turns, to occupy mind and body in these exercises, since the spirit that keeps watch amid such labors offers no entrance to perverse thoughts. Hence the divine Jerome, admonishing a monk, says: "Always do some good thing, so that the devil may find you occupied." For he is less assailed by the enticements of the flesh who, always usefully occupied, admits no weariness of mind.
