*Homily XI.*
*Fol. XXV.*

Let him consider that he has spent uselessly the day on which he has not sent ahead a good work for which he may expect reward. Let the good monk at all times strive to cultivate the field of his heart, and utterly to root out, as far as he can, all thorns and thistles of evil affections. Let him learn to keep the Lord Jesus above all things with a pure mind and the greatest diligence. Let him sow in the tears of devotion and constancy what he may reap with all the saints in exultation. Let the continual difficulty of laboring not deter the mind; let the hard struggle of the flesh not break the spirit, since he who labors little gathers little fruit at harvest, and he who now sows much in tears for the love of Christ brings back most abundant fruit on that day. For [this?], brothers, is the destination of our journey which we undertake in the present pilgrimage: that, purified by continual exercise of labors and adversities, we may depart from here clean and pure to our homeland.

*On Avoiding the Company of the Wicked: The Eleventh Homily.*

The company of the wicked has drawn many back from the way of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Therefore, my most beloved brothers, we do not cease to admonish you to walk in the way of the Lord with the good and holy, by whose instruction and example you may daily become better. For, as the holy apostle of Christ says, imitating the iambic verse of a certain Greek, Menander: evil conversations corrupt good morals. He does not truly desire to be good whom the conduct of the wicked delights. For no one speaks wicked things unless he is first found corrupted and depraved in his own heart against the purity of the evangelical tradition. As each person is, and as are the things he turns over and performs in the secrecy of his heart by ruminating and thinking, such speech more frequently proceeds from his mouth. For the speech of the speaker is the truest witness of the heart, as our Lord and Savior says in the holy Gospel: out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For a good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and an evil man likewise brings forth evil things. Since, therefore, he who speaks evil is not good, we must beware of all who use depraved, scurrilous, and pernicious words; in their speech certain shameful things often resound, while words of holiness are heard either never or very rarely. Shameful speech is the sign of an immodest heart, even if deeds do not follow.

You, therefore, my dearest brothers, who promised according to the rule and the ecclesiastical statutes of the holy fathers to preserve until death, not only in deed, chastity and every kind of purity by a profession both implicit and express, but who ought also to be far removed from every unclean thought: flee the company of wicked men. And he who has fellowship with the proud will put on pride. Hence Saint Pope Gregory says: the weak ought to avoid the society of the wicked, lest they delight to imitate the evils that they frequently behold and cannot correct. And Saint John the bishop says: if a good man is joined to an evil man, either they are made equals, or they are soon separated from each other. For friendships either seek equals or make them. For the nature of things is such that whenever a good man is joined to an evil man, the evil man is not made better by the good, but the good man is contaminated by the evil. For diversity of things can never have concord, and wicked society tempts many.

There are among you certain brothers, I say it groaning, men professed to regular discipline but very forgetful of it: to them the cell is a prison, the choir a siege, prayer nausea, silence a punishment, the reading of the Scriptures disgust. The love of God either never or rarely raises upward the hearts of those whose whole way of life consists in lower things. Whenever I happen to be absent, they immediately come together among themselves; not about Christ, nor about the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom, nor about the integrity of regular observance, but a long discourse is brought forth about the vain and useless affairs of this world; and concerning things about which it is not lawful for a monk to think, they involve themselves in conversation with tales and the wars of kings.
