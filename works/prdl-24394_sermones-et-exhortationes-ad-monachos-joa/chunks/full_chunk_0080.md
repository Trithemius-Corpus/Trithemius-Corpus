*On patience and meekness*

If you wish to keep the bounds of tolerance, there must first of all be someone present who inflicts insult. “If I had been,” says the lazy and unspiritual monk, “in the days of the martyrs, I would have feared no kinds of punishments to undergo for the love of Christ; I would voluntarily have exposed my body to the torturers, so that I might carry away the crown of martyrdom.” Great rashness of folly. You have never been able to endure a word patiently for the love of God, and you promise the steadfastness of the holy martyrs? You fear a slight correction, you who promise that you will give your body for Christ.

If martyrdom delights you, hear what the most blessed Pope Gregory says: “If, with Christ helping us, we strive to preserve the virtue of patience, and live in the peace of the Church, we also hold the palm of martyrdom. For without sword and flame we can be martyrs, if we truly keep patience in the soul. To die at the hands of a persecutor is martyrdom in open deed; but to bear insults and to love one who hates us is martyrdom in hidden thought.” Patience, indeed, is to suffer another’s evils with equanimity, and to be gnawed by no grief against him who has inflicted the evil.

What now will you say, lazy monk, who, unable to endure the very slight martyrdom of words, boast so insolently of steadfastness in shedding blood? Learn, wretched man, to have patience in light matters, and consider that, as Scripture says, “he who is unfaithful in a little thing, who would entrust him with what is greater?” You murmur at everything that is done in the monastery; when reproved, however gently, for your grave faults and negligences, you endure it most impatiently. No one in the monastery walks rightly for you; no one except you, in your estimation, conducts himself religiously. As it seems to you, you alone are holy in the community; you, the most learned of all, allow no one to know what you alone are ignorant of. You think yourself worthy of all honors above everyone, because you not only lack patience but retain neither true humility nor obedience.

This is not the road by which one goes to the kingdom of heaven: when a monk is proud in the school of humility, and a Christian lives in his conduct contrary to Christ. But a monk, a minister solemnly sworn to Jesus Christ, in order that he may imitate his Lord, ought not to be proud but humble, obedient not rebellious, gentle not cruel, nor insolent but mild.

A monk, however, who is proud, wrathful, contentious, and a lover of his own will, such as, alas, those set over temporal affairs often are, whom among us according to the Rule we call cellarers, their pride has made for many the road to destruction. Grief compels me to speak, and shame forbids me, although you all know the fall by which the devil lately put us to shame in a brother. Who in our whole congregation was prouder than he? Who went about filled with greater insolence? Because he seemed among us to have more experience of temporal affairs than all the rest, for that reason he thought himself more excellent. The Almighty permitted him to fall, so that he who [unclear] refused to recognize his own weakness might at last, confounded and not without the scandal of many, understand that God is the adversary of the proud.

I grieve vehemently, and more than can be said. The fault of the one who fell seems to me the heavier, because when he fell we are all put to shame. May God grant him to do true penance, so that those who had been scandalized by his fall may be edified by his satisfaction before God and men.

Pride has always been destructive to monks, and the mind of a man which it has once invaded it plunges into the pit of all vices. Beware pride, my brothers, because it is the beginning of every sin; it is what, through the envy of the devil, introduced death into the world. Pride is the death of all virtues and the source and spring of all vices. But humility, the nurse of virtues, especially befits monks, because it makes them both mild and meek. For every proud man is impatient, and every arrogant man is an enemy of meekness. He who is humble of heart has patience in adversities for the love of God; and he who is tranquil in soul before injuries merits a double crown. A patient man in adversities becomes very near to God; but he who is proud and impatient gladdens his enemies. For Scripture says: “Better is the patient man than the strong man, and he who rules his own spirit than the conqueror of cities.” Likewise the holy bishop Augustine: “A just and pious man ought to be ready to sustain patiently the malice of those whom he desires to become good, so that rather the number of the good may increase, and he may not by like malice add himself to the number of the wicked.” For patience is very necessary to us, brothers, so that, doing the will of God, we may receive [unclear] in Christ.
