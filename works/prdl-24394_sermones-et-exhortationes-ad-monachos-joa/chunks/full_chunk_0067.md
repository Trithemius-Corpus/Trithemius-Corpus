*On the abstinence and fasting of monks.*

[Who] hates abstinence, who greedily gulps down foods, who drinks wine or strong drink and whatever can intoxicate, to satiety, is unworthy of the very name of monk and cannot be a disciple of Christ. Woe to our times, in which already very many clerics and monks have shamefully cast aside the virtue of abstinence; and those who ought to have shown the people the way of salvation no less by example than by word, given over to feasting and continual drinking and pleasures, immersed against all clerical decency, hasten to go before them into the perdition of eternal death.

Scripture says: “The beginning of man’s life is bread and water.” But now our monks and cloisterers buy for themselves, at great expense, whatever is sold most dearly, whatever is found most sumptuous; they bury it by gulping it into their bellies, and through intemperance corrupt soul and body alike. Amid banquets they debate fasting, and when they have become drunk they praise temperance. There each man boasts himself conqueror of the vices, when he has already been conquered by gluttony and drunkenness.

“Take heed,” says our Lord Jesus Christ in the holy Gospel according to Luke, “lest your hearts be weighed down with dissipation.” It is not fitting for a man dedicated to God to eat or drink to satiety, nor is it lawful for a monk to eat more than nature requires. Let eating allay hunger; let it not bring on repletion. Far away be the belchings which satiety is accustomed to generate in feasters; let vomiting and indigestion never be heard among monks. By the moderation of [sound discretion?] all cloisterers must be guided, so that each nourishes the body with foods and drinks in such a way that, serving the spirit, it may be able to live, and not so that, grown fat, it begins to run riot against the command of the mind.

Let satiety always be far away, for nothing so inflames the body and excites the generative members as undigested food and a belly hot with wine or beer. Hence blessed Jerome, admonishing Eustochium toward temperance, the preserver of all virtues, says in a certain letter: “Let the bride of Christ flee wine as poison, for wine and youth itself are weapons against youth, which is more prone to gluttony. Avarice does not so shake youth, nor pride puff it up, nor ambition delight it, as those vices of fleshly pleasure which seem to be born with us. We shall easily lack the other vices if in fear of the Lord we keep watch even moderately. But this enemy is within, always shut up inside; wherever we go, we carry this adversary with us. Wine and youth: a double fire of pleasure. Why do we add oil to the flame, or why do we supply fiery fuel to a little body already burning?”

Gluttony has laid low many who walked chastely in the middle of the road; and those whom the devil could not overcome by his own promptings, excessive drinking of wine cast into the abyss of luxury. Therefore Benedict, the most holy founder of our life, when enumerating the instruments of good works, set among the chief ones: to chastise the body, to embrace hardships, to love fasting. For no one truly follows Christ who, delicately nourishing his flesh, lives in a monastery without daily abstinence.

Moderate abstinence, pleasing to God, works many good things in us if it is continued with religious intention to the end of life. For first of all it increases merit and nourishes in us every kind of virtue. What was the reason why almighty God forbade our first parents the eating of the apple under threat of death, unless to show the occasion of their merits through the good of obedience? For, as Scripture bears witness, God created two intellects from the beginning, namely the angelic and the human, to which he might communicate himself through grace for the enjoyment of eternal happiness. Yet although he created each from the gift of his grace, he most rightly ordained that they should become forever happy also from their own proper merit, so that in them both mercy and conversion might obtain the merit necessary for salvation.

The angelic intellect was at once divided in two: while some fell by pride of their own will, others, humble and subject, adhered through love to their own beginning. Those, indeed, apostatizing from their beginning, departed unhappy; but these, for the merit of their adherence, were endowed with everlasting blessedness. The human intellect, however, was sent down into flesh; and soon, at the devil’s persuasion, it transgressed the commandment of God and feasted on the food forbidden to it.
