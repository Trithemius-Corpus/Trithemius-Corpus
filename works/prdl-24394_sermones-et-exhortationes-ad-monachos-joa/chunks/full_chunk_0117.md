This multitude of saints in the order, brothers, is set before us for imitation, not for vain boasting. How can their multitude conduce to our salvation, if we do not imitate the manner of life in which they went before us? Whenever we desire usefully to praise the most holy men of our order, let us hasten by holy conduct to come into their company. For then we shall glory fruitfully in the multitude of saints of our order, if, by living rightly, we diligently strive to increase their number. For this reason we set before you so great a multitude of saints: so that, when you see that many before you in this very order conquered the world and the flesh, you may at last rise up from your lukewarm manner of life. The virtue which God’s compassion bestowed on them he will not deny to you if you ask in true charity. They conquered the world because they loved Christ with total devotion, despising all things for love of him. They despised all things, and the Lord Jesus provided for them whatever seemed necessary for the sustenance of life.

This fervor of the monks’ devotion endured for many years after the first foundation of the order in great constancy, and converted the souls of many thousands of men to the Lord. But nothing is stable among human things, and nothing lasting among perishable things. Holy religion bestowed riches on the monks, until in time the daughter suffocated the mother. For, as Scripture says, all who wish to become rich fall into the temptations of the devil. The beloved grew fat and kicked back; made corpulent and enlarged by earthly possessions, he abandoned the holy pursuits of the first institution; he went after vanities and false follies, and abundance of temporal things made him poor and wretched in spiritual riches. This lamentable deformity of our order began about two hundred years after the passing of the divine father Benedict, and grew more and more with time, until at last the harmful poison spread itself through the whole order. Yet this deformity did not occupy the order suddenly, but at last, through the succession of many years, almost the whole of it; although almighty God never wholly deprived it of good men, but always left for himself some who had by no means bent their knees before Baal. For men of mercy arose, whose righteous deeds will never be handed over to oblivion; taking pity on the ruin of the order, they put their shoulder beneath the labor, and, with the Lord generously granting aid, restored it in many places.

To be sure, many varied particular reforms flourished in the order many times and at different periods, but there was never any general reform that brought the whole order back to the integrity of regular observance. Finally, however, we see that the particular restorations, which we have said were numerous, have almost all collapsed again. Most recently, namely in the year of the Christians 1420, when the general council was being celebrated at Constance, a certain new and hitherto latest particular reform took its beginning in Germany; from a certain monastery in the parts of Saxony, then indeed the poorest of all, called Bursfelde, it [unclear] the [unclear] of its name.

The first author and leader of this most holy reform was Johannes Rode, abbot of the distinguished monastery of Saint Matthias at Trier, who, being appointed general visitor of the order by the aforesaid Council of Constance, first exhibited reform in himself, lest, while preaching to others, he himself should be judged reprobate. For he first reformed both himself and the monks of the mentioned monastery; he issued a confirmation in conformity with the Rule; he obtained from the Apostolic See confirmation of the holy work. Among all the monasteries of the order, however, Bursfelde, a humble and poor little monastery, first received this reform, which afterward spread to Reinhausen and to a few monasteries of Saxony neighboring it.

At length, when Johannes Rode, abbot of Saint Matthias and leader of our reform, had died, since no suitable successor for the enlargement of the enterprise begun could be found in that same place, Johannes de Hagen, abbot of Bursfelde, who had already reformed certain monasteries, a holy man and very zealous for the order, was elected at the urging of Jakob, archbishop of Trier. Content with his poverty, he did not consent to leave Bursfelde, lest he seem to have preferred riches to poverty. Therefore he remained in his own monastery, and another was appointed in the place of the first reformer. Since this man seemed comparable to his predecessor neither in learning nor in zeal, he could accomplish little for the continuation of the enterprise begun, nor expand the reform among many.
