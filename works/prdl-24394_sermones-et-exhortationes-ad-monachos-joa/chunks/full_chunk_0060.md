*Homily xiv. Of monks. Homily xiii. Folio xxxii.*

Although our whole way of life, my dearest brothers, can rightly be called penance, since indeed we seem both dead to ourselves and already buried to the world, yet because we are surrounded by mortal flesh and sojourn in the midst of a sinful nation, it cannot happen that, by the impulse of our own frailty, we do not daily offend in some matter; and for the expiation of this, satisfaction must be made by doing penance not only generally but also specifically.

For penance is necessary for all mortals, by which they may both bewail the evils they have committed, with the intention of amendment, and henceforth, so far as can be done, resolve to live without sin. It is proper to monks to do penance without interruption, and continually to pray both for their own sins and for those of others. For a monk does not have the office of teaching, as Saint Jerome says, but of lamenting: one who, if weak, should mourn himself, or, if perfect, should mourn the world, and in fear await the coming of the Lord. If you are a perfect monk, do penance for the sins of others; but if you are weak, you must much more do penance for your own.

Therefore it is always time for a monk to do penance, since he is appointed as intercessor before God not only for his own sins but also for the offenses of others. But who among you is there, brothers, who is not burdened by his own fault? Which of you, I ask, does not need penance? I see you offending daily in many things, and I grieve; I perceive how you neglect penance for past things, and I tremble. Do you not recognize that you are mortal? And why do you not satisfy the name of monk, by doing penance, necessary in time? Remember, I ask you, the threatening Scripture which says: Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For his wrath will come suddenly, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you.

For you will not escape death, which for you, old men, is at the door, and for the younger lies hidden in ambush; but when you least think it, the spirit, forced from the body, will depart. Therefore, if you recognize that you are sinners, if you desire to come to God's mercy, I pray you: take it up, and amend your conduct in all things according to the purity of the rule. It is enough that until now you have neglected true penance, and have continued for so long a time in a way of life too lukewarm. For God, who overlooks the sins of men because of penance, has not promised prolongation of life to those who put off repentance. Consider the long-suffering of God, who has so long mercifully borne with you as you sin daily, and do penance, so that you may find the forgiveness you desire.

For the Lord says through the prophet: I do not want the death of the wicked man, but that he turn from his evil way and live. In the holy Gospel also he says: Do penance; for the kingdom of heaven will draw near. Penance is a great good for mortals: it cleanses filthy souls, and recalls obstinate minds from destruction. Here divine Augustine says: Penance heals illnesses, cures lepers, and raises the dead; it increases health and preserves grace. Penance restores a step to the lame, sight to the blind, abundance to the withered, and strength to the weak, as all things truly commend it: where contrition pierces the mind; where humble and pure confession brings forth hidden sin; where obedience makes satisfaction according to its powers; where the penitent so grieves over past things that he truly guards at all times against future ones. True penance is that which the love of God adorns with groans and tears, where the sinner grieves more over the offense against God than he fears the torment of hell.

And who of us, most beloved brothers, would not grieve; who would not groan that, among the lawless monks, he has sinned against the Most High, from whom we have not only life, but also await the promise of eternal happiness? Woe to us, brothers, if we neglect to do true penance in this life, for the sake of which, that we might always do it, we were made monks. For our way of life ought to be occupied in Jesus Christ crucified alone, and from the consolations of the world, O monk, [unclear]: not pleasures, not delights with the world, nor laughter, nor any consolation of the flesh, but grief and bitterness with tears; and if you do not continually give yourself to these, you have lost the name of monk. It is not permitted you to rejoice with the age, you who are dead to the world; nor ought you to seek any consolations of the present life, you who promised the Lord to serve according to the monastic rule in solitude. Yours it is, monk,
