*Sermon V. Folio LXVIII.*

What shall we wretches say on that day, or where shall we stand before the stern judge, if, as the blessed apostle Peter says, the righteous man will scarcely be saved? If Jerome, a righteous and most holy man, feared the judgment to come, O good God, where will Trithemius, an unhappy sinner, appear? If the righteous man fears, where will the sinner stand? For what shall we say? Or what shall we answer, when we are gravely accused on every side? All the elements will stand against us as testimony; earth together with heaven will accuse us; and even if all things were silent, each person’s own conscience will be weighty. He who knows all things is ignorant of nothing, since there all things will be manifest to the one judging. There will be present the evils we have done, gravely accusing us; around and about us will be present, as testimony against us, countless demons; we shall see a dreadful chaos open beneath us; above us we shall behold the judge enraged; and from every side the utmost danger will threaten us.

These dangers of the future examination, my brothers, always turn over in the memory of your heart, and consider diligently how dreadful it is to fall under the future sentence of the judge. In that terrible hour pure hearts will be worth more than the clever words of rhetoricians; a clean conscience will profit a man more than all the money in the world, since he who is to judge all things is not deceived, nor indeed changed by payment or prayers. O what incredible horror will invade all the impious when they are to hear the fearsome sentence: “Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels.” O the deepest sorrow of all evils, the most profound sadness of all sorrows: to be divided from the company of the saints and to be tormented with demons forever.

Truly the day of judgment is dreadful and to be feared by all, because it is exceedingly bitter, where the gnawing worm of conscience does not die, where one once condemned to punishment is never freed. Therefore the monk who desires to obtain everlasting salvation should always keep himself, as far as he can, clean from every sin; and that voice, “Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire,” should never depart from his memory. Hence the third thing which we judge greatly conducive to declining from sin is diligently to ponder the bitterness of the penalties of eternal damnation. For no temptation of carnal pleasure is so [unclear] that it will not at once fail, conquered, if the tempted person reflects with careful meditation on what the damned suffer in hell. What do the riches once possessed now profit the damned? What do past pleasures confer on the lustful placed in hell? What do the emptiest honors confer on the swollen with pride? What does power once widely held confer on the proud? Consider, brothers, what the damned now suffer in the lower regions, and, lest similar things befall you, persevere continually in good and holy works. If you fear hell, flee sin. For hell will harm nothing if a man remains in the faith of Christ, free from sins; nor will he burn in punishment who has not first burned here in guilt.

The place of hell is to be feared, in which there is no redemption; but whoever once enters there condemned will not afterward go out to salvation. The wretched cry out in torments, in anguish, placed in everlasting horror, saying: What now does our pride profit us? Riches, honors, dignities, lust, gluttony, and all past carnal pleasures: what do they now profit us, damned forever? Behold, all things have passed away like a shadow, and all have vanished like the brief dream of one night. But we have been assigned to eternal torments, in which we know most certainly that there is no redemption. In hell, as Augustine testifies, even if there will be the sting of repentance, there will nevertheless be no correction of will in the damned; iniquity will be blamed by them in such a way that the equity of justice can by no means be loved or desired by them.

There then the devouring flame of hell burns those whom carnal delight now defiles; and each person is burned so much the more in torments as here in the flesh he takes greater delight against God. In the lower regions, as Gregory bears witness, there will be intolerable cold, inextinguishable fire, the immortal worm of conscience, intolerable stench, palpable darkness, inestimable pains, scourges striking without interruption, the dreadful sight of demons, the confusion of crimes committed, and despair of all good things. But of all the punishments in hell, this is to be judged by far the gravest: that there the damned are deprived forever of the vision of eternal light. Here the most blessed bishop John Chrysostom says: Gehenna is more intolerable, and all men shrink from it. For who does not know that that punishment of hell is very horrible? Yet if anyone should posit a thousand, indeed ten thousand Gehennas, all that he says is nothing compared with this: the soul hated by Christ, driven away from the glory of the saints, and hearing that terrible voice from the mouth of the judge of all: “Depart from me, workers of iniquity, for I do not know you,” and “Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire which has been prepared for you and the devil,”
