Therefore great discretion is needed, so that only such food as is necessary for sustaining life is supplied to the body, and no excess of indulgence occurs. Hence the holy pope Gregory says: “It is a great labor of discretion to give something to the body that demands food, and to deny it something; by not giving, to restrain gluttony, and by giving, to nourish nature.” For through abstinence vices are to be extinguished, not the flesh; and through food the body is to be nourished, not sins. Thus, therefore, it is necessary that a monk maintain the art of continence in his diet, so that he kills not the flesh but the vices.

For often, while we pursue the enemy in the flesh, we cruelly slaughter, through immoderate abstinence, the spirit of devotion which we love. More often, too, while out of too much compassion we spare the flesh as if it were a fellow-citizen, we nourish an enemy for battle against ourselves. But when the vice of gluttony rules, men lose everything that they have done bravely, because when the belly is not restrained all virtues are at once overwhelmed. For a man does not rise up to the conflict of the spiritual contest if the enemy set within ourselves, namely the appetite of gluttony, is not first subdued by us with God’s help; for unless we overcome what is nearer to us, we hasten in vain to fight against what is set farther away. Indeed, war is waged in vain in the field against foreign enemies if within the city walls there is a citizen lying in ambush. We never overcome the assaults of demons unless we first overcome gluttony in ourselves.

Let no one, brothers, deceive himself by empty persuasion, so as to believe that there is no sin among beans and vegetables if the mind slackens, with excessive pleasure, the appetite for receiving them. For wherever appetite, made wanton in consumption, extends beyond the norm of nature, [unclear]. See, whether in the monastery or outside it, we never eat meat; fish here in this rural district are seen very rarely; no one among us has ever seen more sumptuous foods; we neither can nor indeed ought to have delicate dishes. Our foods are barley, oats, beans, peas, and vegetables, milk also and cheese and eggs. From these we receive the daily supports of our life; each day hunger stirs appetite, lest disgust arise. Outside the season of the Lenten fast, fish are so rare for us that scarcely once in a month are they brought from our river, and then very small. In the whole Bursfelde reform there is no monastery less provided for in the necessity of fish.

But can we therefore not fall into the vice of gluttony because we cannot obtain more sumptuous foods? Indeed, since the fault is not in the food, as has already been said, but in the appetite, one who eats peas or beans can sin no less in eating than he who is accustomed to consume the most sumptuous and choice foods. Poverty does not make us altogether secure from gluttony, though it removes occasions for sinning in more delicate things. We do indeed have a simple and rustic diet, by which nature may be sustained somehow; this, my brothers, I ask that we take care of: that in that which produces nausea in the delicate, our conscience may not be polluted by excess. Let us take with patience and thanksgiving from what the Lord has given as much as necessity itself requires; and let us not think that the snares of the devil cannot harm us, for he overthrew the first human beings in paradise not with more sumptuous food, but with a modest and cheap apple. For the fault is not in the food but in the appetite, when either we desire things forbidden to us, or, gaping after things granted [unclear], we use them otherwise than we ought.

Abstinence befits a woman more than a monk [unclear], who, since he has been specially called to Christ’s warfare, ought not, according to his profession, to devote himself to any delicacies. Our poverty imposes upon us a certain necessity of abstinence, because we can restrain ourselves to temperance the more easily, the less we can find anything into which we may flow away in delights. Therefore, I ask, let us turn the burdens of want into weapons of salvation, so that the less we are refreshed with more sumptuous foods, the more purely we may fast in holiness of mind. For temperance is more easily preserved in poverty, though in riches it is certainly more quickly endangered.

What the food of monks ought to be, blessed Jerome shows, saying in a certain letter to Paulinus: “Let your food be cheap and in the evening: vegetables, that is, and legumes; and sometimes regard the eating of a few little fish as the highest delicacies.” Whoever desires Christ and feeds on that heavenly bread does not greatly seek what dung he may make from costly foods. The lover of Christ loves abstinence; he cherishes fasting as one remedy for soul and body alike; he takes the greatest care that his heart not be weighed down by excess, nor does he take more food than nature, content with little, demands.
