*Sermon I.*

...so that I might move mountains, but have not charity, I am nothing. And if I should distribute all my possessions as food for the poor, and if I should deliver my body so that I might burn, but have not charity, it profits me nothing.

Consider yourselves carefully now, my brothers, and question your consciences, whether you have true charity of God; without it all other things avail nothing for salvation. For you have heard the apostle’s clear declaration, in which he says that charity is necessary for salvation. Let no one trust in the wisdom of this world; let no one vainly exalt himself over natural gifts received; let no one presume upon bodily continence, since if charity is lacking, the observance of the rest contributes nothing to salvation.

But lest anyone presume a false estimation of himself, the holy apostle set forth certain signs and operations of charity, saying: Charity is patient, is kind. Charity does not envy, does not act perversely, is not puffed up, is not ambitious, does not seek what is its own, is not provoked, does not think evil, does not rejoice over iniquity, but rejoices with the truth. It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Charity never fails.

Have the charity of God in you, my brothers; unless you keep it, you labor in vain by striving. We have often told you that humility is altogether necessary for you; it is not only a great virtue, but also the nurse of the other virtues and the chief guardian of them all. For Pope Gregory says: He who gathers virtues without humility carries dust into the wind.

For humility is the greatest virtue of monks, since they are bound to be humbler than the others who live their life in the world, inasmuch as by profession they are constrained to strive and tend toward greater perfection. Humility is the stable foundation of the virtues; whatever has been built upon it will be overturned by no whirlwind of storm. Through pride the devil fell from heaven; through humility man ascends.

We are called monks in vain if we have not been humble; for just as fire is extinguished if it lacks the fuel of wood, so a monk without humility is judged very unworthy of his name. By Christian humility the devil is conquered and, as if struck by a nail, [unclear]. No jewel is seen more splendid in monastic adornment than humility; if a monk despises it, he has denied both his profession and the humble Christ.

Prudence succeeds humility; it always directs all things according to the rule of reason and does nothing except what is right, honorable, and praiseworthy. Prudence knows what it ought virtuously to seek, and it knows well what, according to the right judgment of reason, it ought to avoid. True and perfect prudence is that by which we seek that highest, unchangeable good and order all things to their due and right end.

Prudence preserves the observance of regular discipline stable in its vigor, and brings wandering monks back to the complete reform of the purpose they have lost. Prudence directs the monk on the right way of salvation and lets him turn aside to neither side. Prudence preserves peace and concord among the brothers, and orders right action in each case.

Prudence is very necessary for you, my brothers, so that by it you may know in every action what things you should undertake and what, in turn, should be avoided. True prudence, not of the flesh but of the spirit, teaches you to despise the world and to desire only those things that are future and eternal. Prudence teaches a man to stand firm in himself, and to turn aside from the purpose of holiness neither in prosperity nor in adversity.

Prudence teaches one to order present things beautifully; it brings past things back to memory for instruction, and foresees future things lest anything happen unexpectedly. Prudence corrects offenders vigorously and fruitfully, and orders everything that it either does, says, or thinks, according to the judgment of reason, to its due end.

Be therefore prudent as serpents and simple as doves, because prudence [without?] goodness is wickedness, and simplicity without rational prudence is called foolishness. For the most holy Paul, apostle of Christ, says: Be prudent, lest you be deceived by the fraud of others, but simple, lest you deceive anyone. For this is the office of a prudent man: neither to be able to be deceived, nor to wish to deceive anyone.

A prudent man does not abandon the judgment of reason, and does nothing of which one must repent; for he is called prudent from foreseeing. Justice accompanies prudence; it is the most splendid of all the virtues. For without justice, all the remaining virtues are nothing. Justice is the preserver of religion, the mother of grace, the nurse of observance, the nourisher of life, the begetter of peace, and the source of piety
