*Homily 23. Fol. LVI.*

He who works the righteousness of God is not sorrowful to no purpose. A good conscience always advances in the love of the Lord; and he who lives innocently will not fear danger. A good conscience has peace and concord with all in God, and cares for its own salvation in humble simplicity. A good conscience strengthens the soul; but he who is constant in evils will fall into despair. A good conscience confidently reproves the foolish man; but he who is evil keeps a bitter silence. A good conscience gladdens the soul; but he who is conscious of his own evils suffers continual desolation of mind. A good conscience rebukes the impious man; but he who has lost the testimony of uprightness always fears reproof. A good conscience is the most beautiful adornment of the soul; and he who does not care for wisdom in this life will die in want. A good conscience is the dwelling-place of virtues and the true mansion of heavenly mysteries. A good conscience is the consummation of all philosophy and the fullness of all sciences. A good conscience is the best teacher of virtues, and the completed discipline of all studies. A good conscience bears the best fruit of holiness; and, standing founded in the fear of the Lord, admits no sin. A good conscience is more precious than all the wealth of the world, and much sweeter than all desirable things that can be had in this life. A good conscience is the merit of Christian perfection, and the beginning from which future blessedness may humbly be presumed. A good conscience is a fertile field of virtues, and the sweet and pleasant fragrance of the paradise of God. A good conscience is the sweetest companion of this life, and a glorious mediator between God and man. A good conscience is very fearful to demons, but a pleasing friend to the holy angels. A good conscience makes the dying man secure against the snares of demons, and deserves to have the escort of angels. A good conscience does not fear to appear in the sight of the judge, nor does it fear the judgment of accusing synderesis. A good conscience has hope in God, and, without hesitation, in quietness, the best confidence; for he who remembers that he has committed no evil comes confidently to the examination of judgment. A good conscience will be richly rewarded by the Lord God, and is changed into everlasting joy. A good conscience is not one that commits no evil, but one that, for love of God, preserving purity from every sin, also most becomingly adorns itself by the exercise of all good works. For a good conscience, according to the admonition of the prophet, wholly turns away from evil, and with the most zealous intention always and continually does good. A good conscience especially adorns a monk, and makes him far removed from secular actions. A good conscience follows the Lord Jesus in simple purity, and is stained by no spots of errors. For a conscience that is good, and as long as it is good, cannot be erroneous, but always remains founded in the truth of the Holy Spirit. But a conscience that has become erroneous is not good. For error both opposes truth and in no way shares in goodness. When conscience is erring, danger is near, and the descent to hell is very steep.

There are many among cloistered persons who are weak and too feeble in spirit, who, preoccupied by various causes, are not always guided by the right judgment of their consciences. And often, indeed, a small or great error of conscience arises from ignorance: namely, whenever someone less experienced in spiritual exercise doubts whether the way he is considering in his mind is good or bad. For who, walking rightly in his simplicity, understands all things? In doubtful matters the counsels of elders are to be sought; and what they have decreed, the monk should not hesitate to embrace as a judgment of truth. Sometimes conscience errs from negligence, when a monk neglects to examine himself by the scrutiny of reason and does not labor to seek out truth in doubtful matters. From this a pernicious error arises in the conscience, through which reason is gradually darkened. Frequently also pride makes conscience erroneous and far removed from the knowledge of truth. For the proud man, although he understands that he does not know, nevertheless desires to appear wise and learned to all; therefore he is ashamed to confess his ignorance. Sometimes, moreover, error of conscience arises from singularity, with which presumption is always [said to be] mingled. But this plague is often troublesome to monks and to those who profess holiness without the necessary foundation of humility. For the sheep that walks singular paths is more quickly caught and strangled by wolves. Sometimes also an excessively disordered affection hurls conscience into error: namely, when a man loves his own will more than is fitting. For whoever is led by the affection of his own sensuality is often blinded in the judgment of truth.
