But since we cannot remain firmly and without alteration of mind in one state of divine love, it is necessary for us, lest we fall away entirely, to renew our holy purpose every day, so that as often as, through the negligence of human weakness, we begin, however slightly, to grow sluggish from spiritual fervor, so often, examining ourselves by the memory of future things and by contemplation of the Lord’s Passion, we may at once bring our conscience back to its former purpose of making progress.

But this must be most carefully guarded against by us: that we not put off this holy effort to make progress until tomorrow, since it has often been harmful to have deferred to a future time a good thing that could have been done sooner. Therefore the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For his wrath will come suddenly, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you.” Augustine also says: “Why do you defer the good until tomorrow, saying: life will still be long? For who knows whether it will be long? But if it is not good, what does it profit that it will be long?”

Without a good life, the conduct of a monk should be called death rather than life. The remedies of a more corrected life, therefore, must not be deferred by any delays, lest the time for conversion perish through tardiness, when now no delay is granted for repenting. For he who promised pardon to the penitent did not promise tomorrow. No one who fears God delays turning to repentance, nor prolongs today’s salvation until tomorrow. But every man who knows himself to be established in lukewarmness of mind, and who defers amendment of life from day to day, is deceived by the hope of a longer life; for if he knew that he would die tomorrow, today without doubt he would change his purpose. The hope of a longer life has wretchedly deceived many, who, while they daily defer the amendment of their manner of life, suddenly fall into the abyss of death.

Who among you, brothers, does not resolve in his mind to take up the purpose of a more corrected life before he dies? For I believe that each one of you intends still to practice greater piety toward God before he falls into the danger of death. And why do you wait so long? Why do you defer from day to day? Behold, you who now live healthy and do not devote yourselves to the spiritual exercises of divine love when you can, while the Lord waits patiently: the last day will come for each of you, and it is already hastening, on which, when the angel of death presses urgently, the power whose exercise you have delayed so long will be taken away from you.

Therefore again and again I ask you, brothers, and with whatever diligence I can I admonish you: turn to the Lord our God with your whole heart while you live safe and sound, and do not delay your will any longer, lest you also lose the power granted to you. Think, and think again often, that we are not citizens of this world, but strangers and pilgrims; nor do we have here a lasting city, but we seek the one to come, and day by day we are hastening toward death, even though we do not know it.

*On the love of quiet and solitude.*

*The sixth homily.*

There are two intellects created from the beginning, which I know is hidden from none of you, dearly beloved brothers: namely, the angelic and the human. But when the angelic intellect was created, Holy Scripture does not plainly disclose; yet the opinion of the Fathers counted the creation of the angels as a work of God and reckoned it among the works of the six days. There are, however, some who write that the angels were created long before this visible world; and just as we cannot prove this by the testimony of the divine Scriptures, so neither should we rashly attack it. For there are several grave and holy men who have placed the creation of the angels long before the beginning of the world.

Now the angelic intellect was made from the very beginning in the actuality of all knowable things, and it knew all that it could know, and afterward admitted nothing toward its own perfection. For it was created by the Lord in the natural perfection that it could have, and required no manner of increase by which it might understand more. For [unclear], joined to God, was made a pure intellect by nature, and does not, in our fashion, need exercise in order to understand; its being admits no corruption.
