*On the Sleep and Wakefulness of Monks. Homily XVII.*

The monk who always thinks that he is going to die allows no time to pass idly. Scripture says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” On the contrary, concerning the forgetful and sluggish it is written: “They are a people without counsel and without prudence. Would that they were wise and understood, and provided for their last things.” The remembrance of death brings watchfulness into the soul and extinguishes every disordered thought of the mind. He who rightly fears death never grows sluggish in good work. For the wise man says: “Whatever your hand is able to do, do it diligently, because neither work, nor reckoning, nor wisdom, nor knowledge will be in the underworld to which you are hastening.” For the monk who is a true worshiper of God spends no moment of time uselessly, but night and day, in the exercise of good work, he carries out the watchfulness of the mind with the greatest devotion; and while sleeping in body he constantly says in spirit to God: “I sleep, and my heart keeps watch.” Sleep is an image of death and a binding of the body’s sense; but he who is a true lover [unclear].

*On the Sleep and Wakefulness of Monks.*

For they mercifully allowed monks in community a space of five hours before the night lauds, and they likewise added two or [unclear] hours after Matins for sleep. He for whom this, namely seven hours for resting, is not enough shows himself to be an excessively lazy and slothful monk. Therefore we are admonished to keep watch not only in mind but also in body, because no man can preserve himself in true purity if he disdains to keep bodily watch in God’s service. Let us therefore keep watch, dearly beloved brothers, especially in these holy days of Lent, in which we have declared war on the vices and are vigorously prepared to conquer and overcome the ancient enemy, the devil. For hear the holy prince of the apostles admonishing us with these words: “Brothers, be sober and watch.” Why did he give this admonition? “Because,” he says, “your adversary the devil goes about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour; resist him, strong in faith.” And so, my brothers, let us keep watch as long as we are set in battle, lest, weighed down by drowsiness or idleness, we be overcome by the adversary of our salvation. For as long as we live in the flesh, we must fight bravely against the enemy by watchfulness and the chastisement of our body. Let the labor of the contest not frighten you, because, even if our adversary is troublesome, nevertheless he is so weak and feeble that he can overcome no one except one who is willing, and one who has entirely cast away all guard over himself. But the Lord Jesus Christ, our helper and protector, is strong; he gives strength to those who fight eagerly, and in heaven he will grant the victors the unfading crown with all his saints. Just as dust is scattered by the wind, so our adversary the devil, confounded and terrified by the voice of those singing psalms with humble devotion, is put to flight. Therefore let none of you, dearly beloved brothers, act negligently; let no one grow sluggish at the time of prayer through laziness of mind and drowsiness; let no one fear the devil’s contest with disordered fear. Stand manfully for the everlasting salvation of your souls against the [raging?] enemy; regard your own flesh as no less suspect than demons.
