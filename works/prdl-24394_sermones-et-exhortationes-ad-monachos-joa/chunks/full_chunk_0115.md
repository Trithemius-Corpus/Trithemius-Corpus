*That monks must live carefully.*

Justice is the virtue that distributes and preserves to each person what is his own; it neglects private advantage in order to preserve common advantage and equity, without which no commonwealth can long subsist in peace. For the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “A kingdom is transferred from nation to nation because of injustices, and injuries, and insults, and diverse deceits.” The order of justice first has regard to God, then to the fatherland, thereafter to parents, and afterward to all in common according to distinctions of rank and the merits of dignities. To God, first of all, at the command of justice, we owe religion, faith, worship, devotion and acts of thanksgiving, and true love from the whole heart under obedience. To ourselves, next, we owe purity of mind and body, continence with zeal for perfection, so that through obedience we may be permitted to return to him by whom we were created. In the third place we owe to the fatherland its advantage, honor, and defense, and we must procure its usefulness as much as we can, together with justice itself. Fourth, we are bound to parents by honor and obedience, and, in their necessity, by care and every kind of support. Fifth, we owe obedience to our prelates and superiors, and humble subjection for the love of Christ. Sixth, as prelates, we owe doctrine and example to subjects, and correction to those who offend. In the seventh place, moreover, we are bound to elders and princes by honor, and by subjection without crime for the love of God. Eighth, we owe reverence to venerable persons, and honor to priests. Consequently, to equals we are bound by friendship; to inferiors by consideration; to benefactors by return of service; to friends by fellowship; to enemies by patience; to the poor by mercy; to the wretched by consolation; to all by charity. This, moreover, is true justice: that we love God with our whole heart for his own sake, and our neighbor in God as ourselves, and our enemies for God’s sake, distributing to each what is owed to him by right and providing for the advantage of all.

But since excessive justice easily falls into sin, temperance is necessary for you in all things; it alone makes men perfect. Temperance is the virtue that, by reason, discerns and overcomes the movements of the soul and every lust, so that impulse in acting may not exceed the equity of the law. Temperance is an excellent virtue: it rules pleasures and preserves all things in due order. Apply temperance to all your actions, words, and also your thoughts, and apply the discretion necessary to the virtues. Whatever good you do with discretion receives the name by the merit of virtue; but what is done without discretion must be called vice. Discretion is the moderator of all virtues, and the greatest ornament of prudence and justice. Discretion is necessary not only for prelates but also for subjects, since it gives to virtue its fairest and most honorable beauty. Hence a certain wise man said: “There is a measure in things; there are, finally, fixed limits, beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.”

For this, brothers, strength of soul is necessary for you; through it you may become not only steadfast in adversities, but also ready and cheerful in difficult matters. For who, without the greatest strength of soul, could despise this world with its pleasures, and persevere in holy monastic religion under the laws of obedience even unto death? Strength of soul sees the powers of pride, fearfully to be dreaded, from the charity of Christ. Strength belongs to the little ones: to despise peaceful pleasures. Strength lies hidden in prosperity, is shown in adversity, and is manifestly declared through patience. A strong soul is not one that avenges injuries inflicted on it, but one that patiently endures them; and if it has taken an adversary into its power, it thinks that it could have taken vengeance. The strength of monks is to despise the world, conquer the flesh, spurn all the honors and pleasures of the world, and place all hope and confidence in God alone. The measure of strength is to be neither fearful nor rash, because one walking on the right road turns neither to the right nor to the left. That monk is to be judged strong who is temperate in all things, moderate, and just. Hence the most holy [unclear] says: to despise the flatteries of prosperity; to overcome in the heart the fear of adversity. The saints were made strong in battle, because they tame the flesh, shine with virtues, are strengthened in spirit, fail in earthly things, seek heavenly things, can be killed, cannot be bent, nor can they endure false things. Through weakness they fear, and, though harmed, they never depart from conquering truth.
