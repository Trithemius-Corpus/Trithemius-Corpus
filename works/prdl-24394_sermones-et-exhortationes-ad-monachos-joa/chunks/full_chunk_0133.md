*How sin is to be fled.*

[Whoever does this] imitates not God but the [unclear] of sin, the devil. Let us love God, and sin will not have dominion in us; let us love Christ, and we shall do nothing contrary to our salvation. Nothing is better, nothing stronger against sin, nothing safer than to love God, who first loved us and washed us from all sins in the blood of his only-begotten Son. For we are bound to God, and are obliged to love him with all the powers of the heart [unclear].

First, indeed, God is to be loved by us supremely for no other reason than for his own sake, who is the highest and only good; in him alone are rest and the soul’s salvation; without him there is no salvation, no rest, no blessedness, no happiness, either in heaven or on earth. For since God is the cause, maker, and beginning of all things, he alone is the object of the soul’s blessedness; to the everlasting enjoyment of him one attains by charity alone, and one comes to love of him by purity of mind alone. Therefore, since God is the one and highest good of the soul, he is to be loved by us for his own sake with all our powers. Outside God there is nothing good, nothing holy, nothing by which the soul can be gladdened forever. For God alone is the center of the soul’s blessedness.

Second, we are commanded to love the Lord our God, because he deigns to show himself to us as a most loving father. For thus he taught us to pray: “Our Father who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name.” Truly God is the father of us all: he created us all, nourishes, feeds, and preserves us in being, lest we perish. There has never been any father on earth so indulgent, who loved his only-begotten son with so great a charity as that with which God almighty, our father, loves, cherishes, directs, and follows us with loving-kindness. Therefore we are obliged to love him with a pure heart above all things, who gives us both being and living. For he himself is our father; and Adam, who was created first of all men, did not know us.

Third, we ought to love God above all things because of the likeness according to which man was made in the image of God, following his creator in his intellect by a certain likeness. But God is perfect intellect, infinite and everlasting; by participating in his goodness, our intellect is made blessed through grace in him who created all things. For although there is no comparison between God and man, nevertheless there is, in the rational soul itself, by the nobility of its created condition, a likeness to its beginning, of dignity not to be despised. For the prophet says: “I said, you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High.” The Lord God also said in the beginning: “Let us make man to our image and likeness.” Great toward our race is the gracious condescension of divine mercy; it is fitting that we remember it at all times with a pure mind, in the observance of charity.

Fourth, we ought to love our God above all things on account of his inestimable munificence, whom we acknowledge to have bestowed so many benefits on us though we did not deserve them. When we did not exist, he first established us in being by his goodness, and ordained us for a share in his glory, which will never end. Thus Scripture says concerning the nobility of man: “Thou hast made him a little less than the angels; with glory and honor thou hast crowned him, and hast set him over the works of thy hands.” Do you wish to know, brothers, in what respect man is less than an angel? Attend carefully to what we say here. Two intellects were created from the beginning, the angelic and the human. The angelic intellect, immediately from the beginning of its creation, was made full and perfect in the actuality of all knowable things, and by nature knew and comprehended everything that it ought to know; but the human intellect at first exists in potency with respect to all knowable things, ignorant of everything, while at the end, through many actions, it will at last acquire knowledge of all things, as the angelic intellect has it. For the perfect consummation of nature, which is in the angelic intellect from the beginning, will be given to the human at the end. Hence it is clear that between these two created intellects there is only this difference: the angelic is what it can be, while the human is to receive the likeness of angelic perfection at the end, that is, after the death of the flesh. For when the angelic intellect was first created, without any action of its own, immovably, substantially, and by nature, it was made full and perfect in the knowledge of all things; and those of them who adhered by love to their beginning, namely God, were confirmed in eternal happiness, while those who, glorying in themselves, withdrew in affection from God, are perpetually wretched and unhappy. But the human intellect, because it was created in the beginning in ignorance and deprivation of all things, must necessarily, by action, motion, and art, at last come to the knowledge of things set before it. So long as we are in the body and are pilgrims away from the Lord, our intellect, because of its excessive commerce with the body, is as it were placed in the darkness of ignorance and is confounded, and is by no means illumined unto perfect knowledge of future things. But afterward, when the soul has been separated from the body, then the intellect will rejoice in angelic perfection. Therefore we differ from the angels as much as we participate in bodily impurity.
