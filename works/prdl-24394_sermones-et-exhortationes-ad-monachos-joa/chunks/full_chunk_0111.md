*Homily. XXV.*

*Fol. LVIII.*

the brothers who preceded us in this monastery: Just as there is no memory among us of those who preceded us, so also we shall be in everlasting oblivion among those who follow. Let us think on these things beforehand, dearest brothers, while we are alive, [unclear] fall into death. Just as daily we hasten toward death, so daily, according to our powers, let us prepare ourselves; for he dies secure who in life is adorned with good and holy works. Do not let the hope of a longer life deceive you, for the longer you live in this world, the shorter you are going to live. Behold, daily tending toward death, we die; daily one part of life is cut away from us; and the longer our life is prolonged, the more the remaining time of living is shortened. All that we have lived through in time we have wholly lost, because it has passed away; what we live today we share moment by moment with death; how much time for living remains to us in the future we do not know. Why, then, do we trust in the vanity of this life, since we are soon to die? Therefore, brothers, let us continually think that we shall soon arrive there whither [unclear] of all our elders [unclear]. We, placing our hope in the sacrament of my Lord, the Savior of all, and despising the pleasures of the world for love of him, shall be able to drive away the indiscreet fear of dying in this way: if each day we prepare ourselves as though about to die immediately. Yet it is one thing to have dread of death, another to have trembling. For the Lord Jesus too felt dread of death, not trembling, in order to show the properties of the two natures. Continual meditation on death, therefore, is altogether necessary for us who are weak, so that the mind, not yet perfect in the charity of Christ, may be roused to the grace of compunction as if by a certain violent impulse. For the memory of future death, like a nail driven into wood, pierces the mind with useful dread. The memory of death brings in arduous penitence and true sobriety of mind. The memory of death is necessary for beginners, whose conversion begins from servile fear, so that it may successively be lifted through filial fear into charity. The memory of death and of the strict examination of the future judgment of God is especially necessary for those who by evil habit and by [unclear] are drawn to sin. And there are certain among you too lukewarm and negligent in spiritual warfare; admonishing them, we exhort them paternally to remember that they are mortal, and thereafter to live in such a way that they do not fear to die. For it is very dangerous to live in that state in which one would fear to die. We must live so that we await death secure; and this can at last happen if, fervent in all purity of mind and body, we continually burn in the exercises of divine charity. Let us live as innocently as can be done, according to the Rule of the divine father Benedict, which we have professed, and we shall have good hope, because almighty God will have mercy on us, who does not permit a man of good will to perish. Let us always think of God as present to us everywhere, who sees all things, knows all things, understands all things, rewards all things; and let us live with that purity with which we have promised to serve his most holy majesty.

*End of the twenty-fifth homily of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to the monks.*
