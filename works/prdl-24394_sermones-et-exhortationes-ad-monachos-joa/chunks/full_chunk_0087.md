For truly, if true humility is lacking, the gathering together of all the other virtues will be nothing other than a kind of collapse of a house built without a foundation on sand. For in the spiritual building humility lays the stable foundation; charity orders the breadth of that house; the wall is raised on high by the continuation of good works in fervor of spirit; the roof is placed at the summit in faith in our Lord Jesus Christ and in firm hope of the promise of eternal protection and happiness; and its length is arranged in the long-suffering of patience with most beautiful ordering.

Therefore, my brothers, again and again I admonish, adjure, and beseech you to take care to found the building of spiritual exercise in the depth of humility, because no good work is pleasing to God if it has been offered apart from true humility. Now there are many things which have been wont to move the servants of God toward true humility. For consideration of their own worthlessness humbles some within themselves before God, because, when they consider that by experience they have already often found themselves wretched and weak, being humbled they place all their hope and confidence in God. Others, weighing carefully the multitude of God’s benefits toward them, fear the judgment of ingratitude; and therefore in the sight of God, with great fear and reverence of gratitude, they always go forward humble. And who among us is there, brothers, to whom the compassion of almighty God has not also granted many benefits, even individually? If we wish to be grateful for them, we must above all be humble.

Others, after a multitude of sins, converted to penitence by divine kindness, diligently recall to memory how many evils they have committed against the law of the Almighty; and for these, humbled in bitterness of spirit, they detest their impieties with horror. But we offend in many things; whence it is necessary that we humble ourselves always in mind and heart, and even in body, so that grace may abound where sin formerly abounded. Often, moreover, the providence of divine compassion permits certain persons to sin enormously for a time, so that afterward, converted to penitence, they may learn to remain inwardly humble. Better is a humble penitent than a proud man who has not sinned, because humility justifies penitents, while pride condemns the arrogant eternally, even if they have done nothing else evil than this alone, that they are proud.

Others, carrying in their heart by daily meditation the bitter passion of Jesus Christ, humble themselves the more, the more they know that they are greatly indebted for such and so wonderful benefits of that same Redeemer of theirs. The Son of God humbled himself, having been made man for us; and, filled with many reproaches, he voluntarily underwent the most shameful death of the cross, that he might free us from the destruction of perpetual death. What Christian man, and especially what monk, will not humble his soul greatly before God, when he has recalled to memory such humility of his Creator and Redeemer? For truly, the man whom the humility of Christ does not move to meekness will profit from no admonitions of men. In vain do we say that we are monks, unless we have followed Christ humbly according to our powers. For this reason he said in the holy Gospel that he himself is the way, so that we might imitate his humility, and so that the conduct of so great a master might become the spiritual form of the disciple.

Therefore, brothers, as much as we can, let us strive with all our strength that all that pride has made swollen in us may be leveled, renewed, and reformed by humility conceived from meditation on the most bitter passion of Jesus Christ. O how fitting it is for us to humble our souls for love of Jesus Christ, my brothers, since God himself and the Creator of all, made [unclear] of humility, cast himself down for us into such great humility. Therefore the supreme medicine for inducing true humility in a servant of God is for him daily, and often, to recall to memory the most bitter passion of Christ Jesus.

Tribulations also, adversities, and infirmities, together with poverty or want, have made many humble, who perhaps in prosperous and favorable circumstances would have perished, puffed up. And we trust that what we often customarily say is conducive to our salvation: poverty, since we have a great cause of humility, while we find no material for exaltation and pride. Others, by continual exercise of virtues and by memory of the death which shortly they must necessarily suffer, have come to great humility. Whom would not even the attentive meditation of death humble, however proud? Or who is so fierce in spirit that he would not gradually put down his swelling pride, if he reflected more diligently that he is soon to die? Accordingly the conscience of a monk ought never to drive away from itself the memory of future death, so that he may remain both humble and sorrowful, and, standing firm in humility, not [unclear].
