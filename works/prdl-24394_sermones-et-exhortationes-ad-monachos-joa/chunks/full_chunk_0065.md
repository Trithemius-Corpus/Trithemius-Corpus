*On the abstinence and fasting of monks.*

... to bear speech; to admit no vain, useless, or shameful thoughts; to commit nothing unbecoming a servant of God; and to omit nothing that belongs to the consummation of righteousness. For unless the mind has fasted from vices and sins, bodily abstinence will profit nothing for the salvation of the soul. Hence the blessed priest Jerome says: Your abstinence and fasting are pleasing insofar as they are offered with holy conduct, so that what in others are coverings for vices may in you become ornaments of virtues. For what does it profit for the body to be worn down by abstinence, if the soul grows dark with pride? Or what virtue is there in not drinking wine, and being drunk with anger, lust, hatred, and envy? Or what will it avail us for eternal salvation, brothers, that according to the Rule of our holy father Benedict we do not eat meat, if we do not subdue the vices of the flesh?

For the thrice-holy pope Gregory says: In vain is the body worn down by abstinence, if the mind, divided by disordered impulses, is scattered among vices. Therefore Scripture says: Sanctify a fast. To sanctify a fast is, by adjoining other good works, to show abstinence of the flesh worthy of God. Indeed, if abstinence from foods alone made a man virtuous, there would have been no need to add “sanctify”; but because fasting alone is not sufficient for the sanctification of the soul, it is necessary that it be adorned with the ornament of all virtues. For those who abstain from foods and do evil imitate demons, for whom there is always fasting, and sin is never absent. A fast acceptable to God is to restrain the body through abstinence from desire for evils, and through the spirit of compunction to mortify continually the deeds of the flesh for love of Christ.

Let the mind of the one fasting be fervent in the love of God; let the conscience always be clean from vices; and the fasts of humble servants will please the Most High. Let monks have daily fasts, as the canon of the holy preceptor Benedict has established; let their hearts be clean, as the Lord Jesus commanded in the holy Gospel; let their refection be moderate, fleeing satiety, since just as from excessive fullness the body is corrupted by diverse and various diseases, so in it the disordered movement of lust is stirred up. For moderate and measured food is both medicine for the body and a support of virtue for the soul. For Saint John, the golden-mouthed bishop, says: Nothing is so pleasant as food well digested and cooked, nothing so healthful, nothing so sharpens the keenness of the senses, nothing so drives away sickness, as moderate refection. For sufficiency, with measured nourishment, produces health and pleasure together. But abundance makes disease, brings troubles, and generates various illnesses. For what excessive hunger does, immoderate fullness of foods also customarily does. Hunger indeed in a few days takes a man away and frees him from this penal life; but excess of foods consumes the human body and makes it rot, and wastes it with long illness, and at last consumes it with a cruel death.

Therefore, my most beloved brothers, in order that you may live sound in mind and healthy in body, hold to this rule of living which the holy fathers have prescribed for you by word and shown beforehand by living example: fleeing all luxury of foods and drinks, indulge the body only so much as is sufficient, when nature requires it, for good health. Let food [unclear] hunger, but not incite the body to lust; let drink extinguish thirst, but not overthrow the mind or the senses; let clothing ward off cold, but not cause the soul to grow soft in love of vanity. Always remember, brothers, that gluttony shamefully expelled our first parents from the kingdom of paradise and innocence. They did not devour bird, fish, calf, or pig, so that you should think the food itself at fault, but presumed against God’s command in the matter of a very common apple, so that you may know that not the food but the appetite is liable to the vice of sin.

For it can often happen that a person takes richer foods without fault, provided he regulates his appetite, while another takes cheaper foods not without guilt of conscience, when by immoderation he exceeds the measure of temperance. Therefore let none of you think himself secure from the vice of gluttony for the reason that he eats neither rich nor delicate foods, since not the food but the appetite for food is held at fault. For it is very possible to incur the guilt of sin while eating beans and vegetables, and for another, taking the most delicate birds as food, not to sin. Hence one of the holy fathers says: A belly filled with any foods whatever brings forth nurseries of lust; nor can the mind prevail to govern the rudders of discernments when it is suffocated by the weight of foods.
