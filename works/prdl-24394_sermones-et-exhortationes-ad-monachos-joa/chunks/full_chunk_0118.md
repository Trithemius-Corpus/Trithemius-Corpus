*Sermon II. Fol. LXII.*

...the legate of the apostolic see to come into Germany; and since he burned with great zeal for our order, he established and introduced many things for its reform. Hearing, moreover, of the good reputation and most fervent purpose for regular discipline of the aforesaid abbot in Bursfeld, he summoned him to himself and appointed him general reformer of the order, and through his diligence reformed several monasteries. On this occasion at last he instituted an annual chapter for the preservation of the reform; he transferred the presidency from St. Matthias to Bursfeld; he obtained from the apostolic see the confirmation of all the things that he had instituted for the good of the reform.

The first abbot of Bursfeld in the reform was John, a monk from Reinhausen, duly appointed; the second was John called of Hagen; the third was Theodoric, whom we have seen; and the fourth is John, who is now newly appointed in governance. Therefore, dearest brothers, we have briefly touched upon the origin of our reform so that you may know with how much labor it began, and so that you may persevere constant in holy regular observance even unto death. For among all the reforms of our order that have preceded, none was ever more conformable and consonant to the Rule of the divine father Benedict, and more alien from foreign inventions. We have read the statutes of nearly all the other reforms, but we judge none more consonant than ours to the Rule and to the purity of Benedict's intention.

This is the Bursfeld reform, most holy in the order, which both teaches true obedience and preserves subjection in humility. In this holy religious life monks live poor and without private property; they live humble; they live obedient. O venerable union of monks, in which twin charity commands, obedience serves, humility complies, prayer keeps watch, perseverance fights, holy chastity triumphs. This is the school of regular discipline, the instructress of Christian humility, which directs conduct toward rectitude and restrains the vices of the flesh. In this arena of virtues no one is permitted to follow his own will against the Rule; no one is forbidden to do good. In this holy union life is lived according to the purity of the Rule, and if anyone begins to stray from the path of rectitude, he is at once recalled to the observance of justice by the diligence of the seniors. The Rule of the most holy father Benedict is read publicly three times a year, so that no one may attempt to excuse himself from observance under the pretext of ignorance.

There are two things that promise me great hope for the perseverance of this holy reform: namely, the annual chapter and the diligent biennial visitation of the monks. If either of these is interrupted in any way, the reform is at once endangered. Consider now, therefore, dearest brothers, and take the most diligent care you can, lest you occupy this monastery of the divine bishop Martin uselessly under the title of so venerable a reform; for unless you live purely, holily, and blamelessly according to the Rule and the statutes of the order, you trust in the name in vain.

We shall speak to you a few admonitions for your salvation; by zealous observance of them you will deservedly have both the name and the dignity of our Bursfeld reform. First of all, before all else, direct your effort with all desires toward monastic perfection; whenever you sense it growing lukewarm, renew it at once by attentive remembrance of the Lord's passion and most innocent death. For even if we are not bound by necessity of salvation to be perfect, nevertheless we are bound to strive toward perfection with all our desires. For as soon as a monk casts away the effort to advance, he falls into the pit of falling away.

The second thing that the holy custom of our reform has established for you to keep strongly preserves the effort of advancing in the desire once conceived. Therefore, when you rise, having been awakened for Prime, do these things in secret. Examine and discuss the future course of the coming day, and prudently consider what useful thing must be undertaken. Set before your hearts how cautiously you wish to live throughout the whole day, and arm your spirits with patience against all things that could come upon you as adversities. Whatever you do, complete it in the fervor of charity, and always order all words and deeds to the praise of God.

A third thing we judge useful and necessary for you: that through most devout prayers you should always strive to implore divine assistance in all things. And it seems very fitting to us, as we have often said and always admonish, that each one of you should at least once a day keep remembrance of the Lord's passion, and for the many benefits received from God should at least once offer thanksgiving. In order that this may be done uniformly by each of you, listen to this brief form that we have arranged, and if you wish, write it down for yourselves: I adore, praise, bless, and glorify you, Lord Jesus Christ, Son of the living God, savior and giver of life to the souls of all the faithful, and I give you thanks from the depths of my heart
