*On the daily expiation of sins.*

We are not without sin. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the mind that thinks many things. For Scripture says: “There is no righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin.” Likewise the holy evangelist John says: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” Indeed, through commerce with the flesh, which is too much delighted, the soul sins, and the strength of the spirit is diminished by despair. For the mind is deceived by the presentation of sensible things, and readiness to sin is strengthened by long habit. The weakness of the flesh enfeebles the fervor of the spirit, and the frailty of the will resists the holy purpose of the mind. Hence Solomon, the most wise king, says in the book of Proverbs: “Seven times in a day the righteous man will fall and rise again; but the impious will fall into evil.” By the testimony of this saying we are admonished not to grow slack in the purpose of holiness, even if it should happen that we have fallen seven times in a day.

As often, O monk, as you have fallen through sin from the purpose of holiness, so often rise again to your former fervor, not with sloth. It belongs to human frailty to fall, but to diabolical obstinacy to remain cast down in the mire of crime. The demons sinned once, and in their obstinacy they were made hardened and pertinacious, never thereafter to return to repentance. But men, as often as they have fallen, can always rise again to repentance, and by tears and prayers obtain the grace of God which they had lost by sinning. For the heart of man is flexible and can be moved in either direction, because no man in the flesh is to be believed able to become so evil that he cannot, if he wishes, recover from his malice and, with God granting it, reform his mind toward goodness. Conversely, no one becomes so good in this mortal life that he cannot become evil, if he misuses the grace of the Lord.

Since these things are so, my brothers, you must be admonished by my exhortation today to walk carefully: so that he who, with conscience as witness, is already advancing on the paths of righteousness may persevere in them by progressing daily, and he who understands himself to be guilty before the Lord may amend his life as quickly as possible. If human frailty daily impels you, or, to speak more properly, casts you down and makes you sin, let the love of God and of your own salvation also incite you daily to expiate your fault humbly through true contrition. Thus also the righteous man falls seven times in a day and rises again, because although he slips through human frailty, nevertheless, always persevering in the purpose of holiness, he is strengthened more and more in spirit in the love of God. For while he keeps his conscience free from grave sins, he is unwillingly stained by daily negligences; and when he sees that he cannot live without faults, he groans the more, humbly recognizing his weakness. But if he has even been overtaken by a graver fault, he soon rises again to amendment through repentance.

Therefore, considering the condition of human frailty, the ancient moderators of monastic discipline established various kinds of expiation, by which both monks of good will might daily advance toward better things and the wicked might be compelled to the observance of the regular life. For in the Rule the holy father Benedict commands thus, saying: “With all solicitude let the abbot take care concerning delinquent brothers, because the physician is not needed by the healthy, but by those who are ill.” For this reason the first leaders of regular observance established by prudent consideration that daily after Prime a chapter of faults should be held in the appointed place, in which, with the abbot or prior presiding [unclear], the actions of all the brothers should be brought into the open with fair examination.

This place, my brothers, bears the type of the purgatory of souls, because here not only are past sins expiated, but we are also fortified by expiatory repentance [unclear]. For in this place, which we call the chapter room, whatever you have committed openly is corrected and expiated through salutary repentance. Here each day, with the paraphrast pronouncing it, you learn your monastic law, according to the integrity of which you promised to live; here every day you hear the exhortatory word of salvation; you listen to the briefly recited victories of Christ’s martyrs and their manifold sufferings for the orthodox faith. This is the school of monks, necessary for the consideration of regular discipline, in which all that is commanded to be observed by cloistered men is learned.
