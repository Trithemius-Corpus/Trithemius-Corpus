*Sermon I. Fol. LIX.*

*In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ begins the second book of exhortations to monks, by Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim and afterwards [of Würzburg?]. How cautiously monks must live: sermon one.*

Many things indeed, most beloved brothers, are necessary for you to consider in this life, if you desire [to attain?] the glory of eternal salvation. For you know that Scripture testifies that your adversary the devil, like a roaring lion, goes about seeking someone to devour; and we know that he must be resisted with great vigilance in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ and in works of charity. Therefore let no one trust presumptuously in his own powers in vanity; for unless you keep yourselves humbly in the fear of the Lord, you will quickly fall into the snares of the devil. “The greater you are,” Scripture commands, “humble yourself in all things,” for God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble. For it is fitting that a monk be humble, and a true contemner of this world. A monk must always be solicitous in the observance of regular discipline, and continually zealous in the fear of the Lord. A monk must be chaste and modest in mind and body alike, and serve God, as he promised, in all purity. A monk ought not live according to his own will, but according to the rule and according to the direction of his pastor, conformably to the norm of cenobitic institution. A monk ought to love [unclear] and holy [unclear], and occupy his mind usefully in the reading of the Scriptures. A monk, at every time even unto death, ought to be solicitous that he may be perfect in the observance of the commandments of God and of the rule. But so that you may know what things are necessary for you, who live together in holy religion, for perfection, I shall describe for you a certain path of the virtues, by which one must direct the way to the kingdom of heaven. Now all the virtues cohere mutually by the bond of relationship, so that he who perfectly possesses one seems to have them all; but he who lacks even one may be said to possess none at all. The first of all virtues necessary for salvation is the most holy catholic faith, separated from every error and pollution of heresies, and originally rooted in the teaching of our Lord Jesus Christ. and justice and truth; and in action, justice and sincerity; and no iniquity will have dominion. For faith without works is dead, and merits nothing toward the salvation of eternal happiness. To have faith without works of charity is to imitate the demons, who, though they are far removed and alien from all charity, nevertheless both believe that God is just and tremble. Hence concerning believers who do not show works of charity, the Lord speaks through the prophet Isaiah and afterwards through himself in the holy Gospel, saying: “This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.” He who praises Christ with his mouth without works is like a man who doubts in faith. For if he truly believed, he would fear God as [unclear]. Faith without works is like a body dead without spirit, which, once removed by a man, is hidden from sight in a tomb. If you believe rightly and live badly, you destroy your own [unclear] with a sword, because for one who knows the good and does not do it, it is sin to him. Beware, my brothers, lest you admit any error in faith, and take the greatest care that there be no wickedness in action. Follow the faith that holy Church commends to us; live your life as the Gospel commands, and the cenobitic norm. Then have hope in God’s mercy at all times most certain, but not alien from the fear of the Lord. Hope is a certain expectation of future good through the grace of the merciful God, and a solicitude for meriting the crown of justice through the exercise of charity. Hope is the certain expectation of future blessedness, which the grace of God works in us, accompanied by the rectitude of a good will. Hope is that by which one hopes to come to the happiness of eternal life, which he desires; and, by the mediation of God’s grace, he composes and fits himself and his will to meet it. Hope is a good, difficult, future, one and possible thing; for no one hopes for the contrary. For no one hopes for evil, nor for what is trivial, nor for what is present or past; nor are we ignorant that hope is not of the impossible. Hope is the moderator of all labors, and the sole consoler in the midst of tribulations. Hope in future good gives rest in this life to those laboring for Christ; just as, for those contending in combat, the expectation of the crown through continual victory lessens the pain of wounds. Hope strengthened the martyrs in torments, and made them, in the virtue of charity, stronger than all their persecutors.
