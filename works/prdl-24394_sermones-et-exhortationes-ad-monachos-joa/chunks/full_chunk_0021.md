For purity of mind, frequent meditation on death: the Wise Man in Ecclesiasticus warned us, saying: “In all your works remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” Thought of future death brings in fear; fear, however, necessarily shakes the mind, and, like a nail driven by a hammer, fastens all the movements of the flesh and of pride to the wood of the cross. In consideration of this opinion, many of the gentile philosophers used to say that the whole life of a wise man is a certain continual meditation on death.

Hence Saint Jerome also says: Nothing will profit you so much toward temperance in all things as frequent meditation on this brief and uncertain life, which passes away like a leaf carried off by the wind, and, when it is not feared, falls into death. Therefore remember your death, which you can escape by no counsel, and you will easily be able to overcome all movements of the flesh. For he who daily thinks that he is going to die despises present things, and, unless he is utterly foolish, will prepare himself for the things to come.

For I know, and I confess that I have in part experienced it, that the will of the human mind cannot long remain uniform in the holy fervor of the spiritual life; and therefore it has seemed good to call the memory of death often back before your eyes, so that, while the mind is shaken by fear of future things, the fervor of the holy purpose may be renewed daily. For the human spirit, bound to the body for the exercise of itself, is exceedingly fluid and unstable: now it is snatched toward this, now it is moved toward that. Therefore the Wisdom of God says in Proverbs through Solomon: “My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes keep my ways.” As though he were saying: Unless you have enslaved the whole love of your heart to divine wisdom, you will never be stable and confirmed in keeping the commandments of the Lord.

For just as the heart is the first principle of motion in a human being, so there is the greater need to guard it for the preservation of life, since, if it is struck by an uncertain disease, the whole life of the body is already in danger. Hence it is written: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.” By the name of the heart, however, we understand the affection by which someone is drawn aside toward love of some thing, clinging to it so much that it involves the whole will. Hence the psalmist says: “If riches abound, do not set your heart on them,” that is, do not love them from the desire of the will.

Nothing is more changeable than the human heart, which is altered by almost every thought and can with difficulty remain in the same purpose unless it restrains itself by a certain violent intention. Saint David, not ignorant of this, said to the Lord: “But I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty. You turned your face away from me, and I was troubled.”

Therefore, my most beloved brothers, if any one of you desires to serve our Lord and Savior in true purity of heart, if anyone wishes to be a monk both in deed and in name, let him learn first not to trust in himself but in God, and let him take care to renew the good purpose of holy conduct always by fresh instruction, not only daily but, as far as he is able, even at each hour and moment. For the fervor of spiritual conduct, to whose practical operation the whole institution of cloistered life is recognized to be ordered, cannot long remain at the same point, since, if it does not always advance toward what lies ahead, it necessarily runs back toward lower things and, what is worse, weaker things.

In the arena of spiritual exercises there is no lingering permanence of station, because it is the property of the human heart by its natural motion always either to advance toward better things or to fall back into that which is evil. Hence, if you do not make progress in spiritual conduct, you necessarily fail; if you do not go forward toward holier things, you certainly retreat. Since experience has made this clear to us, I do not cease to admonish your charity that you always stretch your desire in good conduct toward what lies further ahead, lest you fall into the destructive danger of failing in the very place where you have not renewed the appetite for making progress.

The fervor of a spiritual purpose directs one toward the love of God, and it is preserved in no other way than in him. Now the love of God is not absurdly to be compared to fire, because, just as fire, when the fuel of wood has been withdrawn, is necessarily extinguished, so the love of God, if the continual exercises of good works are lacking, is in danger of dying and vanishing away. Hence there is that saying of the Savior in the Gospel: “I have come to send fire upon the earth, and what do I desire except that it burn?” O monks, happy is that conscience in whose breast nothing burns except the love of Christ, which loves God above all things, which despises all pursuits of vanity.
