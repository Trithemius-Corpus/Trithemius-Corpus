*On the manual labor of monks.*

Father Benedict, detesting the idleness of monks, says in the Rule which he handed down to us: “Idleness is the enemy of the soul; and therefore at fixed times monks ought to be occupied in the labor of their hands, and again at fixed hours in divine reading. For then they are truly monks, if they live by the labor of their own hands, as our fathers and the apostles also did.” Hence also the holy Christ-loving Paul says in the epistle: “He who does not work shall not eat.”

In the beginning of the monastic state this was the custom of monks: that they admitted no one into their fellowship without labor, not so much on account of the necessities of food as on account of the salvation of the soul, lest through idleness an occasion be given for harmful thoughts. At that time monks had no fixed revenues, no tithes, no rents, no annual proceeds at all; but all, living together in common, procured food and clothing for themselves with their hands, burdening no one by the exaction of debt.

The holy apostles of our Savior also, though they had the power to live from the preaching of the Gospel, preferred to labor with their hands, lest they should seem to have burdened any of the faithful, and so that they might have not only the means by which they themselves could live, but also something with which they might supply necessities to the needy. But afterward, when monks began to have revenues and rents, the former pursuits of simplicity soon failed; and pride gradually increased along with the riches of temporal things, to such an extent that in time monks refused all labor of the hands. As long as the monks were poor and humble in birth, they worshiped God in truth; but afterward, when they began to abound in riches, duplicity drove out simplicity, and the arrogance of nobles uprooted the humility of monks. For the integrity of regular observance gave birth to an abundance of temporal goods; but little by little the riches of monks utterly extinguished the purity of the order. And when the integrity of regular observance had been removed, soon, in many monasteries, the riches of temporal goods also failed by the just judgment of God.

From their number was the present monastery of Sponheim, neither the last, my brothers, nor the first, which for many years remained miserably desolate in both conditions, to such a degree that the five monks only who were living in it, leading a dissolute life, did not have the means from which to obtain the necessities of life for themselves. Hence, when the mercy of God at last provided, Frederick, duke of Transylvania, having called upon the counsel of the fathers of the reformed order, in the year of the Christians 1469, expelled the monks deformed in life, deposed the abbot, by name Otto of Cologne, and placed in this monastery four others of the observance of Bursfelde, summoned from the monastery called Montis Speciosi. Over them he set as abbot Johannes, from the noble family of Budischbach, which they commonly call de Carbonaria Domo, a man such as you have seen and heard, worthy of eternal memory; he found this place in extreme poverty, and, presiding for almost fourteen years with great patience, renewed it into the state in which we now live.

Therefore, since want of temporal goods still presses hard upon us, there would be need of continual labor, by which we might nourish ourselves with necessary food and free the house of God in which we dwell from debt. But what are your labors, or how much can they contribute to us for such great needs? We are few in number, and, being unsuited to rustic labors, the institution of the priestly order does not permit us to spend the whole day in the field turning stones with our hands and to neglect the divine service. We are wretched and weighed down by excessive poverty and by lack of all necessities; and if we have labored much, we profit little from it, and if we have worked little, we shall certainly fail all the more in spirit. For man, as Scripture bears witness, is born to labor just as [unclear] to flying. Therefore, lest you eat your bread idle, you will have to be bound to some honest and useful labor, by which the mind may be relieved from weariness of regular observance, and severe want may be repaired at least in some small measure.

In the summer season for hay, and in the days of autumn for the vintage, we have been accustomed to employ you only outside the monastery, namely when labor is very necessary and the scarcity of hired workers increases the price and wage of servants. At other times we have resolved to keep you in the monastery, because your labors, in comparison with the expenses of the monastery, contribute little to its needs, and very few of you, as it seems to us, willingly come forward of your own accord for rustic pursuits.
