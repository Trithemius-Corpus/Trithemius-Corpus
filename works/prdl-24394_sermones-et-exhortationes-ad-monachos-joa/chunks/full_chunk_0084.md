No drowsy, lazy, and slothful man will ever have come to the kingdom of God; no one without continual labor and many sweats has obtained heaven. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “The kingdom of God suffers violence, and the violent,” that is, those who have learned steadfastly to resist the evil desires of the flesh, “seize it.”

Turn over the deeds of all the saints, and nowhere will you find anyone sanctified without great and continual labor. For even if no one becomes holy by his own powers, nevertheless the gracious mercy of the almighty Lord, which grants to those who strive both the will to do good and the strength, sanctifies no one without the works of a good and right will. Since, therefore, God wills all men to be saved and none to perish, we, my brothers, must take the greatest care not to receive his grace in vain, but rather strive with all our strength so that, like fertile ground, we may always answer the free gifts of so great a sower with fitting fruits from the works of good will.

Let us therefore arm ourselves with the shield of faith and put on the breastplate of constancy and saving patience, so that, prepared against all things that can befall us, through humility and the fervor of the Holy Spirit, with our Lord Jesus Christ helping us, we may overcome. In all these things patience is very necessary for us; without its support there never was, nor could there be, any perseverance in good and holy works. For there was no other way to heaven for the saints than patience; nor could he ever be sanctified who cast patience aside.

Indeed, all who pleased almighty God from the beginning of the world down to this our age triumphed over the prince of the world in no other way than through patience. Hence they could all most rightly say that saying of the psalmist: “For your sake we are put to death all the day; we are counted as sheep for slaughter.” The most holy apostle of Christ, Paul, also, recalling the patience of the saints of old, says among other things: “They went about in sheepskins, in goatskins, needy, distressed, afflicted; the world was not worthy of them; wandering in deserts, in mountains, in caves and in the caverns of the earth.” For all who wish to live piously in Christ will suffer persecution, since, as the prophet says, many are the tribulations of the just, and from all these the Lord will deliver them.

Patience, therefore, is necessary for all mortals, and especially befits cloistered persons, since, just as adversities are never lacking to the just in this world, so no one will be justified without patience. Now the chief degrees of patience, as the diligence of the ancients distinguished them, are three. The first and lowest is that of those men who neither seek adversities nor willingly endure them; but when they happen, they are weighed down in spirit and cast down with grief. They also sometimes complain and murmur for a time against those who inflict them; but after the inflamed agitation of the mind has passed, they return to themselves, and are seen patiently to endure the insult inflicted without revenge.

Yet the patience of these men, not of great virtue, is always very close to a fault of the mind, and for that reason must often be reckoned of no merit, and sometimes of lesser merit. And although such persons in our times are said to be distinguished by the virtue of patience, nevertheless, compared with the ancient saints, they do not deserve this venerable name of Christian long-suffering and endurance. For that does not seem to be true patience which has been darkened by bitterness and indignation of an agitated mind.

The second degree of patience is said to belong to those men who, although they do not seek to endure evils, nevertheless when they come, receive them willingly and kindly, and embrace them most patiently as a gift sent by God. And such persons are truly to be called patient: those who, kindled by divine love, bear with equanimity whatever adversity occurs, and preserve in tribulation the same tranquillity of mind that they previously had in prosperity. I would not doubt that that most holy Job the Idumean was of their number: he not only, struck by extreme adversity, patiently bore all things, but even, placed in a whirlpool of evils, gave thanks and blessed the Lord. “The Lord [unclear] gave; the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done; blessed be the name of the Lord.”
