For an unfaithful and foolish promise is displeasing to him, and it is much better not to vow than, after a vow, not to pay what was promised.” And Saint Augustine admonishes us how necessary it is to pay to God what has been promised, saying: “There are certain things which we owe to the Lord even without vowing them; and there are certain things which we do not owe unless we have vowed them. But after we promise them to the Lord, we are bound of necessity to pay them.”

But now, my brothers, I ask you carefully to weigh what you have vowed to the Lord your God, and diligently to consider under what necessity of paying what you promised you have been placed. For you have vowed to your God, not to a man, obedience and the conversion of your morals according to the Rule of our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and perpetual stability in this religious order. But the obedience which you have promised to God you owe to his vicar, a man, whom you have learned from the Rule that you profess to bear the place of Christ in the monastery. And what the conversion of morals ought to be according to the Rule you plainly see in the fourth chapter, entitled “On the Instruments of Good Works,” in which you find the whole sum of monastic perfection comprehended in a few words.

Indeed, you have all heard what our divine father commanded to be done concerning novices, and you know that it was done in the case of each one of you at the beginning of his profession. For he says thus: “The one to be received shall promise in the oratory before all concerning his stability, and the conversion of his morals, and obedience, before God and his saints, so that if ever he should act otherwise, he may know that he is to be condemned by God, whom he mocks.” Consider now, therefore, brothers, under how great a necessity you have been placed for regular observance, since unless you fulfill in deed what you promised when you received the monastic habit, you will be judged before the almighty Lord not as servants of God, not as friends, not as monks, but as mockers.

Therefore always remember the Rule according to which you promised to establish your morals, and labor like true soldiers of Jesus Christ, continually watchful in the fear of the Lord, since, as Scripture says, unless you hold yourselves steadfastly in fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown. Attend to your calling, because the Lord has called you not to idleness, not to the pleasures of this world, nor to pursuing the desires of the flesh, but rather to continual labor, to abstinence from all delights, and to the constant mortification of the flesh under the image of the Crucified, so that you may bear his stigmata in your hearts.

You have now certainly put your hands to the plough of holy heavenly agriculture, in which you are commanded to sweat continually with labor, and never to look back even [unclear], but, always advancing from virtue to virtue, to pant toward the highest summit of perfection. For to live as a monk is nothing other than to shun the company of men and, in solitude, by the desire of most fervent love, actively to please God alone. Hence Scripture says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he will sit solitary and be silent, because he will lift himself above himself.”

Therefore, if you wish to be perfect, you who are bound by the regular institution to love perfection, begin from the way of life that Christ taught; and you who already, for love of him, despise all things, do not neglect humbly to conquer, overcome, and despise even yourselves. But the servant of Christ has nothing except Christ, loves nothing, seeks nothing except Christ, so that he may be perfect and please him whose service he has voluntarily professed. But the monk who has anything besides Christ cannot be perfect, since true love is not divided, nor is the mind truly united to diverse and contrary things. For this reason the Lord says: “If you wish to be perfect, go and sell all that you have and give to the poor; and come, follow me.”

You indeed, most beloved brothers, have already, as I trust, left the world and whatever you had in the world; and you have laid aside not only all the things of the world that you possessed and could have possessed, but also every desire to have possessions; and you have come to this monastery of the divine prelate Martin, in which the poverty of temporal things is great, and this alone has been given to us in place of riches: not to find occasions for avarice.
