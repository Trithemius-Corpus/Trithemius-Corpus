I have set forth this about food, brothers, so that you may understand that there is nothing so difficult that custom, continued over a long time, does not make it light. That holy man, as he himself confesses, did not suddenly arrive at the height of this abstinence, but with the passage of time and by degrees, little by little, withdrawing from himself somewhat of his food, until he was able to abstain so greatly throughout. For he abstained, as we have said, from every corruptible food for almost [unclear] years. By the example of this man, my brothers, let each one learn to withdraw daily something else from his food and drink for the love of God, and so to minister to his body not as much as he can, but as much as is enough for the sustenance of nature. For, as Boethius bears witness, nature is content with very few and very small things; if it be made to flourish for a time with superfluous foods, what you have poured in becomes either [unclear] or harmful. Many illnesses are produced by many dishes, and gluttony of the belly dulls the mind and torments life. For the wise man says: Sweet is the sleep of the worker, whether he eats little or much; but the fullness of the rich man does not allow him to sleep. The belly must be filled without satiety, and one must rise from table without disgust, so that, namely, we may eat not as much as appetite demands, but as much as nature requires. But variety of foods must especially be avoided by monks; for he who lives on varied dishes, testing what nature demands to be put into the stomach, feasts not without the vice of gluttony. The strength of our body is to be restored with moderate and simple food, not shamefully oppressed with varied or many foods, because the body was not created for food, but food for the body. That variety of foods brings diseases upon the body we prove from the temperance of the ancients, whom the simplicity of all their foods preserved for a very long time in this life.
