*Homily XII.*

And let the guardianship of monastic discipline never depart from your side. Stop your ears with the remembrance of the last things, lest the hearing of vain speech pollute the integrity of your mind. Humbly cast your eyes down to the earth, lest they look upon what it does not befit a servant of God to desire. Do not take part in the conversations of carnal men; if you cannot avoid their presence, beware lest you lend them your mind. Even in the midst of ribald men, do not transgress your rule, and do not abandon your accustomed gravity. Do not fear the insults and reproaches of [unclear], and let your heart not be troubled by the words of men of darkness. The observance of monastic discipline is justice; and those who despise the simple pursue the innocent with laughter. Preserve your holy purpose in all things, and the Most High will avenge the injury.

Do not say in your heart, “I suffer violence,” nor be ashamed at the words of those who mock you. The glory of a monk is to endure insult, and the crown of beauty is to rejoice in reproaches. He who is patient under injuries is stronger than Atlas, and he who is not saddened when mocked is greater than all. If you have fixed your desire in heaven, nothing on earth will disturb you; and if you are a true monk, your heart will exult at injuries. There is a judge in heaven who knows all things, and there is the Most High God who cares for all things. He is the just rewarder of merits, and he will remember his poor. The more deeply you cast down your soul now in this life, for love of God, into the depth of humiliation, the more in future glory you will be exalted above the rest.

For by patience heaven is ascended, and through humility one comes to the kingdom. For it was also necessary for Christ himself to suffer, and so to enter into his glory. A delicate soldier is he who seeks pleasures while his emperor patiently endures insults and mockeries, and while he hangs on the gibbet of the cross for his army, he follows after pleasures. A soldier who goes forth naked to battle without arms, and a monk who disdains to embrace patience and humility amid insults: for both there is swift destruction. The monk who is a sincere lover of Christ is not angered by injuries, does not threaten those who inflict insult, stands fixed in his holy purpose, and, however much the wicked rush upon him, is not moved. As timely rain increases the hay in the meadows, so merit grows for the monk from the humble endurance of injuries. Humility is the sinew of all virtues, and constant patience under injuries for the love of God is the mine of good works.

Therefore, when you are about to go out from the monastery onto the road, prepare your heart for the endurance of injuries, and strengthen your mind against the insolences of mockers. When misty fellows shout after your back, repeating the name “monk,” remember Christ, mocked and spat upon before the governor, who did not answer a word, but silently preserved patience. Imitate him as your leader, O monk; always listen to him as your teacher, for he was meek and humble of heart. Do not answer a word to those who mock you; do not turn your face backward, but say with the prophet: “Those who sought evils for me spoke vanities, and meditated deceits all day long. But I, like a deaf man, did not hear; and like a mute man not opening his mouth. And I became like a man who does not hear, and has no rebukes in his mouth.” This Christ did; this Jesus taught; this the king of all commanded. If you wish to be his disciple, you must always be ready for all injuries.

When you are set upon the road, do not neglect to say your canonical prayers with whatever devotion you can; and always turn over something good with you in your mind, either by praying or by meditating. The Lord walks with you, and his holy angels accompany you as you walk. And therefore preserve a clean heart in all things, lest you offend the majesty of so many and such companions. The Lord is all eye and ear; from his knowledge nothing is hidden. Keep constant guard over your words, and let deliberation of the heart precede every word of your mouth, so that you speak nothing at all of which you could repent. A monk will speak both few words and reasonable ones, and his lips never exceed measure in speech. All his conversation is directed to the edification of his neighbors; and he who teaches very many by his conduct, even when silent, speaks useful things. He who is a true lover of God walks most humbly on the road; and he who fears the Most High restrains his mouth from laughter. In all your life, think yourself mortal, and live dying, as one who is already sending forth his last breath. Happy the kingdom and rich before God the province in which monks rise up with Boötes, since where there is no fear of God, the theaters are filled with cloistered men. A monk on the road
