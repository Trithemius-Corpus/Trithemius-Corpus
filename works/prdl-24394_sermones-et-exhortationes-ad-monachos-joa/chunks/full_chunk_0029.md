I blush to say it, and nevertheless I am compelled to utter it: your labors outside the monastery and the cell bring more destruction than salvation to the purity of souls. For what do you labor at, and what are your pursuits in your labors? Is it not to wander about, to run to and fro, to indulge in feasts and cups, and to occupy the rest of the time with vain and useless conversations?

You do not have in yourselves that holy fervor of the Spirit of God by which we read that the ancient monks of our institution were once inflamed: for the common necessity, with such desire, such zeal, and such effort, all and each labored with their own hands that you would have thought the whole business of the monastery was placed in the providence of each one. For they labored with their own hands as though each individually had to sustain the whole congregation. Yet amid these things they discharged the service of divine worship by day and night with such cheerfulness as if they had not been occupied at all in labors of the hands.

Who among you, brothers, is an imitator of such men, so that he regards the poverty of our whole community as his own, or strives according to his strength to relieve it by laboring in the sweat of his face? They, laboring with their hands, besought God with unceasing hearts; but you, intent on tales, satisfy neither your duty with your hands nor, as monks, with your minds. Also in the time of regular fasting, they either diligently kept the measure of abstinence appointed for them, or, if because of the greatness of their labors they could not, they ate for this reason, that they might be rendered fit and suitable for the labors. But you do not eat in order to labor; rather, you labor in order to eat, when according to the Rule you ought to have fasted. You take occasion from labor to break the fast.

Moreover, how filthily and most vilely some of you treat their garments, what zealous lover of regular discipline can bear with equanimity? Whose, pray, are the things, my brothers, that you wastefully consume? Everything that belongs to the monasteries of the servants of God is the patrimony of Christ, and the whole is dedicated to God; nor is it lawful for anyone to misuse the things of Christ, or for them to be turned to another use. Do you not know what our holy lawgiver Benedict says to you in the Rule? “If anyone treats the things of the monastery filthily or negligently, let him be corrected. If he does not amend, let him be subject to regular discipline.” And in the chapter on the cellarer of the monastery he says: “Let him regard all the vessels of the monastery and all its substance as if they were the consecrated vessels of the altar.”

Finally, I cannot and ought not keep silent about how filthily and negligently most of you treat everything, as though either they were not seen by God, or the sloth of none of them caused loss to the goods of the monastery. Know, brothers, that in warning you beforehand I announce to you that for all these things, as also for your other sins, you are going to render account to the Lord God. I reduced your labors outside the monastery for this reason, lest by working badly you should sin more gravely; and I enjoined upon you manual work: to copy books, to bind them, and similar tasks, which you can perform within the enclosure of the monastery with tranquility of mind and body.

Would that you did even these works of your hands with diligence for the love of God, lest you eat bread altogether idle. There is no manual work which in my judgment befits a monk more than to copy books of sacred reading, and to prepare what is necessary for others who are copying. For this holy labor may often be interrupted by prayer, and one may keep watch for the food of the mind no less than of the body. Necessity also urges us to devote ourselves with diligence to copying books, if we desire to have at hand that with which we can profitably occupy ourselves in turn in spiritual study.

For you see that the whole library of this monastery, which once was distinguished and great, was by the disordered monks before us so thoroughly dispersed, sold, and alienated [unclear] that no more than fourteen volumes were found here by us. The industry of the art of printing, recently invented in our days at Mainz, indeed brings many volumes to light every day; but it is by no means possible for us to acquire them all, since we are still oppressed by the greatest poverty. Therefore I warn and exhort you, who do not come very willingly to external labors of the hands, that for the honor of God you labor with diligence in copying volumes; for just as idleness is the enemy of the soul, so moderate labor is the spiritual preserver of life. For a monk, because he is a soldier of the eternal King, ought never to be idle. Idleness begets acedia, and that brings upon the soul the want of destructive death.
