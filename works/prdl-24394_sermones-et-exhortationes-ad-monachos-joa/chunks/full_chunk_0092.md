Certainly, my brothers, the things we have promised are great, and the necessity laid upon us to keep what we have professed is greater. For unless we faithfully observe all that the rule of our holy father Benedict enjoins upon us, the justice of almighty God will judge us severely as perjured and impious transgressors. For it is very dangerous not to render to God what has been promised, and through forgetfulness of the holy institution to violate one’s purpose.

Therefore, to fulfill the duty of pastoral care, I am accustomed daily to admonish you to the observance of regular discipline; nor hereafter will the necessary word of salvation ever be lacking to you, so long as my spirit perseveres in this prison-house of flesh. But you, I pray, take care that you not be unfruitful hearers, since the matter concerns the soul, which, once lost, is never recovered. Therefore, whichever of you have come together in the monastery to run in the arena of the intellectual contest, whichever of you for love of Christ have once renounced your own will and the world, take up holy obedience for the helmet of salvation, and you will bring back a certain victory over the enemy.

For obedience is the strongest armor of monks, which no adversities will be able to harm. For Scripture says: He who keeps the commandment will experience no evil whatsoever. In Proverbs also it is said thus: An obedient man shall speak of victories. Obedience renders a monk invincible in all temptations, and he who follows in all things the counsel of his senior will never suffer harm to the salvation of his soul. For obedience is a short and rough road, by which one quickly ascends to the height of perfection. But the monk who is a true soldier of Christ never trusts in his own judgment, but in all things follows and observes the direction of his pastor. Indeed, he who is a true lover of Christ, who wholly desires to be a most faithful monk, loves his abbot as God’s vicar, and cheerfully acquiesces in all things, always obedient to his commands, counsels, and ordinances.

In order that a monk may be able to do this with confidence, one to be ordained to the abbacy should not be a child, not unlearned, nor carnal, not lacking discretion, nor light-minded, or inexperienced in spiritual contests, but a man gray-haired in character, learned in the divine Scriptures, walking in the spirit, pious in true conversion, in the conduct of holiness, gentle, tranquil in mind, and discreet, one who knows how to show judgment to the wicked and compassion to the well-disposed. For whenever a restless, turbulent, indiscreet, and inexperienced man is set over monks as abbot, at once the integrity of regular discipline suffers shipwreck. Subjection must necessarily perish, and with it the obedience of humility, when the subjects cannot firmly have confidence in the pastor. For if the abbot is a mocker of his brothers, or an importunate despiser of the weak, who will open to him any secret of his heart?

Therefore one must keep watch with the greatest solicitude at the beginning, so that such a prelate be ordained as pastor over sheep left destitute, who seeks nothing other than the honor of God and the salvation of souls. The monk who will have obtained such a pastor should give thanks to God, because in these our times he occurs rarely, one who knows how to show himself in all things a worthy and suitable prelate. He is rightly to be loved by all his subjects, and, as the vicar of Christ in the monastery, to be honored with continual veneration. Let the thoughts of all be opened to him securely, because he seeks nothing other than the advancement of each one. If a physician of sick bodies must be prudent, experienced, and endowed with the highest discretion, how much more fitting is it for an abbot, who is the physician of souls, to be the best of men?

Happy are the monks to whom God’s mercy has granted a useful and suitable pastor, if nevertheless it has not denied them even the ability to understand this very fact. Otherwise a good pastor, in his uprightness, is not recognized by evil sheep; but those who corrupt the integrity of the order by their depravity rarely judge truly and rightly about pastors. Because of the ingratitude of subjects, rulers are very often changed for the worse; and monks who despise a good and useful pastor when he is present in this life receive, by the just judgment of God, a foolish and useless successor. Those who are subject in simplicity walk rightly, and those who truly imitate Christ do not pass judgment on the one set over them.
