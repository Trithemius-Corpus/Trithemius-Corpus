*Homily VI.*

... contemplates and loves immutably; he is blessed and fears no alteration of himself. But the human intellect, which our teachers have called the soul, was indeed created at the beginning in all ignorance, and knows none of those things which the angelic intellect understands by nature. These two intellects differ as act and potency; light and darkness; knowledge and ignorance; habit and privation; beginning and end; simple and composite; substance and accident; nature and art; truth and likeness. For all things, as divine Scripture testifies, which are present in the angelic intellect by nature and from the beginning, the human intellect will attain through arts at the end, as the Lord Jesus says in the holy Gospel: “In the resurrection of the dead they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but they will be like the angels of God in heaven.”

For in order that the mind may be trained toward understanding through many labors, as if through arts, it has been placed for a short time in the workshop of the body. If there, turned toward God, it has duly completed its craft through love, then at last, going forth from here, it becomes equal to an angel. But if, consenting to the motions of the flesh, it has not kept its proper order, then, disturbed and wretched, like the apostate spirits who fell, it will depart from the body to be tormented eternally. For since our mind, or soul, was created in ignorance and privation of all things, it must necessarily come to knowledge of things by continual action, motion, and art. For the end makes the human intellect equal to the angelic, changing its potency into act; and it will make from privation a habit, from the empty the full, from the imperfect the perfect, from the deprived one who possesses, from the dark the luminous, from the nocturnal the diurnal, from the lunar the solar, and from the earthly the heavenly. This end, my dearest brothers, is the beginning of eternal salvation; no one can happily come to it who does not strive with all his strength toward the summit of perfection.

But since it is difficult in the midst of crowds always to keep unbroken this necessary effort of advancement, where a thousand occasions of sinning are presented, the holy fathers, founders of the monastic order, taught us by word and example to flee the world, and to avoid company not only in mind but also in body. Of those who were stronger in spirit, many hastened to the desert, so that in mind they might become nearer to the Lord in proportion as in body they had withdrawn farther from the world. Of them we read the saying of the prophet Isaiah: “The wilderness will rejoice and will blossom like the lily; sprouting it will sprout, and it will rejoice, glad and praising.” Others, wishing to benefit many, established cenobia, in which they placed a multitude of monks, whose way of life would be separated from peoples and nevertheless social among themselves mutually. For it does not suit everyone to sit in the desert; for some it is more fitting to serve the Lord in the midst of brothers. For our monasteries too have their own solitude, so that whoever wishes to be solitary can do so in the midst of brothers, provided he has maintained the privacy of the cell in which he dwells.

For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is called solitary, because in mind and body he is separated from the tumult of this world. Therefore, as I am about to make today’s sermon, [unclear] your progress in spiritual conduct. I ask you, I say, and exhort you in the bowels of brotherly charity, to love the quiet of the cell as the workshop of your salvation, in which there is much peace for those who love God, and great advantage for perfecting the intellect toward the knowledge of the highest truth. For the prophet Isaiah says: “In silence and hope will be your strength.” He who does not love the solitude of the cell and the quiet of silence by no means loves purity of mind, but, wandering in body, always drinks in new vanities in his soul.

Hence blessed Jerome says: “Why do we desire the crowds of cities, we who are reckoned from singularity? For to me a town is a prison, solitude a paradise.” If the desert and solitude delighted us, brothers, this place would offer great advantage for our purpose, where, apart from poor peasants, a guest is rarely met; no one disturbs our quiet, no one troubles us, no one brings in spectacles or rumors of momentary novelty. But I see few lovers of inward quiet among you, and I grieve exceedingly, because I know very well that a monk cannot advance in spiritual conduct if it is clear that he does not love the quiet of the cell.
