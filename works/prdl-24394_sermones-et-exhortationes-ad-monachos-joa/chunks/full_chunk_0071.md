*On the Lenten fast of monks.*

Only by day, and in that part of the night which after the night lauds I am accustomed to spend sleepless, have I devoted myself to exhorting you, setting down on slips what occurred to me as needing to be said. And so I wished to introduce today’s discourse from the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, who speaks thus about Lenten observance: Although at every time the life of a monk ought to have the observance of Lent, nevertheless, because this virtue belongs to few, we therefore advise that in these days of Lent they guard their life in all purity, and that all alike wash away in these holy days the stains and negligences of other times. This is then done worthily if we refrain from all vices and give our effort to prayer of the heart.

If this virtue belongs to few, namely that at every time they lead a life of Lenten observance, it is now plainly evident that few monks in religion live according to the purity of the Gospel, since all who profess the warfare of Christ are bound to direct their effort toward the summit of perfection. For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is solitary; and in the monastery he is then rightly believed to live according to the Gospel and the rule which he has professed, when, for love of God, he has despised the affairs and honors of the world, and conducts himself before God and men in all purity of heart: strenuous in work, clean in thought, sparing and peaceable in speech.

This is the life of a monk, having Lenten observance at every time: to chastise the body for love of God through abstinence, and to nourish the mind through inner compunction. He lives rightly who lives justly, who loves God above all things, who with the greatest zeal and the greatest diligence observes his commandments in words and deeds. For he is the true monk of Christ who imitates Christ with all his strength.

But if you wish to imitate Christ, O monk, observe most diligently what I am about to say. Flee the world and all consolations of the flesh, so that you may deserve truly to be solitary. Never permit vain, useless, foul, and harmful thoughts to dwell in your heart; rather, always think something good, and let the memory of the Lord’s passion never depart from your soul, so that each day with thanksgiving you may contemplate the Lord hanging on the cross. Consider God present to you everywhere: he sees, knows, and understands whatever you have either thought or done, since nothing is hidden from him.

Keep your heart clean from every stain of sin, and always have a pure conscience fervent in the true love of Christ. Let no idle time fly away from you, but let thought always find you engaged in good works. Do not grow sluggish with much sleep, and make yourself far removed from every sloth of tepidity. Embrace moderation in food and drink, and live as sparingly as is permitted, so that the body, moderately sustained, may always serve the spirit in humility and by no means rise up against the command of reason.

As soon as you have sensed some torpor arising in you, flee to the memory of the most bitter passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, and bring back into your mind whatever can drive away sloth most swiftly. Break up, I beg you, all the hours, since torpor of mind, when ignored, suddenly increases, and sloth which is not at once extinguished by a new fervor of mind is immediately strengthened into grave destruction of the soul. For of all evils in a monk the worst is if, through torpor and sloth, he has provided a place in his heart for the seed-bed of vices.

And you, O monks, must keep watch against this beast not only every day, but every hour and moment, since once admitted into the heart it never enters alone; rather, the whole mass and wellspring of all vices accompanies it. There is no more destructive plague for a monk than the torpor of sloth, through which every fervor of devotion is extinguished; and, faithful service having been abandoned, the mind, shaken by idleness and leisure, is hurled down into the pit of despair. Whoever permits the delay of sloth quickly falls away from the purpose of holiness.

Against this Solomon admonishes you in the Book of Proverbs, saying: Go to the ant, O worker, and consider its ways, and learn wisdom. Though it has no guide, nor teacher, nor ruler, it prepares its food in summer and gathers at harvest what it may eat. And again he says: Desires kill the lazy man, because his hands were unwilling to labor at anything. Nothing is worse for those professing the purpose of holiness than leisure, because it not only stores up no good, but also consumes and scatters everything previously stored up.
