*On the Lenten fast of monks.*

Those who look upon the death of Jesus Christ with [faith?] and devotion are healed from the bites of sins. But if you trust that you have been justified from sin, then all the more and more fervently must you look upon Christ hanging on the cross; without the merit of his passion no one lives justly on earth. For outside faith in Christ there is no righteousness, and however religiously a man may live, if he does not believe in our Lord Jesus Christ, he will in no way attain eternal salvation; rather, the wrath of God remains upon him, and he will necessarily perish forever. The death of Christ opened the gate of salvation, through which no one else can enter except one who faithfully believes in Christ. For this reason Christ humbly submitted himself to the condemnation of death, so that he might free believers from the yoke of eternal damnation.

O infinite charity, piety without measure: who among those who believe in you, eternal Majesty, would not marvel at such astonishing benefits granted to us? For your passion, Lord Jesus, is the price of our salvation, the final refuge of sinners, the singular remedy of weak minds, the fountain, origin, and end of all good things. Let us now therefore, dearest brothers, recall and diligently bring back to memory the bitterness of the Lord’s passion, so that, since we recognize that we are necessarily sinners, we may at last be justified through the merit of that same our Savior.

For meditation on the Lord’s passion surpasses all the exercises of this world, if it is continued for a long time with the fervor of inward devotion. That mind of a monk which, kindled by zeal for divine love, has been accustomed each day, at least for a short hour, to hold the memory of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ with tears, does not easily lie open to the suggestions of demons. For that heart which the love of Jesus possesses always remains inaccessible and closed to all evil thoughts.

Happy indeed is that monk, and never to be named except with honor, who meditates day and night with loving zeal on the teaching and passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; who never forgets his Savior; who, whether he eats or drinks or does anything else, always beholds with the eyes of the mind the Lord Jesus hanging on the cross. No destructive thoughts move about in his heart; no evil or idle word proceeds from his mouth; he thinks of nothing but Jesus crucified for us, and whatever he says sounds of Jesus or of righteousness. As soon as he sees a painted image of Jesus crucified, he dissolves into tears, and, made as if senseless by the excessive desire of his mind, is filled with a most sweet bitterness. He can admit no consolations of the present life, because when he thinks that his Lord is seated in heaven, he weeps over his own exile prolonged on earth.

He loves nothing in this world except Jesus; he seeks nothing other than the Crucified. If riches are at hand, his heart utterly disdains to set itself upon them; but if they are lacking, he by no means seeks to find them. For when he recognizes himself to be a disciple of poor Jesus, he understands very well that the riches of this world are a great impediment to Christian perfection. Having scanty food and a poor covering for the body, he is content with his Lord, who was made poor for us and foretold that those hungering for righteousness are blessed. Happy, I say, is that monk who can truly say with the holy martyr of Christ, Theophorus: “My love has been crucified, and therefore my heart receives no consolation of this world.” And that saying of the Christ-loving Paul: “For me the world has been crucified, and I to the world.”

Now therefore, my dearest brothers, hear my counsel, and sanctify the fast you have begun by the continual memory of the Lord’s passion. For I tell you that among all the exercises of monks none can be holier, none more acceptable to God, none more salutary for you, than that which is practiced in daily diligence in meditation on the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, with compunction of inward love. For this exercise of the saving passion suddenly makes from a sinner a penitent; from one lukewarm and dry, by wondrous compunction, a devout man; from an impious man, a just one. It covers a multitude of sins; it gives strength to the spirit against demons; it sets an end to all [unclear]; and it confers strong increase upon the holy virtues of the soul. The devoutly continued memory of the Lord’s passion purifies the conscience of the one meditating; it illumines the understanding in every knowledge of truth; it sweetly inflames the affection in the love of God. Devout meditation on the passion of Christ causes one to despise all the delights of this world, and to fear nothing, however hard or adverse. It exterminates lusts; it subdues the luxury of both mind and body; it puts envy to flight; [unclear].
