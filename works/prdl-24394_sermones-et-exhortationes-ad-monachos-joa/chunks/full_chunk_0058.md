*Homily XIII.*

*Fol. XXXI.*

[Without?] sword and fire a monk can be a martyr, if for love of God he has kept true [unclear] patience in his soul. For the true monk is neither puffed up by praises nor saddened by blame; he shows himself patient under injuries; at insults and reproaches he is glad and humbly keeps silent. No one is stronger than the patient man who knows how to conquer himself and humbly to endure adversities for love of God with a tranquil mind.

But the hard, impatient man is proud and arrogant, since he does not follow Jesus, the teacher of humility; and therefore he is indignant at just rebukes and [unclear]. As the furnace tests gold, so insults and injuries test a monk, whether he truly loves Christ. Humility and patience in adversity are the true badges of monks; whoever is not clothed in their adornment glories vainly in the name. He who is not humble cannot truly be a monk; he who does not keep patience in adversities cannot be a true disciple of Christ.

Hence king Solomon says in the book of Proverbs: “The patient man is better than the strong, and he who rules his own spirit than one who storms cities.” For a man’s teaching is known through patience, and his glory is to pass over wrongs. The furnace tests the potter’s vessels, and the trial of tribulation tests men among us. The monk who follows Christ in truth and who is a meditator on the Lord’s passion is patient under all injuries, not forgetful; amid insults he exults in spirit and rejoices. For, as blessed [Christopher?] says, all who have wished to live piously in Christ Jesus suffer persecution, and whatever evil befalls them they overcome with the greatest constancy.

And although patience is necessary for a monk in every place and time, nevertheless in the daily examination of faults it must be guarded with especially diligent attention: there correction of morals is carried out, and whatever has been committed against the standard of monastic purity is amended in each one by the senior. Therefore before we enter this place, all should think in the morning, and the mind should be strengthened against those things that can happen, so that if perhaps a rather harsh rebuke occurs, it may not overturn the mind from the state of tranquillity. For even if we have resolved to bring insult upon none of you, nor to do injury, nevertheless we do not deny that it can happen, so far as can be, that someone who has been corrected may think himself rebuked beyond his desert, since he weighs our paternal corrections by the words rather than by the intention.

As God knows, we seek your salvation in all things, and nothing else: to cut away vices by the roots, according to the ordinances of our religion. We sincerely intend to injure no one. We cannot please everyone in all things, but we are especially compelled to displease those who, while sinning against the rule each day, promise themselves impunity for their crimes. Of these king Solomon said: “An evil teaching belongs to those who abandon the way of life, and he who hates rebukes shall die.” The pestilent man does not love him who corrects him, nor does he go to the wise. He who does evil deliberately flees the light and hates correction because he is foolish; but he who is sensible in spirit gives thanks to his corrector.

Therefore in this matter I do not fear the hatred or murmuring of subjects, knowing that I have taken on the care of sick sheep, to whom, whether willing or unwilling, I am bound to apply the remedies of salvation. If it could be done, brothers, I would very much wish to disturb none of you in anything, even slightly, but to live among you with the greatest tranquillity and cheerfully to serve the Lord our God in peace of mind and body. But because not all of you truly seek the things that are God’s, I must rebuke and sometimes disturb the restless, lest impunity for smaller crimes provide boldness for graver ones. For I know what necessity I suffer through the danger of pastoral care; and therefore I neither can nor ought to be silent when you sin, lest I be judged somewhere as consenting. I gladly endure it if, for this reason, it happens that I am hated, because this cannot be done without the gravest danger to your souls.

We ask you through our Lord Jesus Christ, crucified for us, that for love of God and for the remission of your sins you endure our paternal and necessary rebukes at all times patiently and with true humility. Let each one consider in himself that there is no day on which he does good and does not offend in some respect; and let him know that he will receive in this world the reward of his works: good things for good, and for evil things [unclear]. Therefore let him strive, as much as he can, in this life to make satisfaction for his offenses
