*Homily VI.*

...how much more study is wont to be varied, and to use alternations against weariness. Hence again Jerome says: Prayer should frequently interrupt reading, and with a pleasing alternation of holy prayer should approach the soul that is constantly attached to it. The monk who does not love the secrecy of the cell is not a true soldier of Christ, since he bears the name and habit of a monk without the works; and he who ought to have been solitary for the love of God, having returned in mind to the world, loves the pursuit of vanity. Hence the holy pope Gregory says: He who lives bodily removed from human beings, but plunges himself in thought into the tumults of human intercourse and into desire for earthly things, is not solitary, although he dwells in solitude. But if someone is bodily pressed by the crowds of the people, and yet suffers no tumults of secular cares in his heart, he is not in the city, even if he moves in the midst of human beings. And this is what Saint Jerome says against Jovinian: The wise man, he says, can never be alone. For he has with him all who are and who have been good; and he has a free mind, and brings forth whatever he wishes, and transfers himself wherever he wishes. For what he cannot do in body he embraces in mind; and if there is a lack of human beings, he speaks with God. Therefore a good man will never be alone. And so, remaining in your cell, always beware idleness, since nothing so enervates all the powers of the mind as idleness, after it has produced sloth from itself. If you have ardently loved God, you will never be idle, because the true love of Christ does not allow torpor to arise in the soul; it always drives one toward the will of the beloved friend. And just as a certain carnally wise man truly said, “Let him who does not wish to become listless love,” so experience has taught us in spiritual love that it does not permit the mind which it has begun to inhabit to grow torpid through idleness and laziness. For divine love always drives one toward better things; and the more it has advanced by burning, the greater increase it takes of itself. But the workshop of this love is a clean conscience; if a monk desires to have and preserve it clean, it is necessary first of all that he despise the vanities of the world and the affairs of temporal things. For it is not possible that a twin love should reside in one breast; nor can a man love Christ who does not wish to despise the world. For since God, in the Trinity of persons, is one in the essence of substance, either he alone is loved by us, or he is not loved with a partner. Therefore, so that the conscience may remain clean in all purity, and so that the mind may be able to love God above all things, let the monk love the secrecy of the cell, and never withdraw from the exercise of inward sweetness. For in the cell of a good and wise monk there is the highest tranquillity; and if anywhere there is an earthly paradise, it is in the monastery of those who live according to the purity of the cenobitic institution. There is peace, piety, goodness, light, virtue, honor; there is joy, purity, sweetness, and great tranquillity of life, where the devoted praying monk speaks with the Lord, and the future sweetness of the heavenly kingdom is tasted beforehand by the contemplative soldier. O cell, paradise of monks truly serving God: you surely are a little garden of most honorable delight, in which the mind is joined to God through love, and is sweetly raised up through desire. The monk who loves, venerates, and cultivates you is a true soldier of Christ, and serves the Lord in the fullness of virtues. But he who abhors and deserts the secrecy of the cell despises the workshop of his own salvation. Therefore I beseech you, my dearest brothers, begin to inhabit the secrecy of the cell with the desire of the mind; without love for it no one will be able to attain perfection of the virtues in the monastery.

*On the manual labor of monks: Homily VII.*

“The labors of your hands, because you shall eat,” says the prophet of the Lord; “blessed are you, and it shall be well with you.” If all who live from the labor of their own hands are judged blessed, certainly no idle man eats bread without guilt and crime. For almighty God imposed this punishment on the first-formed man for the transgression of the original commandment, declaring to him that in the sweat of his face he would work food from the earth with his hands. But since in Adam we have all sinned, we are necessarily subject to the same punishments to which it is agreed that he was subjected.
