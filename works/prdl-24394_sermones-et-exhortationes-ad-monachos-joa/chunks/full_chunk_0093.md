O lover and devotee of your own salvation, consider the form of obedience which you are to follow. The abbot, whether he is good or bad, holds in the monastery the place of Christ's vicar; he deserves the obedience of all his subjects not by his own merit, but in Christ's name. For we understand the saying, "He who hears you hears me, and he who spurns you spurns me," not only of the apostles, but of any prelate in the Church of God who has been appointed guardian and ruler of souls.

Therefore, if you have a good, suitable, holy abbot, hear his voice as the command of God, since he bears God's place; and whatever he commands you, be obedient in all things. Let his teaching be for you a rule of life, and his holy conduct an example of a purer discipline. For he is a friend of God and likewise Christ's vicar: in the one respect he deserves that you love him, and in the other that you obey him in all things when he commands.

But if you have obtained a shepherd who is untrained, inexperienced, or certainly less suitable, take care, I beg you, not to wish rashly to examine his actions. Rather think that your sins deserve to have an unprofitable shepherd. Consider in him not what he is, but what he is called; and honor the shepherd for the love and reverence of Him whose vicariate he bears. Remember at all times that the obedience which you promised to God is to be rendered purely and simply to His vicar; for if the abbot is bad in his conduct, nevertheless he does not lose the vicariate of Christ unless he is [lawfully?] deposed. Nor does the reprobate life of your shepherd absolve you from the obedience promised in religion.

The meaning of our customary proverb must be upheld: reprobate monks do not deserve to have a good abbot. Do not think, dearest brothers, that this example has been brandished against you. For I trust that I am not a bad shepherd, although I cannot deny that I am untrained and inexperienced; nor have we ever admitted any evil suspicion of you, since we seek nothing more than to nourish and enlarge brotherly charity in all things.

Frequently monks come to us, wandering either with reason or without cause, who for the most part turn the matter of their own instability back upon their abbots. And I, who desire you to be instructed in everything holy and useful, paternally exhort and warn your Charity that henceforth you do not lend your ears to these circumcellions, who disparage the ways of their own shepherds and strive to hide their own crimes by [accusations?] against others. This is not the necessary integrity of monastic discipline: to turn the cause of the rebellion and disobedience of subjects back upon the head of the shepherd.

He who is a true monk does not rashly judge the affairs of others; and he who is a son of humble obedience does not search out causes for rebellion. But he who gives consent to the passions of the flesh can never be at rest; and he who scrutinizes another's life is a despiser of his own salvation. A monk grounded in the love of God does not judge the command of his superior; and he who always beholds Christ as present venerates his shepherd as an angel of God.

Audacity makes a monk degenerate, and presumption has cast many into prison. An insolent, wanton, and headstrong monk has lost the resolve of holiness, and unless he is recalled to his senses by hard chastisement of the flesh, he has already set his foot toward hell. He who weighs the command of his superior with inquisitive rashness is not obedient; and he who is grieved at an order is not a true disciple of Christ. For true obedience does not murmur, does not hesitate, does not seek a loophole, does not delay, does not excuse itself, nor show a hostile sign of reluctance. At the command of the superior, for the love of Christ it always rejoices, comes gladly, and obeys cheerfully with haste.

For what else is a monk but an anxious contender, who, armed with the virtues of humility and obedience, always keeps watch in the arena of contest, and, victorious under the triple battle-line, fights more strongly against his own self? Blessed is that monk who, making himself nothing in his own eyes, has learned to overcome the will of the flesh, and through the continual practice of spiritual warfare climbs the steep mountain of true obedience. Truly blessed is that monk who, despising all the consolations of the world, has placed his whole self in the love of Jesus Christ, and now desires nothing more among visible things.

It is not for monks to seek riches, honors, and pleasures in this world; nor is it fitting for a soldier of Christ to think of anything other than Christ. O monk three and four times blessed, who for the love of Christ mortifies his own will to the end of life and humbly perseveres in true obedience.

How great a good humble obedience is before God, my brothers, no one can adequately express; divine testimony chose to prefer it to all sacrifices. It is obedience that reconciles the soul to God, and through the shedding of blood crowns martyrs.
