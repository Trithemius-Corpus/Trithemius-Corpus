*On the Lenten fast of monks.*

...to act against his command: let him omit or neglect nothing that has been enjoined upon him by him. Let him take the utmost care not to distress or disturb his pastor by restless conduct; nor let him murmur against him, nor disparage him, nor say any evil [unclear] against him. Rather let him revere him with the sincere affection of charity, as Christ's vicar, and not grieve his mind by troublesome or disorderly words or deeds. Let him, moreover, assist his labors as much as he can, and intercede for him before God with continual prayers. Monks sin greatly against God who are not afraid to grieve their abbot by troublesome detractions and wicked manners. Therefore whoever as a monk desires to be good, whoever truly wishes to be devoted in the Lord, should always and everywhere venerate his abbot with pure love as the vicar of Jesus Christ in the monastery; he should humbly obey his commands in all things, and in no way depart from his direction.

But the abbot, for his part, should strive to show himself in all things a worthy pastor to his monks, one who feeds the sheep of Christ entrusted to him both by word and by example, in all the gentleness of charity. Let him not disturb the brothers without just and reasonable necessity; let him not be a despiser of the holy community; let him recognize himself as the steward of the things of the monastery, not their lord. Let him not be a mocker of his subjects, not turbulent, not troublesome, not a tyrant, but their sincere lover, pleasant in speech, gentle and upright in manners, holy in conduct, clean in conscience, always devoted in mind to God. Let him at all times bear solicitous care for the souls entrusted to him, and neglect none of those things that seem able to conduce to their salvation. Let him not on this account raise himself on high because he has been placed as ruler of others, but let him remember that he has so much the more to fear, insofar as he is most certainly bound to render account for all in the dread judgment of God. Let him correct the proud, rebellious, and contumacious as a lord; the humble, obedient, and governable, let him kindly cherish as a most gentle father, and wisely distribute to each one his right without acceptance of persons. Let him always consider himself the mother and nurse of the monks, and therefore those whom he can bring back to better things by gentleness he ought not to exasperate with troublesome words and harsh invectives. Let him always bear solicitous care for the sick, and provide for all in such a way that they have no just cause for murmuring. Let him learn from his own weaknesses how he ought to have compassion on his subjects. For he cannot be a good pastor of souls who disdains from the heart to suffer with the necessities of his sick subjects. But he can become a good abbot among monks who knows how to adapt himself to the persons of the sick in such a way that, as often as a brother asks something of him, taking upon himself the form of the person asking, he says in his heart: What would I wish to be done for me in this matter, according to the right judgment of reason, if I were in the position of the one asking? But if the abbot imagines himself in mind to be a subject, he will let no one go away from him distressed.

We have now said, brothers, what sort of men it is fitting for you to be toward us for the love of God, so that, existing as good monks, you may please the Lord Jesus in truth. You have heard nevertheless what sort of pastor I ought to show myself to you; whether I do this or not, you yourselves will judge better than I. For we shall fast in vain and shall see no fruit of Lenten observance, unless we all remain in true brotherly charity, with purity of heart in the love of God. For what does it profit toward the rest of salvation to afflict the body through abstinence and fasting, and not to be concordant before God in the true charity of ecclesiastical union? Or what good will it do to abstain from richer foods, and to defile the conscience with evil works or thoughts? For in order that the fasts we have begun may be pleasing to God, it is necessary that they be not only alien and remote from vices and sins, but also adorned with the fullness of all virtues. For holy Ambrose says: What does it profit to fast in the belly and to be wanton in hunting; to abstain from foods, to wander in sins? Do you think, brothers, that he fasts who at first light does not keep watch at church, does not seek out the holy places of the blessed martyrs, but, rising in the morning, gathers his servants, sets out the nets, brings out the dogs, ranges through groves and woods; who, I say, drags servants along with him, perhaps hastening rather to church? For his own pleasures he heaps up the sins of others, not knowing that he will be guilty both of his own fault and of the ruin of his servants. And Saint Leo the pope says: The sum of our fasting does not consist in abstinence from food alone...
