*Homily XVII.*

[Unclear] we believe that God is present everywhere; in prayer there is need of great reverence, so that our mind may agree with our voice in every respect. Let us consider, therefore, brothers, before whom we stand, and in whose sight, in the presence of the angels, we recite the psalms in prayer. For a monk who at the time of prayer occupies his heart with vain thoughts is like a man who, standing before a king or emperor and speaking with him about matters very necessary for the safety of his body, is called by his servant, leaves the king, and turns himself to the vain and useless conversations of the servant. Anyone who did this would truly be judged foolish by all, and would rightly seem deserving of punishment with the gravest penalties. But what does the monk deserve who, standing before the supreme king of all and speaking with him about the salvation of his soul, suddenly turns his inner eyes to useless occupations, and, neglecting the presence of the eternal king, admits vain or even pernicious thoughts into his mind? If the mocker and despiser of an earthly king is punished by the cutting off of his head, what does one deserve who despises the heavenly emperor?

For this reason I warn you, brothers, and with all the sincerity I can I ask that you purify your hearts, especially at the time of prayer, from the stain of wicked thought, so that you turn over in your mind only what you chant with your mouth. As the angels, with fear and the greatest reverence, always stand before God, chanting the perpetual hymn of praise, so let us also take care, my brothers, to stand before him with a pure heart, a clean conscience, and most fervent devotion, so that we may chant his praises far more in spirit than in body. Therefore let us separate our minds from all earthly business; let us cleanse our consciences from all enticements of the flesh; let us put on the fervor of the Holy Spirit; and let us drive far from ourselves all sluggishness and idleness. For just as a heavy burden weighs down the back of a slothful man, so sleepiness at the time of psalmody is accustomed to oppress the minds of monks. Nothing harms monks more than acedia, which little by little enfeebles all the powers of the mind and extinguishes the whole fervor of the spirit.

For as soon as a monk begins to fall away from the fervor of holy devotion, countless evil thoughts creep into the mind, and he is then able to repel them all the less, the farther he is found to have withdrawn from his former fervor. From this arises sleepiness in the performance of the divine services; the mind is excessively weighed down by sadness and melancholy, which the monk, growing lukewarm in soul, strives with all effort to relieve not by spiritual but by earthly and carnal little consolations. And the longer he advances in this estrangement of mind, the more he is hardened, as though insensible, against the exercise of compunction; and the more he gapes after the consolations of the flesh, the farther he wanders from the inward fervor of the spirit. For what makes you sleepy at the time of psalmody except acedia? Because the mind that falls into torpor soon makes the body drowse.

But again I warn you, brothers, as I have often been accustomed to do, that you flee idleness and sleepiness, especially at the time of prayer; for just as drunkenness brings forgetfulness upon the soul, so also sleepiness makes the mind mad and foolish before God. Beware this vice of sleepiness, you young men, with which we grieve that some of the seniors have already been infected by long habit; for them drowsing is familiar in choir, and compunction of mind toward God is very rare. Beware, I say, this evil habit of sleeping; after it has once led a monk away captive, it rarely allows him to be free. Therefore, at the beginning of conversion, especially [unclear] delays. But make haste, and do not put yourself off to future hours: he who is not ready today will be less fit tomorrow. For it is difficult to cure an infirmity that is long-standing and has grown old through duration.

Let us see with what diligence and attentiveness servants stand before earthly kings and princes, so that we may not be ignorant how it befits us to stand before God, who alone is the king of all and lord of the universe. The eyes of all look to the lord, and all and each wait most diligently upon his nod, lest, when he commands, anything escape the attendant through negligence. From this, therefore, let us consider with what purity we ought to stand before the Lord God in the assembly of prayer day and night, from whom we desire to obtain the kingdom of eternal happiness.
