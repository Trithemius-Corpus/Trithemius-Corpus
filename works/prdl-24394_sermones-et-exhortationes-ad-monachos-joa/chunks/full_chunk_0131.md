*How sin is to be fled.*

[Unclear], a voice by far the most terrible of all terrible things: O sentence to be feared, and exceedingly horrible to all mortals, to be hated by Christ for one’s deserts, reproached by the judge, separated from the fellowship of glory, and no longer to retain any hope of recovery.

Consider now, brothers, the punishments of the wicked that will most certainly come, and compare whatever pains of this life, whatever torments of the passing age, and all imaginable tortures of this world, with those eternal punishments of the damned, and they will be as nothing compared with something, imagination compared with truth, sleep compared with waking. You will easily overcome the temptations of the flesh and the law of sin, if you continually keep the thought of these things in your memory.

O eternity, what an inconceivable time; an age without end; a world without time. Blessed is he who now foresees in time how, after this very brief life, he may fare well in eternity.

The fourth thing that can draw a person back from sin is to keep continually in the heart the memory of future and everlasting happiness. Truly the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory to come, which shall be revealed in the saints, where there will be no discord, no dissonance at all, but the highest peace of all and the everlasting concord of rejoicing saints.

In the blessedness of the holy ones in heaven there is true joy without sadness, peace without discord, goodness without malice, gladness without end, light without darkness, everlasting life without pain. There is sweetness without bitterness, rest without labor, praise without blame, love without vice, brightness without rust, satisfaction without disgust. Eternity remains without weariness; goodness abounds without defect; happiness continues without misery. For where God is all in all, perfect and full joy will be present for all. There the soul, or holy mind, is joined through love to its beginning; the intellect is perfectly illumined for contemplation of the highest good; from these two the enjoyment of eternal happiness is poured into those who exult. To know and love God in the kingdom of eternity is to possess, by enjoying it, the very glory of happiness. For to this end was man created: that he should understand that God his creator is the highest good; by understanding, love him above all things; and by loving, enjoy and possess him forever. This is the end of monastic institution; this is the destination of the Christian faith: that God, whom we now know through faith and love with our whole heart through works of charity, we may then in life perfectly understand and have as the perpetual object of our enjoyment.

For the attainment of this everlasting happiness, dearest brothers, all good things must be done, even with the greatest labor; all adversities must be endured most patiently, because the very brief exercise of virtue brings us an eternal reward. Hear what on this matter the most holy bishop John says: “If every day,” he says, “we had to endure torments, if indeed we had to suffer Gehenna itself for a short time, so that we might see Christ reigning in eternal glory and be joined forever to the number of his saints, there would not be equal reason for all the sadness that can befall us, in order that we might deserve to possess the glory of so great a good.” But one does not arrive at the highest good except through the greatest labors, because, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel, the kingdom of heaven suffers violence and the violent seize it.

The fifth thing that ought to draw a person back from sin is to consider the honorableness of the human condition, which, by the natural guidance of reason, detests vice and loves the good of virtue. Every person who lives according to the rule of reason is a lover of virtue; but he who, defiled in the dung of vices, chooses to wallow there lives like a beast, not like a man. But by you, my brothers, vices must especially be detested and virtues loved, since for this reason you have despised the world, that in the monastery you might lead a life alien to sins and adorned with holy virtues. Therefore virtues must be cultivated by you, not the images of virtues. I say cultivated not with incense, not with sacrifice, but with love and the purpose of a most upright will. For to cultivate virtues is nothing other than to understand them attentively and to keep them inviolate, for the love of God, even unto death. For as Lactantius says, this is the office of virtue: not to sin. And therefore there is no true virtue except that which, through faith in Christ, tends toward that end where there is the highest good of man, than which there can be no better. But he who does not love God is not virtuous, since charity is the sum of all virtues. Therefore if you love God with your whole heart above all things, you are truly virtuous. Virtue is the way of good things, which through faith and love of Jesus Christ arrives at the kingdom. He who truly loves God does not sin, out of love of virtue.

Consider your dignity, my brothers, and flee sin, because everyone whom pride puffs up, whom lust defiles, whom the other plagues of the soul infect, does not live as a man, but is confounded in himself and proceeds into the perdition of eternal death. For the more each person falls into sin, the farther he withdraws from God and the more he is brought near to hell. But since all people naturally desire the good, it is most shameful to love sin, because every sinner abandons the good and turns himself to evil.
