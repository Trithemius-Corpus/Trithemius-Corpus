*Concerning monks sent on the road.*

He who has God as his friend easily disdains familiarity with men; and he who speaks with angels takes very little delight in the conversations of mortals. Whenever on the road you see a sign of the Lord’s Passion or of the Crucified One, do not pass by unless you say: “I give you thanks, Lord Jesus Christ, for the pain of your wounds, for the shedding of your most holy blood, and for your most innocent death, which you endured for us on the altar of the cross; and I beseech you, by your bitter Passion, have mercy on me now and in the dread hour of my death.” Repeat this very brief prayer often as you go, and never pass by an image of the Crucified One unless you have given thanks to the Savior.

Do not contend with him who walks with you on the road, lest, when he has returned to the monastery, he lie about some evil thing against you. He who provokes the companion of his journey to impatience is like one who drives a mad dog with a blow to bark. A wicked man is overcome by gentleness; and he who is humble in heart calms a soul that has been stirred up. In the midst of secular men do not pour out speech, lest perhaps he take up mockery against you. We allow good things to be spoken [unclear] among young men; but let the one who is lesser in rank listen to him. The weary ploughman hastens to rest; and the monk whole in spirit, running the byways, returns with longing to his cell. Let the word be restrained in the mouth until reason brings forth a judgment of fairness; and let speech that can instruct hearers not be hidden.

Do not put trust in the songs of flatterers, since [unclear] they have shot down the innocent; they soothe the ears with ill-composed speeches. A monk who is nourished on contentions is like [unclear], and like a [spastic?] man who, under the name of religion, stirs up quarrels. In the presence of the learned do not add to the speaking, lest perhaps, when you do not understand the parable, you be laughed at by those standing around. He who recognizes himself as a monk is [unclear] by profound speech; and he who teaches little ones makes [unclear] the question. When you go to men unlike you in purpose, do not boast in anything; but if you are questioned, answer humbly in few words. When set upon a journey, always think of God, and regard the reverence of your elder as accompanying you.
