*Homily IX.*

... urged him to true wisdom. Among other things he said: “My son, attend to my wisdom, and incline your ear to my prudence, that you may keep thoughts, and that your lips may preserve discipline.” For it is no small part of wisdom, dearly beloved brothers, if a monk knows how prudently to discern his thoughts and carefully to guard their movements at all times. It is a great thing to think nothing evil; but it is a mark of no less prudence to be able to repel evil thoughts from their very beginning. For the profound judgment of the human heart is always mobile, changing, manifold, and unstable; and to observe its beginnings and restrain its movements is the highest prudence and a great sign of wisdom. If I have said that the heart of man is a mill driven by perpetual motion, I have not departed from the truth, especially if one should wish to weigh its restlessness exactly. For what else is the human heart than a certain mill, always in motion, grinding something? The human heart is never idle; it never rests without motion, but is always doing something; and just as a mill, empty, consumes itself by its own motion, so the heart of man shamefully wastes away of itself when thought of good does not bind it fast. It is always moved toward some object with the desire of its own love; it always grinds, always acts, always is occupied about something delightful, whether it be good or evil. As the body is not moved without the soul, so the mind of man does not remain fixed in good unless it has continually been restrained by holy exercises. Therefore the wise man said in Ecclesiasticus: “Gladness of heart, this is the life of man, and a treasure without defect of holiness; and the exultation of a man is length of life. Have mercy on your soul, pleasing God, and contain yourself; and gather your heart in his holiness, and drive sadness far from you.” What is the gathering of the heart, if not the diligent guarding of thought? For he gathers his heart in the holiness of the Lord who continually observes, in the fear of God, the movements of his thoughts. And therefore Scripture says: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.” Thoughts take the beginnings of their movements from those things which are offered by the senses; and they wander the more dangerously, the less they are considered from the beginning by the judgment of reason. Therefore whoever desires to be watchful over his thoughts, whoever wishes to gather his heart in the holiness of the Lord, should first most diligently guard his exterior senses and set the bridle of prudence upon each one. Concerning hearing and sight, Scripture thus commands: “Hedge your ears with thorns, and do not listen to a wicked tongue; and make doors for your mouth, and bars for your ears.” “Blessed is the man who stops his ears, lest he hear bloodshed, and shuts his eyes, lest he see evil.” In holy Job also we read thus: “I made a covenant with my eyes, that I would not even think of a virgin.” But against those who through the ministry of the senses have fallen into the pit of sins, the Lord thus bears witness through the prophet Jeremiah: “Because death has climbed in through your windows, it has entered your houses, to destroy the little children outside and the young men from the streets.” Death enters through our windows whenever an iniquitous thought is admitted to the mind through the ministry of sight or hearing. For the house is the conscience, of which Scripture says: “Unless you hold yourself constantly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overturned,” that is, your conscience. This house is very fragile and is quickly overturned unless it is fortified by the continual exercise of the fear of the Lord. “For unless the Lord has guarded the house, he who guards it keeps watch in vain.” Therefore the house of conscience must be fortified by love of the Lord; the senses also must be restrained most diligently, because it is not enough for one to guard his thoughts if he does not turn his ears and eyes away from the occasion of evil. And so, if you wish to be a prudent guardian of your thoughts and a true gatherer of the heart in the holiness of the Lord, hedge your ears with the thorns of compunction and of the fear of God, lest they hear evil, and turn your eyes away, lest through sight they introduce sin. What it is not lawful for you to covet, do not see with desire; and what it is not expedient to have, do not think about. Many have perished through the ministry of the senses; and those who incautiously proceed to look at external things have shamefully lost the inner goods of their enclosure. He who does not fear danger is certainly already near destruction; and he who presumes on his own virtue hastens swiftly toward the ruin of sin. He who fixes his eye on the flashing of lightning will be blinded for the moment; and he who looks upon the beauty of a woman will not be free from danger. I praise the prudence of that monk who in the midst of crowds is alone, and appoints a censor over his senses, lest it admit anything evil to the lady.
