*Sermon VII.*

Our temptation is wholly upon the earth; in it, if we do not advance in good, we fail; and there, as the holy Pope Leo says, we fall into the danger of failing when we withdraw our desire for making progress. Therefore flee sloth, and always keep watch in good works. Consider what happened to the lazy servant, who hid in a napkin the money of his lord which he had received for trading. Trade, men, I beg you, while there is time, and as long as you live be always occupied in good pursuits.

Fourth, the grace of devotion is very often withdrawn from those who, placed under obedience, follow their own will. The monk who loves his own will most justly loses the grace of God. But he who for God’s sake humbly subjects himself in all things to the obedience of the shepherd, who spurns the will of his own heart, is filled with the various gifts of spiritual grace and illuminated by the light of inward fervor. We can offer nothing more precious and acceptable to God than if for love of him we deny our own will. Indeed, the very profession of monastic institution enjoins upon us, by necessity of salvation, to despise our own will and humbly to submit to him who has been set over us. Therefore the monk who follows the impulse of his own will is judged as an unclean vessel and unworthy of spiritual grace. Deservedly, then, the grace of God is withdrawn from the monk of his own will, who, living otherwise than he promised, lies to the Lord.

Fifth, the grace of devotion is withdrawn from a monk when he thinks he has it by his own merits. He does not understand that sin dwells in him, unless because he ascribes to his own merits whatever good he has received. Against this ignorance of mind the holy prophet said to the Lord: Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty, because it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy. You turned your face away from me, and I was made troubled. No one [has] his devotion by his own merits, no holy man by his own powers.

But let us recognize that all the causes of the withdrawal of the grace of God which we have mentioned pertain to our infirmity. Yet as for those who walk in perfection, another account is given, so long as they persevere. For from them the grace of God is never withdrawn, although the sensible fervor of compunction is sometimes taken away for a time. But we shall state in order the causes of this removal. First, almighty God sometimes withdraws the grace of sensible devotion from his lovers for a time, so that, while he seems not to be present when invoked, the desire of inward love may grow. All who feel the fire of divine love know this alternation of holy ardor, who are true emulators of the holy fathers. For whenever the bridegroom withdraws himself, the desire of the lover increases. She who seeks the Lord, burning with heat, whom she loves, groans for the desire of love.

Secondly, this grace is withdrawn from the devout for an hour, lest frailty be lifted up into pride. For when, even unwillingly, we lose the grace of compunction, by humble examination we judge our infirmity, which can do nothing of itself. Therefore, so that devout worshipers of God may remain humble in their exaltation, it is necessary that they recognize their frailty.

The third cause, indeed, merit requires, because to seek inward consolations belongs not to the strong but to the weak. The Lord must be served freely because he is good; but those who seek sensible sweetness in their devotion are delicate in the least things and seek the Lord Jesus for little consolations. The Lord must be loved usefully, and all womanish and soft affections are to be driven from the soul. Therefore, so that the Lord may increase merit for his worshipers in holy compunction, he sometimes denies the grace of sensible devotion to those who ask for it. But lest, affected by the bitterness of weariness, they grow wholly sluggish from the purpose of holiness, the consoler of the deserving does not withdraw far, but remains nearby. He swiftly returns to his friend, and the one whom he had pretended for an hour to be sad he consoles with the sweetness of spiritual presence.

A fourth cause we join to these: that we are warned to walk more cautiously. For if we fear to lose the grace of compunction, let us learn to guard more diligently what we have. For a rare and precious treasure, the more it is loved, the more diligently it is guarded by its possessor. Therefore the monk who has deserved to receive from the Lord the grace of devotion must watch with the greatest care lest he lose it through his own negligence. But so that you may know how you ought to preserve the granted grace of compunction, lest it perish, the order of what is to be said will show you clearly. First, he who does not wish to lose the grace of inward compunction granted to him should strive, as far as he is able, to keep himself clean and pure from every sin. Let him beware not only of mortal and horrible crimes, but also of small ones, which are called venial, for he who despises the least things gradually falls, and he who does not beware of many small things often by habit makes one great thing.
