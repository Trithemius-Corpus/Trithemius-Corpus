Indeed, when in the year of the Christians one thousand four hundred and sixty-one, Frederick, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, a most Christian man, who was residing at [Simmeren?], held the depraved life of monks in his principality in great hatred, he reformed three monasteries, expelling the deformed monks, and introduced men excellently trained from other monasteries. First, indeed, he brought two monasteries of regular canons of Saint Augustine back to the observance of regular discipline: Rebdorf, that is, among the [unclear], and Schwabenheim among the [Stauronesians?] in the district of the [Nagavienses?]. Finally, in the aforesaid year of the Lord, he also renewed this present monastery of ours, Sponheim, for reform of the order; and, after removing the abbot with five monks, he restored four others in their place for observance, setting over them my predecessor Johannes of Kolenhusen, now serving as abbot in Seligenstadt.

They found the place so oppressed by poverty and burdened with debt that there was no one who believed it could be restored, since all the annual revenues had either been sold or alienated under title of redemption; indeed, nothing at all was left on which they might live. Brother Heinrich, one of the first four reformers sent from Mainz, is present as narrator and witness of the things we say; from his mouth you will know all things more fully. And behold, the seventeenth year is now underway since this place began to be restored under the chapter of the reformed union of Bursfelde, and still we are tortured by no small poverty.

Therefore I have said these things, brothers, so that you may know that we do not bear alone the want that we suffer, since those four men who first reformed the monastery endured heavier things; beans and peas filled their bellies, and a thin pear-drink supplied them drink in measure. The mercy of God has called us to this place, in which through patience we may overcome the [antidicuus?] of our salvation, so that, according to the counsel of the divine Apostle, having scant food and clothing with which we may be covered, being content and giving thanks to the Lord in cheerfulness of mind, we may [serve?]. For we have not been called to feasts of the flesh, nor to the delights of this world, not to pleasures nor to the consolations of the present life, but to serve as soldiers for our Lord and emperor Jesus Christ in abstinence from all the delectable things of this age, in pure conscience and humility of heart.

Attend now therefore to your calling, O monks, and do not [unclear] the purpose [unclear] you desire to attain. Woe to you, faithless monk, who, having become a despiser of your calling and a transgressor of your vow, do not live monastically in the monastery; you who were called to endure tribulation patiently for the love of God, and [unclear] concerning refreshment. For your profession commands you to go humbly content in every usefulness and extremity of garments, and you do not fear to apply zeal to the fastidiousness of clothing. You have been called to thirst, and you, not content to drink water, delight in drinking wine to satiety, contrary to the monastic canon. You were made a monk to fight strongly against the adversary of your salvation, and you, having cast away from yourself the courage of the virtues, go about sluggish, [unclear], and naked; indeed, what is graver, accompanied by a band of vices. You were invited to keep watch in holy prayers, and you are dissolved in inert sleep and shameful sloth. You were called into the subjection of humble obedience, and you, swollen with pride, scorn to obey the precepts of your elders. You have been called into charity, and you do not fear to carry hatred in your breast against your brother. You have been called into weeping and tears, and [unclear] laughter with disordered deep guffawing. The Lord called you in the monastery to repentance, and you, dissolved into wantonness, join sin to sin. You were called to meditate day and night on the law of our Lord Christ, and you, walking after vanities, devote yourself to tales and perishable things. You have been called to heaven, and you, loving the world, pursue earthly things. The Lord called you, humbled, into meekness, and you go about full of rancor and pride.
