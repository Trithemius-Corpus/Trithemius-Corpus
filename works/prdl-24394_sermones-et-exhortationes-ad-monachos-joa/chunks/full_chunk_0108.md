*On serenity.*

From this it undoubtedly comes about that many have errors in their consciences, because they love too much the pleasures of their own flesh. Every proud, lustful, unclean man, entangled in great or public crimes, darkens his conscience by deliberate perversity; and how wrongly he thinks about the future recompense of deserts, he often makes manifest both by words and by deeds. A malicious affection perverts the judgment of rectitude, and the disordered concupiscence of the flesh clouds the understanding. Sometimes, moreover, excessive and immoderate faintheartedness makes the conscience err; this evil is born partly from ignorance, and partly is generated from one’s own sensuality. In this scrupulosity, however, let the monk follow the counsel either of the abbot or of some elder more experienced, and let him not rashly defend the opinion of his own head. Excessive scrupulosity is dangerous, because it hurls the conscience into error and unhappily trips up many. For everything that is done against conscience is said to build toward hell. Excessive perplexity of mind has also harmed many, and has cast them headlong into various errors. But he who is truly a humble monk brings himself to the examination of his ruler. For whoever, in doubts that arise, makes use of the counsel of his superior is not easily tripped up by the snares of demons. For the more the consciences of monks have been exposed to the examination of a wise prelate, the more secure and more brightly shining they will be.

Therefore, dearest brothers, care must first be taken that you have consciences free from every error; then pure from all vice; and finally adorned with the holy works of all virtues. Therefore let the monk who desires to make progress in truth guard his heart with every custody, and, so far as can be done, apply a closure to his senses, nor permit anything deadly to enter. But if, by any occasion, he recognizes that he has fallen into some sin, let him at once strive to blot it out by true penitence, and let him not grant time to the wicked seed to grow stronger for the worse. While the wound is small, let a remedy be applied, because if it is neglected it grows beyond measure. Therefore, my brothers, resist bravely the first movements of demonic suggestion, and overcome the enemy when he is small and weak. But if the sensuality of the body has become altogether wanton, let it be brought back to obedience by harsh mortification, just as we know our holy father Benedict did, when he rooted out the slippery wantonness of the flesh by the stings of nettles and briars. Finally, if you diligently recall to memory the dreadful future punishments of the impious, you will easily repress the illicit movements of the flesh. For there is no one who does not fear pain more than he loves pleasure. Bring back more often to your mind also the joys of heavenly felicity, to which you desire to come; for if you consider them faithfully, you will strongly mitigate the fire of the flesh. Whatever delights in the world is corruptible and momentary; but the joys of eternal pleasures remain without end forever. It is therefore foolish and of extreme madness to choose a transitory advantage and to despise that good which remains forever. He is judged foolish among men who prefers a basket to gold; but he is held most senseless before God who sets temporal pleasure before eternal goods.

He who loves a serene conscience should flee idleness, since, as Scripture says, every idle man is in desire, and idleness has taught much wickedness. The soul must be occupied with good exercises, and the fire of holy conversation, which burns with charity, must be nourished by continual use. For the idle man lives neither for himself nor for his neighbor, but is always given over to his belly or to sleep; he opens his mind to vice, and keeps it closed and empty of virtues. Idleness, the kindler of lust, quickly extinguishes the fervor of spiritual devotion. Abstinence also is very necessary for a monk above all, so that his conscience may remain clean. For, as Saint Jerome says, a full belly easily foams into lust, and he who nourishes his servant delicately will find him insolent. The lover of serenity of conscience is constant in holy prayers, since, as dogs flee a stone, so demons flee devout and continual prayer. Let him also diligently beware of acedia and, at the same time, sadness of mind; and as soon as he feels some temptation creeping in, let him have recourse to the consolation of the Scriptures. For [unclear] the mind which the word of God has filled, a foul thought will enter. Whatever we do, whatever we think, whatever we perform, let us know that the presence of God is always at hand, and we shall easily overcome disordered delight. A good conscience never forgets the presence of God, but always meditates that its judge is present.
