*On the true penitence of monks.*

Let us [unclear] with supplications. Let us preserve charity inviolable in all things, since it is charity that covers a multitude of sins. Each day the good soldier of Christ, who desires to be faithful to his Lord, should examine his conscience by scrutinizing it at least once, and should weigh carefully how much on that day he has advanced or fallen short in holy desire. Whenever he discovers that he has sinned in anything, let him at once do penance, and with tears ask mercy from the Lord. Let him not await the uncertainty of tomorrow: salvation is today; and one who can immediately find pardon should by no means interpose delay. Let him at once grieve in his heart that he has sinned against the Lord; let him also renew his holy purpose with fresh fervor, and let him consider that day wholly lost on which he has not, by making progress, become better. Prayer should always be on the monk’s lips; continual compunction should be in his heart; tears of devotion should flow continually from his eyes; and the exercises of inward love should never depart from his memory. Often there should be in the mind of the cloistered man the most bitter remembrance of the Lord’s Passion; and all the prayers he offers to the Father should be presented in the power of the blood and wounds of our Lord Jesus Christ. On his lips there should always be words bearing before them the mark of a contrite mind, and either prayer should be offered to God or meditation on his words should be turned over.

Let the devout monk turn away from evil and do the good that he can, with cheerfulness and the discretion of the Spirit of God, since, as has been said, for doing penance it is not enough to change one’s habits for the better and withdraw from evil deeds, unless one also strives to adorn one’s conscience with good works. Therefore, my brothers, as you know, four things are required for true penitence. The first is true contrition for sins, which is a grief of heart voluntarily undertaken in detestation of those evils that one has committed. Of this Scripture says: “A contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.” And again: “Those who sow in tears shall reap in exultation.” For the companion of penitence is the grief of contrition; the greater it is, the more fruitful penitence will be. For true contrition is commended by grief of mind and attested by the continual cleansing of tears. Great is the usefulness of tears, because there is no sin so grave that contrition accompanied by tears does not wipe it away. Hence the holy doctor Augustine says: “O humble tear, how powerful and virtuous you are, because yours is the kingdom, yours is the power. You do not fear the gaze of the judge; you impose silence on those who accuse of wrongs; alone you enter to the king, but you do not depart alone; you conquer the unconquerable and bind the almighty.” The same man also says: “The holier anyone is, and the fuller of holy desire, the more abundant is his weeping in prayer.” True compunction has grief; grief, however, is shown by tears, and with these penitence is adorned as with certain ornaments. True compunction makes the soul shudder at sin; and when it has begun from fear, increased by its own growth, little by little it rises up into love, whose virtue is great and whose power is truly admirable; to it every enormity of sins immediately yields. For whomever true compunction of heart has made a suppliant, the merciful Lord immediately hears him. O happy tears, O saving affliction and truly sweet bitterness, which the devil fears, through which the compunct soul rises up to the mercy of God. For, as holy Pope Gregory says, the more abundantly fault is lamented, the higher the knowledge of truth is reached; because, for seeing the inward light, a conscience long polluted is renewed when baptized with tears.

The second thing that belongs to the truth of penitence is a firm purpose of amendment, since whoever has the word of confession in his mouth and does not have in his heart the purpose of amendment is either deceitful or empty. But whoever has it in his heart and not in his mouth is either proud or timid, and in no way fit for true penitence. For Scripture says: “With the heart one believes unto righteousness, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.” Although this saying, beloved of Christ, is principally to be understood of faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, nevertheless it seems rightly applicable to true penitence. For without confession of the mouth, contrition of heart alone is not enough for penitence, especially from that time when the statute of the Church decreed confession necessary for salvation. Therefore the third thing necessary for the truth of penitence is confession of the mouth; without it neither contrition nor the purpose of future amendment is sufficient. What sort this saving confession ought to be, I know none of you is unaware.
