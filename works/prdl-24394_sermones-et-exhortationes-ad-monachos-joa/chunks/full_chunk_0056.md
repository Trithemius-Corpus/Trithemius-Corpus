For the first purpose, our holy lawgivers, wishing you to be ready, command that the brave deeds of the martyrs, whose imitators you ought to become, be read; and then, so that you may be teachable in spirit, they ordered one passage from the Rule to be added. And so, that by imitation of the holy martyrs you may be strong and patient under necessary rebukes, the sequence of their passion is read here in your ears; and, in order that you may prepare your minds for saving doctrine, a reading from the Rule which you profess is recited to you together with it. The holy martyrs of Christ encourage you to patience by their example; and the blessed Benedict admonishes you by his teaching that you should also be ready for injuries.

Therefore let none of you, most beloved brothers, in this place refuse humbly to receive penance daily for his negligences; let no one disdain it. This place is the public place for expiating sin, in which the severity of judgment is so tempered that the kindness of discretion is not violated. For I wish to be the physician of your souls, not their destroyer. Therefore come securely to the examination of our judgment, and humbly proclaim yourselves openly concerning negligences committed. I do not wish to burden you beyond the Rule, so as to transfer the most secret offenses of conscience from sacramental confession to the public examination of faults, which we know that certain men have done, to the grave [unclear] of the souls of their subjects. For thus our lawgiver Benedict says in the Rule: But if the cause of the soul’s sin has been hidden, let him make it known only to the abbot or to spiritual elders, who know how to heal their own wounds and those of others, not to disclose or publish them.

There are two places in the monastery for the expiation of sin: one for those sins that are manifest, and the other for those that are hidden. For manifest sins must be expiated publicly before all in the daily proclamation of faults; but hidden sins, and those whose disclosure would scandalize the weak, must be referred to confession. For he whose sin is manifest must also be punished publicly; and he who has offended in secret must also be corrected in secret. There are certain rulers of cloistered men who do not fear to bring the hidden sins of their subjects to public examination; and in my judgment they do not execute the rights of prelacy prudently enough, since they close off from their subjects the way of salvation and bind their confession, most free though it is, under the bond of ill will. How dangerous this is, there is no one even moderately wise who does not understand. For the holy founder of monks rebukes this rashness of a pastor, saying: Let the abbot exercise solicitude with great care, and with all sagacity and industry take care not to lose any of the sheep entrusted to him. Let him know that he has undertaken the care of sick souls, not tyranny over healthy ones.

We have appointed for you four spiritual confessors from among the seniors, so that if any one of you has been seized beforehand by some hidden temptation, and has found our [unclear] inexperienced or suspect, he may securely have recourse to them for his consolation. Although we have found these things hitherto known to the whole congregation [unclear], whom we have observed to have withdrawn trust from us both in confession and outside it. For all know, and you plainly see, that I seek nothing more in you, desire nothing more, than your salvation and the daily increase of spiritual progress. For you are my crown, you my honor, you my joy, because then I consider myself happy when I know that you have made progress in the love of God.

You have obtained a great opportunity for making progress, dearest brothers, since the place in which we serve the Lord as soldiers is removed from all tumult, and we do not cease to advance you toward better things by daily admonition. But whenever any one of you has fallen into some sin through human frailty, he is not permitted to lie long in dejection of spirit, where one is daily proclaimed by another in true fraternal charity for his negligences. This is a workshop of salvation, in which the mind is fed with the nourishment of heavenly doctrine, and the soul is daily cleansed from the stains of offenses by public satisfaction. This place is an earthly purgatory, in which the monk is corrected with discretion for his negligences, and through humble patience, for the love of Christ, is daily offered to his Creator in a long martyrdom.
