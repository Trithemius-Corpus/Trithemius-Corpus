They seek holiness without merit, and desire to have merit without the exercise of the necessary virtues. They seem to themselves great before God in devotion and pre-eminent, and in their animal passions they are not mortified.

You err, O wretched men, and shamefully deceive yourselves, because without the foundation of most humble subjection and obedience you think to build the tower of holiness. First the disordered desires of sensuality must be mortified; the animal passions must be restrained: anger, murmuring, the rancor of indignation, and all impatience. For as long as vices rule over you, whatever offerings of private singularity you offer are not pleasing to God. All things are vain where there is not a pure conscience. The conduct of a young monk is suspect to us, however devout it may appear, when the humility of voluntary subjection does not commend it. For a monk without humility and obedience cannot be good, although by fasting and prayer he daily seeks holiness.

The devil deceives many in the hiddenness of elation and pride, whom he permits continually to keep vigil, fast, and pray. For just as no works without humility and charity are pleasing to God, so fasting, vigils, prayers, and the studies of monks without these are not to be feared by the devil. The devil is not moved by this, by which he is not tormented. He does not much esteem the vigils of the proud man; then certainly he is moved against you, because he is tormented with envy at your virtues; he turns against you all the weapons of his cunning that he can, and strives with all his strength to overthrow your purpose of holiness. For nothing in a monk displeases demons more, nothing torments them more vehemently, than if for the love of God he has truly humbled himself in all things, and has always kept, with a cheerful mind, the service of the monastic institution under the guardianship of obedience.

You must beware the impulses of demons, you who think yourselves something great without the merit of true virtues. I see that you are unwilling toward the commands of your elders, impatient and murmuring under correction, rather quick to rebuke another's life, but very slow in considering your own faults. These are the surest signs of the impulse of demons, and manifest indications that the necessary mortification of one's own will has been despised. You believe yourselves to be perfect and holy cultivators of great religion and inward devotion, and you have neither brotherly charity nor true humility, necessary for you as monks. You seek sensible devotion in prayers, and with eyes full of tears you now consider yourselves friends of God. For what? Do tears in prayers make one [holy?], when prayer is made with a certain elation of mind and without true humility? God gives tears; the soul struck by adversities gives them; the devil too, the deceiver of the proud, gives them. God makes fruitful with sweet tears the mind humbled in true charity, as though it were a meadow; nature stirs the weeping person in adversities; the devil pours little streams of waters into the proud under the likeness of devotion.

Humility is the one virtue that, in the breadth of charity, makes human hearts truly devout. Nothing benefits you, O monk, whatever you may have done in your singularity, if you do not have true humility with the fervor of twofold charity. For to rise before others for the night praises; to say long prayers in private beyond the custom of the order; to continue a fast beyond the statute of the rule while others lawfully eat; to remain in secret while the rest go out to common labor: two things make these meritorious, and if they are absent all will be entirely fruitless. Indeed, whatever of a monk's works charity and humble obedience have not commended will be judged empty of merit before God and will lack reward. But you who with a swollen brow despise the precepts of the elders, doing by your own rashness not what they command but what pleases you; you who set your own inventions before the common holy exercises of the order, are still far removed from the way of life of the saints. It is not permitted you by your own rashness to abandon those things that belong to the monastic institution, and to prefer to necessary things the new and vain things that please you.

Whatever work you do is unpleasing to God when the thing to which you are bound has been despised. The inspiration of divine grace teaches humility, persuades obedience, and follows true charity in all things; it keeps the commandment, admits no pride, despises no one, but pursues the perpetual integrity of regular discipline. But the impulse of the devil, under the appearance of good, cunningly trips up the unwary monk, and makes him subject in all things to his own judgment. It falsely tells him that all the things that pertain to preserving monastic integrity are badly instituted and contrary to him, and therefore are to be postponed to his own pursuits.
