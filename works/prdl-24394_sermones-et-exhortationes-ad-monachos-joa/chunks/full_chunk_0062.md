*Homily XIV.*

*Fol. XXXIII.*

*[unclear] — nor does the delay of repentance [bring] security.*

He who, while healthy, does not repent is a fool; and he who does not amend his vices when he can, by the just judgment of God loses the deferred power to do so. For repentance is fruitful when it has been voluntary, by the freedom of choice; but when the danger of death is already imminent, it seems rather to be necessary than undertaken by the penitent’s own choice.

Let no one say in his heart, my brothers, let no one think that the labor of repentance is hard, and for that reason must be put off until the very last particle of this life. That thought is exceedingly dangerous, because the fixed term of life which divine providence has appointed for each person, just as it wished to be hidden from all, so it will not prolong for anyone who deliberately puts off repentance.

If we wish to be safe and secure in conscience, let us do repentance while we are healthy, while we can sin, while as yet we are not being pressed by any necessity of imminent death. For it is a very doubtful matter to do repentance when necessity already compels, and the imminence of death makes you no longer able to sin: when, that is, you have not abandoned sins, but they, because you are powerless, have abandoned you.

O how many, deceived by the hope of a longer life, descend to the abyss of eternal damnation, who when healthy held repentance in contempt. For the longer you put off repentance, the less disposed you will always find yourself to repent. For wickedness takes strength with time, and there is no sin so small that it does not grow when neglected.

Therefore embrace repentance while you are healthy, and do not put it off from day to day, because you understand what you can do today, but you do not know what will happen tomorrow. Each new day brings its own small inconveniences, and he who today is not disposed to repentance will perhaps be less fit tomorrow.

But let none of you say: What have I sinned, that repentance should seem necessary to me, since my whole life is nothing other than a certain hard and prolonged necessity of repentance? You have heard Scripture testifying that there is no man upon earth who does good and does not sin, since no one is clean before God. For even if you do not commit the great crimes of human impiety, such as homicide, theft, adultery, fornications, and things like these, nevertheless, because you have been placed in that calling in which you ought with all your strength to raise every effort to the state of perfection, sins which are small for mortals living in the world are certainly imputed to you as more serious.

For all sins must be avoided by cloistered men, as far as they can be, not only those which hinder salvation, but also those which stain the perfection enjoined upon them. And which of you lives with such purity as monastic perfection requires? It is a great sin for a monk if he has laid aside his effort toward perfection, if he has lived negligently in the monastery, if he has transgressed even one commandment of the canon and the Gospel.

But who among you, brothers, loves the Lord God with such sincerity, such charity, and such fervor of love, as a man is bound to love his maker and redeemer? Or whom will you give me from among all of you who does not sometimes exceed either in taking food or in drink beyond what accords with the integrity of monks? Which of you does not offend in word, or who is not sometimes troubled by evil thoughts? Whom among you has sloth not struck, and whom has gloomy laziness not disturbed? Who is there in this little congregation of yours whom pride does not touch, and whom impatience has not rather often made a murmurer?

O, let each one of you consider the paths of his manner of life, and he will find that he fails in many things at any moment whatsoever. For what if a monk has committed no evil? What if he has kept himself clean from this wicked age? He will not be able to be perfect unless he has both turned away from evil and done the good which his perfection requires. For one without the other does not suffice, but perfection in man arises from both.

Therefore, brothers, we must always keep watch with the greatest zeal, so that we satisfy the institution of the holy fathers, according to which by public profession we have solemnly bound ourselves to live, in order that we may direct our effort without interruption toward the perfection set before us. Let consideration of our own weakness not draw us back from the holy effort, because we are helped no less by the grace of the Creator than we run by our own desire.
