What if I speak more boldly, as a lover of truth: adversities profit us more than prosperity, if we are learned and not forgetful [unclear] of our proper condition.

But a third degree of patience remains, certainly the highest and most perfect, rightly granted to those who not only, when there is need, bear contrary things calmly for the love of God and the usefulness of the brethren, but also, more fervent and inflamed with the spirit of charity, hasten gladly to expose themselves to receive insults and injuries. And this kind of patience is divided into two parts, just as a twofold class of martyrs is found. For some, although they could have hidden when persecution of believers was raging, with Christ’s charity burning in them, and with no one compelling them, but by their own free will, presented themselves before the tribunals of the persecutors, publicly confessing Jesus, the Redeemer of the world, and received the torments inflicted on them with joy. Others, although they did not thrust themselves forward unbidden, nevertheless joyfully accepted martyrdom when it was offered, always prepared to endure for the faith of Christ whatever the cruelty of tyrants might have thought to inflict.

To these most illustrious confessors rightly obtain a neighboring degree: if the cruelty of the impious, by persecuting them, did not remove them from this world, nevertheless they in no way lost the glory of martyrdom. For, crucifying their flesh in this world for the name of Christ, they endured both a voluntary and a daily martyrdom; they were made glorious through abstinence and humility, always steadfast in mind and most patient, and most ready to accept bodily martyrdom. You also, O monks, should take care to be [unclear] with all your powers; and although enemies who kill the body for Christ are now lacking, by resisting the desires of the flesh and patiently sustaining adversities for the love of God, you should show yourselves martyrs in mind and spirit. In the peace of the saints, the best kind of martyrdom is to afflict the flesh by abstinence for the name of God, to desire nothing in this world, to advance always in good works, and, if any evil occurs, to bear it in all things gladly and patiently with cheerfulness of mind. To this new [unclear] of patience, dearest brothers, henceforth...

...lives, if you desire to reign with Christ forever. Bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ: so that what each person desires to be done for himself, he should also cheerfully bestow upon his brother.

*On True Humility*

*Homily Nineteen.*

It is the opinion of nearly all learned men, dearest brothers, that the highest and chief virtue of monks is none other than humility, because it is the mother and nurse of all virtues. Humility indeed makes the monk; pride destroys and corrupts him. The Lord says through Isaiah: “To whom shall I look, if not to the poor little one, and the contrite in spirit, and the one trembling at my words?” For God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble; because the beginning of every sin is pride, and without true humility no one can attain salvation. Since this is so, I think it necessary for me, brothers, to exhort you without interruption that, fleeing pride, you should always preserve deep humility in all your works. Indeed, no good work is believed to please the Lord God when it is presented without the seasoning of humility.

For humility is a most healthful medicine for the souls of the faithful, because it aids all: it presses down the swollen ulcers of the spirit; it restores what is wasting away; it corrects what is depraved; it removes what has grown out excessively. Humility is the mother and parent of all virtues: you strengthen patience; you arouse compunction; you illuminate charity; you adorn chastity; you make prayer fruitful; you commend fasting and abstinence. O holy humility, truly beloved of God: through penitence you justify the impious; you preserve the just in their purpose of holiness; you powerfully open heaven to your lovers; you close hell to penitents. O humility, venerable and exceedingly pleasing to God: you compelled the Son of the eternal Father to descend from heaven into the womb of most chaste Mary, the perpetual virgin; you made him appear visible to us; you caused him, wrapped in poor cloths, to be laid in a manger, so that he might adorn us in heaven with the ornaments of perpetual happiness.
