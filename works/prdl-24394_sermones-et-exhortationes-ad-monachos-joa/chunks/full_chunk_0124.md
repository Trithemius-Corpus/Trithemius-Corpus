*Sermon VI. Fol. LXV.*

He longs to put on [unclear]: he gladly uses cheaper garments, and takes very great delight in those things which others despise. Fine garments, beautifully arranged, do not befit a monk, but the very cheapest, with which the body may be protected only from the injury of harsh weather. For all fastidiousness is forbidden to cloistered men, since by reason of their profession made before God they are bound to contempt of the world. Humility flees lofty things; it seeks the things that are humble.

The twenty-third sign of the humility of the monastery shows a monk servile toward the brethren and voluntarily devoted to acts of service, and ready for everything whatsoever he may be ordered to do. For true humility obeys even inferiors with a most ready will, and is never indignant against the one who commands. As much as he can, he desires to serve all for love of Christ, since he faithfully loves God with his whole heart. For whatever service he has bestowed upon the brethren, he firmly believes that it has all been shown to Christ.

The twenty-fourth sign of humility a monk then attains in truth, when, for love of God, he delights to take care of the meaner offices in the monastery, which the rest despise. We have read that many have ascended this degree of true humility, and we do not doubt that they are of great merit before God. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Everyone who humbles himself shall be exalted; for God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble. The humble monk imitates Christ; the proud one, the devil.

The twenty-fifth sign of monastic humility is, in an assembly of any people whatsoever, always to seek the lower place for sitting, and to prefer oneself neither to an equal nor to an inferior. It is not for monks proudly to contend over preeminence of place, nor does it befit a soldier of Christ to wish to surpass others in honor. For the true monk who loves humility, content with every lowest position, desires to excel no one.

The twenty-sixth indication of humility most especially suits him who, though he is learned and educated, because of the lowly humbling of himself, hides all learning and knowledge. For the monk who is truly humble is always found readier to listen than to speak. For the divine priest Jerome says: A monk has the office not of teaching but of lamenting, that he may either mourn himself or the world, and await the Lord's coming in fear. The arrogance of monks is hateful both to God and to men, and is always liable to sin.

The twenty-seventh sign of humility is perfectly attained by him who, for love of regular discipline, delights more in being silent than in speaking. The humble lover of Christ loves silence, and, lest he offend in speech, is accustomed to say very little. Humility is rarely found among the talkative; and he who is swift to speak casts down his own soul. A talkative and garrulous monk is not without pride, and he who is foolish multiplies words. Whoever desires in spiritual combat to overcome his adversary through humility must not refuse to restrain his tongue from much speaking and from every idle word. It is written in the book of Proverbs that in much speaking sin will not be lacking.

Whoever has attained the twenty-eighth sign of perfect humility will avoid laughter and all [unclear] gestures of those guffawing. Weeping, not laughter, most befits a monk, since he is dead to all the frivolities of the world. For the wise man says: The fool raises his voice in laughter, but a wise man will scarcely laugh silently.

The twenty-ninth indication of perfect humility takes its beginning from the fear of the Lord; for whose honor, as much as he can, it flees and detests every sin. For a monk who does not fear to sin has not truly been humble. But the monk who follows Christ according to his powers in true humility is accustomed to flee every sin, and, as much as he can, to exercise himself in the pursuit of virtues. Humility confesses its sin; and as often as it has offended even in the smallest matters, it does not rest until it has amended all things through true penitence. He who is truly humble is also always found zealous for preserving purity of conscience. For the nourishment of humility is charity, which does not permit the one who has it to be idle.

The thirtieth sign of true humility shows a monk confirmed in the purpose of holiness, and makes him subject to all the brethren, even the younger. Whatever the shepherd commands, he obeys simply; he never debates the precepts of the elder; he does not ask the reason why he commands so. If impossible things are commanded, he is ready in spirit; for whatever is hard and arduous, he is prepared without contradiction. He is directed by the counsel of his shepherd; he believes him, trusts him, obeys him; he willingly is governed by his command. You have now heard, my dearest brothers, the thirty indications of true humility. O good God, where are the works?
