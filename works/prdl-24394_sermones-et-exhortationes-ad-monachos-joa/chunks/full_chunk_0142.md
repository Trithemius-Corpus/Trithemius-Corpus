*Sermon VII.*

To God and the power of acting. For no one has offered more to God than the monk who has given himself. But some who climb this sixth degree have hidden within themselves a certain defect of pride, because, while they look incautiously to themselves, they do not entrust themselves in all things to the divine will as they ought. This defect takes its beginning from a certain very subtle vanity, when the soul, trusting in its own purity, asks that some secret be revealed to it by the Lord. And in this one thing those who climb the sixth ladder differ from those of the seventh, who, being bound over to God, flee signs of vanity. For signs were given not to the faithful but to unbelievers; hence the monk who asks a sign from the Lord shows that he has not yet submitted himself perfectly to the divine will.

Those ascend the seventh degree who keep all the things prescribed above most zealously, and, subject to God in all things, live without the stain of vanity. They flee every sin, both venial and mortal, as far as they can; they perform works of virtue for the love of God alone; [unclear] they seek no miracles of vanity. Whatever almighty God has ordained concerning them they receive gladly; they in no way at all depart from the observance of monastic purity. Yet in one respect they are inferior to those who follow, because they perhaps suffer some defect of which they are unaware, by whose impulse they sometimes commit something of their own sensuality.

Whoever has deserved to ascend the eighth degree grants nothing further to sensuality. Here is the sweetest rest of the soul; here is perfect tranquillity of mind. Nothing vicious is left in this degree of beauty; nothing is found here that can sadden the soul. Here all things are clean, all things pure, all things holy, all things most sweetly burning with the fervor of charity. If only we may strive to ascend this degree; oh, if only we may sometime deserve to come into this harbor of tranquillity. The more one, standing in this holy ladder, has begun to taste the sweetness of inward delight, the more, hastening toward what lies ahead, he longs for it. Ever to be proclaimed are the desires of holy love, the exercises of venerable perfection, where the clean conscience of one who loves God shines without sins, where the wicked contests of vices have ceased and great tranquillity has succeeded.

Those ascend the ninth degree who, already consummated in the eighth, keep all the things prescribed above purely and simply for the love of God, and desire nothing other than to praise, bless, and glorify God. Whoever has ascended this degree of perfection, as if forgetful of himself, resigns himself wholly to God and submits humbly. He counts himself unworthy of all God’s benefits; he cares nothing at all for himself, because he has already learned to commit himself wholly to God and humbly to be subject in all things to his ordering. Hence he neither asks for miracles nor seeks any consolations in this life, but with the holy apostle of Christ truly cries: To me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain. For the world is crucified to me, and I to the world. For the true monk, lover of Christ, has offered himself wholly to God out of charity, and has despised the world with all its riches, honors, and vanities. Hence the world is said to be crucified to him, in which there is nothing that seems able to penetrate his mind. Happy are those monks who serve the Lord God with a pure conscience in true charity, who run without offense under the command of humility and obedience, who ascend this last degree of perfection. These are true monks, who seek nothing other than the honor of God. Indeed, my brothers, although monks are not bound by necessity of salvation to be perfect, for otherwise very few would be saved, nevertheless we are all bound with good will to strive toward perfection, and with whatever strength we can to long for this ninth degree. For it is very contrary to the monastic purpose to be unwilling to advance, and a kind of apostasy, after the vow of perfection, to wish to remain in imperfection. For the enemies of our salvation are the vices that attack us, and until they either conquer or are conquered, they do not cease to pursue us. Let us imitate the history of the ancient battle, when the sons of promise destroyed their enemies not in one day but successively. For it is difficult to conquer all things at once, unless the charity of God suddenly overshadows the mind. Against all the vices that attack us in whatever way, let us wage war: let us conquer gluttony today; when it has been rightly laid low, let us overcome sloth; and let us act so with the rest, that at last we may emerge victors over them all. The Lord will be present with us as we fight, and God will give victory to those who love him. Amen.

*The end of the second book of exhortations to monks by Johannes Trithemius, abbot, on the first day of September, 1486.*

These two books were printed at Strasbourg by Johann Knobloch, printer, at the expense of Johann Haselberger of Augia, in the diocese of Constance, in the year of the Lord 1516, on the twenty-fifth day of the month of August.
