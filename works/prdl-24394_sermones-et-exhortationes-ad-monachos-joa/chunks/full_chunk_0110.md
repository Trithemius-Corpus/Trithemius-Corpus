*On continual preparation for death.*

Continual meditation on necessary death makes a person never trust in earthly things, but await his departure in every place and time with foresight. Meditation on death always expects the end of life in every place, because it is wholly ignorant of the point of dying. Whoever daily meditates on the coming of future death disposes himself for dying at every time with all the diligence he can. For nothing is more certain to us than death, although we all do not know the hour of dying. Therefore, brothers, with all the urgency we can, we admonish your charity that you always think that you are going to die, and that with such foresight you diligently prepare yourselves for dying that, when the hour of departure comes, you may not fear.

Let no one promise himself a long life in this world, because nothing so deceives mortals as the hope of a longer age. For what does a more prolonged age accomplish in human beings except that it makes them liable to more offenses and always less disposed for dying? The longer we live in this world, the more we fear death. A longer age accumulates greater debts of spiritual obligation; and the old man fears all the more to come before the sight of the strict judge, the more he recognizes before the Lord that he is bound to render account in many things. For the soul leaves its body unwillingly when it is driven by the heavier goads of its conscience. But he who is pure from all wickedness of iniquity and adorned for salvation with the necessary comeliness of virtues does not fear to die, because through death he goes to everlasting life. Therefore, O monk, as you wish to die, so live now, because death cannot be evil when the integrity of a good life has preceded it.

For anyone dies securely who loves the Lord Jesus above all things and, despising all the goods of this world for love of him, truly keeps his words. He departs securely from this life who always lives in the love of God without stain of conscience. He dies securely whose life is defiled by no pleasures of this world. But the death of sinners is worst, whose life was evil and contrary to divine ordinances. For him who lives in sins, contrary to God, death becomes a dreadful entrance into hell. He does not die by death who has lived well; and rarely does he die well who is shown always to have lived badly. He dies securely who loves God above all things with a pure mind and at every time keeps himself unstained from this wicked world. Death is desirable to the good and upright in heart because it sets an end to exile and restores life in heaven. Death is the abandonment of the body, the overturning of a dark prison, and the laying down of a heavy burden. Death is the end of present evils, the finish of sorrow, and the grave of every earthly pleasure. A good life in the faith and charity of Jesus Christ makes death precious and especially desirable to the servant of God.

Now therefore, brothers, strive to anticipate death with holy works, and by zeal for virtues make pleasant for yourselves what nature has willed to be necessary for all. Remember that we all are necessarily mortal, and that when we do not think it, we shall pass from this world. For, as a certain venerable prelate said: we are all taken from the midst as the fates decline, and we are drawn where life, when completed, calls us. Therefore, my brothers, let us do whatever good we can while we have time, lest, overtaken by the command of death, we grieve too late that we were very negligent. Time alone is ours; all other things are another’s. The labor of our spiritual warfare, even if it seems somewhat hard to lukewarm minds, is nevertheless very brief and will quickly be ended with life; but the reward of good works that will be given to us remains forever.

Let meditation on death soon to come never leave you, but by consideration of the strict judgment, which no one can escape, let it continually pierce your minds in the love of God. Behold how many monks have gone before us in this monastery of Sponheim, by living and dying, through almost four hundred years from its first foundation; of them there is today among us no memory at all left. As many as we are, they were; what they are, after a short time we shall be. They all were mortal, as we are; they died and returned into the dust of the earth, as we shall be. I am the twenty-fifth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim; unless twenty-four had gone before me to death, I certainly would not have come as the twenty-fifth to this abbacy. I too shall shortly give place to another, so that the twenty-sixth number of abbots may be filled.
