*On All Things, XII.*

The servant of Christ is believed to act in Christ’s stead in the monastery, and therefore he is deservedly honored by all. Therefore live as though you always had him present before your eyes; and whatever you would not presume to do or say while he was looking on, do not attempt in any way when he is absent. For it is foolish consolation, when conscience is biting with guilt, for sin to be hidden from men and to flee the judgment of the pastor, when one knows oneself to be sick unto death. The monk who does an evil which he wishes to be hidden from his prelate has violated the integrity of the monastic canon, and has gravely sinned against the fifth degree of humility. For thus it commands: The fifth degree of humility is this: if he has not concealed from his abbot, by humble confession, all evil thoughts coming into his heart and evil deeds done by him in secret. These are the admonitions of father Benedict; these are the holy teachings of monastic instruction. If you, monk, despise or neglect them, you will be convicted of forgetfulness of your own salvation.

It is a sure sign of humility if a monk does not refuse to open his heart to his elder, and does not wish any of the evils he has committed to be hidden from his pastor. He who confesses his fault with humility will be strengthened against the adversary of his salvation; and he who hides his thoughts will make progress in evil. For there is no thought of evils so small that it does not gradually grow if neglected; and no vice so slight that it does not gain strength if it has been dissembled. Therefore evil thoughts must be repelled immediately at the beginning, because when a depraved thought is not suddenly extinguished it produces delight; delight produces consent; consent casts one headlong into action. Then an action of evil, often repeated, begets habit; habit begets necessity; necessity brings in despair; despair contempt; contempt obstinacy; and that brings forth the eternal death of the soul. For Scripture says: When the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.

Is not your abbot, O monk, the pastor and physician of your soul? Are not I, my dearest brothers, after God, the one who feeds your mortal bodies with necessary nourishment? Am I not compelled to minister the word of salvation to you? If you sheep flee the pastor, what are the pastures of the spirit which you expect from us? The sick man who flees the physician despises his own salvation and willingly kills his own soul. Therefore the monk who walks in the way of malice and turns aside from the paths of justice is near to death; and he slips so much the more deeply into the depth of vices, the less he reveres the judgment of those who correct him. But he is truly a monk who keeps his soul from every iniquity and does nothing at all which he would wish his pastor not to know. He who conceals his way is not pure; and he who flees the presence of the judge increases suspicion. Like a sheep walking in the desert, which is seized by the wolf, so the negligent and impure monk who withdraws himself from the knowledge of his pastor is devoured by demons. The sick man running to destruction, and the monk hiding every sin: there is no salvation for them unless they return to the physician.

Monks returning from a journey should immediately on that very day, according to the monastic canon, prostrate themselves in the sight of all the brothers, so that whatever has been committed on the road through negligence may be expiated by the common prayers. For the common prayer of many avails much for washing away sins, if it has been poured out to the Lord with the fervor of devotion.

*Here ends On All Things, XII: On Monks Sent on a Journey.*
