This is the excellent and praiseworthy practice of the brothers of the Bursfeld reform: that after Compline no one should lay himself down upon the coverlet of his bed unless he has first diligently and earnestly examined his conscience concerning faults or negligences committed during the day. If, after this examination, you find that you have offended in anything during the day, you know from learned custom what must be done. Hidden things are to be carried over to the morrow with contrition and humble confession; manifest things, however, are to be reported daily at chapter for blame, as is observed. *What are the signs of true humility: third sermon.*

Most beloved, let no one deceive himself by his own estimation; let no one lead his heart astray by foolish and empty presumption. Not all who wish to appear humble before God are humble, nor are those who keep silence under injuries always patient. For the roots of true virtue are hidden in the secret of the heart, and not in the display of outward show alone. For often one who wishes rather to be considered humble than truly to be so appears among you, and shows many signs of pride, but either no signs of humility or certainly very few. If he suffers vehemently and excessively from those who err, as by right we ought [unclear], who desire to have not virtue but the appearance of virtue. Why, I ask, do you deceive yourselves, [unclear] mercy, and shamefully destroy yourselves by misleading yourselves, you who, despising truth, love falsehood?

So that you may know how proudly you live, we shall say plainly the signs and true marks of true humility, because some of them remain hidden in the heart alone, while others must also break forth and become manifest to all who behold them. Let this be for the true monk a mirror of perfect humility, in which, looking each day, he may examine himself. Therefore the first sign of true humility, I say, is one that each person can recognize in himself: that he is able to regard himself as lower and more vile than all people, not with painted speech, but by the humble judgment of the heart. This is the greatest sign of profound humility: that the true monk, always advancing toward a better manner of life, believes himself a useless and unworthy servant of God. True humility belongs to the heart, not to speech; it is the pursuit of the mind, not of bodily display alone. That monk is truly humble who loves God above all things; who always keeps himself pure in all things from every transgression of the command of God and of the rule; who does great works of piety and justice and, by a true estimation of himself within himself, feels humble.

The second sign of true and profound humility is attained by the man who, running well in the fear of the Lord and persevering, would confess his negligences at all times before God and human beings.
