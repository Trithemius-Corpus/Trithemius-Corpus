A stream flowing out, by its muddiness, stains the beauty of the meadows lying open round about; and when a monk gives attention to the tales of secular people, he defiles the purity of his conscience. Therefore, before you set your foot out of the monastery, arm yourself with holy prayers; and before you go out on the road, do not neglect to commend your way to the Lord with prayers. Then carry out what the lawgiver has commanded, and entrust yourself to be protected by the prayers both of the abbot and of all the brothers. Prescribe for yourself the canon of the severity of regular observance, and, with a stable remembrance of it, guard your mind from every outflow of vanities that meet you.

Do not go anywhere out of the cenobium without the abbot’s blessing, because, since you ought to be dead and buried to this world, it will not be lawful for you to do anything further without the command of your elder. Recognize yourself as a soldier of King Jesus Christ: you must follow your emperor, without whom there is no salvation. He, fixed to the cross, is taken down not by his own hands but by the hands of others; and you, if you desire to be saved, must have your will governed by the abbot’s command. For the monk who goes out anywhere from the cenobium without the abbot’s permission is like a little sheep in the darkness that is burned up in the fire of the house. But the true monk of Christ, even when he is outside the cenobium in body, remains in the cloister of the mind; and he is no less alone among many than he is accustomed to be solitary in his cell.

The lover of Christ thinks of Jesus even in the midst of a crowd; and he who is a true worker of virtues keeps his heart firm in the fear of God in every place. For even if he is not in the monastery in body, yet in heart, because of the vigor of his spirit, he has always stood firm and stable, as if alone in the cloister. The spirit is not polluted by the movement of the body, when the solitude of the mind is preserved. You must live with the same holiness on the road, O monk, with which you are commanded to conduct yourself in the cenobium, and you must walk with a certain greater caution, lest you scandalize anyone by a more lax example, where there are as many judges of monks as there seem to be onlookers present.

The eyes of all secular people look up to monks, and they seek in their works the holiness which they hear them to have professed. A monk gives more offense by one rash word than a man given to the world does by ten thousand. For however light and buffoonish a worldly man may seem, nevertheless he has not learned to tolerate the insolence of monks with an even mind, because in his rustic simplicity he believes all cloistered men to be not such as they are, but such as they ought to be. Hence an evil word or deed among them is ascribed to the monk for damnation, whereas it is imputed to the worldly man for pardon. Nor without reason. For the monk who has professed a holy and approved canon is bound to direct his whole effort toward perfection, and in word and deed, in all purity of mind and body, far to surpass the conduct of secular people. For a light or idle word, which is granted to secular people for pardon, is judged for destruction in cloistered men.

Gold is more precious than all metals and is more desired by mortals, and in no other is a stain or corruption of falsity more quickly charged. And the flaw which is discerned in gold with judicial severity is passed over in the other metals because of the baseness of their nature. Gold gladdens the eyes by its beauty and strengthens the innermost parts of life by its virtue. Gold, when smelted in the fire, is not diminished; and the true monk of God is not changed from the purpose of holiness in the tribulation of persecution. A small [unclear] in a golden vessel is very displeasing in the sight of a powerful man; if an earthen vessel is crooked, no one detests it. When a monk offends in small matters, a great outcry of many people rises against him, because his conduct is compared to gold. The canonical life of monks is gold, a mirror. Gold in the midst of mud receives no stain of its nature, but preserves all its beauty inviolate.

So too the true monk, a lover of Christ, remains among men, when sent on a journey, just as he has learned to conduct himself in the secrecy of his little cell, because he walks pure in mind as well as in body before the Lord. For if he is a true monk, he is not polluted by the dust of another’s manner of life, nor does [unclear] delight him away from the rectitude of [unclear]. He who is secure in heart is not divided even in the midst of those making a tumult. Therefore, monk, you who desire to be good, gather your mind back to yourself; and before you leave your cell for the journey, dispose your heart. Let the fear of the Lord always be the companion of your journey.
