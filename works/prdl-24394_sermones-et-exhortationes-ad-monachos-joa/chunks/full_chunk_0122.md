*Sermon III. Folio LXIII.*

...he bears continual [unclear] in his heart; and, always contrite in spirit, praying and groaning to the Lord with tears, he asks pardon and mercy. Nor does he pray only for his own sins, but also for the sins of the whole world. The greatest remedy against elation of mind is the remembrance of one’s own faults.

The third test of humility is that he desires to keep in secret with himself all the good works that he does, and conceals his own progress out of zeal for humility. For he who is humble does not boast vainly, nor does he seek praise from men, since he hopes for the glory of eternal happiness. Whoever among you considers himself humble and a true monk should not, for God’s sake, despise this sign of profound submission and abasement.

The fourth mark of true humility is this: descending voluntarily into the abyss of contempt for himself, he regards all his brethren, even those openly sinning, as better than himself before God. Forgetful of his own good deeds, he wisely considers his own evils; but passing over the evils of others in silence, he makes known at the proper time the good things they have done. This is the condition of true humility: always to [unclear] and proclaim the good works of others, but prudently to disparage and conceal one’s own for the avoidance of pride.

Having attained the fifth sign of pious humility, he is quiet in his simplicity and does not speak of others’ defects, unless, when reason cannot do otherwise, he strives to admonish gently and meekly. At times he also wisely dissimulates rebukes, and waits for and observes a more opportune time.

The sixth sign of humility is shown when a monk, out of love of humility, is found slow and not ready to accuse and reprehend the sins of others. For one who is truly humble always considers his own weakness, and silently ponders in secret how many evils he himself has committed against God’s justice. Hence he is greatly afraid to reprehend the deeds of others, because he recognizes himself as a sinner and therefore unworthy to judge others. We except the prelates and rectors of monks from this rule of humility to the extent that they know themselves to be obliged to the correction of subjects.

The seventh sign of true humility is that he gladly receives corrections and amendments for his faults and negligences, and is not embittered by any pain against the one who reprehends him. Whenever the humble man is corrected by someone, he rejoices in spirit with the prophet, saying: Let the just man correct me in mercy and rebuke me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head. Of the proud, however, Scripture says: They have hated the one who rebukes in the gate. He who is truly humble loves the one who corrects him, but flees the flatterer.

The eighth mark of true humility is plainly shown when, after being corrected, he does not defend, deny, cloak, or excuse his fault, but confesses it with simple shamefastness. A truly humble monk, when he is corrected, does not murmur; he does not protest that he has exceeded the measure of equity, but immediately, falling to the ground before the judge, cries out most meekly: I have sinned; I confess my fault; I ask pardon; I promise amendment.

The ninth mark of monastic humility makes those who possess it mute in speech: sweet, modest, and not clamorous in voice. A monk whom you see clamorous and headlong in speaking you will judge not humble, but proud and rash. The humble man, however, speaks modestly and as if under silence, and is always found readier to hear than to speak before others. It is shameful for a monk to raise his voice too much while he speaks, since, crucified to the world, he is commanded to love silence.

The tenth mark of perfect humility is possessed by the one who is accustomed to interpret everything whatsoever he hears about his neighbor in the better part. For our Savior says in the holy Gospel: Do not judge, and you will not be judged. And again: With whatever measure you have measured, it shall be measured back to you. The apostle also says: Do not judge before the time, until the Lord comes, who will also illuminate the hidden things of darkness and manifest the counsels of hearts. Who are you, who judge another’s servant? To his own lord he stands or falls, not to you.

The eleventh mark of holy humility is that he despises no man in his heart on account of any excesses whatsoever, but sets all before himself by merit of sanctity. He also considers all who publicly offend to be better than himself, and therefore condemns no one. He always considers in his heart with himself what evil he himself has done; but he humbly weighs the good deeds of others as outweighing his own. Therefore he spurns no one, disparages no one, contemns no one, since he desires all to be saved in the Lord.
