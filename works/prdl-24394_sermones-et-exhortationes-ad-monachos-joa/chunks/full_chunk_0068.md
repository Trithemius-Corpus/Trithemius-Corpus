For if man had abstained as almighty God had commanded him, certainly through this obedience, in his innocence, he would have merited the enjoyment of eternal happiness. For it is established by the tradition of the Scriptures that man cannot be saved without the merit of free will; for although the grace of God works all things in all, nevertheless it will save no one without the cooperation of his own will, except only infants baptized in the name of the Trinity, and those who cannot have the free use of reason.

For just as free will accomplishes nothing toward salvation without the grace of the Savior, so the grace of God will not save an adult without the merit of a good will. But the first man, placed in paradise and having transgressed God’s commandment through intemperance, did not have the merit of obedience in a good will; therefore, wretched and unhappy, he was cast out. But the almighty mercy of God resolved from eternity to restore the one who had fallen through weakness, whom he knew to have been deceived by the envious serpent’s suggestion rather than by his own malice. He assigned this guilt to the passion and death of his only-begotten Son, to be born in time of a virgin, so that he who by his own rashness [unclear] might through the obedience of the mediator be restored to salvation.

For it was impossible that the human race, lost through disobedience, should come to the Creator’s mercy except through the obedience of such a mediator, who is God and man, in whom there is both the strength of power and the innocence of nature. For we were blind through ignorance and mute through silence. The mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus, came, appointed to enlighten every man coming into this world; he handed on the knowledge of God to mortals, and, by dying for the lost, set the greatest cause for love of the Redeemer. He instructed the understanding through faith, by which we know God; he stirred the affection through his passion, so that we might deservedly love him above all things. Therefore, if we love our Lord and Redeemer with complete sincerity, it is plainly fitting that we chastise our body for love of him.

For our abstinence will conduce to merit for us in two ways: namely, when by fasting through obedience we [unclear] the statutes of the fathers, and when, by abstaining through charity toward God, we show ourselves pleasing to our Redeemer, who for our sake endured death. Abstinence increases merit, commends obedience, bestows wisdom and learning, purges the affection, enlightens the understanding, grants victory against demons, perfects religion, obtains pardon for sins, subdues illicit motions of the flesh, brings effectiveness to prayers, and displays to one’s neighbor an example of virtue. Abstinence purifies the mind, adorns the soul with virtues, and heals the weak little body. He who loves abstinence is wise; and he who, by loving fasting, [unclear], is strong. [unclear] the dwelling-place of wisdom; but he who pursues banquets will die in poverty of soul. It is difficult for a man who passes his life in delights to become wise. Nature is content with few things, and the health of life is preserved in modest things. Long-continuing voracity has harmed many, and more have perished by drunken excess than by the sword. For habit seems to alter nature; and the longer it has grown strong, the more forcefully it now [unclear] in any matter.

Among the Helvetians there is a man named Nicholas, whose abstinence has turned the people of all Germany to admiration; and today, as you have heard, he is named in everyone’s mouth with the title of holiness. He was once a farmer in a certain Helvetian village, and in the rustic manner he was accustomed, among fourteen [unclear], to administer justice to disputing villagers. It happened at a certain time that he could not revoke a judgment of his associates which he considered unjust; and, fearing danger to the salvation of his soul, he left his house, wife, children, and all the business and goods of the world, and withdrew alone into a nearby wood for the love of God, in which he leads a solitary life. And behold, today is the twentieth year since he entered the solitude, and he has taken no human food. I speak of things most well known to all, nor do I think there is any man living in Germany who has not heard the fame of this miracle. But lest perhaps anyone in the future should believe that we have been deceived by pretence, we cite Helvetia, together with the bishop of the church of Constance, as our witness; they have observed his conduct and life most carefully.
