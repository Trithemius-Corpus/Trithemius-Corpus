*Fol. XLIII.*

promise. Adversities are present to us every day, by which we may be exercised; therefore, just as the cause for endurance is never lacking, so, I pray, may necessary patience always be present. We are oppressed by want and poverty of necessary things; we are burdened by the commerce of weak flesh; moreover the world tempts us in order to entice us; the devil tempts us in order to lead us astray. Among so many adversaries of our salvation, brothers, it is necessary that we keep watch in all patience and humility, since in this pilgrimage, as long as we live, we are placed, so to speak, in the midst of evils.

Our poverty ought to suffice us, because it is not the part of monastic religion to be exalted in temporal things, but rather to be humbled, so that monks, poor and imitating Christ, who was made poor for us, may deserve to glory with him forever in heaven. What if we see certain wicked men and sinners in the world abounding in riches? If good and just men are rich in this world, provision is made by God’s providence not for their strength but for their weakness, so that by benefits they may be summoned to better things. “I was young,” says the prophet, “and I have grown old, and I have not seen the just man forsaken, nor his seed begging.” “Cast,” he says, “your thought upon the Lord, and he will nourish you; he will not allow the just man to waver forever.”

The Lord Jesus knows what is fitting for the salvation of each one of his little servants, and therefore he distributes the gifts of the present life unequally according to the capacity of individuals. For not all things are fitting for all people; therefore, among the just in this world, with respect to temporal goods, some are poor, while others are wealthy. Concerning the poor the Lord Jesus says in the holy Gospel: “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” And he speaks through the prophet, saying: “Behold, I have refined you, but not as silver; I have chosen you in the furnace of poverty.” For it is not conducive to eternal salvation for all men to possess the riches of this world, but it is advantageous for certain persons, according as the Lord of all has foreseen to be more useful for each.

Concerning just and good rich men Solomon says in the book of Proverbs: “The blessing of the Lord makes men rich; affliction shall not be joined to it.” But if the wicked and reprobate abound in riches in this age, this ought not to move the good to indignation; rather, let them fear him who leaves no good unrewarded and no evil unpunished. For the wicked, whom he cannot reward in heaven because of their iniquity, he rewards in this life for their good deeds; and for the good, whom he nourishes here in poverty for righteousness’ sake, he preserves eternal rewards in heaven. But just as not all the poor in this world are just and good, so not all who are rich in temporal things in this life are wicked. The reward of the wicked on earth must rightly be borne without indignation, since not the love of God but justice has willed it to be present.

Therefore, just as the wicked have nothing in heaven, so you who have professed monastic poverty ought to have nothing on earth, but, in hope of that good toward which you are striving, you ought to endure most patiently whatever adversity befalls on the way. For, as Paul, the holy lover of Christ, says: “The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the glory to come, which shall be revealed in us.” Those who are rich in Christ should join mercy to humility; but those who are poor should take up patience, with innocence, for the love of God. The delight [unclear] of Christ is cast down neither by poverty nor raised up by riches, but, standing firm in himself, he rules his spirit calmly and cares nothing for transitory things. Thus he who is a true monk is neither lifted up by praises nor broken by reproach; he does not swell with an abundance of necessary things, nor is he contracted by poverty. He despises both joyful and sad things alike, since, as the prophet had said, “the sun shall not burn him by day, nor the moon by night.”

But we, dearest brothers, should make a virtue and a good will out of necessity, and from the hardship of poverty which we necessarily suffer we should obtain for ourselves a good and a great advantage; and this can happen only if, for the love of God, we bear this very poverty manfully and with a tranquil spirit. The deformed way of life of our predecessors has brought upon this monastery the want that we suffer; if we keep his commandments with a perfect heart, the mercy of almighty God will in time, without doubt, relieve it.
