*On the daily renewal of resolve.*

... the more zealous a monk has been in learning the Scriptures, the less is he inflamed by the fires of carnal pleasures. For although, according to the opinion of Saint Jerome, the monk seems to have the office not of one who teaches, but rather of one who laments, nevertheless, because he professes the warfare of Christ, it is fitting, indeed necessary, that he know the well-pleasing will of almighty God, to whose words and arms he has sworn. But just as a soldier cannot know the will of his lord when he is far absent except through messengers and letters, so the monk will not understand the commands of the eternal King except through sacred readings and sermons, the evangelical Scriptures.

The reading of the Scriptures opens the gate of salvation to those who do not know; it leads the studious reader by a straight path to the knowledge of God; it makes the lover of it despise all worldly things and love nothing other than true wisdom. But nothing is more shameful than for a monk in the spiritual life to be unwilling to make progress, and knowingly to wish to be ignorant of the will of God. Let no one flatter himself on account of his weakness or ignorance, since he who is ignorant will be ignored, and he who hates saving knowledge is deservedly condemned. When the Lord was about to send the preacher into all the earth, he forewarned him, saying: Be prudent as serpents and simple as doves, lest either you be tripped up by someone through ignorance, or you contrive injury against someone through deceit. For there is little difference in fault between being able to deceive and being deceived. I ask you therefore, dearest brothers, that you be willing to attend to the Rule of our lord father Benedict, which admonishes you in many places to toil very frequently in readings of the divine Scriptures. For in the Scriptures we are taught, instructed unto all piety, how we ought to live in this very brief pilgrimage of our sojourn, so that we may be able to please the Lord our God above all things. For it belongs to monks to despise the world and all the pleasures of the present life for the love of God; manfully to restrain the unlawful motions of the flesh; to devote themselves also to prayer, reading, and meditation on the Scriptures; and continually to raise the mind from earthly things to heavenly. Blessed is that monk who has not gone away in the counsel of the impious, and has not stood in the way of sinners, and has not sat in the chair of pestilential men, but meditates on the law of the Lord day and night, learning here in the Scriptures the wisdom of God, whose fruit, after the end of this pilgrimage, he is to receive: eternal life.

*On the daily renewal of holy resolve: [unclear] the fifth:*

You have heard, dearest brothers, the reading recited to us from the book of holy Job, in which he laments the misery of the human race, and the calamity of this life, together with its unstable brevity. “Man,” he says, “born of woman, living for a brief time, is filled with many miseries; he comes forth like a flower and is crushed, and flees like a shadow, and never remains in the same state.” “Behold,” he says, “the brief years pass by, and I walk a path by which I shall not return.” “All flesh will fail together, and man will return to ashes.” What, brothers, do you observe among these things, or what do you think? Is there any man among us who does not know himself to be mortal, who could truthfully say, “I shall not be moved forever”? Alas, my brothers, how blind we are, though we hold our eyes open; how forgetful and sluggish we are as travelers, we who know most certainly that we are going to die, and yet trust in the vanity of the present life as if we were not mortal. But who is the man who will live and not see death, who will rescue his soul from the hand of hell? Behold, Scripture says, all die, and like water we flow away upon the earth. Therefore remember, my brothers, that death does not delay, and all that has passed will be counted brief; because, as the holy lover of Christ Paul says, it is appointed for men to die once, and no one will be able to escape this common law of necessity. O if now we were daily to set before the gaze of the mind the hour of coming death, which each one of us must necessarily undergo shortly, and if we were so to order our life and conduct that we might always be found prepared, how happy and secure our departure could become. Did not the King of the living and the dead, Christ himself our Lord, wishing us always to be prepared for death, forewarn us in the holy Gospel, saying: Therefore you also must be ready, because at the hour when you do not think it, the Son of Man will come.
