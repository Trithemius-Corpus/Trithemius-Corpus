*Homily XVI.* For the root of despair is sloth, and the mother and nurse of all vices. For just as a garment both produces and nourishes the moth, so the most destructive plague of acedia both begets and fosters despair. Indeed this beast is accustomed to vex monks more than others, and especially those who lead a withdrawn life in solitude. Therefore, monks, you must take the greatest care against this enemy of the salvation of souls; you must fight not by fleeing, but by resisting bravely; and you must diligently consider that no one has attained the honor of holiness by torpor and sloth, nor has anyone deserved the kingdom of eternal happiness except by great and continual labor.

Therefore the mind must always be renewed by daily and fresh increases of virtues, and this perilous path of life is to be measured not by what has been traversed, but rather by what remains. Let no one promise himself security in this life, which is wholly called temptation, full of as many dangers as there are affections in the soul; but let each one beware that he who could become better from worse does not become worse from better. And especially on these holy days let us all take care, as far as we can, to become better, adding for ourselves, according to the counsel of our divine teacher, something to the customary burden of our service: namely special prayers, abstinence from food and drink, so that each one, beyond the measure imposed on him, may of his own will offer something to God with the joy of the Holy Spirit; that is, let him subtract from his body something from food, from drink, from sleep, from talkativeness, from security, and with the joy of spiritual desire await the holy Pasch.

For he is proved to be a monk of little religion at other times who on these holy days is not found more religious and, in the love of God, more fervent and more devout. Hence the divine Pope Gregory says in a certain homily: Lent must be observed with the greatest observance, so that fasting in it, on account of the Lord’s days which have been subtracted from abstinence, unless illness has hindered someone, is by no means relaxed, because these days themselves are the tithes of the year which we are commanded to offer to the Lord. Saint Ambrose also says: He keeps Lent who by fasting and watching ascends to Easter. For just as at the remaining time of the year to fast is a reward, so in Lent not to fast is a sin. For those fasts are voluntary, but these are necessary; those come from choice, but these from law. To those we are invited; to these we are compelled. And therefore he who passes over the appointed number by eating on one day is not accused as the violator of one day, but is charged as a transgressor of the whole of Lent.

On these sacred days all desires of the flesh must be restrained; the body must be chastised by abstinence, so that if perhaps at other times we have lived with too little moderation, we may in some way make recompense by this Lenten fast. Therefore, dearest brothers, let us follow the admonitions of our most holy father Benedict, and with all the diligence we can let us exercise ourselves in holy works, subtracting something each day from our body, for the love of God, from food and drink, so that we may prove ourselves true servants of God.

Next, however, our holy founder adds, saying: Nevertheless, this very thing which each one offers, let him suggest to his abbot, and let it be done with his prayer and will, because what is done without the permission of the spiritual father will be attributed to presumption and vainglory, not to reward. Therefore everything is to be done with the will of the abbot. The words of our holy lawgiver, brothers, are clear, lest anyone be able to have recourse to the veil of ignorance. For if everything is to be done with the will of the abbot, then without his permission absolutely nothing is to be done. Indeed, the more a good work is adorned by many virtues, the greater its merit before God must without doubt be. Therefore when a monk, by the license and blessing of his abbot, subtracts something of food or drink from his body for the love of God, he is to receive from the Lord not only the promised reward of eternal fullness for that abstinence itself, but also the kingdom for humility, and for the merit of holy obedience he will attain without end the fellowship of all the saints in heaven.

For the monk who, having once professed, has subjected his head to monastic institutions has resigned his own will to Christ in the hands of the abbot; and if thereafter he should again usurp it by his own judgment, he has already shamefully denied Christ. For what else is a monk who usurps the ownership of his will, if not an unjust professor of a thing that belongs to another? Therefore let the monk be humbly subject to his abbot for the love of Christ always in all things; let him presume nothing at all.
