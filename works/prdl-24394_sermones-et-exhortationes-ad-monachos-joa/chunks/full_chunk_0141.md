*On the nine steps of ascent.*

[Hasten?], my dearest brothers, to ascend this step of the spiritual ladder as quickly as possible, so that you may at some time be able to reach the summit of perfection. For if you, who have been placed under the obedience of monastic perfection, have advanced to this fourth step, you will now ascend the fifth without labor. For those who are placed on the fourth step do the good works of righteousness by their own will, since they have not submitted themselves, by vow or profession, to monastic constitutions. And in this one respect they are inferior to those who follow, because a good work is the more acceptable to God, the more it is commended by the merit of obedience.

Therefore those ascend the fifth step who guard themselves from sins with all their strength and, being placed under obedience, always remain strong and constant in their resolve of holiness. Loving God above all things with a pure mind, they do good works chiefly for love of him; and in their intention they neither fear punishments nor seek rewards. They humbly obey their prelates for the love of God, and in no way wholly depart from the purity of regular observance.

Among these are some whom the weakness of the flesh at times makes unstable in mind, and somewhat obstinate in their opinions against obedience. At times they try to defend their exercises and private little observances against the determination of their prelates, and to think themselves wiser than their rulers in the examination of spiritual matters. Flee this, O monks, because the devil lies hidden in ambush; and those whom he cannot conquer by open attack he strives to overcome by hidden contrivance. For the holier and more perfect a monk has been before God, the more readily he obeys his prelate without dispute. But if a perfect monk disdains to obey his abbot, though imperfect, in lawful and honorable matters, then surely he has lost the name of perfection. Therefore let each one be careful to apply himself to the pursuit of progress, so that he may presume to violate obedience in nothing. For Scripture says: Obedience is better than sacrifices, and to hearken is better than to offer the fat of rams. Whatever you have offered is another’s; but the truly obedient man offers himself.

Therefore the sixth step is especially fitting for those who, living in all purity of mind and body, love the Lord above all things; who do every good they can for the honor of God alone; who do not depart from the commands of their prelates. Great is the virtue of obedience, which doubles a good work done and, increasing the merit of the one who performs it, presents him to God like a tree with its fruit. Let not the words of men who despise obedience move you, my dearest brothers: those who prefer freedom of will to the subjection of monks, and set a good work done from liberty before the merit of obedience. For even if a monk obeys from the necessity of his profession, nevertheless the good will by which, for love of God, he bound himself to obedience commends this necessity in many ways. There is a necessity by which a man is compelled to do something, where the will does not consent but always resists. This necessity diminishes the praise of virtue, because the good work was never in the will. There is also another necessity, arising from the inward inclination of the mind, which increases and does not diminish the praise of a good work, because it kindles the will more intensely toward the work itself. There is a third necessity, which has regard to the end and does not compel the will. For if you wish to cross the Rhine by boat, a fitting necessity requires an instrument. It is therefore necessary for a monk, on account of the end, to do what the abbot commands, so that he may be able to fulfill the vow which he made for the love of God. Therefore, since that end is very praiseworthy, by which the creature subjects itself out of love to its Creator, the necessity of obeying in a monk does not diminish the praise of a good work, but makes it greater. O happy necessity of monks, which took its beginning from the treasury of a good will, and is ordered to the end which is God, the unchangeable good. It is of greater praise, of more excellent merit, and of more abundant reward to do a good work from obedience by reason of a vow than if it is done without a vow from the liberty of one’s own will. But the monk once offered himself entirely, and all his will, to God, and reserved absolutely nothing for himself. Whatever good he does under obedience is brought back to the end of the religious life he has undertaken, to be preserved for the love of Christ; to this no work after martyrdom is compared.
