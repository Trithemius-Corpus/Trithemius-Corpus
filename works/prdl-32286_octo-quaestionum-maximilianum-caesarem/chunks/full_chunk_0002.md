The angelic and the human intellect differ as any two opposites do, such as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, state and privation, beginning and end, unmoving and moving, substance and accident, truth and likeness, nature and art, health and infirmity, whole and part. For the angelic intellect is as act, light, knowledge, state, beginning, the unmoving, the immediate, simple substance, truth, nature, and the whole. But the human intellect is as potency, darkness, ignorance, privation, end, the moving, the mediate, the composite, accident, likeness, art, and part.

Hence whatever things belong to the angelic intellect from the beginning, these same things are present in the human intellect at the end, after it has laid aside the burden of flesh. For just as beginning and nature perfect the angelic intellect, so the end and art, after many exercises at last, raise the human intellect upward. Therefore between these two intellects there is great inequality from the beginning, although in the end they are at last made equal, when the human intellect will be as great by art as the angelic intellect surpasses it by nature.

Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect, as soon as it was created, from the beginning, without any action or motion of its own, immovably, substantially, and by nature knew all things that it could understand, and that, created in an instant, it came forth full and perfect in all knowledge. But the human intellect, since it was created in all ignorance and privation, and was destined for the body for its own exercise and by degrees, must by continual action, motion, and art come to the knowledge of God and of all things. The end will at last make it equal to the angelic intellect, changing its potency into act and bringing it forth from mere potency and possibility into essence; from empty it will make it full, from imperfect perfect, from deprived possessed of state, from dark luminous, from nocturnal diurnal, from lunar solar, and from earthly heavenly.

This can never happen while the mind is bound in the body, as the Christ-loving Paul says: “As long as we are in the flesh, we are away from the Lord.” To know God belongs to a pure, simple, and perfect intellect; but the human intellect, so long as it dwells in the body, is impure, mixed, unstable, and imperfect, and for this reason, without faith, wholly ignorant of the knowledge of God and of heavenly things.

The object of each intellect, both human and angelic, is the same: that highest and immutable good, God, the one principle of all things, to which they return by a natural desire for knowledge. But the angelic intellect, if compared with the human, is as nature is to art, and as simple intellect is to reason, which touches the principles of learning; but reason proceeds from principles to conclusions. Hence it is manifest that the angelic intellect immovably apprehends its proper object, to which the human intellect, through the angelic medium, after many exercises of its own, as has been said, at last and at some time makes ready an ascent for itself by reasoning.

The human intellect, however, is separated by the medium of angelic knowledge from the perfection of its natural object, and for that reason necessarily becomes mobile, returning through that medium to its own principle. For every motion is from an extreme through a medium to an extreme; but where there is no medium, the extremes are now immediate and near, and for that reason immovable. But the human intellect, placed as it were in a dark prison, as long as it is held in the body, is moved by sense and reason toward understanding, though confusedly and all too imperfectly, and therefore it suffers a medium between itself and God, which we have called angelic; for the corruptible body, as Wisdom 9 says, is a very great impediment to perfection, as a certain lover of wisdom said: “The body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things.” Therefore it was wholly necessary for us to believe God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible to know that superdivine and uncircumscribable majesty.
