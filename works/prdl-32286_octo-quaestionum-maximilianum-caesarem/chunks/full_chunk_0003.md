QUESTION ONE

Therefore it was wholly necessary for us to believe God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible for that super-divine and uncircumscribable majesty to be comprehended by us, who understand nothing without the ministry of the senses and the discourse of reason.

But God, existing far above every affirmation, remains subject neither to the senses of mortals nor to reason, as he is read to have said to Moses: “Man shall not see me and live.” Hence also the divine evangelist John says: “No one has ever seen God.” For since God is the first and everlasting intellect, most pure, most simple, most perfect, immovable, the supreme incomprehensible creator, immeasurable and uncircumscribable far beyond every affirmation, he could not be comprehended by the human intellect, which is impure, subject to composition, imperfect, movable, created, comprehensible, lowest, and established beneath the measure of affirmation.

But as for the question which seemed to arise from the first, whether God, since he is omnipotent, could have made himself knowable to the human intellect, as to the angelic, I say that God can do all things, which no one of sound mind has ever denied. We say that God cannot do those things which imply contradiction, because they are contrary to the order of the unchangeable wisdom that disposes the universe, just as the destruction of a whole thing is undoubtedly contrary to its construction at the same time, if I may speak of things wholly opposed.

For it was neither fitting, nor could it in any way have come to pass, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect; for if mortal man, by the guidance of human nature, comprehended the immortal God, then certainly he would have to be called no longer human or mortal, but an angel; and either man would be compared with God, or God with man. For so long as the angelic intellect intervenes, the human intellect cannot understand God. But it intervenes until the death of the flesh, when the mind, having obtained its natural freedom, will become wholly equal to an angel.

You now see, I believe, most manifestly, Emperor, that it was neither fitting nor possible that the human intellect should comprehend the divine, the created the creator, the temporal the eternal, the changeable the unchangeable, the weak the omnipotent, since of the finite to the infinite, as a certain philosopher says, there is no proportion. And therefore it is necessary for mortals to believe in the immortal God, whom by no manifest reasoning can we comprehend.

Although the order of the whole universe providently declares the author of all things, and we understand that nothing besides him was produced without a beginning, nevertheless what God is and in what manner he is, the human mind cannot grasp, since he is far above all that we can understand concerning him, or anything mortal whatsoever, and is far outside and above every affirmation, as holy Dionysius says. Indeed, he is neither soul nor sense, nor imagination or opinion, nor reason, nor intelligence; nor does he possess reason or intelligence; nor is he spoken of or understood; neither is he number nor multitude, nor order, nor magnitude, nor smallness, nor equality; neither likeness nor unlikeness; neither does he stand nor is he moved; neither does he act rest, nor does he have power, nor is he power; neither is he light, nor does he live, nor is he life; neither is he substance, nor age, nor time; nor is there spiritual contact of him; neither is he form, nor truth, nor kingdom, nor wisdom; neither one nor unity, nor deity or goodness; nor is he spirit, so far as we can know; nor is there for him the denomination of son or father, nor anything else among those things which are known to us or to anyone else in the world; nor is he any of those things which are not, nor any of those things which are; nor do beings know him as he is; nor is there speech of him, nor name, nor knowledge; neither is he darkness nor light, neither error nor truth; nor is there of him any affirmation at all, nor any negation. For he is above every affirmation, the perfect and singular cause of all things. And above every negation is his excellence, that is, of him who is simply absolved from all things and is above all things.

Therefore we say, as Dionysius says, that since as the cause of all things he is above all things, he is neither without substance nor without life, nor does he lack reason or mind; nor is he a body, nor figure, nor form, nor [unclear].
