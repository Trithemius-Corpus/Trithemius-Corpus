For a man’s work will repay him, and according to the ways of each he will restore to them. Therefore every person who rightly understands his own ignorance and weakness openly acknowledges that he is nothing in comparison with his creator. For God justly permits man to sin, both so that freedom of choice may be preserved, and so that the excellence of the great God may become known to men.

The second reason is that the inscrutable wisdom of God may become known to mortals, by which he knows how to draw good from our evils, when he makes one person’s sin become another’s merit for an increase in virtue. For if he did not permit evil to be done, who among us would understand the good? For God makes and orders the good; but evils he does not make, though, once they have been done by the impious, he disposes them so that they do not come about without order. Nor does the malice of men hinder the will of God, nor can man do anything that God does not permit.

The third cause for which the Almighty permits evil deeds to be done is that he may manifest his most merciful goodness toward those who have sinned and done penance, which he could in no way show if he did not permit the creature to sin. For if there were no sinner, neither could God be one who shows mercy. But since in many things we all offend, everywhere God finds something to forgive; everywhere the guilty man presents himself, in whom the gracious Creator may show his mercy. Hence the holy prelate Augustine says: If the disease were slight, the physician would be despised, not sought; if the physician were not sought, the disease would not be ended. Therefore where sin abounded, grace also abounded, which alone distinguishes the redeemed from the lost, whom the mass of perdition, drawn from the origin of creation, heaps together into one. Thus the mercy of the Lord is over all his works.

The fourth reason for divine permission is that the justice of the Creator may be declared upon those who have sinned and have not done penance; since if all acted well and no one sinned, God’s justice would have nothing to punish. Therefore, so that justice may have its place, the creature sins by divine permission. For the Lord is just, and he will repay each person according to the works of his will.

The fifth reason is the dignity of the human condition, because if God did not permit man to sin, he would seem worse off than brute animals, which, with no violence compelling them, freely follow the motions of nature. For although brute animals lack reason, nevertheless they freely have the motion of nature which they follow; in comparison with them man would seem to be of an inferior condition if he did not freely have free choice of the will. But God permitted man a will.
