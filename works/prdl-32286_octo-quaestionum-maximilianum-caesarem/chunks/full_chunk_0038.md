God, the maker of the universe, both understands all things and, distinguished in a singular manner, is not divided into many things, but remains in himself one indivisible being; by beholding him as the pattern of the whole universe and the cause of cause through causes, he contemplates the cause of all things, and by contemplating all things he cares for them and provides for them, directing each one to a good end by an everlasting order. Come now, most wise Caesar: in order that we may understand the divine providence that governs all things, let us use arguments, as briefly as we can, for our purpose.

Thus, if God’s action is his own proper intelligence, as no wise man fails to admit, since it is at once most eminent and most inward, then insofar as he acts, so far he surely understands, and conversely. But he acts in individual things, not only in great things but also in the very smallest; therefore he understands individual things. For the influence of the first being extends most widely through each thing that in any way exists, following in fixed orders; and the things that act after him are moved and act everywhere by his power. Either, then, God understands or does nothing outside himself, which is false; or, by acting on all things and on each thing, he also understands them, which is true. For they would nowhere come to be unless first, to speak in our manner, they were understood by God himself, who makes them, just as they are not made by the sun and yet not perceived by it. But because the almighty understands all things and each thing that he makes, there is no doubt that he disposes, rules, and orders all things that he has made, so that things not only have from him that they exist, but also that they are certain causes of other things. And so, if things have from God that they are causes, from this they receive the proper office of a cause, that is, to preserve and care for, according to their powers, what they have made.

Therefore, if all causes have this property from the first cause, insofar as they are certain causes, then rightly does that first cause itself have this goodness and natural providence: to care for all things and each thing that it has made, inasmuch as it is supremely the cause of them all and of each one. Indeed, unless he who makes them guards them one by one, the common things that are preserved in individual things are never preserved. Nor will he preserve the whole, which consists of parts, if the parts themselves are neglected. Therefore from the intention of the first and greatest common intelligence comes the universal distinction of things; but it itself is distinguished from others by itself, and distinguishes all other things among themselves by the rule of its own knowledge. Hence, just as God knows all things and each thing most distinctly, so also he most distinctly rules, governs, and cares for them.

I think, therefore, most merciful Emperor Maximilian, that it is clear and sufficiently shown that the providence of God, the highest ruler, embraces, disposes, rules, and governs all lower things and each of them; for otherwise, without the provident disposition of one who observes them, they could in no way maintain this most beautiful order that we see. Nor am I unaware how variously the wise men of this world once held opinions about providence. Some of them thought that this world was made not by providence but by a cause; and if their opinion were true, no place would be left for providence, but all things would necessarily drift, since the world could perish by the same cause by which it was made. Against their false opinion others taught that the world could neither have been made without reason, nor certainly continue to exist unless it were ruled by the highest providence of its maker. Hence Boethius, a most Christian man, said: “O you who govern the world by everlasting reason, creator of earth and heaven, who command time to proceed from eternity, and, remaining stable, grant that all things be moved.” And again he said: “The generation of all things, the whole progress of changeable natures, and whatever is moved in any way, draw their causes, order, and forms from the stability of the divine mind.” Others, however, thought that God cares only for incorruptible things, such as the heavenly things and the separated substance, and at the same time the species [unclear].
