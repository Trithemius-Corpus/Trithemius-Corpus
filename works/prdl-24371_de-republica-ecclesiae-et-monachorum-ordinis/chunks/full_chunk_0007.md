He advanced so far in regular discipline that, regarded as especially intimate with him before many others, he described the life of that man, after his death, in clear and elegant meter. Thus that most illustrious senator Cassiodorus, a man most learned in every respect, despising senatorial rank for love of God, became a monk in one monastery of the holy father, and finally abbot.

But what am I to do? The day will fail me if I wish to recount by their proper names all who, while our lawgiver was still living, adorned his order with wonderful learning and holiness. But in the succeeding time there was among monks the highest zeal for the Scriptures, and an inestimable love of learning in every branch of knowledge, even secular. For those who had come learned to conversion supplied the younger brothers with precepts for study. Hence it came about that they became most learned in every Scripture, because [unclear] dangerous in morals and deadly, one wishing to learn the Scriptures usefully should begin from humility and amendment of life. Thus our monks did in antiquity, who applied to the study of letters an unwearying cultivation of virtues.

Such was Bede, priest and monk in England; such was Alcuin, his disciple, a Levite; such were Boniface the martyr and Rabanus, archbishops of Mainz; such were all the monks truly learned. What shall I report about the other pillars of the Church? Gregory the Great, pope; Leander of Seville; Gregory of Tours; John of Girona; Caesarius of Arles; Hincmar of Reims; Haymo of Halberstadt; Aldhelm of the West Saxons; Radbod of Utrecht; Amalarius of Trier; Ansegisus of Sens; Remigius of Auxerre; Ratherius of Liège; Adelbold of Utrecht; Notger of Liège; Burchard of Worms; Guinomundus of Aversa; Alfanus of Salerno; Peter Damian of Ostia; Lanfranc and Anselm of Canterbury, bishops and archbishops, who published almost innumerable books and shone in our order at different times, no less in holiness than in knowledge of the Scriptures.

I pass over the abbots and monks of our order, once most learned servants of the Lord: Eugippius, Columbanus, Eutropius, Paul the Lombard, Regino of Prüm, Paschasius of Corbie, Smaragdus, Hermann the Lame, Rupert of Limburg, William of Hirsau, Rupert of Deutz, abbots, all of whom, learned and most holy, after the publication of many books, shone also with miracles in life. It would be a long matter if I wished to run through even only the names of those who in our order blazed forth as wonderful both in learning and in holiness.

Great devotion burned in them, an inestimable zeal for regular discipline, which never allowed them to be idle. Always in their studies there flourished a divine love, which, conceived from holy reading, called those keeping watch to prayer. Those who had exercised themselves more fully in the divine Scriptures, in the hours free from the service of God, applied themselves to expositions of the same; they composed books and various treatises, by which they called the studies of their brothers to the love of God. The other brothers did not have leisure for idleness, but after their prayers devoutly applied themselves to the work of their hands according to the rule. Those capable wrote books for the adornment of the library, and from sheets brought into fair order works published by others. Others skillfully bound written codices; others corrected them; others marked them out with red minium. All rejoiced to be [unclear] of holy labor. They were trained in the scholastic disciplines: some in grammar, others in dialectic, and others in other disciplines, each according to the force of his nature and talent. Once grounded in the elementary sciences, however, they were called to the theoretical Scriptures.

No place was given to idleness in the monasteries, with each one applying himself to his own labors and studies with the greatest fervor. They sent the little works which they had composed to emperors, kings, princes, and prelates of cathedral churches, by whose favor and authority they more safely came forth into the light for our edification. Thus Alcuin dedicated innumerable little works to Charlemagne; thus Rabanus to Louis; thus Berno of Reichenau to Aribo of Mainz and to Pilgrim, archbishops of this holy church of Cologne.
