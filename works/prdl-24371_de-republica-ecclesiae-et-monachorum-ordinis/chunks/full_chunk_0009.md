Behold, reverend fathers, we have briefly heard what the condition of our commonwealth is; it remains now for us to consider its remedy. For, descending from Jerusalem to Jericho, it has fallen away from its former beauty; it has fallen among robbers, because from the time of Henry IV it has lost more than five thousand monasteries from the order. I do not speak hypocritically, nor do I report uncertainties, but things evident which I have found in the writings of the ancients. Therefore the robbers who have stripped our commonwealth are, first, demons, who slay the souls of monks with the wounds of vices; second, greedy men, who plunder for themselves the monasteries of our order. Thus our commonwealth lies naked and torn, since it is seen to have been stripped within by its own, without by strangers. The priest and the Levite, seeing its desolation, pass by, because, despising our affairs, all seek the things that are their own. But our Samaritan, the Lord Jesus Christ, seeing the desolation of our order, moved by mercy, [unclear] bound up its wounds, when at various times he renewed it to regular observance. On the next day, bringing forth two denarii, namely love of God and of neighbor, or certainly the increase of spiritual and temporal goods, or understanding and action, he gave them to the faithful innkeeper, saying: “Take care of him,” etc.

The faithful innkeeper was Charlemagne the emperor, who first reformed our order when it had collapsed in Germany. An innkeeper was Berno, abbot of Cluny, under whose reformation there were fifteen hundred monasteries. Innkeepers were Richard of Fulda, William of Hirsau, and another certain abbot of [Hersfeld?], who, each at different times, were authors in the order of the Italian reform of Santa Giustina, which, persevering down to the present day, has been honored with many privileges by the supreme pontiffs. Innkeepers were Emperor Sigismund with his helpers, who began the Melk reform in Austria. Faithful innkeepers also were those who supplied the beginnings for the Kastl reform. Finally, most faithful innkeepers were Johannes Rode of St. Matthias at Trier and Johannes Hagen of Bursfelde, very illustrious abbots, who laid the foundations of our Bursfelde reform.

Innkeepers were the other pontiffs, princes, and many abbots, whose names we omit for the sake of brevity, who in diverse provinces, places, and times reformed our order according to their ability. Innkeepers also are you, O reverend fathers, who have assembled individually for the preservation of our commonwealth, who cultivate regular observance, who love the union of the Bursfelders. You, I say, are innkeepers; see to it that you are found faithful, you to whom the true Samaritan has entrusted the order, sick as it is. It is required of you that you pour into its wounds the wine of the holy Scriptures and the oil of devotion. For, as we said above, by these two things our order once shone forth in glory. Therefore, if you desire to reform the commonwealth, or even to preserve it in reform, devote yourselves to the study of letters and to holiness of life. Let none of you say: “What need is there for me, by studying, to run through many things, when few things can suffice for living well?” Believe me, fathers: for an abbot to live well, it does not suffice to know few things. For if few things sufficed, the holy fathers would have written so many volumes for the instruction of morals in vain. For an abbot must be learned in divine law; even if the study of letters conferred nothing else, nevertheless it is useful for the extirpation of vices, as blessed Jerome says: “Love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh.” Nothing so contemns the world; nothing so subdues the flesh and lust; nothing so kindles the love of God, as burning zeal and love for the Scriptures.

Therefore let no one excuse himself from holy study; let no one blame the lack of teachers: love conquers all things; charity can do all things; humility endures all things. If a teacher is lacking, O brothers, read the holy fathers for yourself in their volumes, [unclear]. Nor do I wish any monk to run about to schools, because in the monastery he will very well be able to learn thoroughly what he is commanded to know.
