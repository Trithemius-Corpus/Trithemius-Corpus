In proportion as it has departed over time from its first institution, it has, as it were, descended so many steps from Jerusalem to Jericho. Just as once the order of our commonwealth was deservedly exalted above all other orders, when it nourished men mature, zealous, and most learned, firm in faith, strong in hope, radiant with twin charity, pure in mind, outstanding in devotion, and adorned with the honourableness of all the virtues; men in whom there was no care for earthly things, no enticement of pleasures [unclear], but who, turned with their whole mind toward God, despised the world with its vanities. From these, because of their extraordinary learning and their inestimable holiness, men almost innumerable are read to have been taken up at different times to the summit of episcopal dignity; and the more they had been placed in a higher station, the more brightly they shone forth in virtues and holiness. What church would not seek a priest from the order of venerable Benedict? What people devoted to the Christian name would not desire a teacher from the order of monks? I have said these things not for the empty glory of our present commonwealth, but rather for useful confusion. For just as the life of the saints is set forth for glory to those who imitate it, so it is recited for judgment to the sluggish and idle. Hence I am especially afraid that, while I have praised the most holy former state of our order, I shall be compelled, though truthfully, to condemn the present one, more than the patience of many will bear. But let the half-learned be angry with themselves if they please; let those puffed up by false justice be driven into fury: I would by no means dare to compare the order of our own tempestuous age with the holiness of the ancients. For, as I had begun to say, our order once flourished with most holy men, who, living according to the purity of the Rule, became renowned for innumerable miracles. For while placed in the flesh, they overcame the vices of the flesh, since, despising the pleasures of the world, they loved God with their whole heart. They were always burning in the love of God; constant in prayer and devotions; profound in meditation; lofty in contemplation; uniform in holiness of morals. In their way of life there shone forth [unclear]; in their action, purity; in their thought, the integrity of mind. They were devout and most holy men: poor in spirit through contempt of worldly things, continent through frugality, obedient through humble subjection, and rich in every virtue. Founded upon the firm rock, they were humble in prosperity and constant in adversity. Of them that saying of the apostle Paul is most aptly understood: They went about in sheep-skins and goat-skins, needy, distressed, afflicted, of whom the world was not worthy; wandering in deserts, in mountains and caves, and in dens of the earth. The integrity of the life of monks then deserved to be exalted, when by word and example it converted many from iniquity to justice. Of such monks indeed the world is now unworthy, for it spurns the love of God, casts away truth, and despises knowledge of the Scriptures. The Church too flourished in learning and holiness together with our commonwealth, while it received none to the rule of souls except the learned, and admitted none except the devout. For devotion does not suffice for the rule of the Lord’s flock if knowledge of the Scriptures is lacking. But now our monks and abbots are neither devout nor learned, and therefore they are judged unworthy of the governance of churches. What if I were to say that the commonwealth of the Church is in this tempestuous age unworthy of learned and holy rulers? For I think there is no one who could rightly be angry with me on that account. For Scripture says of God, the supreme ruler: He makes a hypocrite reign because of the sins of the people. Rare in our times are prelates of churches who are at once learned and devout. Why? Because they are chosen not by the Holy Spirit, but by men. And so it is. But enough of this; shall I proceed to what remains of our subject? I remember often having said, reverend fathers, that two things above all made our order glorious: namely, the learning of the Scriptures and holiness of conduct. Of holiness we have briefly made mention; it remains now that we say a few things about learning in the Scriptures. And, to begin from the origin of our commonwealth: from the beginning of our order down to the times of King Henry IV, as I have found from the catalogue of the ancients, many most learned men were distinguished in it. For while Saint Benedict was still living in the flesh, many very noble and very learned young men submitted their necks to his Rule, who were considered most skilled not only in the divine Scriptures but also in secular letters.
