But where now is such study among monks? Where is the abbots’ love for the divine Scriptures? For shame. Now he is called great and useful in the order, not who cultivates and loves the knowledge of the Scriptures, but who soils himself in gathering perishable riches. O fathers, O brothers, our commonwealth did not once flourish thus; our order did not grow in ancient times thus. But abbots devoted to the world, friends of monastic vanity, are accustomed to answer and excuse themselves from study: “We gladly wish to remain in our simplicity, because we see that even learned men go through the precipices of vices into perdition.
Deep knowledge will not be required of us at the Last Judgment, but a good life.” O fools and senseless men, who do not hide your sloth, but, to your greater confusion, openly show yourselves enemies of the divine Scriptures. It is well said in the proverb that knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man. You lovers of the world, you followers of vanity, who foolishly cast away from yourselves the study of the Scriptures, why do you not love it in others? Or do you not know that all Scripture, divinely inspired, is useful for teaching, for arguing, for rebuking? What will you teach your monks, who are ignorant of the Scriptures? How will you be able usefully to accuse those who offend, when you do not know how to adduce the testimonies of the Scriptures? Remain, then, in your simplicity, not upright; remain in asinine humility; refuse to understand, so that you may act well; refuse to heed the Rule of Saint Benedict; refuse to withdraw from your folly. Do you know also what our lawgiver says in the Rule? I know that you know. “It is fitting,” he says, “for the abbot to be learned in divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth speech.” An unlearned prelate is a mute dog. A soul not loving the Scriptures of God is an empty vessel. O you ignorant abbots and enemies of saving knowledge, who spend the day in obscene gossip, who drink wine to satiety, who pursue earthly gains, who play stupidly at the gaming-table: what will you answer almighty God and your lawgiver Benedict, when they require from you the care of their sheep? For you are the men who, lying in the deformity of your former life, confound our commonwealth, spurn discipline, and scatter the order. O holy religion, O venerable mother, O commonwealth of monks, once honored, to what misery you have come. Where now is the adornment of your former beauty? Where the holiness of life? Where the saving study of the Scriptures? Where the learned and holy abbots? Where the monks devoted in regular observance? Behold, you hold more than ten thousand monasteries under the order, of which we believe scarcely a thousand to have been preserved in some observance of the Rule. For your abbots, having despised regular discipline and neglected the care of souls, are given over to the vanities of the world, intent upon earthly riches, submerged in the exultations of the flesh. For as a certain excellent versifier, still living, says: “Our abbots and the prelates of churches, neglecting the worship of those above and spurning the command of the Thunderer, indulge Bacchus and serve Venus. They furnish sacred altars with gold and with golden coins. With gold they pour libations of wine; with gold the ceilings gleam. They cover whores with gems; they set feasts before dogs. Adorned with the spoils of the needy, and rich from plunder, veiled in success, they laugh at those above and at Acheron alike.” They have no care for the salvation of souls, no love of God, no reverence for the saints, no practice of virtues. Would that, fathers, our observance did not have such abbots; would that our commonwealth knew no such rulers. But I fear, indeed, if it is right to say so, I know, that among our own also such men are found, who are reformed in name, but in truth are deformed. There are, I say, abbots glorying in the name of reform: ambitious, vain, proud, given over to the pleasures of the flesh, who, impatient of seclusion, consider every occasion suitable by which they may migrate here and there at very great expense.
