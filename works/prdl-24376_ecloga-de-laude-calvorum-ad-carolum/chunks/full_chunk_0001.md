Hucbald "of Saint-Amand."

*Eclogue in Praise of the Bald, addressed to the emperor Charles the Bald, with the Life of Hucbald from Johannes Trithemius's* On Ecclesiastical Writers.

[Mainz], [ca. 1496].

[Library stamp: BIBLIOTHECA REGIA MONACENSIS — Royal Library, Munich.]

*A marvelous poem in praise of the bald, by the monk Hucbald, to the bald emperor Charles.*

From the book *On Ecclesiastical Writers* of Johannes of Trittenheim [Trithemius], abbot of Sponheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict:

Hucbald, monk of Elnon, of the order of the holy father Benedict, a Gaul by nation, was a man supremely learned in the divine Scriptures as in secular letters; in music, poetry, philosophy, oratory, and the other humane arts second to none in his time, as the many small works he elegantly composed attest — of which I have so far found only those set down here. To the emperor Charles the Bald, a marvelous work of one hundred and thirty verses, every word of which begins with the single letter C — thus: *Carmina clarisone caluis cantate camene*, "Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald" — which he entitled the *Eclogue in Praise of the Bald*, one book. *On Music*, one book. *The Deeds of Saint Lebuin*, one book. *Letters to Various Persons*, one book. He also wrote the lives of many saints in ornate eloquence, and composed chants in their honor of sweet and regular melody. He flourished in the time of the emperor Charles the Bald, in the year of the Lord 880.

*Here begins the eclogue of Dom Hucbald, monk of Elnon, of the Order of Saint Benedict, to the emperor Charles the Bald.*

A crack-brained man once sang songs of abuse, attempting to carp at the bald:  
now let the bald man be celebrated in song.  
Take note, all of you, of this poem by a man of conspicuous brilliance.

*The prooemium, in which the Camenae are summoned to the praise of the bald.*

Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald!  
I shall try to dress the bald in a worthy song  
and to confound the curls on the curling neck.  
Let the skilled Camenae join in bright canticles;  
let them praise the bald — praise them, clear voices, against the slander —  
those who try to carp at the bald with crackling cackle.  
Let the cause of the bald ascend the summit of heaven.  
Silence, all you long-haired with your clotted locks,  
who carp at every balding man in crack-brained song:  
let harmonious songs be sung in concert for the bald.

*That baldness comes upon certain men as a presage of the future, and upon whom, and how it is seen to arrive. Chapter 1.*

Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald!  
When the crown takes shape at the nape of the head,  
it seals the bald man as a sworn fellow of the clergy,  
soon to capture, Christ granting it, a shining crown.  
As he plucks the hair from his head, he plucks the purse from his heart;  
with his heart he beholds the Creator, with his body, heaven,  
longing to reach the citizens of heaven by his devotion.  
He condemns all sins together with their perishing cares;  
craving with eager craving the ascent of heaven  
and the concord of the heaven-dwellers, he praises the Creator of all.

*That the bald are cantors, abbots, and teachers, and bishops too, and priests. Chapter 2.*

Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald!  
The tuneful bald exult together with ringing cry  
and keep their choirs in motion, dancing chaste rounds,  
all of one accordant body, conformed to their head.  
The bald man strives to build pleasing folds for the clergy;  
the horn is caught up, and the heavenly trumpets sound.  
They confect the chrism, bright with the name of Christ;  
they gather the chrism-sprinkled flocks into one column;  
in concord and with care they celebrate the banquet of the Supper,  
sating Christ's table-fellows with His flesh and blood.

*That the bald are kings and emperors, and consuls too, lawgivers, and judges. Chapter 3.*

Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald!  
The bald reign together; illustrious, they mount the heights,  
conspicuous, well-groomed, a golden crown at the neck;  
clement in their judgments, they frame decrees for their clients.  
Curly-locks, beware: the bald condemn crimes.  
It stands agreed that a bald man founds the censor's censure.  
Prancing fop, believe it: the stout-hearted bald man is one to fear.  
Only a blind man reckons the balding blighted in body.  
You are the blinded one: cease carping at the bald man's baldness —  
cease, you balding mocker; cease your gnawing; cease!

*That the bald are commanders of armies, and themselves warriors both learned and strong. Chapter 4.*

Sing your songs, clear-sounding Camenae, for the bald!  
The bald lead columns famous in combat;  
the bald fight back, crested with the helmet's cone;  
swift, they wheel and shake whole squadrons amid the slaughter;  
they seize the curls and pound the long-haired under heel;  
they run them through with the spear-point — slashed by the flashing blade.  
Necks fall; the bald raise the victory-chant with a shout.  
Roused to the contest, the bald man is cunning in battle:  
he tramples, he strikes, he forces the long-haired to yield;  
he takes captives, and pens the captured by the neck.

*Readings.* The English follows the 1496 print, checked line by line against the Bavarian State Library scan. Every word of the poem begins with the letter c; several apparent OCR errors are the print's own. The prooemium's fifth line ends "crimine cluras" (so the print; "cluras" is corrupt, perhaps for "claras"); in chapter 4 the print's "curros" is evidently for "cirros" ("curls"), and its "capros" ("goats") in the final line for "captos" ("the captured") — though "goats" would not be beyond this poet. The epigraph's "Conatus" is the participle ("attempting"), not a name.
