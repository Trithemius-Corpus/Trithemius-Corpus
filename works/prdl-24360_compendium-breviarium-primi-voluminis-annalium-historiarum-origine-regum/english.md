With grace and Privilege of His Imperial Majesty.

Maximilian, by the favor of divine clemency elected Emperor of the Romans, ever august, and King of Germany, Hungary, Dalmatia, Croatia, etc., Archduke of Austria, Duke of Burgundy, Brabant, etc., to the honorable and devout John Trithemius, Abbot of St. James the Greater at Würzburg, beloved to us: imperial grace and every good thing.

Since you have humbly explained to us that you have composed various editions of both ancient and modern authors, collected not without great labor, wakefulness, and diligence, which you desired with all your mind to have brought into the light; and since Master Johannes Haselberg of Constance, beloved to us and to the Holy Roman Empire, has offered himself with a most ready mind to be the agent of this matter, and for that reason has procured at great expense the formes by which he may print the aforesaid works: We therefore, desiring that provision be made for the indemnity of the aforesaid Master Johannes and for the adornment of your honor, lest your works, composed by you with great labors and wakefulness, lie hidden as if covered with rust, but rather that they shine forth more and more day by day, have therefore given and granted to the aforesaid Master Johannes Haselberg of Constance full power and authority in every respect, just as by the tenor of these presents we give and grant it.

We decree that he may print, and sell when printed, wherever he pleases and according to his will, whatever of treatises and little works has been published by you, the aforesaid Trithemius, Abbot of St. James the Greater at Würzburg, and has been reviewed and approved by the honorable Conrad Peutinger, doctor and our councillor, faithful and beloved to us. And we forbid all and singular chalcographers and printers of books, of whatever condition, under the penalty written below, strictly commanding them not to dare or presume, throughout the whole Roman Empire and in our hereditary lands, to print, publish, or, when printed by others, publish, buy, and sell such works, or similar ones, for ten years after they have been published by the said Master Johannes.

Moreover, we command all and singular our faithful subjects, and those of the Holy Roman Empire, beloved to us, of whatever estate, condition, dignity, or office they may be, whether ecclesiastical or secular, under the penalty of our most grave indignation and that of the Holy Roman Empire, and of ten marks of pure gold, to be applied, as often as action is taken contrary to this, one part to our fisc and the other part to the aforesaid Master Johannes Haselberg, that they assist the same Master Johannes of Constance with their favor and aid in their places and lands, and not permit the said works and treatises to be printed, or those printed elsewhere to be sold. Rather, they shall immediately declare in our name that all persons presuming to do anything against this our grace and prohibition have incurred the sentence of such indignation of ours and of the Holy Roman Empire, and shall execute our will expressly stated in this matter.

In witness whereof these letters have been furnished with the impression of our seal on the back. Given in our town of Innsbruck on the tenth day of the month of November, in the year of the Lord one thousand five hundred fourteen, in the twenty-ninth year of our Roman reign, and the twenty-fifth of Hungary.

By the king himself.

At the command of the lord Emperor himself.

N. Ziegler,

*The Letter of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St James, formerly of Sponheim, to the most reverend father in Christ and prince, lord Laurence, from the noble and most ancient family of the Bibra, bishop of the distinguished church of Würzburg and sole duke of Eastern Francia, concerning the compendium of the first volume of the annals on the origin of the Franks*

To the most reverend father in Christ and most excellent prince, lord Laurence, bishop of the noble church of Würzburg, sole duke of Eastern Francia, Johannes Trithemius, unprofitable, abbot in name only of the poor little monastery of St James the Apostle in the suburbs of the aforesaid city of Würzburg, offers ready service in all things, no less voluntary than owed by right. Most reverend father, illustrious prince, gracious lord, I have written, as your most reverend lordship knows, three large volumes, of no small labor, on the origin, progress, and particular deeds of the kings and princes of the people of the Franks, taking the beginning of the narrative of the succession of kings from the four hundred fortieth year before the birth of Christ, at which time, after Antenor, the great king of the nation, had been killed by enemies, his son Marcomer succeeded to the kingdom and led his people, namely our forebears, from the borders of Scythia into the borders of Germany and Saxony, to the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is. In these three volumes I have encompassed a period of one thousand nine hundred fifty-four years, in which the kings of the Franks are numbered, succeeding one another in proper order, from that same Marcomer already mentioned down to the twenty-ninth year of the emperor Caesar Maximilian Augustus: one hundred and three. There are many collateral kings, outside the direct genealogical line, who are excluded from this number of kings. Through the whole first volume, however, I have continued the successions and deeds of the kings of the Franks down to King Pippin exclusively, through one thousand one hundred ninety years, in which there were sixty-one kings succeeding one another in a direct line. To Pippin succeeded Charles, surnamed the Great, his son; then Louis the Pious. When he died, his sons divided the kingdom of the Franks into several parts. When they too had departed this life, their grandsons again divided the kingdom of the Franks in such a way principally into two kingdoms, of which one is called, is, and shall forever remain Germanic, while the other is Gallic, and neither is subject to the other. And this division is as follows.

*Compendium on the origin of the Franks*

With the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have written, not without very great labors, three volumes of our annals, of no small size, in which, from the origin of the race of the Franks down to the twenty-eighth year of Caesar Maximilian Augustus, we have arranged a fuller history, distinguished, that is, by the following sequence of division. In the first volume, indeed, we described both the origin of the aforesaid race of the Franks and their kings, beginning from Marcomirus, who began to reign in the four hundred thirty-ninth year before the birth of Christ; and after the death of his father, whom the Goths had killed in war, he reigned for twenty-eight years, and was the first to lead the people from the mouths of the Danube into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine. This people, sprung from the offspring of Trojans once fugitive from captured Troy, while it was still among its own fellow citizens, had the Trojan name; after this, dispersed among various nations and wandering, it often changed both its settlements and its name.

For at first, with the Trojans, they were called Trojans; afterward, among the Scythians, they were also named Scythians. But when they came into the borders of Germany, for a time they were called Sicambri; most recently they were called Franks from Francus their king. Yet the Sicambri could not at once obtain from the neighboring peoples, and especially from the Romans and Gauls, to whom their power was always suspect, that they should be called Franks, until at last, enlarging their kingdom and their name, they powerfully subjected to themselves the Romans, together with Italy and all Gaul. And because they lived with the Germans, they were more frequently named Germans than Franks by writers of deeds done. Thus the whole period of the first volume of our annals contains one thousand one hundred eighty-nine years, from Marcomirus down to King Pippin.

The second volume of our annals takes its beginning from German affairs, from the seven hundred fiftieth year of the Lord’s birth, when Pippin, mayor of the palace, was elected king of the Franks, Childeric having been shorn as a monk. And it contains the period of years from that point down to the eighth year of Richard, king of Germany, who succeeded William amid the dissension of the princes: five hundred fifteen years, as is clear to one counting the sequence of the order, in which the history of the German kingdom is woven.

The third volume of our annals takes the beginning of its annotations from the eighth year of King Richard, which was the one thousand two hundred sixty-sixth year of the Lord’s birth. We continue it to the twenty-eighth year of King Maximilian, that is, the one thousand five hundred fourth year of the Lord’s birth.

Therefore, as we are about to write the epitome of the first volume of our Annals, a compendium or breviary from the sure consideration of our mind, we warn the reader not to carp at the work before he has prudently examined it. It is the manifest mark of a rash man to judge what he does not know, and to suppose that no one can know what he himself perhaps does not know. I know that many have written various and diverse things on the origin of the Franks; some of them contend that the people was indigenous, while others say that it came from some city of the Sicambri, I know not what. We believe that no one can either distinguish or reconcile their diverse opinions unless Hunibald’s compilation has illuminated him. For he flourished as a sound historiographer of the Franks in the times of King [Clovis?], whom Saint Remigius, bishop of the Romans, baptized, in the five-hundredth year of the Lord’s nativity; and after Doracus the philosopher, Wasthald the historian, and many other very ancient writers of deeds, he wrote a distinguished work, which he divided into eighteen books. In the first six he traces the earliest origin of the Frankish people from the destruction of Troy down to the death of King Antenor, whom the Scanzians, Goths, and Suevi or Suedi killed near the mouths of the Danube, namely in the four-hundred-fortieth year before Christ’s nativity. In the other six books he includes the period from the death of the aforesaid King Antenor down to Faramund, king of the Franks, the forty-third of the second order. From Faramund the remaining six books continue down to the last year of [Clovis?], the first Christian among the kings of the Franks, who died in the five-hundred-fourteenth year of the Lord’s nativity. And because we have written more fully about all these matters in the first volume of our Annals, the nature of the name “breviary” demands that here we aim at brevity. Here ends the prologue.

*The breviary of the first volume of the Annals of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James of Würzburg, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins succinctly.*

In the four-hundred-fortieth year before the nativity of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ from Mary, the ever-undefiled virgin, when the peoples who were afterward called Franks, but before that Scythians, and still earlier Trojans, were settled at the maritime borders of Scythia, not far from the mouths of the Danube, King Antenor ruled. Against him a most savage nation, numerous beyond number, coming out of the Scanzian islands, which are now called Gothic, wore down his people by frequent incursion.

Joining weapons with weapons and hands with hands, Antenor, now defeated, now victor, was at last killed by the Goths in battle, together with many of his men, in the aforesaid year, as we have said, 440 before the birth of Christ. His son Marcomer succeeded him in the kingdom.

*Marcomir, the elder son of the slain King Antenor, appointed in his father’s place, reigned for twenty-eight years.* He began almost in the same year in which his father was killed. He was a high-spirited, brave, bold, and warlike man, who avenged his father’s death upon the neighboring Goths by frequent incursions, although he seemed far inferior to the Goths in number of fighters and in strength. Moreover, in the fourth year of his reign he held counsel with the chief men of the kingdom as to how either the Goths might be kept from the kingdom, or, if this could not be done, how he might otherwise provide for the people, lest it perish.

At last it pleased all who were rightly wise, since they could in no way withstand so immense a multitude of Goths, to change their dwelling-places by the will of the gods. Therefore, when the ministers of their gods had been summoned, the elders commanded that they should be stirred up by the divine powers as to which direction they were ordered to go. They prepare rites suited to prophecies; they offer sacrifices, composed by art, for summoning infernal powers. A priest stands in the midst, raving before the altar of Jupiter; he twists his head, utters verses in a murmur, gnashes artfully with his teeth, bent out of the ordinary manner, with his fingers he twists his arms together with his hands, brandishes them, and, flying about, bends himself back in like manner; to one seeing him sane he seems foolish, and more like a madman than a man. At last, when the divine power had been summoned by the chant, it gave back a prophecy to Menolpus as he prayed, and, beginning thus, thundered forth: “Marcomer, prince of Jupiter, the divine power bids you be calm, and not to fear the chances of the long journey, which stretches toward the west after your brothers to the mouths of the Rhine, where Brutus holds the back, the Trojans the face, [exulrophaea] at rest. Each is your brother by nature, by nation a faithful friend. Go, with swift course take possession of the dwellings prepared, to be extended by the will of the father’s divinity into a third part of the world.”

That same night King Marcomir, in his fatherland’s incantation, called upon the divine power, singing beforehand with a murmur the one whom from her sagacity they named Aliruna as she prophesied. He compared the monster. He confirmed by the authority of the Sibyl the prophecy of the priest, and ordered the people immediately to be led across to the place where, the offspring having multiplied exceedingly, by the foretokening vision it would remain stronger.

Marcomir beheld a three-headed divine power standing before him, of one [unclear] in eternity. He heard the particular prediction of each head, and saw a distinct [unclear] set above each one. At last it will stand divided in two, when the eagle has been joined as a friend to the lion, and the toad has held the left-hand part of the eagle in ambush. King Marcomirus asked Alruna what the meaning of the prophecy was. She said to him: It is the ancestral god, O king, who accompanies you and your people; informed by his predictions concerning the future for your people and for the kings who will be born from you, you should know from this that you must migrate as quickly as possible toward the farthest lands of the lion, toward the west. The lion designates the people who dwell on this side of the river Rhine, sprung from the ancient stock of the Trojans.

On the other side of the river, however, dwells the people of the toad, inhabiting a wide and fruitful region. The other, third people, designated by the eagle, are the Romans, whose pride labors to subject the whole world to its dominion. Therefore go with your people toward the west, and you will dwell between those two peoples, of the lion and of the toad, between the sea and the Rhine. A reason pressed the Saxons, on account of which they gladly consented to their Scythian brothers: namely, because they greatly desired to set them between themselves and the Gauls as a wall and bulwark.

At last, after many labors and innumerable difficulties, King Marcomirus, with his dukes and the whole people, came into Germany; and they were received kindly by the Saxons as brothers, and received the dwellings they desired between them and the Rhine, namely that province in which those now dwelling there are called the Western Frisians, and also the Guelders and the Hollanders: around that region, namely, where the Rhine flows into the sea by three mouths. He entrusted it to his brother Sunno to guard, and he brought in by ship peoples of his own race who would inhabit and cultivate it.

But when the Gauls were taking ill the incursion of a new and newly arrived people into the borders of the Hollanders, and were harassing it with various attempts, King Marcomirus, in the twenty-fourth year of his reign, gathered a great army, both of his own men and of Saxons, crossed the Rhine by ship, entered western Gaul, and, devastating everything with sword and fire, came as far as the Meuse. King Antenor had a wife named Cambra, who was the daughter of Belinus, king of Britain. She was a woman more beautiful than the daughters of the whole kingdom, and of such prudence that the king and the princes of the Franks were always accustomed in all things to obey her counsel no less than that of some divine power. The customs of the people, still excessively rough with Scythian savagery, she rendered more cultivated by laws; she taught the nobles to build cities and castles. There she instituted the adornment of the forehead; she gave women the knowledge of sowing flax and committing hemp to the earth, and of weaving and preparing them for clothing and varied use. She composed laws; she pronounced justice. She presented herself as a seer, a priestess of Diana, and an Aliruna.

And since she was a woman in sex, but bore the mind of a king and priest, she became a laughing-stock among the Saxons in a proverb: whenever they heard anyone, beyond the ordinary, doing or saying something rather prudent, as though he wished to imitate the prudence of Cambra, they would rebuke him and say, “Look, camer.” In their language this is said: “Si camer,” as though they were saying, “Do you too know the prudence of Cambra?” Thus with time the proverb took root, so that the two latest words, that is, the adverb and the noun, “si” and “camber,” yielded a single word, which as a whole makes Sicamber.

King Diocles, however, throughout all the days of his reign, stood vigorously in conflict with the Gauls; meanwhile there was scarcely a year in which he did not harass them with some incursion or battle.

In the twenty-sixth year of his reign, King Basanus, after many battles bravely fought with various nations round about, wishing to show himself pontiff to the gods, renewed their sacred rites with much superstition. He appointed several priests, highly skilled in the worship of the divine powers, curious in the courses of the stars, in prophecies, in foreknowledges, in the interpretation of dreams, educated in ethical and physical doctrines after the manner of the ancients, and also in songs, in the histories of ancient kings and heroes, and of the race only of his own, very well practiced in composing and singing in both languages. Among them Heligastus, son of Theocalus the priest, once surpassed the rest in talent and practice in writing. He was in charge of educating the sons of dukes and nobles, and described the deeds of heroes in verse.

Hunibaldus, historiographer of the Franks, writes many marvelous things about this man. Unless they are granted to have been performed by the artifice of some demon, they should be judged to have been fabricated by some writer rather than carried out by a man devoted to idols. He foretold many future things to the Sicambri. He made Jupiter visible to the priests, brought men’s secret plans into public view, and disclosed to King Basanus the hidden counsels of enemies in difficult causes and affairs. Nothing at all seemed to escape him, even what the enemies had discussed against the Sicambri in the most secret counsel. This matter instilled security in his friends, but despair in his enemies.

For King Basanus, together with all the princes of the kingdom, depended wholly on the counsel of Heligastus; and what he ordered, all did, but whatever he forbade, no one dared to attempt. At his command the Sicambri met the enemy, and if he commanded the contrary, they remained peacefully at home. As often as he foretold victory for the Sicambri, they conquered; as often as he foretold otherwise, they were conquered. While he lived the Sicambri prospered in all things; whenever their name was heard, it terrified their enemies and made them fearsome to all round about.

But afterward, when he had completed thirty-two years in his office, and on a certain solemn Thursday, standing in the midst of the priests, hurling thunderbolts in the temple and troubling the upper air with his voice, he was resounding with frenzies in the manner of one prophesying, he suddenly disappeared; and, vanishing completely, no one was ever found who could know where he had gone. The Sicambri, thinking that he had thus become a god, proclaimed that he had been taken into marriage with Diana, and decree sacred rites for him every year.

In the thirty-third year of his reign Basanus moved his army to the upper parts of the river Rhine, against the people called Tegarania, which dwelt toward the south between the Saxons and the Mainz people on this side of the Rhine, under the protection of the Treveri, having its own petty king named Thaborinus. King Basanus killed him together with many others, and subjected his whole region to his dominion. These things happened 251 years before the birth of Christ. The place in which the aforesaid King Thaborinus of the Tegarania fell was called Mount Thabor from his name; it is about four miles from the confluence. There Basanus, king of the Franks, built a very strong castle, which, in perpetual memory of the victory, he called Mount Thaborinus; but by a corrupted word it is today called Montabaur by the inhabitants.

I do not know what I should bestow on these little tales: indignation or laughter? Whether he was taken away by trickery or by art remains uncertain. But I think this one thing was the cause: the desire, in a man undoubtedly removed either by his own wit or by the spontaneous will of a demon, of obtaining the estimation of divinity. For, as Amerodagus, philosopher of the Sicambri, writes, King Basanus was most eager for divinity. He cultivated justice. He loved his people. He laid low the enemy. But he vanished, or, as I should more correctly say, withdrew himself, as Amerodagus is the authority, from the public assembly of the nobles, as we have said, in the thirty-sixth year of his reign, and in the year 248 before the birth of the Lord. The elder son of Basanus, succeeding in the kingdom, just as his father had ordained with the consent of the princes, reigned eighteen years. In the first year of his reign, as soon as the Gauls learned that Basanus had vanished, whom they themselves did not doubt was dead, they sent envoys to Clodomirus and demanded back from him all the land between the Meuse and the Rhine, and all around in the circuit, which the Sicambri, at their first entry into the territories on this side of the Rhine, had violently usurped for themselves against justice. If he should scorn to restore it, they threatened that they were prepared to recover everything by the sword, whether he sent back a negative reply through the appointed envoys, or answered nothing at all.

But King Clodomirus, as though he were going to hold counsel with his princes about the urgent matter, prudently and carefully delayed the envoys of the Gauls and kept them with him for a rather long time; and, taking the greatest care that no secret of the design in his mind should come to their knowledge, he ordered his servants to feed them abundantly and arrange for them to have pleasant days. With the consent of all the dukes and nobles of the Sicambri, confirming the friendship on silver tablets, according to the ancient custom of the people, they established the conditions of the agreements in this way: that the Sicambri and the Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians, under their distinct kings and princes, should thereafter be regarded as one people, like brothers and kinsmen, and should remain in it; and war that whatever threatened the kingdom of one of these, from whatever people, should concern all, and should be repelled with united arms and common expenses.

This confederation of kings terrified the senate and people of Rome more than can be said; they labored in various ways, with diverse ingenuity and manifold contrivance, to dissolve the compacted alliance of the Germans, and in every way in vain. From that time the courage and power of the Franks were always suspect to the Romans, since they feared, as afterward happened, that the Germans would gain possession of the monarchy of all Europe. In the fourth year of his reign the Romans again launched an expedition into Germany; the allied kings, meeting them with no lack of energy, did not cease bravely to repel force with arms until they compelled the enemy to retreat and flee. For King Clogio, in the name of the Franks, having engaged in battle with the Roman army, fought most bravely for his people, and at last, after many had fallen on both sides, the Romans departed without victory.

In the eighteenth year of the reign of the aforementioned Clogio, on the eighth day before the Kalends of January, there was born in Bethlehem of Judah the Lord, the savior of the whole universe, Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, from the untouched ever-virgin Mary. In that year, according to Hunibald, by an edict of Caesar Augustus the world subject to Roman rule was enrolled head by head; this, besides the mystical reason, being done by divine providence, was not without literal meaning, the Franks providing the occasion.

In the same year also King Clogio, at his mother’s urging, made his son Phrisus, his second-born, duke of that region which lies by the German sea toward the north; and from the name of that duke it was called Phrisia, as Hunibald maintains, down to the present day. Again, after ten years, with the consent of the aforementioned King Clogio and of all the princes of the people of the Franks, the same son Phrisius was raised to kingship, on this condition, namely, that all kings of Phrisia, like the other dukes, should remain forever subject to the king of the Franks, should pay each year a tribute of 260 oxen, and should fight with the Franks, in the manner of the other allies, against whatsoever enemies.

But Clogio, king of the Franks, died after many brave deeds, in the thirtieth year of his reign, which was the twentieth year of the Lord’s nativity. Hunibald writes many things concerning his character, prophecies, and oracles.

Herimer, the elder son of King Elogion, succeeding his father, reigned for twelve years, a warlike man and excessively bold. In the third year of his reign the Gauls made an incursion and came as far as the Meuse, laying waste with hostile rashness everything that belonged to the jurisdiction of the Franks, except fortifications and castles; and after this they hastily withdrew to their own territory with booty and spoils.

But afterward, when this incursion of the Gauls became known to King Herimer, he was moved to anger; and, having summoned to himself the dukes beyond the Meuse, through whose negligence the Gauls had broken in, he at once proceeded against them according to the laws of the Franks. For thus it was contained in the law: Let a prince established under the king of the Franks in ducal power diligently guard the province entrusted to him. But if through his negligence enemies enter the region, if they injure our people or despoil the inhabitants of the land of their goods, let him pay half of his possessions into the king’s purse, and let him be duke no longer, but live deprived of honor. If he willingly permitted entry to the enemies, let him be buried alive or punished by beheading; but let his wives, together with his children and all his goods, pass into the king’s service.

Then, having called to his aid his German, Teutonic, and [Doringian?] allies, King Herimer entered Gaul with a great multitude, and subdued them with such great slaughter that for many years they could not recover their former strength. Finally, however, in the last year of his reign, when he had again led an army into Gaul and, being too bold, not to say rash or presumptuous, fought rather incautiously, he was killed by them in battle, in the year of the Lord 32.

Marcomirus, brother of the aforesaid King Herimer, who had been slain by the Gauls, succeeding his brother in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned for eighteen years, a man tall in stature, but in his character feared and terrible. He engaged in many battles with the Romans and Gauls, and often grievously afflicted the people of Mainz and Trier, together with those of Cologne.

In the second year of his reign, the only-begotten Son of God, the Lord and Savior of the world, Jesus Christ, born as man of Mary, ever and most pure Virgin, without union with a man, according to that same humanity was crucified, suffered, and died for our salvation, and was buried, at Jerusalem, the city of the Jews, under Pontius Pilate, governor of the Romans. He descended to hell and rescued the righteous from there; on the third day he rose from the dead; and ascending into heaven, he sits at the right hand of the Father, to judge the living and the dead, and he lives forever.

In the tenth year of King Marcomirus, Clodius, Caesar of the Romans, led an army into Britain, which we now call England by the customary name, and made it, together with Armorica and the Orkney islands, tributary to the Romans.

*Clodomir, son of the aforesaid King Marcomer,* succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for twelve years. He was a restless and very warlike man; of him Hunibald reports that he was most hostile to the Romans and the Gauls, and desired nothing more than to erase the memory of them. Scarcely a year passed in which he did not launch an expedition into Gaul at least once.

In the first year of his reign, three suns appeared at once, which gradually returned into one; and there followed throughout all Europe a great famine and an immense mortality not only of men, but also of beasts of burden.

In the ninth year, King Clodomir, with a great army of his men, crossed the Rhine and the Meuse, drove out the Gauls, and recovered everything that had previously been lost. He fought with the Romans near Mainz, with the aid of the Germans, but he could not prevent them from repairing the city. He died in the twelfth year of his reign, in the sixty-second year of the Lord's Nativity, in the fifth Roman indiction.

*Antenor, son of King Clodomir,* reigned after his father for six years. Throughout the whole time in which he reigned, he waged war with the Gauls and the Romans; and at last, on this account, he perished miserably with many nobles of the kingdom, when, as we shall say, the bridge broke on the river Meuse and he was drowned.

In the last year of his reign, after a bridge had been built of wood over boats in the ancient manner, he led his army across the river into Gaul; and, after ravaging the region, he hastened his return into Francia with much booty and many spoils. And when the greater part of the army had already crossed the river, while the king himself, with the nobles and the rest of those at the rear, was still waiting on the bank, suddenly the bridge, weighed down by the number of horsemen, fell; and the boats, loosed from one another when the ropes broke, presented a pitiable spectacle to those watching, as many fell into the river.

The king, thunderstruck and surely troubled in mind, began to take counsel with those who were present about repairing the bridge, when behold, they are soon told that the Gauls were coming behind them in a great multitude of fighters, ready for battle. When this was heard, all who were with the king; for them time and place denied flight, while their smaller army forbade battle. Therefore, since no way of escape lay open except the way of swimming through the river, they threw into the water whatever arms could weigh them down, and tried for safety by swimming. The king also, having cast off his cuirass and girded over his tunic only with a sword-belt and sword, lowered himself with his horse into the waves. All the rest, compelled by necessity, lowered themselves into like danger.

They all began to swim at once, some on horses, others sustaining themselves against the force of the waters with hands and feet. Of these, many reached the bank by swimming; many also who had no skill in swimming, choked by the waters, perished in the midst of the waves. Among them King Antenor was submerged, with nine princes, whose names are these: Duke Priamus, Duke Walther, Duke Clogio, Duke Herimer, Duke Antenor, Duke Marcomerus, Duke Clodius, Duke Richimer, Duke Antenor, Duke [Ruutvuicus?], with sixty-three other nobles of the Franks. Thus, then, Antenor, king of the Franks, perished in the sixth year of his reign, in the sixty-eighth year from the Lord’s nativity, in the eleventh Roman indiction.

Ratherus, or, as another writes, Ratherius, the elder son of the aforementioned King Antenor, after his father’s death reigned over the Franks for twenty-one years. He was a magnanimous, daring, strong man, who with his sword always most bravely defended the people of the Franks from every incursion of the Gauls, fearing no one, dreading no one, but himself to be feared by all. In his second year, Nero, the wicked emperor of the Romans, stirred up the first persecution against the Christians; at Rome he killed Peter and Paul, princes of the apostles, and throughout the whole empire he cruelly destroyed many thousands of believers.

Ratherus, king of the Franks, however, wore down the Gauls in many ways; he avenged his father’s death with arms, fire, and iron; he restored the territories taken from his people; he built castles, cities, and fortifications; he renewed the treaty with the Germans, Saxons, and Doringian kings, which Francus the king had once concluded a hundred years before, as we said above; and he enlarged the boundaries of the kingdom of the Franks.

After these things at last he died in the twenty-first year of his reign, in the eighty-ninth year from the Lord’s nativity, in the second Roman indiction. He was buried in the city Ratherama, which he himself had built new from the foundations, dedicated to his own name. His magnificent deeds Arebaldus, bishop of the Franks and poet, described in verse after the manner of the ancients; these Hunibald later reduced to prose.

Richimerus, son of King Ratherus, succeeding his father, reigned for twenty-four years: a prince magnificent in all things, daring, warlike, and excessively superstitious in the worship of his people’s idols, since he wished not only to equal the Romans in the number of gods, but even to defeat and surpass them.

Finally it pleased all the kings and nobles that, from the people of the Franks, Saxons, Thuringians, Germans, and Teutons, who around the sea were nearer neighbors to the Dacians and Goths, they should bring together one people, with children, wives, and beasts of burden, which would inhabit the land beyond furthest Saxony and nearest to the Scanzians, and would prevent the incursions of enemies.

When that agreement had been concluded, Richimer, king of the Franks, assigned eighteen thousand men with children and wives to that region, and set over them as duke and prince his second-born son, Sunno. To him, according to the agreement, many later came from the Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians; and from diverse peoples they became one, and in a short time they grew into a great nation, and thereafter did not permit the Scanzians to lay waste Saxony. Yet they were often harassed by the Romans.

This Duke Sunno established the seat of his people with power in the borders of Saxony, having to the east the Sarmatians, to the south the Slavs of Bohemia, to the west Saxony and the Teutons, and to the north the Cimbri, the sea, the Dacians, and the Scanzian Goths, and men living on islands and in marshy places.

After Sunno had died, Clodomercilius succeeded in the duchy across the sea; he enlarged the boundaries of the possession he had received by no small amount. After him his son Sunno received the leadership of the people; in the year of the Lord’s nativity 146, in the fourteenth indiction of the Romans, in memory of his homeland, [unclear] of the Franks, he built a new city on the banks of the river Oder, which, out of love for his forefathers, he called Frankfurt. After this he also founded another city, at the mouth of the aforesaid river, not far from the sea, which from his own name he called Sunden, or, as Hunibald attests, Sunnonia.

The name of the Franks remained for some time in these lands, until at last, when the dukes had multiplied, the region was divided into several parts, and The several divisions received their names from particular princes or events. For from Duke Marcomer some were afterwards called Marcomeri; from Brando, his son, who founded the city of Brandenburg, others were named Brandenburgers; and others received other names from other sources.

In the fifth year of King Richimer, Domitian Caesar had previously crushed the Sarmatians and Dacians, who dwelt in the borders of Germany toward the east, and afflicted them exceedingly. And, adding sin to crime, he stirred up the second persecution against the Christians after the most impious Nero, and was the first of all the Caesars to command everyone to call him god and lord.

In the eleventh year of his reign, Richimer, king of the Franks, had a war with the Romans and Gauls not far from the city of Basana, aided by the common support of the Germans; in it many fell on both sides. Meanwhile, when the Roman legions had returned to Cologne Agrippina, Nerva Caesar was announced dead; in his place the soldiers raised Trajan, a man of Spanish nation, to be Caesar. He ruled nineteen years, six months, and fifteen days.

But Richimer, king of the Franks, after many deeds bravely done, at last finally died in the twenty-fourth year of his reign, and in the year 113 of the Lord’s nativity, in the eleventh Roman indiction. Ruthvuigus, bard of the Franks, wrote his life, character, deeds, and worship of the gods in verse.

Odemar, the elder son by birth of the aforesaid Richimer, king of the Franks, reigned after his father for fourteen years. He was a prince of the Franks very beloved by the people, who refrained from wars as much as could be done without harm to the commonwealth. For he made truces with the Gauls and the Romans, which the Franks call treugae, as if “confidences,” because faith was pledged between them; and he showed himself so friendly to them thereafter that he compelled all to lament his death.

Thus, as long as he lived among men, he kept the peace he had promised, and expended all his effort on the honor and adornment of his people. He founded very many castles, cities, and fortifications throughout the kingdom; by the counsel of the priests he both enlarged and renewed the laws and rites of sacrifices. For all the Franks, Saxons, and Germans at that time were still subject to the vanity of idols and far removed from knowledge of the Christian faith. They worshipped Mars as the fierce god of war, from whose divine power they sought victory at all times. To each of their gods they dedicated not only individual altars and temples, but also individual cities and villages; nor was there any number to the insensible gods.

After this he obtained the priesthood, and was held in such high esteem that he was worshipped by all almost as some divinity, and commanded not only the common people whatever he wished, but even the kings of the Franks and all the nobles. Writing the histories of the people in verse, he continued them.

The Franks had peace during all the days in which Marcomirus reigned, and under his direction they greatly increased in riches, power, and honor. Doracus writes in the annals, as Hunibaldus bears witness, that the Romans always regarded the Franks as suspect, in peace and in war, as future overthrowers of the empire, having long before been warned by the oracle of Phedius; and for that reason they often conspired with all their forces for their destruction, though in vain.

For he was of such great virtue and power that he was a considerable object of fear not only to the Gauls but also to the Romans; to them the Frankish name was so hateful and odious that they were accustomed never to write or name that people otherwise than as German.

The Franks gloried in their name; but the Romans, troubled by hatred of them, begrudged them the word. Nor did the writers of histories among the neighboring peoples, especially the Romans and Gauls, by common agreement grant the Frankish name to the people of Sicambria before, by Frankish arms and virtues, they had subjected almost all Europe to their dominion. For earlier, as we have said, the Romans, the Gauls, and the remaining nations hostile to the Franks, as Hunibald bears witness, moved by envy, called them by no other name than Germans. For thus says the author already mentioned: “Our ancestors had no less common concern for the name of their people, which they had once received from Franco their king, than for the kingdom, because they thought it would be glorious for them not thereafter to change the Frankish name into another, once it had been taken up by their forebears with the highest honor.”

In these times Hildegast, philosopher, counselor, seer, kinsman of kings, and elder, most learned of all, flourished in great esteem among King Hilderic, the nobles, and also the whole multitude of the Frankish people. In his own age he said, did, and wrote marvelous things. And he was of such great estimation and authority among his people that all always obeyed his command.

On the birthday of Hilderic, king of the Franks, while standing in the temple of Jupiter at Neopagus before the image of that god, after prayers had been poured out for the obtaining of a prophecy, he suddenly began to rage, as is the custom in madness, with a song of the alrunae; and, shaking his head and with his hands bent back, he prophesied thus, not using verses composed by the rule of meters, but rhythms without measure, uttered in fury and in great confusion. *What the emblems or arms of the ancient Franks were*

They depicted a lion, with the forepart of its body, on a fleece of blue color, its feet stretched out for seizing and its mouth open in the manner of one roaring; the lower part of its body ended in a serpent’s tail, with which it seemed to press and suffocate the neck of an eagle, depicted beneath on a golden fleece with wings spread, and most strongly bound.

The Romans had the eagle as their standard when fighting. Desiring to suppress and annihilate its proud and arrogant power by strength and prudence together, the Franks took the lion for the one and the serpent for the other. For by the lion the Franks wished mystically to signify their strength, and by the serpent wisdom and prudence, by which they undoubtedly hoped that they would at last overcome [unclear]. All subsequent kings of the Franks used these emblems or arms, though they are improperly so called, down to Clodoneus the Christian, who is read to have taken up, in place of the lion, three lilies sent down from heaven, as will be said in its place.

Therefore Clodomir, king of the Franks, appointed his brother Genebaldus commander of the army that was to go, and entrusted to him eighty thousand fighting men. When the Germans, having gained this victory, were dividing the spoils in the camp in the customary manner, a dangerous quarrel arose between two soldiers, of whom one, a Thuringian, was named Gunther, while the other was a Sueve named Adelbertus. This was the cause of the disturbance: that the Sueve soldier had accused the Thuringian of having secretly carried off something or other from the common booty. When he denied it, a serious sedition of the Thuringians arose, while the Sueves defended the Sueve. For each had many adherents from his own people, who were stirred up in spirit.

But when the Thuringian steadfastly denied the theft, the Sueve offered that he would prove the truth by duel. When the princes had permitted this, single combat was entered into between them on the appointed day; in it, at the second encounter, the Thuringian was killed by the Sueve. When his corpse had been stripped of clothes and arms, the very theft over which he had been accused was found hidden on him. After the Thuringians received this confusion, they were exceedingly angry, But, concealing their anger, they afterwards held secret counsel among themselves as to how they might avenge Gunther’s fall upon Adelbert. And when the plan had been settled, namely that they should kill the Swabian that same night, one hundred and twenty of them conspired together to keep the matter secret.

Therefore on the following night they silently rushed upon the tent in which they suspected Adelbert to be, demanding that the murderer be handed over to them to be killed. Roused by these voices, the Swabians ran to arms, firmly resolving either to defend their innocent soldier from death or to die with him. An attack was at once made upon the Thuringians, and they slaughtered almost all those one hundred and twenty.

When this was announced in the camp of the Thuringians, they were greatly moved and, as though turned to fury, immediately seized their weapons; and, having formed a battle-line, they sprang upon the Swabians, who suspected no such thing, raising shouts on high like madmen. But the Swabians, not slow, when they could not call the Thuringians back from the tumult with fair words, themselves also put on arms and strove to resist bravely. A savage battle was joined between friends, in which more than two thousand men of each side perished. Yet the Thuringians lost more than the Swabians, and at last, overcome and compelled by necessity, they withdrew.

This contention had remained hidden from both the Franks and the Saxons until the struggle came to an end, since they had pitched their camps not in the same place but in different stations. Afterwards, however, when they learned the cause, they undertook many negotiations for peace and concord between the disputants; but since each side complained that it had been gravely and unjustly injured, they could not restore full concord between them. They did, however, establish truces, or an armistice, between them for three years, during which they ordered the parties to abstain from war, until anger should cool with time and the disturbance be calmed.

In the sixth year of King Richimer, in the fourth indiction, the Romans, assembled troop by troop into one force with the Gauls under Constantius, son of Constantine, the great Caesar, declared war on the Franks. But King Richimer, since he was a high-spirited, strong, and bold prince, terrified by nothing, summoned to his aid, as was his custom, his friends the Teutons, Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians; and when these had been united with the Franks, he set against the Romans and Gauls an army of nearly two hundred thousand, with whom he fought spiritedly. In the first encounter the Romans were victors; in the second, defeated and overcome, they fled with loss to their own men.

In the last year of his reign Richimer, king of the Franks, when he was about to fight rashly again with the Romans, was slain in battle, namely in the thirteenth year of his reign and in the three hundred fiftieth year of the Lord's nativity, in the eighth indiction of the Romans.

Theodomirus, son of the aforesaid King Richimer, reigned after the death of his father for ten years. During all the days of his reign he lived in contention with the Romans and brought to completion many battles against them with varying outcome. His mother, named Hastila, daughter of the king of the Saxons, [unclear] the Gauls [unclear]. In antiquity the part of the Tungri was beyond the river Meuse, in the fortress which antiquity called Dispartum. From the boundary of the Franks as far as the river Loire the Gauls dwelt under the rule of the Romans; beyond the Loire were the Goths, mixed with peoples of diverse nations.

In these times the borders of the kingdom of the Franks were greatly enlarged beyond the Meuse, and they had occupied a great part of Gaul, though the Romans were unwilling. Nor did they desist from what they had begun until at last, reckoning from this time onward, within nearly seventy years, they subjected all Gaul to their dominion.

In the times of Theodomirus's reign, Dagobert, son of Genebald, the first duke of the eastern Franks, succeeding his father in the principate, gathered a multitude of fighters, crossed the Rhine, and devastated cis-Rhenane Gaul with plundered spoils, fire, and sword. He despoiled and burned the very ancient city of the Treveri, built in the times of Abraham, prince of the Jews.

But King Theodomirus at last was seized by the Romans inhabiting Gaul together with Hastila his mother, was beheaded, and perished in the tenth year of his reign, and in the 360th year of the Lord's nativity, in the third Roman indiction.

The son of King Theodomirus, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for eighteen years: a high-spirited prince, strong, and of singular prudence, who engaged in many battles with the Roman Gauls and harshly avenged the injury of his enemies. In his first year Saint Antony, abbot of the hermits, departed to the Lord, on the seventeenth day of the month of January on the day, in the 105th year of his age.

At the same time also the Alemanni had a war with the Romans, not far from the Rhine, in the territory of the people of Strasbourg. In that battle many fell on both sides; more of the Alemanni were killed than of the Romans, whose commander of the army was Julian Caesar, despiser of God and apostate.

Clogio, king of the Franks, wishing to avenge his father’s death, gathered a huge army and, entering Gaul in force, stormed the city of Cambrai, where he slew without pity many Romans whom he found there.

Proceeding from there, he subjected Gaul to his kingdom as far as the river Saône. Against him the Romans, fighting more by stratagems than by strength, could do nothing.

In the thirteenth year of Clogio, king of the Franks, meanwhile, because the commanders with their armies were ravaging the Gauls, Valentinian, Caesar of the Romans, having secretly and swiftly led legions through [Sarmatia?] into the territories of the Germans, waged war with the Saxons in the territories of the Franks, on this side of the Oder, not far from the sea. In it many on both sides fell, slain by the sword. Yet in the end victory fell to the Romans, and the defeated Saxons took to flight.

King Clogio died in the eighteenth year of his reign, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 378, in the sixth Roman indiction, leaving three sons by the queen: Marcomir, who first succeeded to the kingdom; Dagobert, who after the death of the aforesaid brother held the kingdom under the title of duke, as we shall say below in its place; and Hector, surnamed Degenbart, from whom Pippin and Charles, later the great emperor, drew their lineage, as we have said in the second volume.

*Marcomir, the elder son of Clogio*

Marcomir, Clogio’s elder son, succeeding first in the aforesaid year to the kingdom of the Franks, reigned sixteen years, a high-souled prince, strong and bold, who engaged in many battles with the Romans.

Indeed, in the first year of his reign the Romans, aided by troops of the Gauls, moved war against him. He, with the Franks and Saxons, met them in an undaunted encounter not far from the city of Cologne, fought bravely, laid low many thousands of the enemy, and distributed the spoils among his helpers and friends. This war lasted four years.

But the Franks, fierce in their inborn savagery, received the Romans for battle no otherwise than a lion is accustomed to tear apart a little dog with outstretched claws, destined at once for death and without mercy. At last, when four years had passed, the Romans, considering that they could not tame the Franks and subject them to themselves, recalled by edict the remnants of the legions that had remained, and assigned them to other places. The Franks, however, did not therefore cease from harassing the Gauls, but continued what they had begun.

In the ninth year of Marcomir, king of the Franks, in the fifteenth Roman indiction, Maximus, king of Britain, sprung from the lineage of the Caesars, received the name and diadem of the Roman Empire, being appointed by the soldiers in England.

*How King Maximus came into Britain.*

A few years earlier Octavius, king of Britain, having no son, with the consent of the nobles summoned this man from Rome into Britain; for on his mother’s side he was a Briton, but on his father’s a Roman. And he gave him his daughter as wife, with whom, after Octavius had died, he obtained the kingdom of the whole island. He had come, when summoned into Britain, with an army of Roman soldiery; the greater part of the soldiers remained with him, while a smaller number returned to Italy.

Then, after the death of the Caesars Valentinian and Valens, in the fifth year of his British reign, raised up in pride of mind and impatient of quiet, he caused himself to be made Caesar of the Romans by those soldiers who had remained with him, against Gratian, whom the Romans had set over themselves.

Soon, therefore, when he had been hailed as Caesar by the soldiers, he entrusted the kingdom of Britain to a vicar to administer, and, gathering into one place all the noble youth fit for war of the whole island of Britain, which is now called England, he sailed with them into Gaul, having this intention in mind: that, once the Gauls had been conquered, he would proceed to Rome and subject the empire to himself.

Therefore in the ninth year of Marcomir, king of the Franks, and in the year of the Lord’s birth 387, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans, Maximus, king of Britain, with a huge multitude of his own men, entered Gaul by ship and ravaged everything round about the region in hostile fashion.

And first he came with his ships to the shore of the Armorican land, which from that time thereafter has been called New Britain, or Lesser Britain, down to the present day. At that time the Franks inhabited it, under the rule of Duke Iubalchus, whom the kings of the Franks had appointed vicar of the kingdom in those places. Duke Iubalchus, greatly disturbed by the arrival of so great a multitude of peoples, gathered his forces as best he could, and, advancing to meet those who were coming, joined battle with them; in that battle he himself was killed with fifteen thousand Franks, while the rest sought safety in flight.

Then, in order to strike terror of his name into all the surrounding peoples, he used great cruelty, since he slaughtered without mercy women, virgins, old men, young men, and little infants, as many as he found. Now the Frankish kings, dukes, nobles, soldiers, and all the people were still blinded and polluted by the empty superstition of idols; but Maximus, though a tyrant, was nevertheless a Christian, together with all his soldiery and his people.

After this victory over Iubalchus had been obtained from the Franks, Maximus conferred that Armorican principality upon Duke Conan, who was, through his brother, the nephew of Octavius, the aforesaid king of Britain. Having left with him a good part of the army of the Britons for protection, he himself departed thence with the remaining multitude of troops, and, ranging through all Gaul, by war and arms made those who resisted his command obedient, no less cruelly than powerfully.

Meanwhile Gratian, Caesar at Rome, understanding that Maximus that he had usurped the diadem of the Roman empire, and that, having gone out from Britain with a great army, he had begun to ravage the Gauls; he also gathered forces and moved against him into Gaul, and entered the city of Paris with his army. Afterwards, when he joined battle with Maximus, he was defeated through the treachery of Merobaldus, master of the soldiers, and, slipping away in flight, came to Lyons, where he was afterwards killed.

Conanus, however, whom Maximus had established as king in the region of Armorica, endured frequent attacks from the Gauls and Romans who were in Aquitaine. Meeting them bravely, he fought with them manfully, and, after very many of them had been killed, turned the rest to flight.

At last, having obtained peace and quiet possession of the Armorican kingdom, he began to take counsel with his nobles as to how they might as quickly as possible have their wives and children, whom they had left behind in Britain, brought to them with all their household goods, lest the propagation of their race should fail.

The number of soldiers who had come from Britain with Conanus into Armorica was thirty thousand fighting men. The common people and farmers, however, as Geoffrey is the authority, were one hundred thousand, all of whom, by order of Maximus, had sailed from Britain into Armorica. Of these, many did not have wives; the rest, who did have them, had left them in Britain with their little children when they were compelled to follow Maximus.

But Conanus and all his people, since they were Christians, in no way consented to take as wives the daughters of pagans who worshipped mute idols. Even if these women had been Christians, nevertheless it did not please them to have any traffic of marital union with the Gauls.

In those days there was in Britain, which is now called England, a certain sub-king named Dionotus, who, after the death of his brother Caradocus, held the principality of Cornwall; to him also King Maximus, when about to set out for Gaul, had entrusted the kingdom, as to a very prudent man. He had an only daughter named Ursula, by everyone’s judgment the most beautiful among all the virgins of Britain, and very devoted to Christ.

Therefore Conanus, the new king of the Armoricans from Britain, not only knew very well Ursula, the daughter of the aforesaid prince of Cornwall, Dionotus, but also loved her greatly. Hence, after messengers and letters had been sent to him, he asked that she be sent to him as his wife.

Secondly, he asked that the wives and children of the soldiers and common people, left behind in Britain when their husbands departed at the command of Maximus, be put on ships with their household goods and sent to him in the Armorican kingdom.

Thirdly, he instructed that, according to the number which he had specified in his letters, virgins should be sent: noble ones from noble lineage for the soldiers, and common ones also for the common people, whom each of those who then had no spouses might take as wives.

Meanwhile all the things that the Romans, under the command of Valentinian, had done with the Franks were announced to the dukes of the Franks, Dagobert, Sunno, Genebald, Priam, Antenor, and the other elders who were directing the wars of the nation in the region of the Gauls: how they had condemned both the king to death and the nation to tribute, a nation which had always been free and had been subject to no one from its Trojan origin. They were angry, stirred up, and greatly troubled, and in many deliberations they considered how they might prudently avenge this injury.

After much and prolonged consultation, therefore, they at last came together into one opinion: that they ought rather to preserve their fatherland in its ancestral liberty than to press forward with affairs in Gaul. Leaving, therefore, Duke Priam in the territories of Gaul with a sufficient guard of soldiers, the other princes returned with the army into Germanic Francia. Thereafter, after the death of King Marcomir, the kingdom of the Franks remained vacant, without a king, under only two dukes, for nearly twenty-six years.

Dagobert, brother of Marcomir king of the Franks, who was killed by Valentinian, and son of the aforesaid King Clogio, after the death of his brother was, by the consent of the Franks, interrex, or governor of the kingdom, for about five years, as Hunibald indicates, a high-minded and warlike man. In his time Marcomir, son of Clodius, held the dukedom among the eastern Franks for sixteen years. Dagobert began the government of the kingdom under the name of duke in the year of the Lord’s nativity 393, in the sixth indiction.

In the second year of Duke Dagobert, Caesar Valentinian, having sent messengers to the people of the Franks in Germany, demanded the tribute imposed the previous year. To him the dukes and people of the kingdom sent back word through the same messengers as follows: the everlasting freedom of the Franks [unclear] to impose tribute on nations as he was accustomed with the rest, not to pay. But you, who, when the strength of the Franks was absent, fought against an unarmed few, and conquered by deceit, not by courage, if you trust that you have the right of exacting tribute from the Franks, come whenever you wish, and by the sword we shall show that we are most free and always have been, and that you are a slave. By this we make you certain, lest you send anyone to us unarmed concerning this matter, since indeed we strive rather to give up life than to lose liberty.

When Valentinian had received this message, he wondered at the constancy of the Franks, and said to those standing around: “Frank, Frank, you are a hard people, to be named rather from ferocity than from liberty.” And the matter rested in the current year, because at that time the Roman commonwealth was suffering many varied hostile incursions from different nations.

But in the following year Valentinian, deceived by the counsel of his own men, again sent to the Franks Sisinnius, a count of the court, so that he might induce them by good persuasions to the payment of tribute and to the very firm friendship of the Romans. But the Franks, not forgetful of those things which they had previously sent back in answer to Valentinian, and also wishing to avenge their most beloved king Marcomir, slew with the sword the count sent to them, together with the whole company which he had brought, in contempt of Valentinian and of all the Romans.

After this the Franks, having gathered a very great army of their men into one body, under the leaders Sunno and Genebald, whom the Gauls called Gundebaud, crossed the Rhine with force and, bursting into the borders of the Romans and laying everything waste, struck fear even into the people of Cologne. For they strove to subject to their dominion everything that lay between the rivers Rhine, Meuse, and [Tabula?]. But afterward, when the incursion of the Franks became known at Trier, Nannenus and Quintinus, chiefs of the Treveri, who, like the people of Cologne, followed the Roman side, gathered an army and determined to make a movement against them. For meanwhile the Franks, after the region of the Gauls had been ravaged and many of them killed, sent the spoils back to their native seats with part of the army, while they proceeded farther.

When Nannenus and Quintinus, chiefs of the Treveri, had come with an army, the Franks, as we have said, in good number and already laden with plunder, had crossed the Rhine, intending to return to their comrades in order, with all their strength, to lay waste what remained untouched among the Gauls. A great confusion overtook the Franks because of the ill-considered and insufficiently cautious division of the army.

For the first seats of the Franks were, as we have often said, around the borders of the mouths of the Rhine, where now are Holland, Zeeland, the people of Utrecht, Guelders, and the Frisians. Therefore Nannenus and Quintinus, when they had arrived with the army of the Treveri at the places in which the remnants of the Franks had remained, awaiting the return of their companions, drew up the battle line and pursued the fugitives as far as the Carbonarian Forest, forcing them to withdraw, after no small number of them had been slaughtered. From this arose the hatred of the Franks against the Treveri, on account of which they afterward crossed the Rhine and began to winter in the territory of the Treveri and to lay everything waste.

When these things had been done, it seemed useful to Quintinus that they should lead the whole army of the Treveri into Francia and forestall them, unprepared, by a sudden incursion. But Quintinus’s plan did not please Nannenus, who did not doubt that the Franks would be bolder in resisting on their own soil. Therefore Nannenus, parting from Quintinus with his men, returned to Mainz.

But Quintinus, persisting in his plan, crossed the Rhine near the fortress of Musium and led out the army, intending to destroy the Franks at the first assault, with a spirit higher and greater than his strength for winning. But the Franks, not sluggish, met the Gauls with a manly encounter, and when battle had been joined many thousands of the Treveri perished, with few saved. In that engagement Heracleus, tribune of the Roman legion, fell, and almost all who had been in command of the soldiers perished with him by the missiles and sword of the Franks. For, with the exception of a few to whom dark night and the woods offered safety and the opportunity of hiding, the whole army of Quintinus perished by sword, bow, and marsh.

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 394, in the seventh indiction, the emperor Valentinian died by a noose at Vienne, a city of Gaul, through the deceit and severity of Arbogast, master of the soldiers; and a certain Eugenius, a Roman, by the plotting of the same Arbogast, seized the rule of the Gauls and reigned for three years. Afterward, when war was joined with the emperor Theodosius, he was slain by him.

The Franks, having heard of the death of Caesar Valentinian, resolved to cross the Rhine and invade Gaul. Arbogast, understanding their plan, forestalled them: in the depth of winter, when the Rhine was frozen, he led an army across at Cologne Agrippina and began to lay waste with hostile incursion the Franks nearest the Rhine.

Against him, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s nativity, 394, the leaders of the Franks moved a powerful army; engaging the enemy, they killed more than twelve thousand Gauls and put the rest to flight with Arbogast.

In the year of the Lord’s Nativity 403, in the second Roman indiction, Marcomir, duke of the eastern Franks, died. He was a vigorous man but a pagan, dwelling among the Suebi at Würzburg among the [Dorigi?], and was buried according to his ancestral superstition on the mountain called Frankenberg.

After this, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 410, in the eighth Roman indiction, while the Franks, from hatred of the Romans and Gauls, looked the other way, Carocus, king of the Vandals, came out from the Scanzian islands, or, as they are now called, the Gothic islands, with an innumerable multitude of people. He made his way through Germany and Saxony and, with the consent of the Franks, crossed the Rhine and entered Gaul; after ravaging the region round about, he grievously afflicted the natives of the land.

He, immediately at the beginning of his reign, commanded all the Franks by edict to grow long hair, so that from the Gauls, whom they were either going to attack or who, already conquered, were dwelling with them, the Franks might be distinguished at first sight. In the eleventh year of Meroveus, king of the Franks, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 457, in the tenth Roman indiction, the Franks took the city of the Treveri on the following occasion; Attila, king of the Huns, had captured it in war a few years before and had crushed it with grievous devastation. At that time there was in the city of the Treveri a certain senator named Lucius, who had a wife judged by very many to be most beautiful; Avitus was wasting away in shameful love for her. For this reason, feigning illness, he lay down on his bed and, as though to obtain relief from his sickness, ordered all the senatorial women of the city to come to visit him in succession. Among the others came the wife of Lucius, suspecting no evil; Avitus at once violated her. When the crime had been committed, the woman returned weeping to her husband’s house. On the next day Caesar Avitus rose from his bed, healthy in body but polluted in conscience by the crime, and said to Lucius when he met him: “You have beautiful baths indeed, Lucius, but you wash in cold water,” hinting at the foul, unnatural discharge of his wife. But Lucius understood the figure, took it grievously, and, messengers and letters having been sent in the utmost secrecy to the Franks, promised that if they came as quickly as possible, he would hand over the city of the Treveri into their power without doubt, delay, or difficulty.

For up to that point the Treveri, for many years past, had sided with the Romans and their kings with stubborn attachment. But the Franks, not forgetful of the slaughter and wrongs which they had once suffered from Nannenus and Quintinus, princes of the Treveri, at the Carbonarian Forest in the time of Duke Dagobert, the interrex, immediately gathered forces and hastened gladly to the destruction of the Treveri. When the city had been captured, the Franks plundered everything and subjected it to their kingdom.

In these times the kingdom of the Franks gradually took on great growth and extended its borders in many ways throughout the Gauls. Hence the kings of the nation, who from their first entrance into Germany had held seats around the Rhine, where Holland now is, with the peoples who are called Gelderlanders, Westphalians, Clivenses, and Frisians, for almost eight hundred and seventy years, at [Neopagus?], Duisburg, and in the other places and castles on this side of the Rhine, transferred the dwelling-place of their habitation into the Gauls, rarely resting from wars, until at last they subjected all Germany, Gaul also, and at the same time Italy, to the kingdom of the Franks.

And so the kingdom of the Franks, which took its beginning from one small people, through much labor, through most bloody wars, through the very great bloodshed of kings, dukes, nobles, soldiers, and commoners, over about nine hundred years at last grew immeasurably; it took on growth beyond all the kingdoms of all Europe, so that it ruled with power over many kingdoms and peoples, and from many kingdoms there was made one which, gathered by the sword, for the honor of the conqueror, would be called, and was, by the name of the Franks From this it is plainly clear that, just as the Franks who remained with the Germans use the language and customs of the Germans, so also those who entered Gaul with the kings and remained with the Gauls changed both their customs and their manner of speaking together with their settlements; and those whom nature had made Germans from the beginning of their birth, change of place in the midst of the Gauls likewise made Gauls in speech and customs. Indeed, when they were in Troy, they practiced Trojan customs and speech; among the Scythians likewise Scythian ones; in Germany they learned and kept German ones, and in Gaul Gallic ones. So too Aeneas, coming from the Trojans into Italy with his people, gradually forgetting his native speech, began to use the customs and language found at Rome.

After this Meroveus, king of the Franks, seized by sickness, died in the pagan worship of idols, as all his forebears had done, in the twelfth year of his reign, but in the year of the Lord’s nativity 458, in the eleventh indiction. The son of King Meroveus, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned twice through a varied change of affairs. For first after his father he reigned only one year; then, because of his insolence and debauchery, as we shall say, he was deprived of the kingdom and fled into Thuringia. After only one year had been completed in the kingdom, the nobles of the kingdom, conspiring against him, wished to kill him, because, living too insolently and wantonly, he had defiled by his foul conduct many of their daughters and wives and those of the nobles. When he learned this, he fled with a few men into Thuringia, and remained with Basinus, king of the Thuringians, his kinsman, for three years. He withdrew, however, after being deprived by the Franks, in the year of the Lord 459, in the twelfth indiction.

After his departure from the kingdom, in that same year, the princes of the Franks at once set over themselves as king a certain Aegidius, a Roman prefect at Soissons, who occupied the kingdom of the Franks for three years while Hilderic was in exile. He, continually raging against the chief princes of the kingdom, slaughtered many, having no reasonable cause apart from empty suspicion. Therefore the rest, fearing that similar things would at some time threaten them, unanimously arranged a conspiracy against the tyrant, judging that it would be more tolerable to suffer that man to rule the kingdom who abused the daughters and wives of his subjects than to endure longer that man who thirsted for nothing more than the blood of princes.

In the eleventh year of his reign King Clovis, having gathered his forces, led an army against Alaric, king of the western Goths; when Alaric met him at the tenth milestone from the city of Poitiers, he killed him together with many others, and added all his treasures and that very city to his own kingdom.

In the fifteenth year of his reign, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 499, in the seventh Roman indiction, when the Alemanni, again raised up in pride, would neither be subject to the Franks nor any longer pay tribute, Clovis summoned a great army of his men and launched a strong expedition against the Alemanni for battle. Once the contest had begun, the fighting was fierce and long with the fortune of war undecided, and many fell on both sides.

At last, by the ordering of divine providence, while the Alemanni were fighting bravely, the Franks gradually began to give way; and when it already seemed almost certain that they would flee, the king, bathed in tears, prayed silently within himself, saying: “Lord Jesus Christ, whom my wife proclaims to be the Son of God, and able to grant all things asked by those who faithfully believe in you, if today you give me victory over these enemies of mine, I shall believe in you and shall have myself baptized in your name, and I shall be a faithful and devoted servant to your name as long as I live.”

And while he was praying thus, with hands and eyes raised to heaven, and sighing deeply, the help of Christ suddenly came to him; and when it was present, the Alemanni were turned to flight, and those who before had fought immovably like a wall now suddenly fled just as the most terrified women are accustomed to do. In that battle the king of the Alemanni fell, with many others. But when the Franks did not cease to pursue the fleeing Alemanni more insistently, they cried out to King Clovis: “Do not pursue us any further, most victorious king, because we are now yours; we promise that from this time onward we shall serve you forever.” Moved by these pleas, he forbade the pursuit; and, after receiving the Alemanni in surrender, he returned to Gaul.

When he had come to the palace, on the advice of Queen Clotild he called to him Remigius, the most holy bishop of the church of Reims. And in the days that followed, the bishops, priests of the Lord, baptized all the rest of the people with great joy, giving thanks for the mercies of almighty God, who through his grace, in order to give peace to the Church, subjected a most fierce people to the yoke of faith.

Thereafter great things were done for the honor of Christ, not only by Clodoneus but much more by his successors, the kings of the Franks: churches were built, several monasteries of those serving God were founded throughout Germany, Italy, and Gaul, and the ministry of divine worship in time was increased manifold. But King Clodoneus, though he was a man most dutiful toward his subjects, is nevertheless read to have been exceedingly cruel toward his own kinsmen, since he caused many most noble men, sprung from the ancient stock of the kings of the Franks, kings, dukes, and princes, to be cruelly and treacherously killed, so that he alone might hold the kingdom.

But that we may commend a few out of many to posterity, love of historical truth impels us. At that time there was reigning at Cologne Agrippina an old man, born from the seed of the kings of the Franks, named Sigebert, whom Clodoneus’s father Hilderic, after Cologne had been acquired, had placed there as vicar of the kingdom. He had a son named Ludovicus, to whom King Clodoneus wrote thus in cunning and deceit: “Your father is now old, useless for the kingdom; if he were to die, his kingdom would come to you, with our friendship, and not to another.”

The young man, seduced by desire for the kingdom, resolved to kill his father. And when King Sigebert one day, suspecting no evil, went out from Cologne, crossed the Rhine, and was hunting with a few men in the forest of Buronia, the son, as though he would possess his kingdom according to Clodoneus’s promise, had assassins sent against him and caused his father to be killed while he was sleeping in his tent at bright midday.

After the murder, indeed the parricide, had been most wickedly carried out, Ludovicus wrote to King Clodoneus: “My father is dead; I have his treasures in my possession along with the kingdom. Send men, therefore, and whatever pleases you from my father’s treasures, I will send to you with good will.” King Clodoneus wrote back to him: “I give thanks for your good will; I ask that you reveal everything to our men when they come, since you alone are henceforth to possess your father’s kingdom with our friendship.”

When the envoys of King Clodoneus came, Ludovicus showed them his father’s treasures. While they were inspecting various and diverse things, the young man said: “In this chest my father was accustomed to store gold coins.” “Put your hand in,” one of those standing by said, “all the way to the bottom, so that you may test whether it is all gold.” When he had done this and was bent far down, the very man who had ordered it raised his hand and swung an axe into his brain; he died at once in that same place.

When these wicked deeds had been done, King Clodoneus came to Cologne, and, as if unaware of the crimes, summoned all the people of the city and said: “Listen, excellent citizens, to the unspeakable evil that lately occurred. [Chloderic?], the son of my kinsman Sigebert, ensnared his father with false words, saying that I was plotting against his life. For I cannot shed the blood of my kinsmen, since it is a sin.”

And thus he received into his own power the kingdom of Sigebert with all his treasures. With the brothers and their sons and grandsons dead, or cruelly killed, Lothar alone obtained the monarchy of the whole kingdom of the Franks, and thereafter, after all his brothers, reigned still for five years.

In the second year of King Lothar, Sigismund, king of Burgundy, who had built the monastery of the Agaunensian martyrs, killed his son Sigeric, having followed the wicked counsel of that boy’s stepmother. Led to great penitence, he devoted himself wholly to the holy martyrs of Agaunum; and that he was worthy of pardon is attested by frequent miracles after his death, for in his honor health is granted to those suffering from fever.

In the fourth year of King Lothar, Theodoric, king of Austrasia, his brother, having been asked by Ermenfrid, king of the Thuringians, under the promise of half his kingdom, treacherously killed the brothers Bertharius and Balderic on the road, while they were returning toward Thuringia, suspecting no evil, from the city of Metz, to which they had come for the sake of greeting him. But when the crime had been carried out, and Theodoric sought from Ermenfrid the half of the kingdom of Thuringia promised to him, the latter neither gave what he had promised nor sent a fitting reply to the petitioner, deceived by the counsel of his wife, who was the sister of that Theodoric and of Lothar. This matter afterward, as we have said in the first volume of the Annals, brought on the final destruction of all Thuringia and extinguished there the royal name and title. In the same year the holy virgin Brigid, dying in Scotland, ascended to Christ.

In the seventh year of Lothar, king of the Franks, Boethius, the Roman consul, worn down by long imprisonment, was killed by Theodoric, king of the Goths, who, after Odoacer had been slain, was occupying Italy; he wrote many useful works for the faith of Christ. Father Benedict, our holy lawgiver, also flourished in these times in Italy, the first founding abbot of the monastery of Cassino, who was the prince of all western monks. To them he handed down a rule of life outstanding in every discretion, and a lucid teaching of words, made honorable by his deeds, is called by the name of Saint Benedict himself. In the tenth year of Lothar, king of the Franks, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 524, the seventh indiction of the Romans, when the king of the people of Metz sought satisfaction from Ermenfrid, king of the Thuringians, for the murder of his brother, as we have said, and did not obtain it, he was roused to anger. Having called to his aid his son Theudebert and his brother Lothar, kings, he moved an expedition into Thuringia, captured King Ermenfrid with his wife and children, and subjected the whole kingdom to himself.

From that time the Thuringians did not have a king of their own.

And Radegund, among the other captives, daughter of Bertharius, once king, whom, as we have said, his brother Ermenfrid had caused to be killed through Theodoric king of the Metensians, King Lotharius joined to himself in marriage. She shone with great holiness, and afterward, becoming a nun, was radiant with very many signs and miracles. Concerning this destruction of the Thuringians, Fortunatus the archbishop wrote more fully in verse and in prose.

In the twelfth year of his reign, Lotharius king of the Franks, with his brother Hildebert, besieged Autun, a city of Burgundy, and, after King Godomar had been put to flight, powerfully subjected the whole province of the Burgundians to his rule.

In these days Saint Nicetius flourished, formerly abbot of the monastery of Limoges, archbishop of Trier, a man of many virtues in Christ, whose deeds are extant. Theodoric king of Austrasia, who ruled over the Metensians, the people of Trier, Liege, Cologne, Mainz, and all those dwelling this side of the Rhine, brought many clerics from among the Arvernian citizens, whom he ordered to be enrolled for service to the Lord in the church of Trier.

At the same time Saint Maurus and Faustus, disciples of the divine Benedict, flourished in Gallic France, distinguished in conduct, learning, holiness of life, and miracles.

He reigned in the city of Soissons two years before the death of his father, and, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-two years. And thus the whole time that he reigned was twenty-four years. When Audovera had given birth to an infant while the king was absent, since no one who would raise the boy to be baptized from the sacred font was at hand, the queen mother herself, deceived by Fredegund’s wicked counsel, fulfilled the office of co-mother, and raised from baptism the infant whom she had borne. Fredegund, the contriver of the deceit, joyfully announcing this to the king when he soon came, said: “You will not have Audovera as wife henceforth, O king; she preferred to be your co-mother rather than your spouse. Heribert, however, the eldest son of Lotharius, in the division of the monarchy of the kingdom of the Franks received Neustria, holding the seat of his kingdom at Paris; and he reigned nine years, and, doing nothing worthy of memory, died in the year of the Lord 573, in the eleventh indiction of the Romans, leaving the kingdom to his brothers.

Gunthramus, King Lotharius’s second-born son, reigned at Orleans for thirty-three years, a prince excellent in his own condition, but he obtained a most wicked wife, named Austrigildis. Among the many evils she perpetrated, when she was sick unto death and could not be helped by the physicians, she circumvented the king with crafty persuasion, so that he confirmed by an undoubted oath that he would grant her the fulfilment of one request. “Before I die,” she said, “have the heads of my physicians, who have tormented me in vain with their medicines, cut off while I am watching.” What more? Those who were to bring in the physicians were sent at once; when they entered the queen’s chamber, the witch said: “Ha! You are not physicians, but liars, who by your medicines force me to die before my time. I have prepared such a reward for you that, compelled to die, you shall pass over before me.” Soon, to satisfy the lust of the impious woman, the executioner, as ordered, cut off the heads of the two innocent physicians while that beast herself watched and laughed. King Gunthramus died in the year of the Lord 597, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans.

Therefore in the year of the Lord’s nativity 582, in the fifteenth Roman indiction, the Saxons who were with the Lombards in Italy, no longer enduring the pride and unjust domination of the Winili, for so the Lombards were formerly called, returned with their children and wives, under the conduct of Sigebert king of the Franks, through southern Gaul to Cologne Agrippina. There, after crossing the Rhine, they sought again their former seats in Saxony, which they had left behind.

But the Suevi, to whom King Sigebert had given them to inhabit, at first denied the Saxons entry; secondly, they agreed to restore to them a third part. But when the Saxons demanded back not a part but the whole, the Suevi offered half the region. The Saxons remained constant in their judgment, demanding back not a part, not half of the land, but the entire whole.

At last the matter was committed to the sword; the fight for possession of the settlements was waged very fiercely by both armies. At length the Suevi were defeated; more than twenty thousand of them perished in that battle by the sword of the Saxons. And thus the Saxons recovered by war the land which, by the persuasion of the Winili, they had left quite imprudently, in the fourteenth year after their departure, some of the Suevi being killed, others expelled.

From that time the aforesaid Saxons, returning from Lombardy into Germany, are called Westphalians, that is, western Gauls, down to the present day, although they are nevertheless true Saxons and Germans. The city they built in the middle of their region a new [city], which they called Milan, in memory of the other one in Lombardy; in it there is now the episcopal see of the church of Münster. Hence some writers also called these Saxons, or Westphalians, Lombards, because they cast off their customs and rites in Saxony.

When they heard this, the citizens were exceedingly terrified; and after holding counsel among themselves, at last, lest it happen that all should perish, they cut off the head of King Theodobert, their lord, and threw it out over the wall to the impious Theoderic, as he had ordered, thus purchasing peace disgracefully in the blood of their own king.

For some time ago, at the insistence of the abbots Blasius and Johannes, we wrote two volumes, of no small size, of chronicles or annals of the distinguished monastery of Hirsau of our order, which is situated in the diocese of Speyer; in the first of these we proved sufficiently, by many arguments, that the aforesaid monastery of Saint Peter at Erfurt was founded originally not by Dagobert, king of the Franks, but by Saint Wilhelm, twelfth abbot of Hirsau. And because we have now learned that this assertion of ours is all the more true from the most foolishly fabricated letters which we saw in the aforesaid Erfurt monastery, secured by no seal of anyone, and which we read under the name of Dagobert and transcribed, lest anyone accuse us of falsehood, we have thought it worthwhile to join a copy of the same letters, together with other arguments, to our assertion.

Far be it from me, Trithemius infers, to fight against the truth of this so ancient asserted foundation, if it holds up; but how truth can hold up in it I do not sufficiently see. Therefore I shall also note here briefly what reasons for doubting occur to me, after those, namely, which I wrote for Hirsau.

The first consideration which prevents me from conceding that Dagobert was the founder of the often-mentioned monastery of Erfurt is that the reckoning of the years does not agree; for if this very foundation is ascribed to Dagobert the Great, it contradicts the instrument of the letters no less than the truth of the computed time. For he died in the year of the Lord’s nativity, as we said above, six hundred forty-five, in the third Roman indiction, not less than sixty years before the donation of this document. But if it is attributed to that latest Dagobert, who was Clodoveus, nevertheless a discordance of years follows, because he began to reign in the year of the Lord 715 and died in the year 719. How, then, could he have delivered letters of a foundation of this kind in the sixth year of the Lord?

The second consideration which made me doubtful is this: that In the aforesaid instrument of letters the ninth Roman indiction is set down, although it was the fourth and by no means the ninth, as a skilled ecclesiastical computist will plainly find.

The third consideration gives me a strong suspicion of fabrication, because the aforesaid letters of foundation, which the brothers at St. Peter’s in Erfurt display as originals, are protected by no one’s seal, although a hole appears in the parchment, as if the seal had fallen off through excessive age.

The fourth consideration is by no means inferior to the foregoing, because it is found plainly written in the life and deeds of blessed Wilhelm, abbot of Hirsau, that he constructed seven new monasteries of our order, among which the often-mentioned monastery of St. Peter in Erfurt is counted fourth in order. I deliberately pass over several other considerations, lest prolixity create tedium for the reader.

Therefore, just as I wrote and long ago showed by many arguments in that very first volume of my Hirsau chronicles, it was necessary for me to think thus and to prefer to fabrication the truth, which I believe has simply perished.

In the same year Charles Martel died, mayor of the palace of the Franks, duke of Brabant, and count [unclear] of Namur; since he was most warlike and fortunate in all things, he greatly extended the boundaries of the kingdom. He defeated the Saxons, Bavarians, Burgundians, Sorbs, [Bulgarians?] dwelling beyond the Danube, and also the Gascons, Vandals, Goths, Saracens in Spain, Frisians, and many other nations round about; he overcame them by war and the sword. He left three sons: Pippin, Carloman, and Grifo. Of these, Pippin and Carloman divided the paternal principate between themselves, excluding Grifo, and in this way they ruled over the Franks for four years. But after four years Carloman, moved by the divine spirit, relinquished the whole principate to his brother Pippin, went to Rome, and became a monk on Monte Cassino. Pippin then ruled the principate alone for six years; after King Childeric had been deposed, by common vote he was made king.

Grifo, grieving that he had been disregarded by his brothers, by the counsel of his mother seized the city of Laon and declared war on his brothers. His mother was the stepmother of Pippin and Carloman. They, swiftly gathering their army, surrounded Laon with a siege, captured the city, and held their brother prisoner for four years.

In these times there flourished in the kingdom of the Franks Saint Boniface, English by nation, a monk by profession, by dignity archbishop of the church of Mainz, an apostolic man who by his preaching converted the Thuringians and many others to Christ, for whom later, while preaching in Frisia, he suffered martyrdom.

He founded in the Buchonia forest two notable monasteries of our order, one of which is called Hersfeld in the diocese of Mainz, the other by the river Fulda flowing past it, where he was buried, in the diocese of Wuerzburg.

He also built several convents of nuns, among which there still stands out one of the monasteries in the town of Kitzingen beside the Main, in the diocese of Wuerzburg; over it he placed a holy virgin named Hadelloga, whom he named Thecla, of English origin. Another, near the town of Bischofsheim beside the Tauber, in the diocese of Mainz, over which he placed Saint Lioba, a virgin beloved of God, likewise English by country; of this convent nothing survives today.

In the seventh year, therefore, of Childeric king of the Franks, and also in the year of the Lord’s nativity 748, in the first indiction, Saint Burghard, English by nation, a monk by profession, was ordained by Saint Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, as first bishop of the church of Würzburg.
