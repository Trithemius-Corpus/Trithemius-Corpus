Beginning from the region nearest to the Tungri and proceeding thence hostilely through the Bellovaci beyond the Seine, they came as far as Spain, surrounding the noble city of Tarraco with a siege and utterly destroying it.

But Bartherus, king of the Franks, at last died in the eighteenth year of his reign, namely in the year of the Lord's Nativity 271, in the fourth Roman indiction; Hunibald sufficiently sets forth his many deeds.

*Clodius*, the elder son of King Bartherus, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for twenty-seven years. In his third year the Romans, sending legions into Alemannia and mindful of the earlier injury, sought it in war. Against their incursion the Alemanni advanced armed, having in their army thirty thousand fighters. A very hard battle was joined not far from Lake Benacus, in which nearly fifteen thousand of the Alemanni were slain.

In the twelfth year of King Clodius the Franks, under their leaders, invaded Gaul, and killed many thousands of the Roman guards and also of the Gauls; and, occupying a great part of inner Gaul, they dwelt there for seven years.

But King Clodius of the Franks died after this, in the twenty-[seventh?] year of his reign, [in the year] 298, in the first Roman indiction.

*Walther*, son of King Clodius, after his father reigned over the Franks for eight years, a magnanimous, strong, and warlike prince. In the time of his reign, when persecution had been proclaimed everywhere, Diocletian in the East and Maximian in the West overthrew the churches of Christ, burned the divine books in fires, and miserably afflicted the Christians in the tenth persecution after Nero. And Diocletian, in the first year of the aforementioned King Walther, ordered himself to be worshipped as a god; he also at the same time subjected Alexandria, with all Egypt, to the Romans.

In the first, second, and third years of Walther, king of the Franks, there were great disturbances in Britain, which is now called England. For after King Lucius had died some years before, he who, with his people, was the first to receive the faith of Christ from Pope Eleutherius, as we said above, in the one hundred eighty-fifth year, in the times of Farabert king of the Franks, the Romans sent the senator Senerus into Britain to take up the kingdom. When he too had been killed at York by Fulgenius, Basianus, son of that same Senerus, was admitted as king. When he also had been killed, Carausius seized the kingdom and despised the commands of the Romans. When he too died in the second year of Walther king of the Franks, the Romans sent Allectus to take up the kingdom.

Against him Asclepiodotus, a warlike, strong, and bold man, rising up, killed him with two Roman legions, set the diadem of the kingdom upon himself, and, against the will of the Caesars and the senate, reigned for ten years; he cultivated justice and, after the Romans had been excluded, kept the province in peace.
