For under King Faramund afterward, and under his successors, they obtained all Gaul by their own forces and overcame the Roman Empire.

But Marcomir, the aforesaid king of the Franks, died in the twenty-first year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 148, in the first indiction.

Clodomer, the elder son of the already-mentioned king of the Franks, reigned after his father for seventeen years: a man of singular prudence, modest toward his own people, most humane. He had a wife named Hasildis, daughter of the duke of the Rugians, by whom he begot three sons, Farabert, Nicanor, and Roric. The first of these, as we shall say, succeeded his father in the kingdom. The second was king of Frisia. The third, drowned in water not far from Novesium, at his death left his name to the river which is called Rora down to the present day. The place of Roric’s drowning was between [Neocum?], a castle on this side of the Rhine, and the old city which the inhabitants called Moradunum; in those times all Francia was alien to the knowledge of the Christian faith.

Clodomer, king of the Franks, died in peace in the seventeenth year of his reign, in the third Roman indiction. In his time the Gauls were considering an incursion against the Franks. But King Clodomer himself, wisely anticipating their movement, restrained them from doing it.

Farabert, the elder son of Clodomer, king of the Franks, succeeding his father, reigned for twenty years, beginning in the previously noted 165th year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the third Roman indiction. He renewed and again ratified the treaty of friendship, long before contracted through the Frankish king, as we said above, then confirmed by other kings, renewed, and often strengthened by a new enactment, with the Germans, Saxons, Teutons, Marcomanni, and Cimbri; this greatly displeased the Romans and stirred them to arms.

For in the sixth year of this King Farabert, which was the 171st year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the times of Lucius Aurelius Commodus Caesar, the Romans, not bearing with calm minds so great a concord of the Franks, brought forth a great army of their men. And so, when legions had been sent through Sarmatia into Germany against the Dacians, Marcomanni, Teutons, Saxons, and [unclear] Franks, they brought forth a great army of their men. Against their incursion the kings of the aforesaid peoples sent out such great forces of fighters that the Romans, defeated, were compelled to return without victory, with loss and disgrace.
