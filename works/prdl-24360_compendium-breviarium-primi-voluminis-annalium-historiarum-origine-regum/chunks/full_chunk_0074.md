“Listen, excellent citizens, to the unspeakable evil that lately occurred. [Chloderic?], the son of my kinsman Sigebert, ensnared his father with false words, saying that I was plotting against his life. And when he was fleeing through the [Buconian?] forest, robbers were sent against him, and he killed his own father. He too, while he was brooding over the treasures, was struck by someone I know not and perished. But in these matters I am in no way privy to the crime. For I cannot shed the blood of my kinsmen, since it is a sin.”

But because these things had so happened, “I give you counsel, if it seems acceptable: turn yourselves to me, so that you may be under my protection.” And they, applauding with hands and voices, set him up on a raised shield according to the custom of the nation, and appointed him king over them. And thus he received into his own power the kingdom of Sigebert with all his treasures. For God daily laid his enemies low beneath his hands, and greatly increased his kingdom, because he walked with an upright heart in the way of God’s commandments.

He also deceitfully seized King Chararic and his son; first tonsuring them, he had one ordained priest and the other deacon. Subjecting their treasures together with the kingdom to himself, he afterwards had both their heads cut off.

[Ragnachar?] also, the king ruling the Franks at Cambrai and living far too wantonly and luxuriously, together with Richarius his brother, were treacherously captured while fleeing from battle; striking them with an axe by his own hand, he killed them.

He also had [Rignomer?], their brother, who was reigning at Le Mans, killed with similar cruelty.

When these men had died, he usurped their kingdoms with their treasures for himself. He also killed many other kings of the Franks, all of whom had been begotten from the blood of his own kin, especially those of whom he was suspiciously very much afraid, lest they take the kingdom away from him; sparing no one, and in this way, when almost all his kinsmen had been extinguished, he enlarged his kingdom throughout all Gaul. But let Hunibald praise Clodoneus as much as he wished, as though he had done well in these matters: I, in this respect, cannot praise a parricide, and one who killed so many innocent men.

For on a certain day, in the presence of many of his courtiers, he is said to have spoken about these relatives and kinsmen of his, whom he himself had destroyed: “Woe to me, because, like a foreigner among strangers, I remain alone, and today I have no one of my relatives or kinsmen still living who could help me in any way in adversity, if it should come upon me, as friend is accustomed to help friend.” But he said this deceitfully, thinking that someone from among those standing around would answer by way of consolation: “Behold, you still have this man or that man from among your own [unclear],” so that those of his kinsmen who survived might themselves also be killed as quickly as possible.

In the seventeenth year of his reign, King Clodoneus, at the continual urging of Queen Clotild, who desired the cruel death of her parents to be avenged upon her paternal uncle, declared war on Gundebald, king of the Burgundians. When he had been defeated, Clodoneus assigned Burgundy to the tribute and subjection of the Franks.
