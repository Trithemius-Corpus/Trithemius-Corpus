Seventy-one thousand girls, placed on ships, were endangered at sea. The greater part of the girls was drowned; the rest were captured by barbarians. Vuania and Melga, Huns and Picts, took them. The girls perished for love of chastity. Vanius, king of the Huns, and Melga, of the Picts. All seventy-one thousand girls were Christians. Geoffrey, writer of the history of Britain, [unclear]. Marcomer, king of the Franks, was prudent, and trusted neither Maximus nor the Romans.*

When Dionotus had seen Conanus’s messenger, desiring to satisfy his wish, he gathered from the various provinces of Britain eleven thousand daughters of the nobles, and from the others, of lower rank, sixty thousand virgin women; all of them he ordered to assemble on a fixed day within the walls of London. He also ordered ships to be brought from all the coasts of Britain, sufficient for the transport of so great a multitude. Although this had pleased many in so great an assembly, nevertheless it displeased many, who loved their parents and homeland with greater affection. Nor perhaps were there lacking some who, preferring chastity to marriage, would rather have lost their life in any land than be married.

At last, when the things necessary for their transport had been prepared, the women who were to set out boarded the ships and passed through the River Thames into the seas. Finally, when they were turning their sails toward the Armoricans, that is, from north to south, suddenly a great storm arose at sea; the fleet was divided and the ships were separated from one another. Great danger threatened them all; the greater part of the ships was scattered into the deep sea and was drowned with the women.

Those, however, who escaped so great a danger were driven to barbarous nations, islands of the sea, and by an unknown people were either slain or consigned to perpetual servitude. For they had fallen into the army of the wicked princes Vuania and Melga, who, hired for pay by the emperor Gratian, were ravaging first Britain and then the maritime nations and surrounding islands, out of hatred for Maximus; by them they cruelly perished for love of chastity.

Now Vuania, or if you prefer, Gnavia, in the manner of the Gauls, was king of the Huns; Melga, however, was king of the Picts. The emperor Gratian had sent them, hired for pay, into the borders of Germany and also Gaul, so that they might afflict those who followed the party of Maximus. Therefore, raging through the coastal regions with both infantry and naval incursion, they encountered the aforesaid women and girls, cast by chance into those regions; and since they were beautiful Christians, they solicited them for intercourse with themselves. But when the women, faithful in Christ, refused to consent to the shameful lust of the wicked men, they were pitiably killed by the impious.

This lamentable history of seventy-one thousand holy virgins and women, apart from little children, Hunibald does not relate; but the aforementioned author Geoffrey of Monmouth does, at the end of the fourth book concerning the origin of the people and the deeds of the kings of the island of Britain, which was first called the island of giants, then from Brutus, the first king, was called Britain, afterward was named Albion, and finally obtained the name of England, which it retains to this day.

But the aforementioned Marcomirus, king of the Franks, because he was a prudent man, so governed the reins of his principate that he followed neither the party of Maximus nor that of Gratian, because the former had taken Armorica from the Franks, with many of them cruelly slain without cause, and the latter ruled the Romans, a people hostile to the Franks from their origin.
