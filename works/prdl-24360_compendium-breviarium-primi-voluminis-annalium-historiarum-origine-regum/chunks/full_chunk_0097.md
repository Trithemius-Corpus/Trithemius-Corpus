*The fifty-second.* Sigebert, king of the Franks in Austrasia, adopted Hildebert, son of Grunvald, mayor of the palace, as his son, and the kingdom.

In the following year Saint Judoc, son of the king of the Britons, despising kingdom and world, a pilgrim and hermit, rested in Christ in the district of Ponthieu.

In the eleventh year of King Clodoveus, Sigebert, king of Austrasia, his brother, died, leaving his son Dagobert, still very small, entrusted to the loyalty of Grunvald, mayor of the palace, so that by his aid he might be advanced to the kingdom. In the following year Grunvald had him tonsured by Dido, bishop of Poitiers, and sent him to Scotland, and, against good faith, raised up his own son Hildebert as king of the Austrasians. But the Franks, grieving over so great a breach of faith by Grunvald, captured him and presented him for judgment before King Clodoveus at Paris; he had him bound and made him waste away in prison by a bitter death. After Grunvald’s son had been cast down, he made his own younger son Hilderic king of the Austrasians, and avenged the wrong done to his nephew.

Clodoveus, king of the Franks, because of an insufficiently reverent contact with the body of Saint Dionysius, was for two years deranged and out of his mind, and died in the seventeenth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 662, in the fifth indiction.

The eldest son of King Clodoveus reigned after his father over the Franks for four years. His mayor of the palace was Ebervuin, whom [unclear] by nature deceitful, prompt in counsel for crimes, perverse in action.

From this time onward the kings of the Franks, down to Pippin, father of Charles the Great, for almost ninety years, degenerating from the solid men in courage and knowledge, reigned in name alone, accomplishing nothing worthy of the royal office; but all power of governing the kingdom was in the hands of the princes of the court, who were called mayors of the palace. The kings, however, remained at home idle, doing or arranging nothing, but sluggishly devoted themselves to eating and drinking. Only once in the year, at the Kalends of May, did they proceed in public and display themselves to the peoples to be looked upon and greeted, and so returned home.

In these times Queen Bechtildis, mother of Lothar and formerly the wife left behind by King Clodoveus, founded two distinguished monasteries of our order in Gallican France, one of which is called Corbie and the other Chelles; in them many most holy and most learned monks became famous.
