Up to this year Vuasthald the Scythian, or Sicamber in his native tongue, brought down the historical work of his own people, over more or less 758 years, beginning from the destruction of Troy. In it he describes the various dwellings and settlements of his race, and also its leaders and wars, kings, and their various deeds in order. After him Hunibald the Frank, from the songs and writings of the priests of the Franks, continued the history for 1,426 years, down to the last year of King Clovis, who was the first of the kings of the Franks to receive the faith of Christ, baptized by Saint Remigius in the year of the Lord 499.

Antenor, the eldest son of King Marcomir, was raised to the kingship in the same year, and after his father reigned thirty years. He was held in the greatest honor among his people; he secured for himself a very firm friendship with the Saxons, with whose aid he victoriously fought many battles with the Romans and Gauls. Priam and Nicanor, his brothers, assisted him with the other leaders in faithful continuity; after crossing the river, they often inflicted many troubles upon the Gauls. Sunno also, the king’s paternal uncle, with his sons, always adhered to Antenor himself in all things with the greatest fidelity, and trained him in government as his own son, yet revered him no less as his lord.

King Antenor had a wife named Cambra, who was the daughter of Belinus, king of Britain. She was a woman more beautiful than the daughters of the whole kingdom, and of such prudence that the king and the princes of the Franks were always accustomed in all things to obey her counsel no less than that of some divine power. The customs of the people, still excessively rough with Scythian savagery, she rendered more cultivated by laws; she taught the nobles to build cities and castles. There she instituted the adornment of the forehead; she gave women the knowledge of sowing flax and committing hemp to the earth, and of weaving and preparing them for clothing and varied use. She composed laws; she pronounced justice. She presented herself as a seer, a priestess of Diana, and an Aliruna.

And since she was a woman in sex, but bore the mind of a king and priest, she became a laughing-stock among the Saxons in a proverb: whenever they heard anyone, beyond the ordinary, doing or saying something rather prudent, as though he wished to imitate the prudence of Cambra, they would rebuke him and say, “Look, camer.” In their language this is said: “Si camer,” as though they were saying, “Do you too know the prudence of Cambra?” Thus with time the proverb took root, so that the two latest words, that is, the adverb and the noun, “si” and “camber,” yielded a single word, which as a whole makes Sicamber.
