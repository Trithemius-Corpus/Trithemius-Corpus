Walter, king of the Franks, in these times rested from wars, and at last finally died in the eighth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 306, in the ninth Roman indiction, in which year there was an earthquake.

Dagobert, the elder son of Walter, king of the Franks, succeeded his father and reigned for eleven years. He was a man pious toward his subjects, gentle, most humane; and while they held him in singular love and great reverence, they feared him. In the administration of justice according to the laws of the Franks he was most equitable, and, preserving each man’s right unimpaired, he did injury to no one, nor did he permit it to be done by another.

In his fourth year Coelus, duke of Gloucester, rising up against his lord Asclepiadotus, king of Britain, killed him by the counsel of the Romans. Rejoicing at this news, the Romans sent the senator Constantius to Britain with legions, a man no less prudent than warlike. When Coelus, who had already occupied the kingdom, learned that he had landed on the shore, he did not dare to engage him; he sent messengers to him, asked for peace, and obtained it, on condition that the tribute should be paid firmly to the Romans each year according to the ancient custom.

When peace had been confirmed, however, King Coelus died not long afterward, within the fortieth day. Constantius took as wife his daughter, named Helena, a virgin second to none in beauty, wisdom, Christian religion, and piety toward God; by her he begot Constantine, afterward the great emperor, and obtained with her the kingdom of Britain, with all the nobles consenting.

But Dagobert, the above-mentioned king of the Franks, died in the eleventh year of his reign, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 317, in the fifth Roman indiction.

Clogio, the son of King Dagobert, succeeded his father and reigned for only two years. For in the second year of his reign the Romans and Gauls, fiercely invading the kingdom of the Franks in the regions on this side of the mountains, began to lay everything waste with fire and sword. But King Clogio, having gathered forces as best he could, met the enemy with the help of the Germans; when he fought too incautiously, he was killed. Yet the Franks for that reason did not desist from the conflict, but fought bravely.

For Clodomer, the brother of the slain king, having drawn his body back from the battle-line, put on his arms, so that he might be thought by everyone to be the king himself, and so that neither his friends, once the king’s death was known, should lose heart, nor the enemies, exulting, should presume a certain hope of victory. And so Duke Clodomer, clad in the king’s arms and having returned to the battle-line, since he was thought by all to be the king himself, struck constancy into his own men and fear into the enemy.
