Clodomir, great king, to the satraps and Gallic peoples. We marvel at your rashness, in that you demand back from us the land which our forefathers long ago, by right of war, most justly vindicated with the sword from yours, as from unjust neighbors and their manifest enemies. Consider that our forces are far superior to yours, and cease from this wrongful rashness of folly. Remember the disasters which you formerly suffered, as you deserved, at the hands of our divine father, numbered among the gods, and of his parents, and be quiet, hiding in your holes with the foxes. It is ours to command you, and yours humbly to obey our commands. But if you presume to come against us, we have determined to meet you with power and to defend with the sword what our ancestors handed down to us.

When the Gauls had received these letters and messengers, they were stirred to anger, and resolved to take vengeance on the Sequanians for the Sicambri. After a bridge had been skillfully built over the Meuse, they led across a huge army of fighters. King Clodomir met them powerfully with 264,000 men, and, having divided the army into parts, placed one in ambush, sent another [unclear] in the region [unclear], and led the third part, which was the greater in number of soldiers, against the enemy.

The Sicambri engaged with the Gauls in a place not far from the river Meuse, which is called Delon, and for some time the battle was fought with doubtful fortune. At last the Sicambri, feigning flight, began gradually to retreat until they had finally drawn the Gauls rather far from the river Meuse to the place of ambush. There the Sicambri then stood immovable, and those who had earlier pretended flight fought back most bravely, like giants. Meanwhile the soldiers, going around, came to the crossing of the river and, after the enemy guards had been killed or driven far off, seized the bridge first. The rest at once burst out from the ambush and, attacking the enemy fiercely, cut them down from behind, slaughtered them, and killed them without pity. Finally those who could, seeking safety in flight, since they did not know that the bridge had been seized in advance by the Sicambri, were enclosed on every side and cut down by the enemy; nor was there any place to escape death, since those lying hidden pressed upon them. With night coming on, few out of many escaped alive, those who were able to flee into the dark groves and woods.

Thereafter the kingdom of the Sicambri rested from the tumult of wars for a few years; but afterward, when peace was broken, the people often took up mighty arms again. The other deeds and wars of King Clodomir, which he had with [exterminations?], we have written, following Hunibald, in the first volume of our Annals. King Clodomir died in the eighteenth year of his reign, 230 years before the birth of the Lord, leaving three sons by Queen Ermedrude, who had been the daughter of the king of the Teutons: Nicanor, Helianus, [unclear].
