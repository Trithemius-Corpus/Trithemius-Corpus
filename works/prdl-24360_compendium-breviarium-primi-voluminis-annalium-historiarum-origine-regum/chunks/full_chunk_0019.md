He said: “Do not, noble men, fight against justice, and against the laws which I decreed with your mature consent, because it is easier to bind with the hand the wind blowing in the air than to turn the mind of King Basan away from the execution of equity.” Having heard this, the princes were silent; but the father himself said to his son: “It is not I, son, who kill you, but the law of the fatherland, which you knowingly transgressed”; and, having seized him, he beheaded him with his own hand.

From that time the Sicambri began to fear their king Basan to such a degree that no one among them, great or small, presumed either to say or to do anything evil against the law. Hence the priests of the gods called the king by the Greek word [unclear], that is, Just Basan. But the common people called him Theobasan, which in the German tongue signifies Basangoth, because all venerated him as a friend of the gods, afterwards to be deified.

From this a proverb arose among the Sicambri, so that whenever one person saw or heard from another anything contrary to the law, he would say: “Hort opp lieffman, kent gy nit dy grote kunig Basan.” As if he had said: “Cease from such things, good man; do you not know how just the great king Basanus is? If he perceived anyone saying or doing unlawful things, without doubt he would kill him as a transgressor.”

In the fifth year of King Basan, the king of the Orchades, having several island chieftains as auxiliaries, declared war on the Sicambri because of the rejection of his daughter, whom King Basanus, having taken as wife, had repudiated and sent back to her father; the cause of this repudiation we described in the first volume of the Annals. But King Basanus, learning of the arrival of his adversaries, advanced against them with a great multitude of fighters to the seashore, not far from the place where the Franks afterwards built Mesoburg. When the Orchades saw from their ships so great an army of the Sicambri, they did not dare to advance onto land.

When the Gauls and Morini again broke in toward the east, where the Sicambri had placed colonies in the region of the Tungri, King Basanus met them with a strong force of his men, and laid them low with so great a slaughter that scarcely a few out of many escaped. Having obtained victory, he built several castles, very strong fortifications, on both sides of the river Meuse, in which he stationed garrisons of the Sicambri.

But on the upper part of the river he built a very strong castle, which he named Basanburg after his own name; in it the kings of the Sicambrian Franks after him dwelt for a long time, on account of the pleasantness and strength of the place.

In the twenty-sixth year of his reign, King Basanus, after many battles bravely fought with various nations round about, wishing to show himself pontiff to the gods, renewed their sacred rites with much superstition.
