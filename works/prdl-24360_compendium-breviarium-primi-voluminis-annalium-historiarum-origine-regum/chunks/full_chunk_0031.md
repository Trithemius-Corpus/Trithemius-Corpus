*The eighteenth, Clogio, reigned for 30 years.*

Clogio, the elder son of Francus the king, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for 30 years: a man most cunning, versatile in intellect, an augur, magician, and most curious astrologer. Concerning him the aforementioned pontiff of the Franks, the counsellor and seer Clodomirus, set out many stupendous marvels in verse, of which we made mention in the first volume.

His mother, named Lothildis, daughter of the king of the Doringi, was the chief priestess or priest of the temple of Pallas, devoted to the magical arts, by the practice of which she did many marvels. For, as the aforesaid authors wrote, by her songs and incantations she destroyed whichever enemies of her son she wished, made them unfortunate, and, though absent however far away, killed them by the ministry of demons; by sorcery she procured victory for the Franks, and fear, terror, and flight for their enemies. Rains, thunders, flashes of lightning, and terrible storms in the air she brought on by her songs as often as, where, and upon whom she wished.

In the fourth year of his reign the Romans again launched an expedition into Germany; the allied kings, meeting them with no lack of energy, did not cease bravely to repel force with arms until they compelled the enemy to retreat and flee. For King Clogio, in the name of the Franks, having engaged in battle with the Roman army, fought most bravely for his people, and at last, after many had fallen on both sides, the Romans departed without victory.

In the eighteenth year of the reign of the aforementioned Clogio, on the eighth day before the Kalends of January, there was born in Bethlehem of Judah the Lord, the savior of the whole universe, Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, from the untouched ever-virgin Mary. In that year, according to Hunibald, by an edict of Caesar Augustus the world subject to Roman rule was enrolled head by head; this, besides the mystical reason, being done by divine providence, was not without literal meaning, the Franks providing the occasion.

In the same year also King Clogio, at his mother’s urging, made his son Phrisus, his second-born, duke of that region which lies by the German sea toward the north; and from the name of that duke it was called Phrisia, as Hunibald maintains, down to the present day. Again, after ten years, with the consent of the aforementioned King Clogio and of all the princes of the people of the Franks, the same son Phrisius was raised to kingship, on this condition, namely, that all kings of Phrisia, like the other dukes, should remain forever subject to the king of the Franks, should pay each year a tribute of 260 oxen, and should fight with the Franks, in the manner of the other allies, against whatsoever enemies.

But Clogio, king of the Franks, died after many brave deeds, in the thirtieth year of his reign, which was the twentieth year of the Lord’s nativity. Hunibald writes many things concerning his character, prophecies, and oracles.
