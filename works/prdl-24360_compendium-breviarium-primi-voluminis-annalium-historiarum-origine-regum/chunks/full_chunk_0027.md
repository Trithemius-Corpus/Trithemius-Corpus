But King Cassander, after many battles bravely fought with the Gauls and Romans, died in the twenty-first year of his reign; before the birth of the Lord, however, in the year 72. He was buried at Neopagus with many treasures.

*[Antharius?], the son of King Cassander.* After his father he reigned for twenty-five years. Of him Hunibald writes that he crushed the Moguntines, Roman colonists, in a pitiable slaughter; he burned and overthrew the city, which afterward is seen to have been transferred and built in that place now [unclear], across the river Ciana and the valley, on the slope of the mountain by the bank of the Rhine. This lamentable desolation of the Moguntines took place in the sixteenth year of King Arthar, which was the fifty-sixth year before the birth of Christ, as is shown from the sequence of the times.

King Anthar, with the help of the Germans, entered Gaul, burned villages, and many times carried away limitless booty and spoils. He wrought great devastation throughout Gaul, burned cities, destroyed villages, overthrew castles, shattered the strength of the people, and reduced a region long fruitful to a wilderness.

But when King Antharius had grown old, in the last year of his reign, which was the seventh before the birth of Christ, when the Gauls again broke into the region of the Sicambri and crossed the Meuse, he met them with the army of his people to fight. There, when he engaged rather incautiously, he was surrounded by the enemy and perished with twenty thousand Sicambri in the twenty-fifth year of his reign. And the Gauls on that occasion brought great calamity upon the Sicambri.

*[Francus?], the elder son of King Antharius*, from whom the Sicambri were thereafter called Franks, as Hunibald is the authority, reigned after his father for twenty-eight years: a fierce, daring, and warlike man, a most vigorous defender of his people. He avenged the death of his father severely enough upon the Gauls, and waged many wars with foreign nations, always a victor and glorious triumphator. The people of the Franks rightly boast that he was the founder of both their name and their honor. This King Francus was accustomed to fight, not to flee; he was beloved by his own people and very mild, but to all his enemies always exceedingly to be feared.
