In the forty-fifth year of his reign, Lotharius the king, now that his brother Childebert was dead, and all the others, both his nephews and his brothers, had either been killed or consumed by natural death, alone obtained the monarchy of the whole kingdom of the Franks for five years.

In the forty-ninth year of his reign Lotharius, king of the Franks, pursued with an army Chramn, his son, long rebellious and doing many evil things in the kingdom, when he had fled into New Brittany, which was formerly called Armorica, to Conobrus, duke of Brittany. When battle was joined, he killed Duke Conobrus, obtained Brittany, captured his son, and, consumed by fires together with his wife and daughters, reduced him to ashes.

In the following year, that is, the fiftieth of his reign, but the year of the Lord’s nativity 563, in the twelfth indiction of the Romans, this same Lotharius, king of the Franks, dies, leaving four sons, Charibert, Gunthram, Sigebert, and Chilperic, who again divided their father’s kingdom among themselves. The aforesaid King Lotharius built the abbey of Saint Medard, formerly the first bishop of Noyon and Tournai, at Soissons, into which he translated the body of the same saint from Tournai and placed it with the highest reverence and honor. But King Childebert, his brother, built the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Pres, in which he deposited the stole of Saint Vincent the martyr together with his dalmatic. But the order of the abridgement now requires that we pursue the division of the kingdom.

Chilperic the Younger, son of Lotharius king of the Franks, is therefore placed by us before his brothers in this abridgement of the kings, because through him the line of kings descending in straight order is continued down to Childeric. He reigned in the city of Soissons two years before the death of his father, and, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-two years. And thus the whole time that he reigned was twenty-four years. The name of his first wife was Audovera, by whom he begot two sons, Clovis and Merovech, both of whom, killed in adolescence by the deceit of their stepmother, as we said in the first volume, perished.

When Audovera had given birth to an infant while the king was absent, since no one who would raise the boy to be baptized from the sacred font was at hand, the queen mother herself, deceived by Fredegund’s wicked counsel, fulfilled the office of co-mother, and raised from baptism the infant whom she had borne. Fredegund, the contriver of the deceit, joyfully announcing this to the king when he soon came, said: “You will not have Audovera as wife henceforth, O king; she preferred to be your co-mother rather than your spouse. Now I am yours.” For this reason King Chilperic repudiated Audovera his wife, asserting that by decree of the canons it was forbidden him to have his co-mother as wife.
