He was also always in conflict with the Gauls, and fought many battles with them; and often, fighting most bravely with his men, he triumphed gloriously.

At length he died in the thirty-fourth year of his reign, in the one hundred ninety-sixth year before the birth of Christ, leaving five sons: Marcomirus, who succeeded to the kingdom; Antenor, who ruled the maritime regions; Priam, who died at his maternal uncle's in Britain; Helenus, who held the duchy on this side of the Rhine, between Saxony and the Rhine, over the Tegarani; and Clodius, duke of the [Mosanorum?]. *Son of Nicanor, the elder by birth of the king's [unclear], succeeding his father*, he reigned twenty-eight years. A foremost man in his genealogy, an augur, a seer, an interpreter of dreams and of events, he [unclear]. He ordered the great deeds of his people, whenever leisure allowed, to be sung before him, both in the palace and in the temples, so that their spirit, stirred by the example of these men, might be directed to the defense of his nation and always to the enlargement of the kingdom. This was the custom among the elders and the Germans: that the deeds or sayings of heroes worthy of remembrance should be entrusted by the priests of the temples to native songs; and, after the youth had been instructed in learning, remembering, and singing these, [unclear] failed at last.

King Marcomirus, however, ruled the people of the Sicambri at all times prudently and with the greatest moderation; he increased the worship of the tutelary gods; he fought many battles with the Romans, the Gauls, and the other nations round about; and in his time he enlarged the borders of the first kingdom not a little. On these matters Hunibald composed a fuller history, which here, out of love of brevity, we pass over.

King Marcomirus died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, which was the one hundred sixty-eighth year before the incarnation of our Savior Jesus Christ. *The first-born son of King Marcomirus of the Sicambri after his father*, reigned eleven years. In his time the sword of the Romans, directed into Gaul, the Sicambri by no small [unclear] repelled.

After many battles fought with the Gauls, in the last year of his reign King Clodius, having gathered a huge army both of his own men and of Saxons, again fought with them not far from the fortress of Basanburg. In this battle, while looking about with too little caution in the battle-line, he was killed by his adversaries. But the Sicambri, emboldened, notwithstanding the death of the king, continued the war; and, fighting bravely, at length defeated the Gauls, and after killing many of them, put others to flight. And many Franks perished. *The Franks always pursued the Gauls in war. Nicanor, king of the Sicambri, dies.*
