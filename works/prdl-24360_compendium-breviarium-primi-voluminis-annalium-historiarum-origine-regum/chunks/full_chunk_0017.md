*The Goths devastate the borders of the Saxons.*

*Five German kings with 260,000 men.*

*One hundred thousand of the Goths are killed.*

*Twenty-five thousand Saxons are killed.*

*The Gauls make war on the Franks while their king is far away.*

*The Gauls are defeated by the Sicambri through cunning and courage.*

*Many thousands of the Gauls are slain.*

*Three leaders of the Franks were killed.*

*Diocles, king of the Sicambri, dies.*

In the twelfth year of his reign, a multitude of the people of the Goths came forth from the Scanzian islands, which are now called Gothic, and, pouring into the borders of the Saxons, began to carry out a very grievous devastation, consuming with sword and fire whatever it could find. Against this most savage people, which was accustomed to spare no one, five kings went forth to war, namely the king of the Doringi, the king of the Teutons, the king of the Rugii, and Diocles himself, king of the Sicambri, with many princes, commanders, and warriors; their army was numbered at 260,000 men.

War is waged; a fierce battle is joined with the enemy, in which more than one hundred thousand fighters from the army of the Goths were said to have been slain. On the side of the five kings, twenty-five thousand fell, among whom Heriman also, king of the Doringi, perished. But the Saxons, having gained the victory, divided the spoils of the enemy among the allies with an equal balance; and, when the enemy had been driven far off in flight, the kings returned to their own territories.

Meanwhile, when the Gauls dwelling beyond the Meuse learned that King Diocles had gone out of the country with his army, they gathered in a great multitude and hastened to make war against the Sicambri. When the commanders of the Sicambri, to whom the region had been entrusted, had learned this through scouts, they gathered the whole army of the remaining soldiery of the people and, leading it against the enemy, divided it into three parts. Two of these they arranged as separate battle-lines, and the third they placed in hiding-places for ambush. But as the Gauls approached and brought forward one battle-line, when they saw that the forces were being ordered otherwise, with their opponents hastening to close combat.

For as soon as the Gauls came into sight of the Sicambri, the Sicambri, impatient of delay, directed one line against the enemy formation. When the battle had now begun, the second line, circling around from Nijmegen against the enemy on the flank, threw their order into confusion. And when the fighting was fierce on both sides and the army of the Gauls was already inclining toward flight, suddenly the Sicambri burst forth from the ambushes and, making an attack, held the Gauls enclosed among them, so that no place of flight lay open to those preparing to flee. Thus the wretched Gauls, enclosed in the midst of the Sicambri, were slaughtered to death like cattle, and they suffered so great a massacre that, out of their eighty-eight thousand who had assembled for war, scarcely ten thousand, saved by flight through the benefit of night, escaped.

From the Sicambri, Duke Marcomir, Duke Helenus, and Duke Antenor perished, along with almost four thousand. Nevertheless, the victory, together with the spoils of the enemy, fell to the Sicambri.

King Diocles, however, throughout all the days of his reign, stood vigorously in conflict with the Gauls; meanwhile there was scarcely a year in which he did not harass them with some incursion or battle.
