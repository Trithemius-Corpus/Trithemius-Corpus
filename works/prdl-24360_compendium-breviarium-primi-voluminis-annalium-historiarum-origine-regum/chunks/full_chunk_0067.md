In the eleventh year of Meroveus, king of the Franks, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 457, in the tenth Roman indiction, the Franks took the city of the Treveri on the following occasion; Attila, king of the Huns, had captured it in war a few years before and had crushed it with grievous devastation. After the death of Valentinian, whom the friends of the patrician Aetius had killed in the same year, and who after Theodosius had reigned with Marcian, Avitus was appointed emperor by the Romans; under him the city of the Treveri was captured.

At that time there was in the city of the Treveri a certain senator named Lucius, who had a wife judged by very many to be most beautiful; Avitus was wasting away in shameful love for her. For this reason, feigning illness, he lay down on his bed and, as though to obtain relief from his sickness, ordered all the senatorial women of the city to come to visit him in succession. Among the others came the wife of Lucius, suspecting no evil; Avitus at once violated her. When the crime had been committed, the woman returned weeping to her husband’s house. On the next day Caesar Avitus rose from his bed, healthy in body but polluted in conscience by the crime, and said to Lucius when he met him: “You have beautiful baths indeed, Lucius, but you wash in cold water,” hinting at the foul, unnatural discharge of his wife. But Lucius understood the figure, took it grievously, and, messengers and letters having been sent in the utmost secrecy to the Franks, promised that if they came as quickly as possible, he would hand over the city of the Treveri into their power without doubt, delay, or difficulty.

For up to that point the Treveri, for many years past, had sided with the Romans and their kings with stubborn attachment. But the Franks, not forgetful of the slaughter and wrongs which they had once suffered from Nannenus and Quintinus, princes of the Treveri, at the Carbonarian Forest in the time of Duke Dagobert, the interrex, immediately gathered forces and hastened gladly to the destruction of the Treveri. When the city had been captured, the Franks plundered everything and subjected it to their kingdom.

In these times the kingdom of the Franks gradually took on great growth and extended its borders in many ways throughout the Gauls. Hence the kings of the nation, who from their first entrance into Germany had held seats around the Rhine, where Holland now is, with the peoples who are called Gelderlanders, Westphalians, Clivenses, and Frisians, for almost eight hundred and seventy years, at [Neopagus?], Duisburg, and in the other places and castles on this side of the Rhine, transferred the dwelling-place of their habitation into the Gauls, rarely resting from wars, until at last they subjected all Germany, Gaul also, and at the same time Italy, to the kingdom of the Franks.

And so the kingdom of the Franks, which took its beginning from one small people, through much labor, through most bloody wars, through the very great bloodshed of kings, dukes, nobles, soldiers, and commoners, over about nine hundred years at last grew immeasurably; it took on growth beyond all the kingdoms of all Europe, so that it ruled with power over many kingdoms and peoples, and from many kingdoms there was made one which, gathered by the sword, for the honor of the conqueror, would be called, and was, by the name of the Franks
