*The Franks, scattered, are cut down in turn by the enemy.*

Therefore Nannenus and Quintinus, when they had arrived with the army of the Treveri at the places in which the remnants of the Franks had remained, awaiting the return of their companions, drew up the battle line and pursued the fugitives as far as the Carbonarian Forest, forcing them to withdraw, after no small number of them had been slaughtered. From this arose the hatred of the Franks against the Treveri, on account of which they afterward crossed the Rhine and began to winter in the territory of the Treveri and to lay everything waste.

When these things had been done, it seemed useful to Quintinus that they should lead the whole army of the Treveri into Francia and forestall them, unprepared, by a sudden incursion. But Quintinus’s plan did not please Nannenus, who did not doubt that the Franks would be bolder in resisting on their own soil. Therefore Nannenus, parting from Quintinus with his men, returned to Mainz.

But Quintinus, persisting in his plan, crossed the Rhine near the fortress of Musium and led out the army, intending to destroy the Franks at the first assault, with a spirit higher and greater than his strength for winning. But the Franks, not sluggish, met the Gauls with a manly encounter, and when battle had been joined many thousands of the Treveri perished, with few saved. In that engagement Heracleus, tribune of the Roman legion, fell, and almost all who had been in command of the soldiers perished with him by the missiles and sword of the Franks. For, with the exception of a few to whom dark night and the woods offered safety and the opportunity of hiding, the whole army of Quintinus perished by sword, bow, and marsh.

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 394, in the seventh indiction, the emperor Valentinian died by a noose at Vienne, a city of Gaul, through the deceit and severity of Arbogast, master of the soldiers; and a certain Eugenius, a Roman, by the plotting of the same Arbogast, seized the rule of the Gauls and reigned for three years. Afterward, when war was joined with the emperor Theodosius, he was slain by him.

The Franks, having heard of the death of Caesar Valentinian, resolved to cross the Rhine and invade Gaul. Arbogast, understanding their plan, forestalled them: in the depth of winter, when the Rhine was frozen, he led an army across at Cologne Agrippina and began to lay waste with hostile incursion the Franks nearest the Rhine.

Against him, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s nativity, 394, the leaders of the Franks moved a powerful army; engaging the enemy, they killed more than twelve thousand Gauls and put the rest to flight with Arbogast.

After this, in the year of the Lord 497, the emperor Theodosius came from the East into Gaul with a great army against Eugenius. When Eugenius learned this, he was afraid; and coming to the Rhine, with great prayers and promises he implored the aid of the Franks and Saxons, which nevertheless he could not obtain. Therefore Theodosius, coming into Gaul, joined battle with Eugenius, and, having won the victory, slew him with many others. But Arbogast, the instigator of all evils, in despair, killed himself with his own sword.
