From this it is plainly clear that, just as the Franks who remained with the Germans use the language and customs of the Germans, so also those who entered Gaul with the kings and remained with the Gauls changed both their customs and their manner of speaking together with their settlements; and those whom nature had made Germans from the beginning of their birth, change of place in the midst of the Gauls likewise made Gauls in speech and customs. Indeed, when they were in Troy, they practiced Trojan customs and speech; among the Scythians likewise Scythian ones; in Germany they learned and kept German ones, and in Gaul Gallic ones. So too Aeneas, coming from the Trojans into Italy with his people, gradually forgetting his native speech, began to use the customs and language found at Rome.

After this Meroveus, king of the Franks, seized by sickness, died in the pagan worship of idols, as all his forebears had done, in the twelfth year of his reign, but in the year of the Lord’s nativity 458, in the eleventh indiction. The son of King Meroveus, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned twice through a varied change of affairs. For first after his father he reigned only one year; then, because of his insolence and debauchery, as we shall say, he was deprived of the kingdom and fled into Thuringia. After him the Franks set over themselves as king a certain Aegidius, a Roman; when he had been driven out after three years, Hilderic was recalled from Thuringia into Francia and reigned twenty-two years. And the whole time of his reign, from his first establishment after his father down to his death, came to twenty-six years; yet he reigned only twenty-three, since Aegidius, as we have said, occupied the kingdom for three years while he himself was in exile in Thuringia with King Basinus.

After only one year had been completed in the kingdom, the nobles of the kingdom, conspiring against him, wished to kill him, because, living too insolently and wantonly, he had defiled by his foul conduct many of their daughters and wives and those of the nobles. When he learned this, he fled with a few men into Thuringia, and remained with Basinus, king of the Thuringians, his kinsman, for three years. He withdrew, however, after being deprived by the Franks, in the year of the Lord 459, in the twelfth indiction.

After his departure from the kingdom, in that same year, the princes of the Franks at once set over themselves as king a certain Aegidius, a Roman prefect at Soissons, who occupied the kingdom of the Franks for three years while Hilderic was in exile. He, continually raging against the chief princes of the kingdom, slaughtered many, having no reasonable cause apart from empty suspicion. Therefore the rest, fearing that similar things would at some time threaten them, unanimously arranged a conspiracy against the tyrant, judging that it would be more tolerable to suffer that man to rule the kingdom who abused the daughters and wives of his subjects than to endure longer that man who thirsted for nothing more than the blood of princes.

In the year, therefore, of the Lord’s nativity 462, in the [unclear] indiction. *The kingdom of the Franks today is and remains divided in two.* *The Franks are more often reckoned with the peoples among whom they were.* *Meroveus, king of the Franks, dies.* *The forty-sixth.* *Hilderic reigned twenty-three years.* *Hilderic, an insolent and debauched king.* *Hilderic lived twenty-six years in the kingdom.* *King Hilderic, in fear of the Franks, flees into Thuringia.* *Aegidius the Roman is made king by the Franks.* *Aegidius, excessively cruel, is deposed.* *Between two evils one is more tolerable than the other.*
