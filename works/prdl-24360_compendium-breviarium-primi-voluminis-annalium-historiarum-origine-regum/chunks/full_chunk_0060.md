Theodosius, victorious, died on his return at Milan; his body was brought back to Constantinople and buried with due honor. After him his two sons succeeded in the empire: Arcadius in the East, and Honorius ruled among the Romans.

In the following year, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 388, in the eleventh Roman indiction, Dagobert died, interrex of the Franks, son of the late most noble king Clogio, brother of King Marcomir.

*Genebaldus.* Genebaldus, the elder son of the aforementioned duke Dagobert, succeeding first in the government of the kingdom of the Franks, presided over the people and the dukes for twenty-one years. Yet he neither wrote himself king nor was called king by others, but, content with the title of duke, usurped nothing of royal dignity for himself. He had two brothers, Marcomir and Sunno, who, together with the other dukes of the Franks, faithfully administered the commonwealth of the people, its wars, and its affairs by common vote.

In the year of the Lord’s Nativity 403, in the second Roman indiction, Marcomir, duke of the eastern Franks, died. He was a vigorous man but a pagan, dwelling among the Suebi at Würzburg among the [Dorigi?], and was buried according to his ancestral superstition on the mountain called Frankenberg.

To him in the Peapolitan duchy of the Franks succeeded his elder son, named Faramund, a prudent, bold, strong, warlike man, who presided over his people for fifteen years. Afterwards, as we shall say, he was elected king of the Franks by the unanimous consent of the dukes and nobles of the people.

After this, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 410, in the eighth Roman indiction, while the Franks, from hatred of the Romans and Gauls, looked the other way, Carocus, king of the Vandals, came out from the Scanzian islands, or, as they are now called, the Gothic islands, with an innumerable multitude of people. He made his way through Germany and Saxony and, with the consent of the Franks, crossed the Rhine and entered Gaul; after ravaging the region round about, he grievously afflicted the natives of the land.

He had a mother, on whose nod he depended, who said to him: “If you seek an eternal name, listen to and follow my counsel. Destroy all the buildings that former kings and princes constructed; kill the people whom they spared. For you will not be able to construct better or more beautiful buildings than those they constructed long ago; nor, by piety, moderation, or clemency in sparing those whom you have conquered, will you make for yourself a glorious name.”

Using the wicked counsel of his impious mother, Carocus crossed the Rhine and wiped out, burned, and utterly destroyed the city of Mainz on this side of the Rhine, together with all its inhabitants. Then he attacked Worms, and afterwards Speyer, with similar fury; after killing all the citizens whom he found, he overthrew them.

Moving from there, he besieged, captured, and destroyed the very ancient city of the Treveri on this side of the Moselle, which was scarcely breathing a little after the slaughter by the Franks. From Trier he moved to the city of Metz, which, when the wall had miraculously fallen by divine agency, he captured and overthrew, all its inhabitants being killed.
