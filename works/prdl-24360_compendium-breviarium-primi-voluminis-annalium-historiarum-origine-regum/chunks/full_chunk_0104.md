Moreover I gave to Saint Peter and his monks, for various uses, the forest which, because of the great number of deer, is called Hirsbrulis, from the southern side of the city toward the east as far as the boundaries of the region of Orlau, which region I handed over in benefice to Pepin, count palatine. I also gave from my court, for the use of the brethren, fifty modii of wheat, one hundred of barley, and as many of oats, and all my other possessions in Thuringia. For the maintenance of this place beloved to me I gave my ministerials with all their offspring and possession, whose names are these: Gisbotus, Eckhardus, Vuolffholdus, Hysen, Egenhen, Vuolffgirus, Omarus, Sigebertus, Theodobertus, Theodoricus, Adelbotus, Adelgerus, Gelferatus, Hingfridus, Huntzelinus, and all the rest, my male servants and female servants in Thuringia. In order that this conveyance to my Lord Jesus Christ may remain firm eternally, we have caused it to be established by our letters, we have requested it to be confirmed by the ban of Rigibert, bishop of Mainz, and we have bound it with our ring and completed it by the display of our seal. Charter at Mainz, on the Kalends of March, in the presence of Bishop Rigebert. The sign of Dagobert, most unconquered king of the Franks. The witnesses of all these matters are these: Pepin, mayor of the palace; his son Charles; Rigibert, bishop of Mainz; Gunthernus, pagan count; Eckebi, count; the whole church in Thuringia around the Rhine in Mainz. These things were done in the seven hundred sixth year of the Lord’s incarnation, in the ninth indiction.

Far be it from me, Trithemius infers, to fight against the truth of this so ancient asserted foundation, if it holds up; but how truth can hold up in it I do not sufficiently see. Therefore I shall also note here briefly what reasons for doubting occur to me, after those, namely, which I wrote for Hirsau.

The first consideration which prevents me from conceding that Dagobert was the founder of the often-mentioned monastery of Erfurt is that the reckoning of the years does not agree; for if this very foundation is ascribed to Dagobert the Great, it contradicts the instrument of the letters no less than the truth of the computed time. For he died in the year of the Lord’s nativity, as we said above, six hundred forty-five, in the third Roman indiction, not less than sixty years before the donation of this document. But if it is attributed to that latest Dagobert, who was Clodoveus, nevertheless a discordance of years follows, because he began to reign in the year of the Lord 715 and died in the year 719. How, then, could he have delivered letters of a foundation of this kind in the sixth year of the Lord?
