Accordingly Clogio, king of the Franks, fell in the second year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 319, in the seventh Roman indiction. He left two sons, of whom the elder by birth, Helenus, was then in his twentieth year, while the younger, Richimer, was in his eighteenth. But by law it was forbidden to the Franks that they should promote anyone to the kingship before he had completed the twenty-fourth year of his age. Hence it happened that neither of Clogio’s sons came to the kingdom, but their paternal uncle Clodomirus was elected king.

Clodomirus, brother of the slain king Clogio, was elected king by the common decree of the Franks. After him he reigned eighteen years, a distinguished, prudent, and brave prince, who kept his people in peace and enlarged the kingdom of the Franks.

For in these times, when the Sueves were being grievously afflicted by the Romans, they had resolved to call the Franks, Saxons, and Thuringians to their aid; they trusted that they would find them willing all the more readily because they knew that they had always hated the Romans. But the Franks, otherwise ancient enemies of the Romans, having held counsel among themselves, readily gave their consent to the request of the Sueves, thinking it healthier to fight with enemies in borders far away and belonging to others than in their own.

Therefore Clodomir, king of the Franks, appointed his brother Genebaldus commander of the army that was to go, and entrusted to him eighty thousand fighting men. The other kings called by the Sueves to their aid did likewise. The soldiers appointed, coming together into one body, formed a sufficiently large army. They joined battle with the Romans and fought very fiercely, killing many thousands of them, while the rest sought safety in flight; and so the Romans were driven from the borders of the Sueves.

When the Germans, having gained this victory, were dividing the spoils in the camp in the customary manner, a dangerous quarrel arose between two soldiers, of whom one, a Thuringian, was named Gunther, while the other was a Sueve named Adelbertus. This was the cause of the disturbance: that the Sueve soldier had accused the Thuringian of having secretly carried off something or other from the common booty. When he denied it, a serious sedition of the Thuringians arose, while the Sueves defended the Sueve. For each had many adherents from his own people, who were stirred up in spirit.

But when the Thuringian steadfastly denied the theft, the Sueve offered that he would prove the truth by duel. When the princes had permitted this, single combat was entered into between them on the appointed day; in it, at the second encounter, the Thuringian was killed by the Sueve. When his corpse had been stripped of clothes and arms, the very theft over which he had been accused was found hidden on him.
