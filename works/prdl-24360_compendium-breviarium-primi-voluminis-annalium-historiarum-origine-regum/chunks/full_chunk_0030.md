Joined with these, the Franks, with great forces, very hastily led an army of three hundred thousand men against their adversaries. But when Francus the king saw the desolation of the region which the Gauls had wrought, moved by great indignation of spirit, he said: “See, my most valiant fellow-soldiers, you who rightly grieve that the blood of your fathers has been shed, what those most faithless Gauls have done. I swear by the head of my father Antharius, whom those most wicked men killed, that I shall give no rest to my head until I have notably avenged myself upon them.” And they all praised his purpose.

Therefore, when a bridge had been made with ships and much timber, in two days he brought the army across; and, leaving forty thousand men to guard the crossing, he advanced with the remaining multitude into Gaul. And he made such a slaughter as no one of the Sicambri before him either could have made or is believed to have conceived in his mind. For, entering as far as the river Tabul and both the western and the northern sea, he ravaged with fire and sword the Tungri, the Tarubani, the Legii, and whatever lies between or adjacent to these. When the Gauls joined battle with him, he overcame, routed, and destroyed them with such ease that he was thought to be not a man but some divine power sent from heaven. Hence all the nations round about, exceedingly terrified, feared any further to engage the Franks, as most savage monsters. In that encounter and devastation of the Gauls, more than two hundred thousand of them fell by the sword of the Franks, with children, infants, and women. For just as the Gauls had previously done when they devastated the Sicambri, sparing no one, so they received from the same.

Hence the Senate and people of Rome, moved with indignation, sent legions of soldiers against the Saxons into Germany, Marcus Julius being commander. For they did not think it would be safe to engage the Franks, whom they themselves called Germans, before they had first completely subdued the Saxons and the Dorings. When the Romans therefore entered Saxony, at first indeed they conquered, killing eighteen thousand Germans. But when Francus the king heard of the destruction of his allies, he sent his son Chlogio as commander, with a great army, to the aid of the confederates. They, having recovered their strength and again engaged the Romans, obtained the victory and put the enemy to flight, many of them being slain by the sword, in the twenty-fourth year of King Francus.

Finally King Francus, weighed down by labors and old age, after many battles conducted most victoriously against the Romans, Goths, and other nations, and after the kingdom of the Franks had been enlarged not a little and its settlements extended, died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, precisely in the ninth year before the birth of the Lord. His wars, customs, institutions, laws, and victories were written by Clodomirus, the Frank, high priest, in German verse, and by Hunibald in prose, in the Latin language; under their guidance we have material both here and in the first volume of the annals of German and Frankish affairs which we have written.
