By the report of Duke Genebald, our very dear brother, we learned some time ago what the cause of the breach of trust was between you, friends, the Thuringians, and the Suevi, and the occasion of those disturbances which today stand at the gates. We acknowledge that we have been allied with you for a long time, and we neither wish in any way, nor indeed ought, to violate the right of the treaty that has been made. But you yourselves, excellent Thuringians, now see more clearly than daylight amid what great dangers the kingdom of the Franks is placed, since the Romans with the Gauls threaten us daily before our gates, watching most carefully our entrance, and likewise our departure, to see whether by any occasion they can overthrow our kingdom, which our ancestors, with the shedding of much blood, were scarcely at last able to acquire and establish.

Therefore it has by no means seemed prudent to the counsel of the Franks, especially in these perilous times, to divide the people and, neglecting our own affairs, to defend those of others, since, with the gods propitious, we may perhaps be able to suffice for one of the two, but to protect both, that is, our kingdom and yours, in these days from the incursion of enemies is not within our power. Finally, the very great distance of places hinders us very much from being able to bring you aid so often against the Suevi, since they hold the beginning and source of the river Rhine, while we hold its farthest mouths and setting. Nevertheless, lest we seem to have failed you altogether, as friends, it has pleased our counsel that we send our messengers and envoys to the Suevi, through whom we may again obtain for you three-year truces and armistices. Meanwhile let the Thuringians take counsel, so that they may be able either to obtain perpetual peace with their own people, or by some other means whatsoever set up the necessary wall of defense, that is, either from their own people or from foreign friendly nations.

When these things had been set forth by the king of the Franks, the envoys of the Thuringians answered: Your counsel, O king and you princes of the Franks, is excellent, if however you will be able to obtain three-year truces from the Suevi on our behalf. But as to your advice that we should set up a wall of defense between ourselves and the Suevi, either from ourselves or, if any necessity requires, from some foreign source, this is also the opinion of our king and of all the nobles of the people of the Thuringians; concerning this, we beg you, hear our counsel, and at the same time give aid as faithful friends.

We have a land broad in extent toward the south, and fertile with abundance of fruits, whose farthest borders mark the boundary between the Suevi and the Thuringians. If you are willing to place inhabitants there from among your people, and a duke, they will without doubt be for you and for the kingdom of the Franks a perpetual honor and advantage, and for the Thuringians a wall of defense, and for the Suevi a cause of fear. No difficulty concerning the gift can intervene to prevent it from being in your power by the voluntary offering of the princes and people of the Thuringians.
