Concerning the manner, outcome, and miracles of rains in this war, which were then said to have occurred on behalf of the Roman army, following Hunibald we set out a sufficient account in the first volume of the Annals; we judge it superfluous to repeat it here.

Farabert, king of the Franks, after many brave deeds, died in the twentieth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 185, in the eighth indiction.

In the times of this king Farabert, namely in the 182nd year of the Lord’s nativity, Eleutherius, the Roman pope, at the request of Lucius, king of Britain, son of Coillus, sent into that Britain which is now called England the bishops Faganus and Duvianus, so that they might announce the word of salvation to the aforesaid king and also to the nobles and the whole people of the British kingdom or island, and baptize those converted to the faith of Christ in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. When they came to the appointed place, in a short time they converted not only King Lucius but the people of the whole island to the faith of Christ.

At that time there were in Britain several pontiffs, priests of idols, among whom the flamens were preeminent in authority, twenty-eight in number; and there were three archflamens: of London, of York, and in the City of Legions, which the ancient walls and buildings testify to have been situated on the river Usk in Glamorgan. When the aforesaid flamens were converted to the faith of Christ, the preachers consecrated their temples of idols to the honor of the one God, the almighty Father, appointing the same number of bishops in place of the flamens, and archbishops in place of the archflamens. Gildas wrote of these matters more fully in the book which he entitled On the Victory of Aurelius Ambrosius, king of the Britons; after him, Geoffrey of Monmouth, the Briton, who for Robert, duke of Gloucester, completed the history of the Britons and the successions of all their kings in order from the first entry of the people with Brutus from Troy into that island down to the year of the Lord’s nativity 689, in ten books. His contemporary Caradoc of Llancarfan also described the successions, years, times, and deeds of the kings of the Franks in lucid language.

The elder son of Farabert, king of the Franks, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-eight years: a strong, vigorous, bold prince, who fought many battles with the Romans, likewise with the Gauls. In his time, as we said, King Lucius first most humbly received the Christian faith; after he died without children, the Romans sent Severus, a senator, into Britain with two legions. He fought many battles with Fulgenius, by whom at last, in the siege of the city of York, he was killed along with many Romans; they received his son, named Bassanus, as king.
