Heribert, however, the eldest son of Lotharius, in the division of the monarchy of the kingdom of the Franks received Neustria, holding the seat of his kingdom at Paris; and he reigned nine years, and, doing nothing worthy of memory, died in the year of the Lord 573, in the eleventh indiction of the Romans, leaving the kingdom to his brothers.

Gunthramus, King Lotharius’s second-born son, reigned at Orleans for thirty-three years, a prince excellent in his own condition, but he obtained a most wicked wife, named Austrigildis. Among the many evils she perpetrated, when she was sick unto death and could not be helped by the physicians, she circumvented the king with crafty persuasion, so that he confirmed by an undoubted oath that he would grant her the fulfilment of one request. “Before I die,” she said, “have the heads of my physicians, who have tormented me in vain with their medicines, cut off while I am watching.” What more? Those who were to bring in the physicians were sent at once; when they entered the queen’s chamber, the witch said: “Ha! You are not physicians, but liars, who by your medicines force me to die before my time. I have prepared such a reward for you that, compelled to die, you shall pass over before me.” Soon, to satisfy the lust of the impious woman, the executioner, as ordered, cut off the heads of the two innocent physicians while that beast herself watched and laughed. King Gunthramus died in the year of the Lord 597, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans.

Sigebert, however, the third-born son of King Lotharius, reigned at Metz in Austrasia over the people of Trier, and also those of [unclear], Cologne, Mainz, and the other cities and provinces on this side of the Rhine, for thirteen years. He had as wife a Spaniard named Brunia, whom writers have more often been accustomed to call Brunihildis, the murderess of ten kings, who was so hateful to the Franks that the prophecy of the Sibyl seemed to be fulfilled in her, saying:

“There will come Brunia from the regions of Spain; before her sight peoples and kings will perish, but she herself will perish by the heels of horses.” Hilperic begot from her a son named Theodobert, who afterward reigned twenty-one years and died in the year of the Lord 599. His son, named Theodoricus, succeeding, reigned sixteen years; at the instigation of his grandmother Brunichildis he expelled Saint Columbanus from Gaul in the year of the Lord 614, and died shortly afterward, leaving three sons, as is read, bastards. The eldest, Sigebert, was put to flight in battle by Lotharius, son of the aforesaid king, and thereafter appeared nowhere. Corbus, the second-born, was killed by the same Lotharius. But Meroveus, the third-born, whom the same Lotharius had raised from the sacred font, he preserved alive. Brunihildis, however, the murderess of ten kings, he put to death.
