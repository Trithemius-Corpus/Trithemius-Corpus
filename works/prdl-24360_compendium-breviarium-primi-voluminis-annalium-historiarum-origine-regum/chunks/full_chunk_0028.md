This confederation of kings terrified the senate and people of Rome more than can be said; they labored in various ways, with diverse ingenuity and manifold contrivance, to dissolve the compacted alliance of the Germans, and in every way in vain. From that time the courage and power of the Franks were always suspect to the Romans, since they feared, as afterward happened, that the Germans would gain possession of the monarchy of all Europe. And this was the reason why Octavian, Caesar of the Romans, at the birth of Christ had the whole world obedient to the Romans registered by heads, thinking that the Franks were to be destroyed by the multitude of peoples; at that time, as yet, the writers of the histories of nations called them Germans.

But in the third year of the reign of Francus, when the Romans saw that they could by no device break up the alliance of the German kings, they again sent the Goths into the borders of the Saxons; and these, settling for almost ten years in the borders of Germany, were gradually and successively thinking of spreading into the interior of the nation just mentioned. Meanwhile King Francus fought frequently with the Gauls, and avenged his father’s death upon them with harsh vicissitude, and during that time killed many thousands of them. But when the Goths too were daily breaking farther into the interior of Germany, the Saxons together with the Teutons, at last compelled by necessity, resolved to make war upon them. Having summoned their allies to their aid according to the agreements, the Sicambri and the Thuringians led out to battle an army of two hundred and thirty thousand men. But the Scanzians and the Goths too, having themselves summoned allies, opposed to the arriving Germans an almost innumerable multitude. A very savage battle was joined between the parties in the place called Colmasus; with many falling on both sides, they fought from morning until night with the issue of war uncertain.

On the following day the Germans divided the army into three parts and set individual commanders over them. But the Sicambri, fighting most boldly under their king Francus, cried out, repeatedly shouting the name of their commander Francus: “Here, Francus!” At last the Goths were defeated, and more than eighty thousand of them were slain; the rest, slipping away in flight, returned with their children and wives to their former hiding-places. Thus the Saxons, with the aid of the Franks and the Thuringians, recovered their borders, which for ten years had been devastated and occupied by foreigners.

From that time the Sicambri, laying aside the name they had had before, began, from love of their king, to call themselves Franks. Indeed, as Hunibald bears witness, not only the nobles, but also all the soldiers and the whole people of the entire nation were so delighted by the very novelty of the name that they prevailed upon the king by prayers to sanction by public edict that thereafter they were to be called forever not Sicambri but Franks. This the king established all the more willingly, because he more eagerly loved the immortality of his name.
