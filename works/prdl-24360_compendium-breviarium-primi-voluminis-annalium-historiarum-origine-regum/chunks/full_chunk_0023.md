The king was a man utterly unable to endure any injuries from enemies, especially from the Gauls, and he was fierce against enemies. For this reason he often fought against them savagely, with varying fortune.

For from that time when that people, sprung from Trojan stock, first came from the borders of the Scythians into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine, it never ceased from the harassment of the Gauls, until at last, not without [unclear], nor always victorious, with much bloodshed of its own men, it powerfully subjected all Gaul to itself.

No one was ever able fully to overcome the Franks, or Sicambri; no one imposed upon their ferocity the bridle of subjection. Rather, they did not lay aside the desire for ruling, which they had once imbibed, before they had brought all Gaul, Germany, and at the same time Italy under their power, the Roman empire having been annihilated and many kings destroyed.

For, as was said above, it is clear from the reckoning of times that nine hundred and thirty years elapsed from the entry of the Franks, as they are now called, into the districts of Germany on this side of the Rhine, down to Clodio the Great, who, as the first victor over the Alamanni, believed in Christ. During these years the Sicambri fought with the Gauls, never ceasing to wage war against them, although they were often partly routed, until at last they overcame them all. *The Franks fought against the Gauls for 930 years.* *Many of the Franks were also called Germans.*

It should be known, however, that they were not always named Franks, nor Sicambri, but frequently changed their names together with their settlements. For indeed, as Hunibald testifies, when after the destruction of Troy they wandered for many years among various nations and did not long have stable settlements in one place, among the Scythians [unclear], among the Armenians they were called Armenians. When they dwelt at the mouths of the Danube, they were called neither Franks nor Sicambri; but because they bordered on the Scythians, they acquired the speech and name of the Scythians. *Amerodacus, historian of the Franks.* Afterwards, driven by the Goths through the envy of the Romans, as Amerodacus is the authority, they descended into the territories of Germany; and, having established their settlements, as was said above, at the mouths of the Rhine, they learned the language of the German Saxons, and were first called Neomagi, afterwards also Germans. Then, named Sicambri from Cambra, they changed even that name in time, stirred by love for King Francus, from whom the name of the Franks, as will be said later in its proper order, took its beginning. Hence it came about that the writers of other peoples, whenever in the accounts of their histories they introduce the memory of the deeds of this people, more often call them Germans than Sicambri or Franks; and this has usually brought no small occasion of error to those who do not know the true origin of the Franks, while they believe that the Franks were either indigenous or had begun from a conspiracy of a few men.

But King Nicanor, joined with the Saxons and Thuringians, in the fourteenth year of his reign, marched with a great multitude to war against the Goths, who had again burst into the territories of the Teutons and Saxons. *War of the Franks against [unclear].* In the first encounter the Goths were superior and crushed the Germans with a great slaughter. But soon, when the army had been restored, the Goths were defeated; many thousands of them perished both by the edge of the sword and submerged in the waters. The rest sought safety in flight.
