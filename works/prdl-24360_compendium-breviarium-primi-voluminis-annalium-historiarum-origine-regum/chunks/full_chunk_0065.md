*The Forty-Fifth: Meroveus reigned twelve years.*

After these things, in his eleventh year, in the fifth indiction of the Romans, Clodius, king of the Franks, considering that the power of the Romans had declined in no small degree, sent scouts into further Gaul, so that they might diligently investigate the situation of the land, the strength of the people, and the guard of the cities and fortifications, and take care to report back to him as quickly as possible. At that time the Burgundians were living in the province of Lyon, and the Goths in Aquitaine, who, together with the Spaniards and Gauls, occupied the land.

When the scouts returned to Clodius, they reported that the regions were excellent, the land wholly fruitful, the situation also very pleasant, the people unarmed, fearful, and weak, and that the Roman guard in the cities was small. When he had heard this, King Clodius, who had nothing more in mind than to extend the name and glory of the Franks, and their kingdom, gathered a great army of his men, and, moving beyond the river which is called Tabula toward the sea into Gaul, besieged and took the city of Cambrai, killing all the Roman soldiers whom he found in it.

Then, moving through the Carbonarian Forest, he hastened on his way to the city of Tournai, which city was fortified by a Roman garrison. But the Roman soldiers who were in the garrison, when they had heard of the arrival of the Franks, advanced against them outside the city for battle; in that battle they were all killed by the Franks to the last man, so that not even one remained of all of them to carry news of the others’ destruction to the Romans. They subjected the city of Tournai, with the whole region, to themselves.

Then, advancing further, the Franks brought the Morini, the Taravani, and whatever remained of the Gauls as far as the sea under their complete power, and they possess all these things down to the present day, which the Romans afterward neither recovered nor indeed will be able to recover forever. For Rome has withered, though once it flourished.

But Clodius, the distinguished king of the Franks, after he had magnificently enlarged the boundaries of the kingdom, and had powerfully subjected to the kingdom of the Franks all the land of the Gauls which lay between the Rhine on the east, the southern sea on the west, the British sea on the north, as far as the Seine toward the south, that is, the Batavi, Menapii, Tungri, Taravani, Morini, Ambiani, people of Cambrai, people of Tournai, Atrebates, Bellovaci, and whatever lies between these regions, and beyond the Seine as far as the Loire, died in the twentieth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 446, in the fourteenth indiction.

Meroveus, the elder-born son of the aforementioned King Clodius, succeeding his father, reigned over Francia for twelve years, a prince inferior to none of the preceding kings in power, courage, and the greatness of his deeds; he crushed the Roman empire, and, following his father’s footsteps in glorious imitation, not moderately increased the kingdom of the Franks in the few years in which he reigned.

King Clodius of the Franks had carried out his works in Gaul and Britain under his kings. Meroveus, his successor, continuing these magnanimous deeds, but changing him so that he might be ruled in Francia, and afterward he died. But Remigius, successor of Meroveus, who afterward had led in Germany, and afterward reigned in Britain, everywhere they will have the memory of his work. But Clodius afterward had led on behalf of the Roman cause in Germany, and afterward, having returned to Gaul, reigned there, and afterward died with his son Clovis, who afterward will reign in Francia. But what may have been of the merit of Clodius, or whether it was, we cannot now judge certainly; but an example of this kind suffices, whereby a father as teacher, so long as he is able, educates and is educated by his own son; and if his son does a good part, he seems to give thanks to the father for it. And thus Clodius too, so long as he was able, educated and does educate his son Meroveus, and if Meroveus did a good part, Clodius gave thanks to him.
