But, concealing their anger, they afterwards held secret counsel among themselves as to how they might avenge Gunther’s fall upon Adelbert. And when the plan had been settled, namely that they should kill the Swabian that same night, one hundred and twenty of them conspired together to keep the matter secret.

Therefore on the following night they silently rushed upon the tent in which they suspected Adelbert to be, demanding that the murderer be handed over to them to be killed. Roused by these voices, the Swabians ran to arms, firmly resolving either to defend their innocent soldier from death or to die with him. An attack was at once made upon the Thuringians, and they slaughtered almost all those one hundred and twenty.

When this was announced in the camp of the Thuringians, they were greatly moved and, as though turned to fury, immediately seized their weapons; and, having formed a battle-line, they sprang upon the Swabians, who suspected no such thing, raising shouts on high like madmen. But the Swabians, not slow, when they could not call the Thuringians back from the tumult with fair words, themselves also put on arms and strove to resist bravely. A savage battle was joined between friends, in which more than two thousand men of each side perished. Yet the Thuringians lost more than the Swabians, and at last, overcome and compelled by necessity, they withdrew.

This contention had remained hidden from both the Franks and the Saxons until the struggle came to an end, since they had pitched their camps not in the same place but in different stations. Afterwards, however, when they learned the cause, they undertook many negotiations for peace and concord between the disputants; but since each side complained that it had been gravely and unjustly injured, they could not restore full concord between them. They did, however, establish truces, or an armistice, between them for three years, during which they ordered the parties to abstain from war, until anger should cool with time and the disturbance be calmed.

From that time the Thuringians always held the Swabians suspect for treachery, and up to the present day, as Hunibald says, they labor under a hidden and open hatred against them, although many do not know the cause of this mutual persecution.

But when the three years had passed and the end of the truces was at hand, the Swabians declared war on the Thuringians. The latter, having very little confidence in their own affairs, sent notable and [unclear] envoys to Clodomir, king of the Franks, and to the nobles of the kingdom, begging with great urgency that, in view of the former confederation, they would bring them the aid owed and friendly against the Swabians. At that time there were great movements of peoples in Britain and in all the adjacent islands neighboring the kingdom of the Franks; in consideration of these King Clodomir was distressed, fearing the stratagems of the Romans, lest, in the absence of the army of fighters, they should contrive some new incursion into the kingdom.

Having held counsel with the nobles of the kingdom, King Clodomir gave this reply to the envoys of the Thuringians in a public assembly of the magnates.

*Conspiracy of the Thuringians against the Swabian.*

*The Thuringians fight seditiously with the Swabians.*

*Many fell on each side.*

*The Franks with the Saxons labor for peace.*

*Three-year truces are made.*

*The Thuringians and Swabians become enemies.*

*The Swabians declared war on the Thuringians.*

*Troubled Britain groans. Note well.*

*The Franks forestall the stratagems of the Romans.*

*Speech of King Clodomir to the Thuringians.*
