Afterwards, when he joined battle with Maximus, he was defeated through the treachery of Merobaldus, master of the soldiers, and, slipping away in flight, came to Lyons, where he was afterwards killed.

Conanus, however, whom Maximus had established as king in the region of Armorica, endured frequent attacks from the Gauls and Romans who were in Aquitaine. Meeting them bravely, he fought with them manfully, and, after very many of them had been killed, turned the rest to flight.

At last, having obtained peace and quiet possession of the Armorican kingdom, he began to take counsel with his nobles as to how they might as quickly as possible have their wives and children, whom they had left behind in Britain, brought to them with all their household goods, lest the propagation of their race should fail.

The number of soldiers who had come from Britain with Conanus into Armorica was thirty thousand fighting men. The common people and farmers, however, as Geoffrey is the authority, were one hundred thousand, all of whom, by order of Maximus, had sailed from Britain into Armorica. Of these, many did not have wives; the rest, who did have them, had left them in Britain with their little children when they were compelled to follow Maximus.

But Conanus and all his people, since they were Christians, in no way consented to take as wives the daughters of pagans who worshipped mute idols. Even if these women had been Christians, nevertheless it did not please them to have any traffic of marital union with the Gauls.

In those days there was in Britain, which is now called England, a certain sub-king named Dionotus, who, after the death of his brother Caradocus, held the principality of Cornwall; to him also King Maximus, when about to set out for Gaul, had entrusted the kingdom, as to a very prudent man. He had an only daughter named Ursula, by everyone’s judgment the most beautiful among all the virgins of Britain, and very devoted to Christ.

Therefore Conanus, the new king of the Armoricans from Britain, not only knew very well Ursula, the daughter of the aforesaid prince of Cornwall, Dionotus, but also loved her greatly. Hence, after messengers and letters had been sent to him, he asked that she be sent to him as his wife.

Secondly, he asked that the wives and children of the soldiers and common people, left behind in Britain when their husbands departed at the command of Maximus, be put on ships with their household goods and sent to him in the Armorican kingdom.

Thirdly, he instructed that, according to the number which he had specified in his letters, virgins should be sent: noble ones from noble lineage for the soldiers, and common ones also for the common people, whom each of those who then had no spouses might take as wives.
