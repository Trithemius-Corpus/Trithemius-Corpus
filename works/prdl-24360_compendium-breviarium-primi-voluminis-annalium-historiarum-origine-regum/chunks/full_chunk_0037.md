He wrote many things in Greek songs concerning the sacred rites of the gods, and put into German verse the histories of the ancient kings; with these he exercised the talents of the young and greatly raised up their memory. In the fifth year of King Odemarus, he perished in a small river, to which he gave its name when he was drowned, so that from Vechtanus it is called in German the Vecht down to the present day. Over his tomb King Odemarus founded a new city, which he named Odemarsheim after himself; it still stands at present within the borders of the diocese of the church of Utrecht.

But Odemarus, the peaceable king of the Franks, at last, after the founding of many cities, died in the fourteenth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 127, in the tenth indiction of the Romans. His body was buried beneath a pyramid in Odemarsheim, as he had ordained while alive.

Marcomerus, son of King Odemarus, succeeding his father, reigned for twenty-one years. He had a wife named Athildem, daughter of Marius, king of Britain, by whom he begot seven sons, whose names were these: Clodomir, the firstborn, who succeeded his father in the kingdom; Marcomer, who held the duchy among the Nitriones, where he founded a city named after himself, called Marcoburg; Clogio, who perished in Britain; Franc, who was duke on this side of the Rhine among the Hogii, from the Confluence as far as the mouth of the river Main toward the east, among the Moguntiaci, Xones, and Germans, and who restored the old city Helenopolis and named it Franckenfurt; Merodac, who was struck by a boar while hunting and perished; Nicanor, who was duke of the islands at the mouths of the Rhine; Odemarus, whom his father had appointed to the sacred rites after his death, because he had become most learned in all gentile literature.

At that time Doracus flourished, counsellor of the Franks, kinsman of kings, an eminent prophet, formerly hearer and disciple of Vechtanus, born from the noble lineage of kings. After this he obtained the priesthood, and was held in such high esteem that he was worshipped by all almost as some divinity, and commanded not only the common people whatever he wished, but even the kings of the Franks and all the nobles. Writing the histories of the people in verse, he continued them.

The Franks had peace during all the days in which Marcomirus reigned, and under his direction they greatly increased in riches, power, and honor. Doracus writes in the annals, as Hunibaldus bears witness, that the Romans always regarded the Franks as suspect, in peace and in war, as future overthrowers of the empire, having long before been warned by the oracle of Phedius; and for that reason they often conspired with all their forces for their destruction, though in vain.
