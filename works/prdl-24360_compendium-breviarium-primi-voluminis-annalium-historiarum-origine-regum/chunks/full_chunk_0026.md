He also, on his return, grievously afflicted many colonies of the Romans, subjected them to still heavier hardships, and made his name formidable to all. Greatly moved by these events, the Romans, having joined many nations to themselves, declared war on the Sicambri; when the necessities for the expedition had been prepared, they assembled for battle. And lest the Saxons or the Thuringians should bring aid to the Sicambri, the Romans had first induced the Goths and Slavs by promised gifts, so that at one and the same time [the former] would subdue the Saxons, and [the Romans] themselves the Sicambri. King Merodacus, therefore, being of necessity deserted by the Saxons and Thuringians, called upon the king of the northern island, whose people we today call Danes; he came with a hundred thousand and brought aid to the Sicambri.

And so, in the twenty-second year of his reign, he perceived that auxiliary troops, an immense army, were coming against him. For Marius, leader of the Romans, sent the Roman legions with the Gauls, under Theodomenus as commander, beyond the Meuse against the Sicambri, whom they themselves called Germans. They joined battle fiercely, and many thousands of men fell on both sides; the Sicambri fled, having lost more than twenty thousand fighters. After this, Merodacus, having restored his army and become stronger than the Gauls and Romans, inflicted much slaughter upon them, as, following Hunibald, we have said more fully in the first volume of our histories or annals.

Afterward, when he had joined many battles with the Gauls and Romans and had considerably enlarged the borders of his nation, King Merodacus died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, but in the ninety-third year before the birth of Christ.

Cassander, the elder son of King Merodacus, succeeded his father and reigned twenty-one years. He was a very warlike man; in his time the Gauls, with the aid of the Romans, often crossed the river Meuse and invaded the territories of the Sicambri as enemies. Cassander, meeting them in the strength of his men, forbade the enemies the devastation of his nation. For all the days in which he ruled over the Sicambri, he was in conflict with the Gauls and Romans.

In these times Borbista, king of the Goths, entered the borders of Saxony with a countless multitude of fighters, devastating the region with fire and sword. Against him three very powerful kings, namely Arthari, king of the Saxons, Hamer, king of the Thuringians, and the aforesaid Cassander, king of the Sicambri, united their armies into one and advanced to battle. When the encounter had been made, the fighting was fierce for a long time; many fell on both sides. At last King Borbista turned his back, and the Germans obtained the victory.

On another occasion after this, King Borbista again entered Saxony with a very great multitude of Goths, cruelly devastated the region with fire and sword as far as the river Vistula, and, having seized much spoil from the Germans, withdrew; nor did he presume to advance farther that day.
