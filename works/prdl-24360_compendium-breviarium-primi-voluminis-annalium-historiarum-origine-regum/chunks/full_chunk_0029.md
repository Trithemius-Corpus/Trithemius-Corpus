*Whence the Franks are called free, the free Franks, because they never served.*

They gloried in the novelty of this name, since for many times thereafter, whenever they met one another, they greeted each other thus: “A good day, free Frank.” Nevertheless, not suddenly but only after much time did this name at last come into use, so that the people itself was called Franks even among other nations. For to the Romans, and especially to the Gauls, the name of the Franks was hateful. Hence among their writers they were more often called Germans than either Sicambri or Franks. But Hunnibald did not pass over in silence whence, or from whom, the name of the Sicambri had its origin.

That the Franks were formerly called Sicambri is attested not only by Hunnibald, but also by blessed Remigius, the archbishop, who, as Gregory, bishop of Tours, records, when he was to baptize Clovis, king of the Franks, the first of the Frankish kings converted to Christ, as we shall say in its proper place, said before the assembled nobles, as he laid aside the royal ornaments and humbled himself for the sacred laver, in the hearing of all: “Meekly bow your neck, Sicamber. Worship what you burned. Burn what you worshiped.” Of the history of this matter we have already spoken more fully, following Hunnibald, in the first volume of the Annals.

Therefore in the twenty-fourth year before the birth of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, which was the thirteenth year of King Frangus, the Sicambri changed their ancient name into a new one, although both before and afterward among writers they were more often called Germans or Sicambri. For although they themselves took for themselves the name Frank under the aforementioned king and always retained it, nevertheless it could not immediately come into use among foreign nations, especially hostile ones. From this the fact is most plainly established that the writers of histories who flourished in human letters among the Romans, the Gauls also, or other nations, at the time when the name was changed and nevertheless for many years after this as well, whether from ignorance or from detestation of the term, more often called this people German than Frankish. For it is certain that the Romans came to know the name of the Franks not through writings but through arms, not through love but through fear, not through adoption but through subjection.

All of them sent the requested contingents of soldiers with great eagerness: the Saxons, namely, 84,000 fighters; the Germans and Teutons, 60,000; and the Thuringians, 46,000.
