*To Emperor Maximilian.*

The intellect is pure, simple, and perfect; but the human intellect, so long as it remains in the body, is impure, mixed, unstable, and imperfect, and for this reason, without faith, wholly ignorant of the knowledge of God and of heavenly things. The object of both intellects, namely both the human and the angelic, is the same: that highest and unchangeable good, God, the single principle of all things, to which they return by a natural desire for knowledge. But if the angelic intellect is compared with the human, it is as nature is to art, and as intellect simply speaking is to reason, which touches upon the principles of the disciplines; reason, however, runs from principles to conclusions. Hence it is manifest that the angelic intellect apprehends its proper object immovably; toward that object the human intellect, by means of the angelic, after many exercises of itself, as has been said, at length, by reasoning, prepares assent for itself.

The human intellect, however, is separated, by the mediation of the angelic, from the perfection of knowledge of its natural object, and therefore necessarily becomes movable, returning by means of it to its own principle. For every motion proceeds from an extreme through a mean to an extreme. But where there is no mean, the extremes are already immediate, proximate, and for that reason immovable. The human intellect, set as it were in a dark prison, so long as it is held in the body, is moved by sense and reason toward understanding, although confusedly, imperfectly, and less fully; and therefore it admits between itself and God the mean that we have called angelic, bearing the body as the greatest impediment to its destined perfection, just as indeed the lover of wisdom said: “The body that is corrupted weighs down the soul; the earthly dwelling depresses the sense that thinks many things.” Therefore it was altogether necessary for us to believe God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible for that super-divine and uncircumscribable majesty to be comprehended by us, who understand nothing without the service of the senses and the discourse of reason.

*Johannes Trithemius, Eight Questions.*

But God, existing far above every position, remains subject neither to the senses of mortals nor to reason, as he is read to have said to Moses: “Man shall not see me and live.” Hence also the divine evangelist John says: “No one has ever seen God.” Nor is it any wonder: for since God is the first and everlasting intellect, most pure, most simple, most perfect, immovable, creator, incomprehensible, highest, and far beyond every position, immeasurable and uncircumscribable, he could not be comprehended by the human intellect, which is impure, subject to composition, imperfect, movable, created, comprehensible, lowest, and established under the measure of position.

To that question, however, which seemed to arise from the first, whether God, since he is omnipotent, could have made himself knowable to the human intellect as to the angelic: I say that God can do all things; no one of sound mind has ever denied it. But those things that imply contradiction we say God cannot do, because they are contrary to the order of the wisdom of the immutable one who disposes all things, just as destruction is not doubted to be contrary to the construction of the whole at the same time, and, if I may say so, utterly opposed. For it was neither fitting, nor could it in any way have happened, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect, since if mortal man, under the guidance of nature, comprehended immortal God, certainly he would no longer seem to be called man or mortal, but an angel, and either man would be made comparable to God, or God to man.
