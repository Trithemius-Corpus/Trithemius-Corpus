*To Emperor Maximilian* 47

*On the Reprobate and Witches*

*Fifth Question*

The fifth question of Your Most Serene Highness was this: Why can impious, evil, and reprobate persons in matters of faith, such as those women whom we commonly call witches, command malignant spirits, when good men, and the most zealous observers of the Christian faith, can neither command good spirits nor always malignant ones?

I remember that I formerly answered this question as follows: In all the works of the divine majesty there is an order and sequence of beauty, of immovable equity, by which individual things, suitably disposed, persevere in the rank which they have received. For without its proper order nothing subsists. In the world, in this order, the freedom of will which the Creator gave to all human beings is driven by no violence of power so as not always to remain in the order which the first man received from the beginning of his creation. Hence no one among mortals is good except by the counsel of his own will, assisted by the grace of a merciful God; and no one is evil except of his own accord, deprived of grace because of the obstacle of sin. By rectitude of will we are made like good spirits, if we prove the piety of the faith which we have in Christ by holy and necessary works. And just as the consummation of a good will makes good men equal to angelic spirits, so the perversity of the impious renders them like demons. Hence evil and perverse people, the more they have been depraved by the malice of their own will,

the more they become like demons in disposition. By likeness, moreover, the familiarity of friendship is introduced, from which mutual confidence is gradually generated. From the confidence of the lesser toward the greater, command arises, whether it is true or feigned by cunning. Therefore, if there seems to be any command of wicked people over demons, it arises from the greatness of their wickedness: namely, when their disposition is depraved to such a degree that, by a reprobate will, they either surpass or equal the malice of the demons joining with them.

For demons are not all equal in malice or intelligence; rather, one is much more cunning than another in quickness of understanding, and far worse in depraved affection. For those who, having fallen from the high seats, dwell nearer the earth and inhabit caverns and caves, are certainly duller than the rest, to such a degree that they fear swords and rods, and seem to be moved by the threats of men who terrify them. This may be observed in those whom we call energumens. But demons who dwell farther removed from the earth are subtler by nature and meet with human beings more rarely. People full of malice, however, the more they advance in perversity of will, the more like demons they become; admitted by them into familiarity, they thereafter become bold, and receive command over the demons whose familiarity they use: a command not of violence, but of pretense. For demons, in order to keep willing in their depravity those people whom they have once received into surrender, gladly subject themselves to them, as though moved by a feigned dominion; what they do voluntarily, they pretend that they are compelled to do in obedience.
