*To Maximilian Caesar*

[unclear]. But every mutable creature has no comparison with the immutable Creator. Hence in Numbers it is read: God is not like a man, that he should lie, nor like a son of man, that he should be changed. In Isaiah too it is said: God is faithful, without iniquity, just and upright. In the book of Tobit also it is written: Just are you, O Lord; all your judgments are true; all your ways are mercy, truth, and judgment. And in the book of holy Job we read: Far be impiety from God, and iniquity from the Almighty; for he will render to a man his work, and will repay each according to his ways. Therefore every man who rightly understands his own ignorance and weakness openly recognizes how he is nothing when compared with his Creator. For God justly permits man to sin, so that, since the freedom of choice is preserved, the excellence of the great God may become known to men.

The second reason is that the inscrutable wisdom of God may become known to mortals, which knows how to draw good from our evils, when it makes one person’s sin become another’s merit, for the increase of virtue. For if he did not permit evil to be done, which of us would understand the good? For the good of God orders; but evils he does not do, but, when they have been done by the impious, he himself disposes them so that they may not come about in disorder. Nor does the malice of men hinder the will of God, nor can man do anything which God does not permit.

The third cause is that the Almighty permits wicked deeds to be done in order to manifest his most merciful goodness toward those who have sinned and have done penance, which he could in no way show if he did not permit the creature to sin. For if there were no sinner, neither could God be merciful; but since in many things we all offend, everywhere God finds something to forgive, everywhere a guilty person presents himself, in whom the pious Creator may show his mercy. Hence the holy bishop Augustine: If the disease were slight, the physician would be despised and would not be sought; if the physician were not sought, the disease would not be ended. Therefore where sin abounded, grace also abounded, which alone distinguishes the redeemed from the lost, whom the mass of perdition, drawn from the origin of creation, gathers together into one. Therefore the mercy of the Lord is over all his works.

The fourth reason of divine permission is that the justice of the Creator may be declared against those who have sinned and have not done penance, since if all did good and no one sinned, the justice of God would have nothing to punish. Therefore, so that justice may have a place, the creature sins by divine permission. For the Lord is just, and he will render to each according to the works of his will.

The fifth reason is the dignity of the human condition, because if God did not permit man to sin, he would seem worse than the beasts, which, with no violence impelling them, freely use the motions of nature. For even if brute animals lack reason, nevertheless they have the free motion of nature, which they follow; in comparison with them man would seem of an inferior condition if he did not have the free choice of the will. But God permitted man a free will, over which neither kings nor princes rule.

The sixth reason is the praise of the merit of constancy in human virtue, since we praise a just man for this reason: that, although he could have transgressed, he did not transgress, but, with the grace of God helping him, remained in the constancy of a good will.
