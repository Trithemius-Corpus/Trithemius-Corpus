*Epigram of Richard Sbrulius of Forum Julii.*

Who would deny that there is a God, parent of heaven and earth?
No one is so savage as to deny that there is a God.
God himself is everywhere venerated throughout the whole world,
but he is nevertheless sought by a different path.
The barbarian worships him as moving all things by fixed law;
the Turk denies the holy Son and Spirit.
The Hebrew boasts that he holds the true doctrines,
and thinks that, with Moses as guide, he is the race of God.
But the people of Christ, who took their name from his name,
steadfastly proclaim that God is triune.
The greatest Maximilian, who presides over the empire,
continually aids the cause and honor of Christ.
He asks, as it is right for an august king to ask such things,
why the highest God permits there to be so many sects;
why he does not show himself equally to all mortals;
why he wished many things to lie hidden under hard signs. In order,
Father Trithemius unfolds all things to the King,
he the leader and glory of the hooded flock.
Whoever you are who used to doubt concerning the holy faith,
read this: soon you will be a sure pupil of Christ.
The end.

*I. On Faith and Understanding. First Question.*

The first question of your Most Serene Majesty was this: why almighty God wished to be believed by mortals rather than known and understood, that is, by the mode of cognition and understanding by which he is known and understood by the holy angels.

I remember that I answered this question, posed before me by your most sacred Majesty, as follows. Almighty God, who is without beginning, pure intellect, eternal and unchangeable, existing above every [unclear], far more perfect than we are with infinite perfections, by his most ample will created from nothing two intellects in the beginning, namely the angelic and the human. The angelic intellect, at the beginning of its condition, was made in the actuality of all things [unclear], and moved all things. The human intellect, however, at first consists indeed in the potentiality of all things, but is ignorant of all things. In the end, however, through many and continual actions, it will again become like the angelic, knowing both God and all things that pertain to the cognition of the aforesaid cognition and understanding.

The angelic and human intellects differ as any two opposites do: namely as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, possession and privation, beginning and end, immobile and mobile, substance and accident, truth and likeness, nature and art, health and infirmity, whole and part. For the angelic intellect is as act, light, knowledge, possession, beginning, immobile, immediate, simple, substance, truth, nature, whole. The human, however, is as potency, darkness, ignorance, privation, end, mobile, mediated, composite, accident, likeness, art, part. Hence whatever belongs to the angelic intellect from the beginning, these same things afterwards belong to the human from the end, when it has laid aside the burden of flesh; for just as the beginning, nature, perfects the angelic, so the end, art, after many exercises, raises the human upward.

Wherefore, between these two intellects there is the greater inequality from the beginning; yet in the end they are at last made equal, where the human intellect will be as great by art as the angelic had previously been by nature. Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect, as soon as it was created, from the beginning, without any action or motion of its own, immovably and substantially by nature knew all things that it can understand, and that in an instant, created full and perfect with the knowledge of all things, it came forth. But the human intellect, because it was created in the ignorance and privation of all things, and was temporally destined, for its exercise, into the body, must by continual action, motion, and art arrive at knowledge of God and of all things. It will finally equal the angelic, the end changing its potentiality into act, and making it, as it passes from the merely potential and possible into essence, full from empty, perfect from imperfect, possessing from deprived, luminous from dark, diurnal from nocturnal, solar from lunar, from earthly [unclear] cannot, that is, as Christophilus Paul says: as long as we are in the flesh, we are absent from the Lord, to know God [continues].
