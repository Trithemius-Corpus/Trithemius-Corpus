For nothing would ever come to be unless first, to speak in our customary way, it were understood by God himself, who makes these things, just as things are not made by the sun which are not looked upon by it. But because the Almighty understands each and every thing that he makes, there is no doubt that he disposes, rules, and orders all things that he has made; from him things have not only that they exist, but also that they are certain causes of other things. And so, if things have from God that they are causes, from him also they have received the proper office of a cause, that is, according to their powers to preserve and care for what they have made. Therefore, if all causes have this property from the first cause, insofar as they are certain causes, rightly does that first cause naturally have this goodness and providence, so that it cares for all things, each thing that it has made, insofar as it is especially the cause of all and each of them. Indeed, unless these are guarded individually by him who makes them, the common things which are preserved in individuals will never be guarded. For the whole, which consists of parts, will not be preserved if the parts themselves are neglected. Therefore from the intention of the first and greatest common intelligence proceeds the universal distinction of things. But it is distinguished of itself from others, and distinguishes the rest among themselves by the rule of its knowledge. Hence, just as God knows each and every thing most distinctly, so he most distinctly rules, moderates, and cares for them. Therefore, most clement Emperor Maximilian, I think it is clear and sufficiently shown that the providence of God, the supreme ruler, embraces, disposes, rules, and moderates all lower things, each and every one; otherwise, without the provident disposition of one who watches over them, they would in no way continue this most beautiful order which we perceive. Nor does it escape me how variously the wise men of this world once held opinions about providence.

*The Eight Questions of Johannes Trithemius.*

Of these, some thought that this world was made not by providence but by chance. If their opinion were true, no place would be left for providence, but all things would necessarily drift uncertainly, since the world could perish by the same chance by which it was made.

Against their false opinion others taught that the world could neither have been made without reason, nor certainly stand unless it were ruled by the highest providence of its maker. Hence Boethius, a most Christian man, said: “O you who govern the world by eternal reason, begetter of earth and heaven, who command time to proceed from eternity, and, remaining unmoved, grant that all things be moved.” And again he said: “The generation of all things and the whole progress of mutable natures, and whatever is moved in any way, receive their causes, order, and forms from the stability of the divine mind.”

Others, however, thought that God cared only for incorruptible things, such as heavenly things, separated substances, and likewise the species of things, but that he did not know all the rest and utterly neglected them. But these men erred shamefully, since the Almighty has providence and care not only for incorruptible universals, but also for corruptible particulars. Hence holy Boethius again says: “Meanwhile the high creator sits, and ruling all things bends the reins: king, lord, source, origin, law, wise arbiter of justice. This is the common love of all things, and they seek to be held by the end of the good, because they cannot otherwise endure, unless, by love turned back again, they flow back to the cause that gave them being.”
