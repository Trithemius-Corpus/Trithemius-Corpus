Next, indeed, the earth placed in the middle of the world, like a point or center that unfolds the circle, equally distant from the whole universe: nothing more beautiful, nothing more adorned, nothing, finally, more delightful to the sense could have offered itself for contemplation.

In this point of an immense mass, enclosed wondrously by that very mass, we see all things ordered, and that nothing can be left unordered, so that we may know for certain that all things which at times seem to us somehow to drift are subject to order: by the simple and wholly unchangeable nod, the whole structure of the entire universe, assembled from so many diverse and movable things, conspires and is governed in the most orderly way. Certainly we see, in those things that present themselves to our sense, that the greater the intelligence and power that arises with time in any governor, the less can anything unordered happen there by chance. Since, therefore, that supreme intellect, creator of all things, preserver and ruler, almighty God, rules and governs the world by his unchangeable providence, holding all things under his command, surely no place is ever left to rash chance, which might hinder the will of so great a majesty.

Therefore the whole of what is anywhere, or can be, is comprehended under the command of one divine mind, whose infallible judgment nothing can escape. To us, however, unexpected events occur, because we are changeable and are changed almost daily, having no determinate knowledge of future things.

*John Trithemius, Eight Questions* 80

But that best maker of all things, God, since he is altogether unchangeable, knows all things, beholds all things, understands all things together and at once: his providence is not deceived; his will is never varied; his power is not broken; his ordering is not violated; his counsel is never intercepted, even if at times his sentence seems to be changed. Hence, if we attend carefully, we plainly understand that the ends of things and of motions will declare that all these lower things are made, preserved, disposed, and moved by divine providence; from its command nothing at all is alien.

For God himself, ruler of all things, understands both all things and individual things, not dispersed into many things after our manner, but abiding in himself as one indivisible thing; by gazing upon himself as the exemplar of the whole universe and the cause of causes, and through causes beholding the cause of all things, he cares for all things by beholding them, and provides for individual things, directing them to a good end by an eternal order. Come now, most wise Caesar, so that we may understand divine providence, governing all things, let us use reasons, as briefly as we can, for a more attentive [unclear]. And so, if God's action is his own proper intelligence, which no wise man fails to admit, as at once most eminent and most inward, as much as he acts, just so much does he understand, and conversely. But he acts in individual things, not only in great things but also in the smallest. Therefore he understands individual things. Indeed, from the first Being, through individual things that are beings in whatever way, following in fixed orders, there is the most ample influence; and those things which act after him are everywhere moved and act by his power.

Either, therefore, God understands or acts nothing outside himself, which is false; or, by acting, he also understands all things and individual things.
