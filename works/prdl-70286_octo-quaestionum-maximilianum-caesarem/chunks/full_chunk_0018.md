by the cooperation of diabolical depravity. And this can happen in three ways: namely by the manifest invocation of demons, by implication, and by supposition.

By manifest invocation necromancers, and little women whom we commonly call witches, do wondrous things; they, corrupted by a likeness of will, have passed into fellowship with demons. For just as in the Church of God the sacraments are signs of the invisible grace of the Holy Spirit, so in the school of demons certain characters, invocations, and methods are the marks of a depraved man and a malignant spirit. Hence, when witches display the signs of demons, the malignant spirits invoked are accustomed willingly to come to them and to exhibit horrible and at the same time wondrous acts of witchcraft.

But witches and necromancers produce wondrous effects by the invocation of demons in different ways. Witches are subjected to demons by a certain kind of profession; by their service they disturb the air, raise storms, devastate crops, make men and beasts ill, carry on foul commerce with demons in most filthy pleasure, and by destructive charms call back from below into sight whomever they have wished. Their deeds are not to be called miracles, but rather witchcrafts, deservedly to be punished with the ultimate penalty.

Necromancers, however, not by manifest profession but by an implicit condition, promise to compel demons to appear visibly in a circle by the display of characters, and also by sacrifices, fumigations, and vows. Their crimes are infinite, since it is manifest that all enter into fellowship with demons, either implicitly or explicitly. Although not all necromancers are equal either in art or in practice, nevertheless all agree in their own destruction through damnable curiosity, by which, with the aid of demons, they desire to attain what they cannot discover by nature. All their works and books are full of superstitions and impiety, and for that reason must be driven far from the borders of believers.

There is a great multitude of enchanters and necromancers in the East, who by their illusions miserably deceive the human race, and have turned away almost all of greater and lesser Asia from the faith of Christ. Illusions can occur in three ways: by the service of demons, by the operation of nature, and by the deception of human art. No true Christian will doubt that the miracles of those unbelievers occur in that way, where the determined root of the worker is placed. But today there are very many among Christians who are devoted to the works of demons: enchanters, witches, reprobates in matters of faith, who deceive many by excessive curiosity and by their arts promise impossibilities.

By implication of demons, moreover, wondrous things sometimes happen: namely whenever a man not sufficiently rightly confirmed in the faith does or wishes something otherwise than befits a Christian, even if he believes it to be no sin. Since this ought not to happen, nevertheless the Devil, intruding himself, very often supplies the desired effect. Such is that which the [unclear] commonly use: on the holy day of Parasceve, during the office, they make a ring of whatever metal, which they thereafter always wear on the ring finger.
