For indeed, when God for just reason withdraws his grace from unbelievers, their deceiver, the malignant spirit, approaches unrecognized; and, seeing that salvation is far away, he seduces the minds of the ignorant by false miracles, as though the Lord were near. Therefore whatever miracles belong to unbelievers are works of demons. For just as outside the unity of the Christian faith there is no salvation for mortals, so without Christ the most holy power of wonders is not granted. For now demons, lurking in ambush, long since conquered and confounded by the trophy of Christ, fear to appear openly as they did before the incarnation of the Lord, and therefore among unbelievers they falsely present themselves as good spirits.

There are also those who add a fourth to the three modes set down above, saying that the mind or spirit of the man himself can naturally do wondrous things, provided it knows how to recall itself from everything adventitious into itself, above the senses, into unity. For those who ascribe such things to nature promise that it can do wonders, foretell the future, disclose the secrets of men, remove diseases, and suddenly change a man’s counsels. But this mode of miracles cannot be simply natural, certain, common, and infallible; for if it is true, something I have not experienced, it is necessary that some external power also come in and that either a good angel or an evil spirit in part occupy the one thinking. But if no external powers intermingle, the one prophesying must form his beginning either from conjectures or from a bold presumption of speaking, and pronounce a judgment sometimes true, sometimes false. For indeed, as long as the spirit dwells in the body, by its nature it naturally understands nothing without the service of the senses.

Hence I would not altogether resist, if they grant that our confused and crude understanding is aided by a like service. For we see in the case of those who very often, through the desire of inward love for Jesus, pass in contemplation beyond the measure of the flesh, that they are lifted upward from the earth into the air; and we think that they overcome the bodily weight not merely by their own keenness of mind, but no less by divine power. Therefore the three aforementioned principles of opinion, unknown to many, satisfy, in my judgment, few, as the mind is placed in the body. I speak of a thing experienced. When as a young man I was pursuing the studies of letters, four of us were sleeping in one bed on a certain night. One of my own age rose from my side and, asleep as he was accustomed to be in dreams, with eyes closed and with the moon on its fifteenth day shining in, walked about as if awake; he climbed walls and surpassed a squirrel in his agility. While asleep he also crossed over the bed a second and a third time; he trod on all of us with his feet, and we felt his weight no more than if a tiny mouse had touched us. Wherever his sleeping body moved, suddenly all the fastenings of doors opened of themselves. He very swiftly penetrated the higher buildings of the house and clung to the roofs in the manner of a sparrow. I speak of things seen, not heard by vague report. These seem to be functions of the soul, not of the body; the soul uses its natural power, so to speak, almost freely, when, the senses of the flesh being bound, it wanders outside the dwelling of the body.
