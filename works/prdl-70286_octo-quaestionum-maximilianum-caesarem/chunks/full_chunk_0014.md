*To Maximilian Caesar.*

We read in the Gospel: “Many will say to me on that day, Lord, Lord, did we not cast out demons in your name, and in your name do many mighty works? And then I shall declare to them: I never knew you; depart from me, all you who work iniquity.” But outside the Church of God, where the name of our Lord Jesus Christ is not invoked, if any marvels occur beyond the usual course of nature or of human art, no good Christian doubts that they are done by the cooperation of demons. And works of this kind your Imperial Serenity judges, by discernment of their power, should rather be called marvels than miracles. Nor does this judgment of truth depart from reason, insofar as there should be a necessary distinction between prodigies of light and of darkness. I find four agents of marvels, but I recognize principally one alone, God, who performs true miracles, as the cause of all good things.

Therefore the first and greatest agent of all marvelous things is God, who, filling the whole world with signs, miracles, and powers, has illumined it; with him none of the others is to be compared. He created heaven and earth, the sea and all things that are in them; he alone is omnipotent, just, holy and upright, good, incomprehensible, greatest, glorious, immense and everlasting. All other things are beneath him. For whatever can happen marvelously in this world is certainly less than the whole world itself, which he was able to create from nothing. Likewise every miracle that can be performed through a human being is less than the human being himself, since God, creator of all things, formed him in the beginning to his greatness and likeness. And although the miracles of God among visible natures have, through the constancy with which mortals see them, now almost become cheapened, nevertheless, if we examine them wisely, then they will appear greater and rarer than all things that are unusual.

*Johannes Trithemius, Eight Questions.*

But the manner of performing miracles in God is the immutable will of God, who, since he is omnipotent, does all things whatsoever he has willed in heaven and on earth. For he spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created. The will of God is ordered and everlasting; therefore his miracles are incomparably greater than the rest. For greater than all the miracles of this world is that we behold the whole universe, and whatever is contained in it, created by the will of God. The immutable divine will, persisting, works all things, since it lacks both beginning and end; but whatever God wills, he always wills, because, since willing and being able are the same in him, it does not belong to the immutable to labor. Hence all the universal miracles that the maker of all things performs he has offered for both good and evil people to behold. And after the general miracles, which have now grown common by use among mortals, he has also displayed, in almost individual creatures, infinite things specially worthy of wonder.

The second agent of miracles is the good angel, who very often works marvels for mortals both by natural power and by divine authority. For those most blessed spirits are sent by divine ordinance in ministry to the faithful, and they provide to both good and evil people those things that are benefits of nature. But to those alone who believe in God and in our Lord Jesus Christ they administer the gift of saving grace. Hence there is no doubt that those miracles which are done in the Church of the saints by invocation of the divine name are exhibited by their ministry and cooperation; but the marvels of the unbelievers, if any are either done today or are read to have been done formerly, are either fabricated or falsely claimed, or are prepared by the mockery of demons.
