*To the Emperor Maximilian.*

The human [intellect] cannot understand God, [unclear] until the death of the flesh, by which the mind, [unclear] natural freedom, will pass out wholly equal to an angel. You now see, I believe, Emperor, that it is manifest that it neither befitted nor was possible that the human intellect should comprehend the divine: the created comprehend the creator, the temporal the eternal, the changeable the unchangeable, the weak the omnipotent, since, as a certain philosopher says, there is either no proportion at all of the finite to the infinite. Therefore it is necessary for mortals to believe God immortal, things which we manifestly can comprehend by no reasoning. Although the order of the whole universe declares that providence is the author of all things, and we understand that nothing produced exists without a beginning except him, nevertheless what God is and how he is the human mind cannot grasp, since, being far above all things that we can understand concerning him, or that any mortal can, he is far outside and above every affirmation, as holy Dionysius says: inasmuch as he is neither soul nor sense, nor has imagination, or opinion, or reason, or understanding; nor is he reason, nor understanding; nor is he spoken, nor understood; nor is he number, nor multitude, nor order, nor magnitude, nor smallness, nor equality, nor likeness, nor unlikeness; nor does he stand, nor is he moved, nor does he act in repose; nor has he power, nor is he power; nor is he light, nor does he live, nor is he life; nor is he substance, nor age, nor [unclear], nor has his spiritual [unclear] been made; nor is he form, nor truth, nor kingdom, nor wisdom, nor one, nor deity, or goodness; nor is he spirit, so far as we ourselves can know; nor is there denomination of son or of father, nor any other thing among those things which has ever been known to us or to another in the world; nor is he anything of those things which are not, nor of those things which are; nor do the things which are know him as he is; nor is there speech of him, nor name, nor knowledge; nor is he darkness, nor light, nor error, nor truth; nor is there any affirmation of him at all, nor removal. For, as the perfect and singular cause of all things is above every affirmation, so the excellence of him, namely of him who, simply absolved from all things, is above all things, is above every removal.

Therefore we say, Dionysius says, that, since as the cause of all things he is above all, he is neither without substance nor without life, nor lacks reason or mind; nor is he body, nor figure, nor form, nor has quality or quantity or death; nor is he in a place, nor is he seen, nor has sensible touch; nor does he sense, nor is he sensible; nor is he subject to rashness or disturbance, stirred by fleshly passions; nor is he weak, subject to sensible accidents; nor is he deprived of light; nor does he admit alteration, or corruption, or division, or flux; nor is he or has he anything else among sensible things. Hence, since he is far above every affirmation, excelling beyond what is supposed, he could not be comprehended by a created being which necessarily uses the senses for understanding, just as the true Peripatetic said: nothing is in our intellect unless it has first been in the senses. Therefore God, who is not subject to the senses of mortals, far exceeds the human intellect; according to affirmative affirmation he truly is altogether in all things; according to negative removal, the nearer he is approached, he is none of all the things that are subject to the knowledge of mortals.
