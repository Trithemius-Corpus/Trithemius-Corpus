Otherwise, from that time onward, visitors of this kind who put off restoring that double amount beyond this time shall know themselves to be suspended from office and benefice until they render full satisfaction for this double amount to the churches or monasteries or places thus burdened, no remission, liberality, or favor on the part of those who gave it being of any force for them in this matter. And nevertheless, if it is expedient, those visitors so suspended are to be compelled by the presiders over the said chapters, and by each of them, to restitution of the aforesaid double amount, by apostolic authority, through ecclesiastical censure and other suitable remedies, the obstacle of appeal being removed.

And because it proceeds from the virtue of prudence if the way is closed to scandals, we forbid the aforesaid visitors to presume to reveal to others those persons, or the names of those persons, who have disclosed to them any secrets in visitations, except only when, on account of disclosures of this kind, proceedings must be taken for someone’s punishment or penalty. Otherwise, persons making such disclosures are to be punished more or less, as the nature of the deed shall require, according to the judgment of the same presiders.

*There follow from the decrees various rubrics on the state of monks, which it is useful for some to have at hand in visitations.*

*The conduct of monks ought to be set apart from all persons, according to the etymology of the name, because he is called “singular.”* 16, q. 1, c. 1.

*Monks ought not to be among a throng of people,* for they are therefore called singular because they ought to avoid the multitude of human beings. 16, q. 1, *Si cupis*.

*A monk, according to the etymology of the name, should lead a solitary life; he should be content with his cloister,* because just as a fish lacks life without water, so a monk without a monastery. Therefore let him sit alone and keep silence, because he is dead to the world, but lives to God. Let him recognize his name: for *monos* in Greek is in Latin “one”; *achos* in Greek is in Latin “sad.” Hence “monk” is said, that is, one and sad. Therefore let him sit sad and attend to his duty. 16, q. 1, *Placuit communi*.

*Apart from illness, permission neither to taste nor to take meat is granted to a monk.* But if anyone shall presume against the rule, let him be subject for a space of six months to confinement and penance. De cons., dist. 5, *Carnem*.

*Monks ought to be subject to their abbots in all obedience and devotion.* 18, q. 2, *Abbates*.

*No one ought to become a monk in hope of an abbacy.* 16, q. 7, *Per laicos*. See, however, the fine distinction in the gloss in the same place.

*A monk cannot make a testament.* 19, q. 3, *Quia*.

*When someone has become a monk, all things that he had are to be transferred to the monastery,* nor can he thereafter hand them over to anyone. 19, q. 3, *Prelatum*.

*It is not lawful for a monk to vow vows without the consent of the abbot; but if he has vowed, it must be broken.* 20, q. 4, *Monacho*.

*No one should receive or ordain a monk without the permission of his abbot.* 20, q. 4, *Monachum*.

*A monk ought never to be idle,* because every idle person is among desires; but he should always be engaged either in prayers or in manual labor that befits his state. Let him love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and he will not love the vices of the flesh. Let body and soul strive toward the Lord. Let his sense keep watch; and, not lying open to wandering thoughts, let him do some work, so that the devil may always find him occupied, and many other fine things Jerome says to Rusticus. De conse., dist. 5, *Nunquam*.
