To these things, therefore, the visitors themselves are bound under obedience and the penalties noted above. If they have been negligent, whether in many matters or in few, they must be compelled by the presidents and procurators of the chapter, through ecclesiastical censure or some other penalty, not to neglect the office enjoined upon them. But lest they be compelled to do labor without reward and, according to the apostolic tradition, to serve as soldiers at their own expense, it has been ordained that each monastery visited by them must give them three Rhenish florins for travel expenses; and it has been entrusted to their consciences, from good consideration, even to increase those same florins for them. Likewise, that the visited monasteries should guard their horses and other goods with the greatest diligence. And if, which God forbid, anything of their goods should happen to be lost or stolen, before all else the abbot and convent of such a monastery must pay without delay, and without contradiction, the value of the goods or things lost; and nevertheless they are to be punished for negligence by a penalty to be moderated by the presidents. *On the act of visitation. Rubric seven.*

In the act of visitation the eye of consideration of the fathers who are visiting must be directed to four principal things. First, namely, to the places of the monasteries, as to the more visible things. Second, to the persons living within the places of the monasteries. Third, to the conduct and discipline of the persons. Fourth and last, to the divine worship itself. If one considers the first of these diligently, it at once shows what is probably to be thought concerning the second; and again the second, what concerning the third; and so on in consequence.

*On the places of monasteries. Rubric eight.*

The Apostle says: “For what is spiritual is not first, but what is animal.” First, therefore, as has been said, one must consider exterior matters, that is, places as corporeal things. Places, however, fall under a threefold distinction. Some are necessary, such as the oratory, cloister, refectory, dormitory, infirmary, bath, pantry, cellar, place for guests together with the abbot’s house, kitchen, and porter’s cell, with the chapter-place. If any of these places is lacking, it must be built within a year, in virtue of holy obedience, or repaired if it has collapsed; and the negligent must actually be punished by the visitors, as above, chapter 3. Some places, however, are for well-being, such as the garden, bakery, mill, workshop for binding books, and [unclear]. Others, however, are superfluous and harmful and contrary to the regular norm: such are the wandering byways, secluded and suspicious dwellings of certain houses, which must either be destroyed from the foundations or converted to some other better use of the monastery, under the same obedience as above. The oratory, which is first among the necessary places, must be adorned with lights, altars, and ornaments that are neat, clean, and seemly. The choir must be furnished with seats, pulpits, and chant books. The sacristy must be furnished with chalices, corporals, missal books, sacred jewels, and sacred vestments and ornaments concerning the divine office and ministries, as is said in chapter 1, where these things also are added: “We will,” it says, “that provision be made within a month for a clean, seemly, and secure vessel and place in which and where the Eucharist is to be reserved.” And the same is laid down concerning the baptistery there and in the following statutes. Books, together with the other ornaments, are to be kept under diligent custody. These things concerning jewels and seals, and certain following chapters, wish to be understood and observed. Therefore the visitors must diligently attend to each of these things concerning the oratory.
