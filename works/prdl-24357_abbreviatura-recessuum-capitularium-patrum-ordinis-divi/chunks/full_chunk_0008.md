If it seems necessary to the visitors, they may bind those to be visited by a bodily oath to tell the truth, as in the constitution of lord Nicholas [unclear], chapter 3; and these matters must be discussed in their own place. In chapter 15 all those to be visited are instructed to receive their visitors with all reverence and humility and to treat them charitably, and diligently to heed and observe their admonitions and ordinances. The visitors themselves also have authority to impose greater penalties than are expressed in the constitutions, and to compel rebels, by ecclesiastical censure, to obedience to their commands.

*But sometimes many impediments occur concerning the visitations of monasteries. For sometimes the monks themselves and abbots maliciously obstruct these by themselves; sometimes through the ordinary bishops; sometimes through princes; and often through their blood-relations, friends, and supporters. What must be done in these cases, if they occur, I shall try to make clear from the statutes of the fathers. In chapter 15, which was celebrated second at Seligenstadt, we read the statute.*

That no one may obstruct the so salutary office of visitation by any contrived device or pretext: we will that each and all abbots, provosts, priors, and their professed members who refuse or contradict their visitors, and those same rebels, disobedient persons, and those who obstruct the office of visitation by nod, word, deed, or writing, or who arrange, procure, or by any device, by themselves or through another or others, directly or indirectly cause or attempt that it be obstructed by their friends, noble or ignoble, or by any subordinates whatsoever, so that the visitors cannot freely and quietly exercise this office of theirs, incur sentence of excommunication by the very fact. The visitors themselves are also bound to publish this same sentence of excommunication against them and to hand it over for due execution. But if the visitors cannot execute the aforesaid things, in chapter 21, celebrated fourth at Nuremberg, it was thus decreed: that the disobedient and contumacious persons visited should be denounced to the nearest of the presidents, who shall proceed against them by appropriate remedies of law on apostolic authority. And in chapter 23 this statute follows: We also strictly forbid anyone henceforth, by himself or through others, in any way to make arrangements with diocesans, princes, or any other temporal lords whatsoever, so that visitors are not admitted; nor let him attempt in any way that those visitors, who do not need the permission of the ordinaries of the place, since they are directed by apostolic authority, are not to be admitted, under penalty of thirty Rhenish florins, to be paid irremissibly to the chapter as often as any of the abbots and other rectors shall do the contrary. Moreover, this penalty seems to be imposed on the rectors of monasteries, without removing the aforesaid provision that they are to be compelled by ecclesiastical censure to obedience.

*If a Bishop Wishes to Visit with Us. Chapter 9.*

It is sometimes accustomed to happen that the ordinary bishops of the places wish to visit monasteries with us; whether they should be admitted in this way or not is often doubted. To settle this scruple, it must be considered that the bishops’ impulse to visit monasteries with the visitors of the order has two causes from which it arises. Either they do this from zeal for discipline, or from the contrivance of malice. If for the first reason, if their zeal is known and the necessity of visiting the monastery is so great, they may be admitted, but only in their own person, because laymen ought not at all to be present at a visitation unless it is very necessary.
