For it raises up what has collapsed, gathers what is scattered, reforms what is dissolved, reintegrates what is broken, brings back what wanders, joins together what is torn apart, and suitably preserves the things that are holy and just. It restores unity and peace between head and members, abbot and brothers, and reconciles inferiors to superiors. If visitation is carried out zealously, charitably, and discreetly, it compels due obedience to be shown by subjects to chapters. Let it cease, and the discipline of religion will quickly expire. For it makes offenders fear, and embraces those who are advancing in the bosom of charity. Few are those who obey from love; servile fear restrains many from evils. And happy is the necessity that compels one toward better things. For as the bristle introduces the thread, so initial fear introduces charity. De pe. dis. 2, § hec que, before the end. And woe is there where fear has perished from the earth. Micah 7; 16, q. 7. And we have said these things, after me. Since therefore visitation was instituted for this, that gains of souls may be sought through it, 10, q. 3, it is to be guarded against; all visitors must carefully weigh what is required of them. And lest they deviate from the statutes of the ancients, they should always strive to imitate their forms and examples. For the sake of this matter the present formula of visitation, drawn from the constitutions of the fathers, has been brought together in the following manner, so that new visitors, seeing before their eyes the due order of their office, may avoid various and dangerous errors.

*Who has authority to appoint visitors of monks. Visitors of our monks. Chapter 2.*

Visitors are to be appointed by the presidents of the provincial chapter, as in Extra, De statu monachorum, c. In singulis, where it is said as follows: [unclear]

Let religious and circumspect persons also be appointed in the same chapter, who should strive, in our place, to visit each abbey of that kingdom or province, not only of monks but also of nuns, according to the form prescribed for them, etc. And in the constitutions of Benedict XII, chapter 2, it says as follows: Let them also provide which abbots, and which and how many cathedral churches, monastic monasteries, and other places of the same order or religion, and how often, they ought to visit up to the provincial chapter itself. Long custom also, since it is not contrary to the constitutions, is to be held as law, 11, distinction, In his. Hence it is clear that the authority of the presidents has the power to appoint visitors; yet if at any time it should seem necessary and useful, they can apply the counsel of the chapter in choosing visitors.

*What sort the visitors ought to be. Chapter 3.*

Furthermore, in order that the visitors may be able fruitfully to visit others, religious men of the same order are to be chosen, having zeal for God and for souls according to knowledge, as in Extra, De statu monachorum, In singulis. And in the constitution of Benedict XII, chapter 3, it says: Let the visitors be suitable, careful, and discreet in carrying out the office of visitation; and let them observe all the things that are known to have been established in the aforesaid constitution of the same council and in the other constitutions of the said Honorius, our predecessor. Thus there. And they ought to be diligent, De statu monachorum, Attendentes, in Clementines, De censibus, c. Romana, book 6.
