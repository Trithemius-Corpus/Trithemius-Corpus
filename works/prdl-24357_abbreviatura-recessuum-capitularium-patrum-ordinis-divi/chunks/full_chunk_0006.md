But manifest matters do not need accusation. 2, q. 1, [unclear], § In manifestis, etc. and following. I judge him, too, to be not a lover of charity but a deceitful man, who for the sake of defamation or revenge reveals a brother's hidden crime. 5, q. 5, Qui ambulat; 6, q. 1, Summa. By crime one can understand not only a mortal offense, but also every venial one. 25, dist., Unum orarium, § Nunc autem. In serious matters, however, and those concerning reputation, diligent inquiry should be made; but before just proof, except in manifest cases, no one should be judged or condemned. 30, q. 5, Nullum ante veram, etc. and following. And in such matters two or three witnesses must be brought forward. 2, q. 5, c. 1, etc.; Placuit.

Therefore visitors should be cautious in judging such secret matters, in order to avoid scandal, unless the person has been convicted or has confessed of his own accord. 2, q. 1, Nos vero quemquam, and following; Nomen presbyteri, and following. But if the accuser fails in proof, he ought to be punished with the penalty of retaliation. Extra, De calumniatoribus, c. 1; 2, q. 3, Calumniator, and following; q. 8, Qui crimen; 3, q. 10, Eos, and following. It must also be guarded against that matters which have been corrected and punished be taken up again anew. Extra, De accusationibus, c. De his; and 2, q. 1, Prohibentur; 23, q. 4, Si illic. Thus also the holy fathers established.

Let everyone beware, moreover, lest by [unclear] he disturb the minds of his brothers or of his fathers. Furthermore, if any secrets have been revealed to the visitors themselves, they should beware of bringing them into public. 2, q. 1, Si peccaverit; 6, q. 2, Episcopus si clerico; c. Placuit. And concerning hidden matters one must not inquire too curiously. Extra, De accusationibus, Qualiter, iii. Yet concerning manifest matters, as I said above, the outcry of an accuser is not necessary. Same title, c. Evidentia.

If at any time they perceive that some matters have not been properly proposed, let them act as prudent men; and, so far as can be done while preserving the truth, let them not readily believe them. And if they must be believed, nevertheless those things of which it is not known with what intention they are done should be interpreted in the better part, because we ought rather to presume good than evil. De regulis iuris, c. Estote. And in such matters the intention of the deponent is to be weighed rather than his words. For if someone sets forth true things from an evil intention, the intention makes a good work evil. 23, q. 5, Cum minister.

Let visitors be cautious and especially discreet in examining the excesses of prelates; since they have to correct the sins of others, they frequently incur the hatred of many and suffer snares. Extra, De accusationibus, c. Qualiter, ii. In all things let them always act in such a way that they do not lose the form of charity. For correction ought to be made according to each person's rank and with the peace of the Church preserved. 23, q. 4, Ecce, and c. Quisquis; c. Quidam; c. Cum quisque; c. Ipsa pietas; c. Quid faciet circa me. Likewise correction ought to be made with the intention of healing, not of raging. 86, dist., Odio; 23, q. 4, Ita plane circa me. And not from hatred, but from charity. Extra, De accusationibus, c. Qualiter, ii; 24, q. 3, Corripiantur. And sometimes it should have more gentleness than severity. 45, dist., Qui sincera, and c. Licet; because one gently chastised shows reverence to the one chastising. c. Cum beatus Paulus; 12, q. 2, Fraternitas; 24, q. 3, Corripiantur. These and other testimonies of discretion, the mother of virtues, visitors ought not to omit, so that they may proceed worthily and justly.

*The form of the oath which visitors once used to present to presidents. Chapter 6.* Once the presidents, not only in Mainz but also in other provinces, used to require from visitors an oath concerning the diligent fulfillment of their office. This, however, is not done in our times, nor do I judge that it would be useful or necessary; because one whom the spur of his own conscience does not drive to exercise diligence is scarcely or rarely compelled by another's command.
