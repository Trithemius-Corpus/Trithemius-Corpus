Likewise he will be free if every diligence of the shepherd has been applied to a restless or disobedient flock, and every care has been shown for the diseased actions of them. Let their shepherd, absolved in the judgment of God, say with the prophet to the Lord: “I have not hidden your justice in my heart. I have declared your truth and your salvation”; but they, despising it, rejected me. And then at last, for the sheep disobedient to his care, the prevailing punishment is death itself.

He ought to preside over his disciples with a twofold teaching: that is, he should show all good and holy things by deeds more than by word, so that to capable disciples he may set forth the Lord’s commandments in words, but to those hard of heart and simpler, he may demonstrate the divine precepts by his own deeds. But let him show by his own actions that the things he has taught his disciples to be contrary are not to be done, lest, preaching to others, he himself be found reprobate, lest sin should ever say to him: “Why do you recount my justices and take up my covenant in your mouth? But you have hated discipline and have cast my words behind you.” And: “You who saw the speck in your brother’s eye did not see the beam in your own.”

Let no distinction of persons be made by him in the monasteries. Let one not be loved more than another, unless he has found him better in good deeds or obedience. Let the freeborn not be preferred to one converted from servitude, unless some other reasonable cause exists. But if, with justice dictating, it shall seem good to the abbot, let him do this from whatever rank. Otherwise let them keep their proper places, because whether slave or free, we are all one in Christ, and under one Lord we bear the equal service of soldiership; for there is no acceptance of persons with God. We are distinguished before him in this part only: if we are found better than others in good works and humble. Therefore let equal charity be shown by him to all; let one discipline be applied by him to all according to their merits.

*XXIII*

For in his teaching the abbot ought always to observe that apostolic sentence, in which it says: “Reprove, entreat, rebuke”; that is, mingling seasons with seasons, caresses with terrors. Let him show the sternness of a master and the pious affection of a father: that is, he ought to reprove the undisciplined and restless more harshly; the obedient, gentle, and patient he should entreat, that they may advance toward the better; but the negligent and contemptuous we admonish him to rebuke and correct. And let him not dissemble the sins of offenders, but as soon as they have begun to arise, let him cut them out by the roots before they prevail, mindful of the danger of Eli the priest of Shiloh. And indeed, the more honorable and intelligent souls he should correct with words at the first or second admonition; but the wicked, hard, proud, or disobedient he should restrain at the very beginning of sin by blows or bodily chastisement, knowing that it is written: “The fool is not corrected by words.” And again: “Strike your son with the rod, and you will deliver his soul from death.”

The abbot ought always to remember what he is, and to remember what he is called, and to know that from him to whom more is entrusted, more is required. And let him know how difficult and arduous a task he has undertaken: to govern souls and to serve the characters of many. And one he must guide with caresses, another with rebukes, another with persuasions; and according to each one’s quality or understanding, let him so conform and adapt himself to all that he may not only suffer no losses in the flock entrusted to him, but may rejoice in the increase of a good flock.

Before all things, lest by dissembling or making little of the salvation of the souls entrusted to him he have greater concern for transitory, earthly, and perishable things, let him always consider that he has undertaken souls to be governed, concerning whom he will have to render account.
