*What manner of life’s discipline is to be undertaken.*

*Chapter IV.*

The brevity of the present life warns us not to spend uselessly the time granted for penitence. For indeed time passes irrevocably, and the day that is spent without the fruit of good work is reckoned liable to account. For now is the acceptable time, in which we are commanded to do good without interruption, that by it eternal rewards may be obtained. Harmful, and altogether damnable, is the loss of time, when by laboring for a little while we are able to merit eternal rest. Therefore every person zealous for his salvation should take the greatest care not to lose uselessly the time granted to him. For now is the time for laboring; after death no one will any longer be able to labor, when every meritorious action will cease. Let each one, therefore, now choose a discipline of life suited to his own state, in which he may not lose the acceptable time. But whoever has resolved to undertake the purpose of holy conduct [unclear].

“Son, when you come to the service of God, stand in fear, and prepare your soul for temptation.” For no one is able to persevere in a good purpose who undertakes [unclear] patience. Hence he says elsewhere: “Unless you hold yourself steadfastly in the fear of God, your house,” that is, your conscience, “will quickly be overthrown.” For it is written of man that he never remains in the same state.

Now he undertakes the purpose of amending his life with fervent spirit, and after a short time, growing lukewarm again, falls back once more into his former negligences. Now he burns with devotion even to tears, and an hour later cold torpor wholly occupies his mind. When fervor of spirit is present, we make great resolutions; when temptation comes upon us, good resolutions are at once forgotten. But the purpose of amendment was never firm if it could be lost so easily. For a fool changes like the moon, but a wise man remains steadfast in a holy purpose. There are many who in times of peace and tranquillity show great devotion; but as soon as some temptation begins to arise, all their devotion utterly perishes.

They confess the Lord when he has done them good; but when some adversity has rushed upon them, they not only cast aside their former devotion, but, what is more serious, they grow inflamed to murmur against God. And there are some who, when placed under some tribulation, flee to God with constant prayers; but as soon as the breeze of tribulation has passed, all devotion recedes from their memory. And there are some who, neither elated by prosperity nor cast down by adversities, remain steadfast in the fear of God, who in favorable circumstances preserve fear, and in contrary circumstances preserve patience. These alone walk rightly before God: giving thanks in all things, they always build the house of their conscience in fear. Of such persons the wise man says: “Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil.”

Whoever, therefore, desires to make progress in holy conduct, whoever hastens to come to eternal joys, let him not depart from the fear of the Lord and the guarding of himself. For indeed, if he casts aside for a moment the fear and solicitude of the spiritual life, if he admits torpor into his mind, he will not remain long in his holy purpose. For no negligence is so small that it does not grow if it is despised. Therefore the lover of his own salvation must put on the breastplate of patience and avoid all negligences, as far as is possible for man, lest, while he confidently supposes that he has made progress, he gradually grow lukewarm through carelessness.

But since a man cannot long persevere in the same state, even if he has fallen daily, let him rise again. A man cannot attain the habit of a good and holy purpose unless he strives to renew his affection every day. This gift is not given to many; it is granted by God only to those whose manner of life is represented by the eagle. These are holy and perfect men, who, loving God above all things, seek nothing earthly, desire nothing carnal. Such men never draw back the eyes of their mind from the rays of the true sun, except when they yield to the necessity of the body. They direct their gaze toward the truly splendid sun, because without interruption they lift up the eyes of their mind to heavenly things.
