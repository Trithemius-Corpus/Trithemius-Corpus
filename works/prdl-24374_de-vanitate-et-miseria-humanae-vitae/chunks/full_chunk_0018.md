Indeed, you are to render an account to God not only for your own soul, but, placed as ruler over all those subject to you, you will answer to God for the souls of each one. O if you now set that day before the eyes of your mind; O if you foresaw your last things, you would easily despise the world with its vain honors. Behold, death hastens; behold, the end approaches; behold, the consummation of your rule hurries on. Therefore, if you are wise, if you fear the peril of your soul, remember past sins, and let the recollection of death never withdraw from your inmost parts. For the wise man says: Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever. Nothing so recalls a man from the vanities of the world, nothing mortifies the desires of the flesh, as frequent and intent meditation on death. He easily despises the world who always thinks that he is going to die. Hence Solomon, moved by consideration of most certain death, says: I accounted laughter error, and to joy I said: Why are you deceived in vain? Therefore it is better to go to the house of mourning than to the house of feasting. For in the former a man is made solicitous about his end; in the latter, however, nothing is thought about death. What if a man has lived many years upon the earth? What if he has been rich, powerful, noble? Death does not fear the authority of earthly power; it does not regard riches; it does not dread nobility of birth. With equal vengeance it is borne against all; it has learned to seize all without respect of persons; it knows how to spare none. We all die; we are all dragged before the tribunal of Christ for our merits to be examined; to remain long in this world is granted to no one. Therefore let no one deceive himself; let no one presume upon the honors of the world; let no one trust in the multitude of riches. Let each one prepare himself to die well through a good life; let no one place uncertain hope in length of life, because the longer anyone has lived in the world, the less willing he will be to die. Why is this? Indeed, while life is prolonged, guilt also increases in many things. For often one who, as a young man, is seen prepared and willing to die is found, as an old man, unprepared and most desirous of a longer life. With life grows the appetite for living, and the more a man hastens toward death, the more he loves life. For a young man fears death less, because he knows that he has sinned in fewer things. But when a man’s age is extended to old age, it is defiled by many vices and sins, and he fears death with such anxiety in proportion as he recognizes that he has sinned in many things. Human weakness is prone to sin; and the time of life granted to it, which it ought to have spent in good works, it sees that it has very often indulged in the pleasures of the flesh. A man demands that his life be prolonged for him; and while he delays doing penance for past things, he fears to die unprepared. The wretched man desires to live long in the world, and the longer he is permitted to remain in this life, the less he is found to have amended his ways. He desires to be old, but refuses to be called old. He asks to come to old age through many years, and when he has arrived, he is afraid to depart from the body. O most wretched condition of human weakness. Why are you deceived in vain, mortal man? How long will you not acknowledge that you are going to die? You live badly, and for that reason you are afraid to die. You were unwilling to wipe away the faults of your youth through penance; you did not fear to add sins; and therefore you flee to appear before the eternal judge. But you fear in vain; in vain you are afraid: you will not be able to escape death. You demand that time for repentance be extended for you, and however much prolongation you receive, you do not amend your life. What profit is the longest life to you, when it grows wholly old in sins? What are many years, empty of good works? O rare amendment of life in the aged. Old age always joins new vices to old ones; it always advances into worse things; ever uncertain, it tends toward death. For the disposition that it once put on in adolescence it does not lay aside on any of the days on which it dwells upon the earth. For it is very difficult to leave what one is accustomed to and to think new things with an old mind. What a man learned as a young man, he will retain as an old man. The purpose of amendment, when by delaying one neglects it, one shamefully loses. Behold, while we are healthy we put off saving penance; we postpone the amendment of our life from day to day; and while we desire to live longer in the world, overtaken by the hour of death, we depart from this life without repentance.
