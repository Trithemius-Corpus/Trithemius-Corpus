Wretched, plainly. Therefore even infants are wretched? Yes, wretched. For what reason wretched? Because they are human beings. If therefore all are wretched, who are happy? No one in this life. Whatever a person’s status, sex, condition, or age may be, so long as he is in the world, he will be wretched. Does death, then, make people happy? It does not make, but distinguishes.

What does it distinguish? Between the happy and the wretched. For all of us, as long as we live in the world, are wretched. But when through death we are released from this life, we are distinguished as though fish caught on the shore: some of us, who are good, are led to eternal life, thereafter to lack all misery; others of us, who are evil, are destined from temporal misery to eternal misery. For the misery of this life can be understood in two ways: one which, from the necessity of nature, is imposed commonly on all; another which is produced by various accidents. From the necessity of corrupted nature we are all wretched: all changeable by the vicissitude of times or by chance, all passible in the flesh, and all, finally, mortal. From the variety of accidents and fortune, misery is not equal for all; but each person in his own state, according to the quality of his mind, either conquers or is conquered by temptations. Finally, very often in this pilgrimage misery is inflicted on a person according to the merit of his life, where it is not the penalty that is rewarded, but the cause. For whoever suffers in this world for justice is judged not wretched but blessed.

But he who endures punishments or persecutions in the world for his own crimes is wretched and pitiable, and far removed from the blessedness of the saints. Truly, to bear the necessary miseries of this life for the love of God with a patient and humble mind is no small part of justice. For all things that seem necessary to us by nature can become meritorious through the love of God.

If therefore we gladly and humbly endure the necessities and miseries of this life for the love of God, we come very close to justice. Thus, so that after death we may deserve to be made happy in heaven, let us patiently tolerate the miseries of our present sojourn, converting the instruments of necessity even into weapons of piety. For as long as we live in this pilgrimage, we are necessarily wretched. The necessity of suffering is indeed equal for all, but the outcome is very different. He who can convert necessity into virtue for the love of God, once freed by death from the misery of this life, will deserve to obtain eternal happiness. But he who, placed in this life, impatiently tolerates the necessities and miseries of the human condition, who, set aflame by the torches of crimes, murmurs against God, when dead does not escape misery but changes it. What does he change? Misery. What misery? Temporal misery. How does he change it? A small one into the greatest, a brief one into an eternal one. For he who, persevering here in his sins, ends temporal misery by death, enters eternal misery, condemned by the just judgment of God. And he who in this life patiently sustains tribulations and adversities for the greatest love of God, freed after death from all misery, happy in the land of the living and to reign without end, lacks misery. For to the just, from death, the entrance of happiness lies open. Now therefore, since in this life we necessarily suffer misery and various temptations, let us patiently and with a cheerful mind tolerate the evils that we bear also for the love of God. And let no one think himself a stranger to the misery of this world, because then each person is truly wretched when he thinks himself happy. From infancy to old age, no one lacks misery. In every age there is misery, although many do not understand that they are wretched. Whatever is generated subject to change and is corrupted is rightly declared vain and wretched. Therefore man is vain and wretched, his works vain, and he too is troubled in vain. In misery and sins he is conceived and born; in misery and continual pain he is raised; in misery he lives wholly and dies. Finally, every age, status, and condition is harried and burned by its own miseries and temptations. Who will be able worthily to unfold the miseries of infancy and childhood? Conceived in his mother’s womb, man is wretched, and subject to death before he comes alive into the light. He is conceived in the sins of his first parents, and from a corrupt root he is born a son of wrath. Finally, before he begins to live for the world, he approaches death. But of these things we shall speak elsewhere. Misery is wholly lacking to no one who sojourns in this life. The tender infancy of children has its miseries; youth has them; old age has them too.
