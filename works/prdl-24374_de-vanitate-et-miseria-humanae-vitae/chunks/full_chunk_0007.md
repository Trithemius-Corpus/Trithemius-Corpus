It is for all men to die once; nor can we escape death, although we may put it off for many years. All things that are of the earth return to the earth; the spirit, released from the flesh, is hurried to the tribunal of the judge, where it is rewarded according to the quality of its conduct. How long, then, do you now sleep, O man? How long do you disregard the patience of God's long-suffering? Why do you not amend your life for the better? Behold, men die daily before your eyes; children and old men depart suddenly; learned and foolish fall into the pit; and do you, as if secure, think about your death? Behold, the world is collapsing. Men perish. Cities and towns are overturned. Nothing among human affairs remains long, and still your hard mind is not shaken? Soon your destruction will come; death is near; the end approaches. Why do you delay? Why do you linger? Why do you not do penance, wretched man, while you are healthy and strong? Behold, the world passes away, and you follow it; it deceives, and you love it; it lies, and you trust it. What do you think will happen to you? With what perishes you perish; with what slips away you slip away; with what deceives you, you are deceived. Woe to lovers of the world. They are fools; they are wretched; they are unhappy. Truly, while they desire to hold the world, they lose heaven and earth alike. They anxiously seek the riches and honors of the age, and trust that they will come to the heavenly kingdoms? But the contrary is what is spoken in the Gospels. “No one,” he says, “can serve two masters.” Who are the two masters? God and the world. For he who loves God despises the world. And he who loves the world neglects God. “You cannot,” he says, “serve God and mammon,” that is, riches. The present life does not remain but passes away; it does not endure but vanishes; it does not stand but perishes. Thus it is a road to the fatherland, along which, never to return again, we pass at every moment. Therefore he is a foolish traveler who, intent on the spectacles of the roads, forgets so excellent a fatherland; who, neglecting eternal things, pursues temporal and vain things; who embraces the world even now as it is slipping away. For nothing is more fleeting than the things of the world; nothing more fragile than human life; nothing briefer than earthly power. Blessed, therefore, is the man who has learned to despise the world; who has learned to overcome the pleasures of the flesh; who can refrain from loving the riches of this perishing age. For all things that are earthly abandon a man only in death, so that he who, rich, had possessed many things carries absolutely nothing with him. Hence the wise man cries out, saying: “What remains for man in all his labor, with which he labors under the sun?” Behold how many today sweat in the toil of this world; day and night they keep watch over the gathering of riches; they educate children; they construct buildings on high; and in the midst of the work they perish by sudden death, and carry absolutely nothing with them, but return naked into the earth from which they were taken. A generation goes and a generation comes, but the earth stands forever. As some die, others are born; and while generation succeeds generation, nothing remains stable in the world. What is more vain than this vanity? The earth, which was made for man's sake, remains; and man himself, possessing the earth, is suddenly dissolved into dust. There is no memory of the first ones who preceded us; and just as forgetfulness has hidden past things among us, so posterity will be ignorant of those things which are either now being done or are to come; and all things will pass by in silence, and will be hidden as if they had not existed. Therefore all things are vain, and not only vain, but vanity of vanities. Vain is the world, because those whom it endures as lovers it in no way saves when they die. Vain are all the honors of the age, because those whom they make great before men it suddenly leaves alone and ignoble in death. Vain are riches, because they do not free their possessors from death, but condemn them more severely for their misuse. Vain is all earthly power, because, abandoning in a moment those whom it has placed on high, it sends them down to the lower regions. Vain is the nobility of high blood, because those whom it makes glorious before men it grants, when they die, as food to worms. Vain too is a large family of kin, because he who, alive, is set apart from others by the nobility of his stock is, when dead, compared to beasts. Vain is kinship of the flesh, because it cannot interrupt the sentence of death. Vain is sweet marital union, because those whom it loves alive it immediately leaves behind when dead. Vain is the laborious upbringing of children at great expense, because the parent whom it follows while alive it detests when dead. Vain is whatever is seen as precious in the world, because it is quickly lost.
