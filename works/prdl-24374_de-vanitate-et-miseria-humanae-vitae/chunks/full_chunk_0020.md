Woe to hardened sinners, who, while they neglect to do penance, descend to hell to be tormented without end. What use are earthly riches now to those set in the torments of Gehenna? What use are honors held in the world for a brief time? What use the glory of the world, what the majesty of a kingdom? All have passed away, and like smoke have vanished. The wretched lie in torments, to be tortured forever without end. Where are the pleasures of the flesh which they loved? Where the hope of a longer life? Where the resolve to repent, postponed through so many years? Pleasure has been changed into punishment that will endure without end; hope into confusion; pleasures into reproach. Now confession has perished from the dead man as though from nothing; the purpose of penance has vanished; the time for working has expired. Blessed is the man who is always fearful, who thinks every day in advance on the bitterness of death, who prepares himself through penance to meet God. For nothing so stirs the mind to compunction, nothing so mortifies the desires of the flesh, nothing so hastens penance, as frequent and intent meditation on death. Thus Hezekiah, king of Judah, when he had received the message of death, turned to the wall and wept, and obtained a postponement of life by tears. Thus the Ninevites, when they heard the Lord’s proclamation concerning the overthrow of the city, were afraid, and, salutarily pierced with compunction, hastened to do penance in sackcloth and ashes, and received pardon from the Lord together with the safety of the city. Thus Ahab, king of Israel, having received the sentence of death, with lowered head was humbled to the ground, who before, exalted and proud, had despised the words of Elijah. No one is so savage and proud that he is not made gentle by intent remembrance of death. Therefore always be mindful of your end, that you may not forget the last judgment, if you desire to overcome the vain lusts of the world. For indeed he who thinks death is threatening him while he is alive loves nothing perishable in the world. The diligent memory of death despises the world, scorns the fires of the flesh, contemns riches. For the soul is easily led to contempt of earthly things if it meditates anxiously on death close at hand. Finally, if we diligently attend to the dangers of death, it is a wonder if we hold back from tears. For the more sharply does the devil make a din in our [unclear] at the hour of death, the more he knows that after this [unclear] the opportunity of conquering is being taken from him.

For that faithless persecutor of human salvation knows that the reward depends upon the end of the labor, and therefore he strives to resist more strongly at the point of death, lest a man be able to remain in his holy purpose. And therefore we read that many among the saints greatly feared death, because they did not doubt that the whole of salvation consisted in the end. What shall we wretched sinners do in that hour, where the holy and perfect tremble? What will boards do where the great columns of a house are shaken? If the righteous man fears death, how will the impious and the sinner stand? Woe to us. We spend the time of penance badly, and, as though secure, hasten toward death. He is deceived who awaits death without penance. He who lives badly never dies well without penance. When the Lord was hanging on the cross, the plotter against salvation is read to have been present; and he who could never find any sin in him sought something of his own in death. Who now would not fear, if he weighs the craftiness of the cunning enemy? When that most holy bishop of Tours, Martin, was once dying, the devil was present and sought something of his own in him. The saint said: “Why do you stand here, bloody beast? Whom do you await? Depart; you will find nothing deadly in me.” To this the devil is said to have answered indignantly: “I was present when the Son of God was dying on the cross, and ought I not to come near you? No one has ever died at whose death I was not present.” Hence, though holy men always fear the divine judgments in all things, nevertheless they are then vehemently afraid when they perceive that they are drawing near to the strict judge as he comes to exact the debt of the human condition; and fear becomes the sharper, the more eternal the recompense of vices is. What will we wretched little worms be able to do when we are about to die? Let us therefore fear death, which will most certainly come to us, lest we [unclear] it when present. For nothing is more certain than death, nothing among human things harsher, nothing more horrible. And although we are all most certain of death to come, yet we do not know at what hour it will come, or in what state it will cast us down. Hence in the Gospel the Lord admonishes us, saying: “Watch, for you know neither the day nor the hour.” And, introducing a similitude on this, he says: “And you, be like men waiting”
