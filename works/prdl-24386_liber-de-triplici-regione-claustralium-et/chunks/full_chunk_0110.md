And again he says: "Those who are at peace can search the law, provided that their hearts, free from the tumult of the world, are at leisure." Therefore, so that the mind, intent on holy studies, may be aided, let divine love overcome fleshly quarrels. Remain, then, quiet in your cell, and by loving you will be able to know the truth. For the mind cannot be tranquil and capable of divine wisdom when the body is restless. Hence Hugh says: "Be quiet in the dormitory, lest another be disturbed through you." But when you wake, or when you wish to sleep, let the memory of God's goodness always come before you with thanksgiving: how he himself always watches over us, so that he may guard us, humbly serving him, from every evil. When you go to your bed, you should first set before yourself something by thinking or meditating about God, so that amid holy thoughts you may sweetly be able to fall asleep. For as the occupations of one who is awake have been, so also will be the rest of one who sleeps, and such too will be his dreams. For the mind burning with divine love, whether sleeping or waking, will not be able to forget its beloved, just as the bride says in the Canticles: "I sleep, and my heart watches."

*At Vespers. Consideration VII.*

[unclear] After the signal, with the other things that are customarily put first, you can sympathize with those occupied in worldly business; and give thanks that almighty God wished you to be free from them for serving him. Then you can gather yourself back to inward things, or meditate somewhat on the Lord's Passion, until, when the second signal sounds, you go to choir. For in my judgment no meditation is better for beginners and those making progress toward compunction and devotion of mind than that which takes its beginning from the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ through unknown affections.

*At the Office for the Dead.*

[unclear] After the compulsion of vigils following vespers, you can say the hour of the Holy Cross, and afterward think somewhat about the immeasurable charity of God by which he loves faithful souls. Then, so far as you can, think in what great desolation those holy souls placed in purgatory are; how great the pain is for a spirit separated from the body and desiring to return to the center of its origin, not to be able to behold the face of its Creator by friendly enjoyment, but to be compelled to wait in pains and torments most grievous to it. For so great is the desolation, sorrow, and grief of a separated soul placed in pains and desiring to come to God, that no kind of torments known to us in this world could be compared with its sadness.
