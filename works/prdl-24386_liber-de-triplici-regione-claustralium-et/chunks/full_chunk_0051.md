For thus the Rule says: “But if at any time he has acted otherwise, let him know that he is to be condemned by the one whom he mocks.” For if anyone making less progress in religion is believed to be guilty before the divine judgment, what else does one who abandons the effort of making progress deserve than to receive a sentence of damnation? For where, even among those striving to make progress, an account is laid down according to the Rule concerning lesser progress, monks who despise every effort to make progress are without doubt condemned by the sentence of the judge. If therefore you do not wish to incur the danger of damnation, strive continually under the Rule to preserve the effort of one making progress.

Since we have said that the other five degrees of humility pertain to guarding the inner man, we shall also briefly set them forth.

For the monk who neglects to order his outward conduct according to the Rule will not long remain ordered in inward things. For just as a body stripped of clothing is corrupted, so humility without good conduct is emptied of force.

The eighth degree of humility, then, is set down thus in the Rule: if a monk does nothing except what the common rule of the monastery, or the examples of the elders, encourage. In this degree singularity of outward conduct contrary to the Rule is forbidden to monks; but inward singularity of greater charity and humility is forbidden to no one. Therefore let no one prefer his own inventions to the holy Rule, let no one place his own customs before the examples of the elders, because it is not enough to stand in the seventh degree if one does not keep the rule of the community.

The ninth degree of humility follows from the Rule, by which excessive talkativeness is forbidden. For it befits a monk to preserve silence, especially when he is sitting in the midst of his seniors. Indeed, one who speaks without being asked is rightly considered a fool.

The tenth degree of humility forbids readiness to laugh and levity, because whoever takes pleasure in laughter is shown to be foolish and alien to the fear of God.

The eleventh degree of humility enjoins modesty and maturity upon the monk, so that when an opportunity and permission to speak is granted, he should speak with humble gravity, without laughter and without irritation. Let the monk’s speech be rare, brief, and reasonable, because it does not befit him to say useless things who wishes to please the Most High.

The twelfth degree of humility teaches the monk to order the outward man in appearance and bearing from a humble heart, and to show by his outward conduct that he is inwardly devout. And although this degree is last in the order of all of them, nevertheless in the practice of beginners who are making progress it is first. And therefore Bernard, when describing these twelve degrees of humility, begins from this one and, ascending to the first, descends, so to speak, in order. For no one easily makes inward progress toward compunction of heart and the fear of God who does not order his conduct outwardly.
