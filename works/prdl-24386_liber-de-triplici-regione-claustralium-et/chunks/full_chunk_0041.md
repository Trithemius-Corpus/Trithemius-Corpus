[Re]velations of the torments or joys of separated souls, when they are in conformity with the catholic faith, we freely admit; by them we have found that sluggish hearts are very often stirred to compunction. Finally, the passions of the holy martyrs excite in no small measure the fervor of devotion; in them, when we recall to mind the unconquerable charity of Christ, pierced by the example of such constancy and touched as if by a certain fire of love, we sigh over our imperfection. From reading the material for meditation is taken; and such as the former has been, such will the latter be. It is not the shameful songs of poets, not the curious doctrines of philosophers, not the swollen writings of rhetoricians, that excite the fervor of devout compunction in monks; rather, they choke off what has been conceived. If, therefore, you wish to devote yourself to good meditations, do not occupy your soul with other readings than holy and devout ones. For indeed the grape is not gathered from thorns, nor is compunction born from the study of vanities. Let him who studies for the knowledge of this world read those things that are suited to his learning; but let him who seeks compunction think on those things that kindle the soul to the love of God through fear. Therefore let frequent reading take its beginning from the last things that await us; then let the deeds of the holy fathers be read, so that the memory of future things may strike the mind with fear of God, and the exemplary conduct of the saints may kindle the desire to imitate them. For the wise man says: Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever. The conduct of the holy fathers is set before us as an example, and the destruction of the wicked is recounted to us for our discipline, so that rewarded virtue may move desire, and punished iniquity may forbid the license of sinning. Therefore let the devout cenobite always recall to memory the examples and virtues of the saints; let him consider the brief pleasure of the depraved but their eternal torment, so that by fearing unhappiness he may deserve to escape it, and by cleaving to virtues to obtain their perpetual joy. The distinct condition of the good and the wicked is no light matter, since each is described as going to remain forever, and is bounded by no end. O eternity, ever to be feared by all mortals, in which, after this life, there is no fear of condemnation for the just, and no hope of liberation for the perverse. Let him who can think what eternity is; where there is no end, let him who dares imagine a boundary; and when he has found nothing in eternity except eternity, let him understand that all the time in which he lives in this world is nothing. For indeed the spirit, once stripped of the flesh, is necessarily compelled to enter this abyss of eternity, and the condition that it has once obtained according to its merits it will endure without end, and will not exchange for another. For he who dies good in the charity of God will obtain eternal life according to his merit. But he who is evil and, without the reconciliation of penitence, has once departed from this life, will remain to be tormented without end with the demons in hell.
