Behold, now, in a brief span of time, we can earn the joys of eternal happiness; and we all neglect to do what is good. Woe to us, foolish and mad, who have received nothing in this world more precious than time, and yet are accustomed to expend nothing more cheaply. For now an acceptable time is granted to us; now the days of salvation are bestowed, in which we ought to apply ourselves to holy works with the greatest fervor, and always foresee the hour of death.

Seventh, God has set before us heaven filled with all joys, and has promised himself, with all things contained in it, as a reward. For this munificence of our Savior we ought to apply ourselves to holy works of mercy, and to show ourselves, as far as we can, imitators of God. To this the Lord exhorts us in the holy Gospels: Be merciful, as your Father God also is merciful. Likewise at the end of the world, when the day of judgment has come, the merciful, as the Lord himself promises, will go into eternal life, since he who has shown mercy will obtain mercy. And lest any of the monks say to me, “I can expend mercy on no one, since religion permits me to possess nothing at all,” let us know that among the necessary works of mercy, spiritual works are the more excellent. If you, a monk, wish to be merciful, pray to God for the wretched; bestow saving doctrine on the unlearned.

Therefore the sum of meditation on God’s benefits rests upon three exercises, in which it is principally wholly contained. In the first, indeed, we ought to attribute all things to God, not to ourselves; in the second, by remembering God’s benefits, to exercise ourselves in zeal for love; in the third, always to give thanks from a pure heart for the benefits received. But if by these exercises concerning benefits we wish our heart to grow toward the love of God, we ought to consider six conditions in the gifts of the Lord themselves. First, consider that the gift of God is thoroughly noble; second, how especially fruitful and useful it is to you; third, because it is varied, manifold, and very frequently repeated. And these three consider the gift as ordered in itself; the two that follow regard the person of the giver; the last notes the condition of the recipient. Fourth, therefore, consider the great affection of the giver toward you; fifth, his inestimable dignity; sixth, that you, receiving great things from the Greatest, are vile and contemptible, and unworthy of all things. From these considerations of benefits the love of God is accustomed to arise in the mind of a devout person, and, advancing by certain steps, to lift the spirit to contemplation.
