*Passing before the crucifix, let him say:*

I beseech you, most loving Lord Jesus, by all the bitterness of your most holy passion which you endured for me, and especially when your most noble soul departed from your body: have mercy on my soul at its departure. Amen.

*At the holy water.*

Take away from me, Lord, all my iniquities, that with a pure mind I may deserve to enter into the holy of holies. You shall sprinkle me, Lord, with hyssop, etc.

*Before the image of the blessed Virgin Mary.*

O flowering rose, beautiful mother of the Lord; O gentle Virgin, O most fruitful vine, brighter than the dawn, pray continually for us.

*Passing before the altar, let him say:*

Glory to you, Lord, who were born of the Virgin, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, forever and ever. Or: You, the unbegotten Father; you, the only-begotten Son; you, the Holy Spirit; the holy and undivided Trinity, I praise, bless, and adore without end. O highest unity, beginning of all things, have mercy on me for your own sake.

*Before the venerable sacrament.*

Hail, living host, truth and life, in whom the ancient sacrifices are ended. Grant me rewards in eternal life. Amen. Or: O saving host [unclear]

When he has come to the place of his station, or to his accustomed place in choir, if he has leisure, let him think what our holy father Benedict said in the Rule about reverence in prayer and psalmody. Then, considering the matter of the psalms to be sung, let him strive to subject his intention to it, and carefully foresee to what he wishes to refer each thing. If the morning office is being sung for some saint, let the psalmody be said before the Lord in memory of that saint.

But if the subject matter of the psalms agrees with the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, it is to be referred in memory of him. But if the spirit of compunction is present to the one singing, it will teach all things through love. For a mind fervent in the love of God knows very well how to direct its intention to the praise of the Creator, and to vary it suddenly in each verse according to the character of the psalmody. For according to the matter of what is being sung, whenever it wishes, it exercises itself, and now embraces the humanity of Christ, now suddenly, in another verse, is raised into his divinity. Our faithful monk may nevertheless, if he pleases, while his fervor lasts, call to mind under the same sign the holy angels praising God without interruption, and according to his strength conform himself to them through desire.

*Before all the hours.*

Since the power of the Lord's incarnation and passion incomparably surpasses the merits of all the saints, therefore let the lover of God, before each hour, say this prayer to God the Father with attention of mind, in heart or by mouth.

*Prayer.*

Almighty, everlasting God, who created us from nothing when we did not exist, and mercifully redeemed us when we had perished: I beseech you, by your immutable majesty, receive these praises and prayers which I am now, and at all times, about to say to you, in that union of charity and love in which you sent your only-begotten Son into the womb of the most blessed Virgin Mary, and in which you received him as he suffered and died for us on the cross. Amen.

*Note concerning the aforesaid prayer.*

Do not think this manner of praying frivolous, nor invented by human curiosity; for if you believe that God has been accustomed to teach his friends many things by revelation, you will in no way contradict this most holy form. Rather, read the wondrous works of the Lord which he has shown to those who love him, and you will find what he revealed to Mechthild and others concerning this manner and form of prayer. Know, moreover, that I am thoroughly persuaded that a prayer is most pleasing to almighty God when a devout mind has resolved to offer it to God the Father in union with the Lord's passion. And this offering can be put first, in mind or by mouth, not only in the canonical hours, but also before other prayers, masses, and any good exercises or works. But if through forgetfulness it has not been put first, it can be inserted during prayer, or even added after the completion.

*How a monk should conduct himself in the hours.*

At the beginning of divine praise the devout monk should apply great diligence, lest his mind admit images or thoughts concerning transitory things; for unless vain thoughts are at once repelled in the beginning, they will disturb the attention of the heart throughout the whole office. Therefore the faithful one who prays should gather himself wholly into himself, and, according to the Rule, prefer nothing to the service of God.

During the singing of psalms, let him refer the very subject matter of the psalms to the fitting focus of his intention; let him prudently attend to the meaning of the prayer; let him pronounce the words distinctly and in order; let him direct his mind upward.

After each psalm or lesson, let him examine himself by carefully considering himself, and let him test whether the compunction of devotion still burns in his mind. If he finds that fervor is still present, let him strive to approach it further with holy desires and to summon himself to higher things.
