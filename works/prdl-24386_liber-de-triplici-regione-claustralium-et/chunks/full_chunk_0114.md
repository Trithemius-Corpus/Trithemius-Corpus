And generally, in whatever matter he knows that he has sinned against God, against himself, against his prelate, against his neighbor, or against the rule or the statutes of the fathers, let him strive to bring it back to the examination of this chapter; and concerning secret matters, in tomorrow’s confession; concerning public transgressions and breaches of the Rule, in the next chapter, let him faithfully and from the heart resolve to clear himself by accusing himself. Let him grieve for what he has committed; let him make satisfaction for what is [unclear]; let him beware of what is to be committed.

Then let him consider the grave danger of offending God, whether the sin be venial or mortal. For Johannes Gerson, chancellor of Paris, says in the treatise On the Spiritual Life: Sin, even venial sin, is so much against God that, by his justice, he could damn a man eternally for it. But that he does not impute it to a man unto damnation proceeds not from justice but from his infinite goodness. And the holy bishop Anselm warns us that a man ought not knowingly to commit the least sin against God’s will for the sake of the whole world. For the most blessed pontiff Augustine says: No sin is so venial that it does not become mortal when it is pleasing. You might say: This opinion cannot be true, since if a sin has been venial it can never become mortal; but if it becomes mortal, it was not venial. But listen and understand. I concede that venial sin, considered in itself, cannot become mortal; but one and the same sin, which can stand together with charity, is imputed to another, in the same desert, from contempt and aversion from God. Blessed, therefore, is he who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind shall fall into evil. He who neglects the smallest sins when he can amend them gradually falls into graver ones.

Indeed, how greatly sin displeases almighty God is shown by the vengeance that follows. For so often do we read, in the sacred volumes and in the deeds of time past, of sinners against the Lord being gravely punished, that we may deservedly fear the pit of sin.

How greatly even the briefest thought of a spirit turned away displeases him is declared to us by the fall of the first angel. For as soon as he showed himself disordered against the Lord by growing proud, he was cast into hell.

How greatly idle and useless words are against the Lord, a sufficiently clear story in the Lives of the Holy Fathers warns us, concerning the infernal [unclear] appearing among brothers speaking useless things.

How greatly evil and pernicious words offend almighty God was shown in the case of a five-year-old boy who was blaspheming, whom the devil, snatching him living from his father’s lap, carried off to hell, as is believed.

How greatly deeds and negligences concerning one’s office displease him is shown in the sons of Eli, who neglected and cared too little for the rite of sacrifice.

How greatly even good works done contrary to customary and promised obedience displease God, Saint [Astion?] taught us by his deed: although [unclear], nevertheless [unclear], he did not carry the customary waters without punishment. And we read similar things concerning many others.
