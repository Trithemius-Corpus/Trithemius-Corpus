And first, indeed, with regard to the property of the monastery, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered when they demand that these things be granted to them in improper order, or by any means usurp more than the usage of regular institution permits. For our Rule commands that all necessary things be given to monks; it utterly forbids superfluous things.

Second, those make bad use of the property of the monastery who do not fear to give and receive contrary to the master’s prohibition, or who, in things entrusted to them, faithlessly commit deceit and fraud. For a monk who, at his own pleasure, without reasonable cause, contrary to the Rule, in any way dissipates the property of the monastery without permission, sins against the Rule like an unfaithful proprietor and a falsifier.

Third, monks or abbots show themselves disordered with regard to the property of the monastery when they indulge in unlawful uses and commit theft and sacrilege, since they turn the property of the monastery to illicit uses. For the substance of the monastery, dedicated to God, is commanded to be guarded like the vessels of the holy altar, lest it be dissipated in any way through negligence. But he who forbids the goods of cloistered persons to be spent uselessly would by no means grant that they be disbursed for sin into shameful uses. What account, then, will monks and rectors of the lowest region owe to our lawgiver in the strict examination, who do not fear to dissipate the property of the monastery upon parents, kinsmen, mimes, actors, and shameful men as an occasion and reward of sin?

Fourth, carnal abbots and monks sin gravely with regard to the property of the monastery, who, to the detriment of divine charity and their own salvation, gather these things together too greedily. For, inflamed by the torches of avarice, they think of nothing but profits and money. Violating feast days, they do not observe them. They always desire to increase temporal goods. They do not notice the losses of charity. They grieve more for a dying beast than for a perishing sinner. Indeed, all these disordered cloistered persons are so blind that they consider avarice to be prudence and think riches to be reform. They join field to field; they call gain piety; and where they see temporal goods abounding, there they say that the best discipline is being observed. They refuse to expend even a few things for the common good of the order, because inwardly they have lost the order of true charity. They raise useless, ostentatious, and damnable structures on high. They buy precious vestments and ornaments less necessary for divine services. They clothe sacred things in gold and leave the poor of Christ naked. This avaricious ostentation does not befit monks, since we who are commanded to imitate the holy men of old are ordered to sell these things for the support of the poor. But a covetous monk and an avaricious abbot are as far from the purpose of holy religion as the devil is from heaven.
