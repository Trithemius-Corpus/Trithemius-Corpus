The third thing prescribed as to be observed by a monk at work is that he should speak few words, and edifying ones. For Scripture says: “In much speaking sin will not be lacking.” Let a monk never speak without permission, if he truly desires to be good. But once he has permission to speak, let him speak only a few useful words, knowing that idle and scurrilous words are condemned by the Rule in every place. Let him establish for himself, by circumspection, such a manner of speaking in every place and time that by speaking he offends no one. Let him listen more gladly than he is pleased to speak, and let him not say everything that his mind suggests, if he fears the reproach of fools. For thus Hugh also says to the monk: “Do not pour out everything you know, like a vessel without a lid, which, as soon as it is tilted, pours out whatever it contains and lies open to every dust and uncleanness.” Carefully beware lest you ever seem, directly or indirectly, to seek your own glory, or to reveal to anyone, with a desire for boasting, or otherwise imprudently, whatever praiseworthy thing lies hidden in you. If you are silent about yourself and conceal yourself, you will be more pleasing; if you disclose and commend yourself, you will become contemptible before all and will be laughed at. Beware of idle words, because while we do not take notice of such things, we slip little by little into harmful things, from which arise either disturbances, or looseness of conduct, or grave gnawings of conscience. Be such in your inner way of life that, through love of spiritual meditation, silence always delights you more than talkativeness.

*In His Conduct the Cenobite Is Composed and Mature*

The fourth thing is that a monk, both at work and outside it, in every place, is commanded to compose his conduct and to show himself mature before men in all deeds and words. Let him be clean and humble in heart; let him be kindly and useful in speech; let him be modest and grave in his gait; let him be mature and exemplary in his whole manner of life. Moreover, the whole man is easily brought into conformity with the standard of discipline if the effort to make progress is kept firm in his mind at all times. For indeed, while a monk desires to make progress daily toward better things, he avoids every looseness and disorderly manner of life. For a firm effort to make progress has this as its property: it makes the soul that strives very circumspect in all things. But one who does not have a firm effort to make progress toward the better cannot truly be called composed, although he may seem mature to men. For feigned maturity is quickly dissolved into a fitting looseness of mind where the root of discipline, the fear of God, is not present. Therefore, so that our cenobite may always be composed within and without, let him strive to be truly rooted in the fear of the Lord. For he who fears God neglects nothing, and the love of God is not idle.
