*Consideration XI.*

Since, then, we have begun to speak of the maturity and composure that a monk must preserve before all people, it seems useful to us now to say briefly how he ought to present himself to each. For he is instructed to present himself in one way to his prelate, and is commanded to show himself in another way before his brothers and secular persons. For to his ruler, in the place of Christ, he ought to show reverence; to his fellow brother, charity; to those who wrong him, gentleness; to secular persons, modest piety. Therefore let the monk render to each what is his own, and he will be truly composed before God and men. What he owes to God, or to himself, has been sufficiently said above; now let us pursue what has been set before us.

*Let his conduct be reverential toward the prelate in the place of Christ.*

First, the conduct of a monk ought to be reverential toward the prelate, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “He who hears you hears me; and he who despises you despises me.” For the monk who withholds the obedience and reverence owed to his abbot does injury to almighty God. For, as our most holy father says in the Rule, the abbot is shown to hold the place of Christ in the monastery. Therefore whoever despises the vicar of some king undoubtedly contemns the king himself.

But tell me, good brother, why do you withhold due reverence from your prelate, whom you hear to be the vicar of Christ? Perhaps you think him evil and wholly unworthy of such an office, and therefore suppose that it is permitted you to contemn him? Not so, brother, not so; rather consider whose place it is in which Christ’s vicar presides, and you will know that you must honor the one sending you, not the one sent. If you wish to be Christ’s disciple, consider always for what you came, and you will never deny reverence even to an unworthy prelate.

For what did you come? Surely so that, according to the Gospel and the Rule, you might deny yourself for God’s sake; so that under another’s command you might serve as a soldier for Christ; so that with patience you might await the eternal reward for these things. Therefore, brother, do not, do not lose the crown of eternal happiness prepared for you in heaven because of displeasure at one man, which you ought not to exchange for the whole world. Remember that you have nothing except from God, to whom you owe all that you are and all that you know, so that you may fulfill his will and live according to his ordinance. But since you are not confident that you can understand the will of God, you ought humbly to obey your ruler, as his vicar to whom you have subjected yourself; nor ought you to examine either his life or his command, since it is permitted [unclear] to judge the shepherd. Indeed, in your profession you swore that you ought not to carry out your own will, but that you wished to obey the abbot in all things according to the Rule.
