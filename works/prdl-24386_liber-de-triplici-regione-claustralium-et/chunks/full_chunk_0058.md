And note that the Passion of the Lord, with respect to its procedure, was unjust; with respect to its manner, shameful; with respect to its pain, bitter; and with respect to its effect, fruitful; and therefore it is always to be held in memory.

First, with respect to its procedure, it was unjust, because, born of a virgin, he neither contracted sin nor committed it, nor was deceit found in his mouth. For death is inflicted on us for sin; but he could not justly die who was always righteous and innocent, without any sin. Finally, he was killed contrary to human law, if sentence of death is passed upon a man neither confessed nor convicted of a crime. The Savior was accused before Pilate on three charges: that, calling himself a king, he forbade tribute to Caesar; third, that he made himself the Son of God. The Gospel speaks of the first two, and the accusation is shown to be false. For concerning the first, the contrary was plainly established, since Christ said that his kingdom was not earthly. The falsity of the second likewise did not escape Pilate, who knew that Jesus had commanded that the things which were Caesar's should be given to Caesar. When questioned by the governor about the third, he was silent, because Pilate was unworthy to hear of Jesus' divine generation or even his human birth. His Passion also took place contrary to custom, because it received no delay of time. He is not put in prison according to custom; nothing is deliberated in his case; but, once seized, he is immediately dragged to the judge to be subjected to death; scourged, spat upon, and afflicted with many wrongs, blows, and mockeries, he is nailed to the cross. Third, it was contrary to the law of nature, and procured from obstinate hatred of heart. For nature forbids raging against the dead, and shudders at tearing with new wounds a body that has been killed. For our Redeemer, dead on the cross, is pierced with a lance, and, contrary to the law of nature, the deceased is wounded by the impious.

Second, the Passion of Christ, with respect to its manner, is said to have been shameful for the six causes that follow. First, because of the paltry price, since blood of incomparable value is sold by a disciple for thirty pieces of silver, for which no one would deign to sell his little ass. Second, because of the shameful office, for, full of wounds and half-dead, he is compelled to carry his own cross, which is found to have been done to no robber. Third, because of the indignity of the place, since he is crucified outside the city in the place of Calvary, where robbers and wicked murderers are punished for their deserts. Fourth, because of the shameful punishment, since he is hung naked on the cross, which penalty is owed only to robbers and wicked men. Indeed, as Scripture says, the Redeemer of the world was condemned to a most shameful death. Fifth, because of the shameful mockery, since he is hung naked on the cross before the whole multitude, and is blasphemed by all as if foolish and senseless. Of this the psalmist says: All day long my shame is before me. Sixth, because of the shameful company, for, counted with the wicked, he is hung in the midst of robbers. What are you doing now, O Christian soul? Behold, your Savior is hung in the middle between robbers, and you do not fear to sit in the first place in the council?

Third, the most holy Passion of Christ was most bitter with respect to its pain, for seven reasons. First, because of the delicate liveliness of his flesh, which in natural complexion was the best, and therefore was more grievously afflicted in the suffering of pain. For delicate flesh hurts more than flesh coarse by natural condition. Therefore, since the flesh of Christ was subtle and delicate, it emitted a bloody sweat in the garden. Second, because of the quality of those killing him, for Christ was not killed by foreigners, but by his own proper kinsmen and neighbors, just as he had earlier complained through the prophet: My people, what have I done to you? For one suffers more tolerably from foreigners and enemies than from one's own, for whom at least friends, from nearness of blood, have compassion. Third, because of the bitterness of the Passion, since he is afflicted more in those places in which pain is felt more because of the nerves. For, raised up on the cross, he is fastened through hands and feet; thirsting, the Lord of all is given gall and hyssop to drink. For the psalmist says of him: They dug through my hands and my feet; they numbered all my bones. Who could conceive in his mind the pain of your most holy heart, O good Jesus, when the very hard nails began so cruelly to pierce your hands and feet? Would that my soul may always show itself grateful to you; would that the recollection of your most holy Passion may not depart from my memory. Easily shall I be able to obtain from you the effect of my prayers, if it happens that I delight without interruption in the exercise of your Passion.
