The confidence of the righteous, arising from the testimony of a good conscience, is the best hope, and is continually subject to God in humble service. But the impious and sinners, disturbed by their own actions, raise themselves up against God; they do not trust in the testimony of conscience, but rather presume vainly.

*There follow the seven roots of divine fear, which are laid as foundations in the first degree of humility.*

Since this second region chiefly instructs the cenobite, in order, through the degrees of humility proceeding from the fear of God, it is necessary for him to recognize clearly the roots of that fear, if he wishes by ascending to arrive at the love of God. Therefore, so that we may be able to construct this spiritual house to perfection, we must place those roots in order in the foundation. We have not set them out in the first degree of humility for this reason: lest we confuse the order of the things to be said by excessive length. There are, then, seven roots of the fear of God, by which the cenobite is led to compunction and, advancing in true humility, is more strongly rooted.

The first root of the fear and love of God is intent meditation on the incomprehensible divine majesty: God is comprehended the less, the more curiously he is investigated by man. For the prophet says: Truly you are a hidden God. This meditation begets wonder at divine perfection; wonder causes reverence; from reverence arises fear of majesty; and from fear worship is produced. Meditation upon the supreme goodness is divided, according to our capacity, into seven considerations, which are not unsuitably adapted to the seven days of the week, or even to the seven canonical hours. And although that highest and most perfect goodness is abundantly sufficient for us as material for meditation, yet because it belongs to few to be able to ascend to invisible things without visible things, we wish through known things to raise the minds of the weak to things unknown. For the holy apostle says: The invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are perceived, being understood through the things that have been made; his everlasting power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.

First, therefore, the wondrous power of God's might is worthy of praise in the principal work of creation, since he was able to bring forth all things from nothing, and by his will alone created things so great, so solid, so varied, and noble. From this consideration wonder at divine might arises, through which a person is summoned to the fear of God.
