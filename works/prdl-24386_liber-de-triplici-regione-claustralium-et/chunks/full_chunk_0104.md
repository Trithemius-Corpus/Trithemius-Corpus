For every Mass, of whatever kind it may be, which is said in the holy Church of God, is at all times referred to the merit of the Lord’s Passion.

*On the Introit of the Mass.*

We do not intend to expound the office of the Mass point by point, since each one of us can find many expositions by others; but we desire to give occasion for meditation to devout brothers, whom we know to be inflamed with inward desires. Therefore you can, in meditation, refer the first part of the Introit and the verse to the incarnation of the Lord, and refer the repetition with the Gloria to his nativity. Nevertheless, you must carefully consider what the Introit contains according to the literal sense, so that you may be able to find a spiritual understanding suited to you. For nothing is done in vain in the divine office; rather, all things are filled with mysteries and sacred actions.

*On Kyrie eleison.*

Kyrie eleison is Greek, and is composed of a noun and a verb; among them these are two words. For the first is kyrios, which means “lord”; kyrie is said in the vocative. The second is from eleeo, which means “I have mercy.” Eleeson, “have mercy,” is expressed in the imperative. Hence kyrie eleison is formed, that is, “Lord, have mercy,” by elision for kyrie eleison, since a vowel before a vowel can be elided for a better sound.

Thus Kyrie eleison is said three times to the Father, that he who out of exceeding charity wished his Son to be incarnate of the Virgin and to die for us may also be willing, by his mercy, to help us in our necessities.

To the Son likewise we say Christe eleison three times, in which we ask him himself to help us.

We also utter Kyrie eleison three times to the Holy Spirit, in which we humbly invoke him himself, through his ineffable goodness, to come to our aid.

*On Gloria in excelsis.*

Then Gloria in excelsis Deo is said in the singular number, in which we confess the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit to be one sole true God in the Trinity of persons and unity of substance. Consequently, during that angelic hymn the monk ought to be very devout and, so to speak, singly attentive to each word, in which the gift of peace is promised to men of good will, and God is humbly adored by us in various utterances.

*At the Collects.*

When the collects or prayers are uttered by the priest to God, the monk standing on the steps should think of nothing else, but, listening to the words of the priest, should present his own desire to God in concord with them.

*At the Epistle.*

When the Epistle is read, you must attend carefully to everything, and give thanks to God with the greatest fervor of spirit, because, leaving aside the Jewish people, he mercifully willed to call you and your forebears from Gentile error to his faith.

*At the Gradual.*

During the Gradual, Alleluia, Sequence, or Tract, think how long a desire the holy fathers had, who before Christ’s incarnation longed for his coming. Afterwards consider with how many labors and injuries your Savior was wearied for you in this world for thirty-three years, he who voluntarily gave himself to death for your salvation. Then remember the long and dangerous exile of your soul in the flesh, so that through this meditation on present misery you may be able the more to inflame your affection toward divine love.

*During the Gospel.*

During the holy Gospel, with your mind raised to the Lord, give thanks exultantly, because the merciful Father willed you to be born of Christian parents, and because he made you a participant in his holy and most sweet teaching.

*During the Creed.*

When the symbol of the Catholic faith is sung, consider all the words and mysteries with humble fervor of spirit. Hasten at once to recollect yourself wholly within yourself, so that you may be able to give thanks for having been called to the faith without any merit of yours, and to raise yourself up to him through spiritual love. Think, if you can, or rather as much as you can, how great, exalted, and immense is the Son of God, who willed to be incarnate for you; how hard and bitter were the things he endured for you.

*At the Offertory.*

During the Offertory, apply your mind with the host to be consecrated in offering; weigh what is done there mystically. For the host is round and white. In its whiteness is set forth the purity, innocence, and suffering humanity of our Savior, to be offered to God the Father on our behalf against all our impurities; in its roundness his immeasurable divinity, set before us as a reward, is offered. Therefore say to the Lord:
