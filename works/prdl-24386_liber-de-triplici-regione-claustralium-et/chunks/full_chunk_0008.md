Others, compelled by necessity, come unwillingly; certain ones, scattered by outward thoughts, are present at divine services in body, but outside they wander in mind through various workshops. Others do not fear to seek false occasions by which they may, as if licitly, be able to absent themselves from choir. Now they feign a severe headache; now they mendaciously allege this, now that; and while they deceitfully withdraw the service owed to almighty God, they incur the reproach of eternal malediction owed to the negligent.

Fourth, there are many who are disordered toward God by the working of habit: although they may at times be free from the malediction of the negligent, they nevertheless never approach the fruit of inward devotion. For they come to the service of God from habit alone, nor are they moved to any good work by devotion. Others, placed under grave necessity, obey the commands of superiors quite unwillingly, and fear temporal punishments more than the judgment of God. These bear the cross of the Lord with Simon under compulsion; like the heifer of Ephraim led to the threshing-floor, they apply themselves to good works by habit alone. But those who come to the service of God with an intention that is not due do a good thing not well. They also perform the deed of a just man without the reward of a just man, who in good works do not also take on the good will of a just man.

Fifth, the citizens of the lowest region are disordered toward God through the vice of ingratitude, which is the greatest crime of a disordered will. And indeed, one who is found ungrateful to the Lord for benefits received is often exposed, by his own demerit, to be scourged by various and most grievous temptations. And therefore he deserves to lose the benefit that he has received, because he neglects to show himself grateful to so great a benefactor. And what greater ingratitude can there be than to live irreligiously in religion, to break a vow, and shamefully to violate a resolution solemnly undertaken? For a monk who entangles himself in carnal vices, who neglects to live according to the rule that he has professed, voluntarily incurs the greatest vice of ingratitude.

*In what manner the monks of this lowest region are disordered toward their prelate. Article II.*

Toward their prelate, carnal monks are disordered in many ways: they tear him apart with continual detractions; with a contumacious heart they disparage the pastoral office; they refuse to show their pastor the affection of filial love according to the rule. For, blinded by various pleasures of the flesh, the monks of this lowest region disdain to consider whose place the abbot holds in the monastery; and the one whom they should humbly venerate as Christ’s vicar, they think, as being like themselves by reason of infirmity, worthy of no honor. Beneath the snare of this disordered way of life, the ancient enemy deceives carnal monks, whom he leads in various ways into displeasure with their pastor.

For the greatest power of raging against the monastic flock is granted to the devil when the one who presides is hated by his subjects. For just as a sick man who hates the physician and does not allow him to come near cannot be healed, so a monk who, because of aversion, does not wish to disclose his temptations to his pastor cannot be saved. For how will a sick sheep be able to escape the teeth of the ravening wolf, if it cannot endure the presence of the shepherd? First, therefore, disordered monks disparage the life of their pastor, and contumaciously accuse both his deeds and his words, because, when they see their ruler proceeding according to the traditions of the holy fathers, they fear being corrected in their own depravity. For among reprobate minds the life of the good is always drawn into suspicion; envy is always tormented by the virtues of others; and the monk who voluntarily subjects himself to vices and sins always hates a pastor of purified conduct.

Secondly, the citizens of this shadowy region are disordered toward their prelate when his office is disparaged, though they do not fail to know that he was ordained by God to preside in the monastery. They murmur against him on slight occasion; his commands are not observed; and those who long ago had subjected themselves, for the love of God, with the arms of holy obedience, now, after obedience has been despised, in the manner of Sarabaites usurp for themselves the pernicious rule of their own wills. They desire to have an abbot suited to their own depraved morals. They reject one who is holy and useful. If they find a lax and sluggish pastor, they intoxicate him by flattery with many poisons; they call him the best and most pious father, and subject themselves to him not humbly but gladly, because from him they fear no correction of their depravity.
