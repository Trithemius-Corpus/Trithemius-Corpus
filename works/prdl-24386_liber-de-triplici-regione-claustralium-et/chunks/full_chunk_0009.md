Thirdly, carnal monks show themselves disorderly toward their pastors, when they withdraw themselves from them in the affection of filial love and scorn to obey according to the Rule. Yet since a monk is bound to love his abbot with sincere affection as the vicar of Christ, he will not be able to hate him without a most grievous crime. For indeed, he who does not love his pastor for the love of Christ is not a true lover of God. For how would Christ, who wishes even enemies to be loved, permit his own vicars to be hated? Therefore look to him who wished the abbot to be set over you in his place, and henceforth there will be no cause of displeasure against him.

Fourthly, the disorderly citizens of the shady valley detract from the reverence due to their pastor, when they not only scorn to pay him the honor owed by the Rule, but, what is worse, do not fear even to inflict insult upon him. For the Rule states that the abbot bears the place of Christ in the monastery, to whom all monks ought humbly to obey as to Christ himself, since the obedience that is paid to superiors is shown to Christ. But perverse cloisterers, always ungrateful for the presidency of prelates, do not fear stubbornly to oppose the divine ordinance; they scorn to obey the commands of the holy Rule; and they dishonor their abbot, to whom they ought to have shown every reverence, tearing him with hatred and detractions. Whatever he has done, whatever he has said, displeases them as though badly done, since they detract at all times from his conduct and, despising his office, these diseased sheep seek nothing more than the confusion of their pastor. Some of them, judging their abbot in secret, despise him; others contradict him to his face. And while they neglect to show reverence to their pastor, they confound every observance of discipline. Pride is always familiar to these men, and the foulness of the flesh, because those who voluntarily withdraw themselves from the affection of their pastor fall into abominable temptations and the worst crimes. For whoever despises Christ’s vicar in the monastery hands over to the devil the power of raging against him, and confounds the union of the mystical body.

Fifthly, carnal and disorderly monks detract from the authority of their pastor, when they think that nothing is permitted to him in the monastery unless they themselves have consented to it. With uplifted neck they despise the virtue of humility; they spurn regular subjection; they scorn to yield to their betters. They wish to thrust themselves into divine affairs and causes, and to usurp for themselves the primacy of counsel, so that the abbot dares to do or arrange nothing unless the business first proceeds with their consent. But what does the blessed lawgiver say in the Rule? “The abbot,” he says, “hearing the counsels of the brothers, should consider the matter within himself, and do what he judges more useful.” And again: “Let the brothers give counsel with all humble subjection, so that they do not presume stubbornly to defend what has seemed good to them; rather, let it depend on the abbot’s judgment, so that all may obey what he has judged to be more salutary.” And again, concerning the appointment of a prior, he says: “We judge it expedient, for the preservation of charity and peace, that the ordering of his monastery depend on the abbot’s judgment.” This ordering certainly concerns the appointment and removal of all officials of the monastery; it requires not the consent of the brothers, but the counsel of those who fear God. Finally, the Rule commands all things to be done according to the will of the abbot, and that absolutely nothing be presumed which has not been enjoined by him. Carnal monks, rash usurpers of their own will, try in various ways to weaken this authority of his and to disturb the order and charity of the whole monastery, when they think that the rector should do nothing except what they themselves think right.
