The first degree of obedience is to obey willingly: that is, to have no will of your own at all, but to believe that the good pleasure of your shepherd is wholly your own. The second is to obey simply, so that you do neither more nor otherwise than has been commanded you, nor, by doing less, omit anything. The third is to obey cheerfully, so that you show serenity in your face and sweetness in your voice. The fourth is to obey swiftly, so that the hand is not slower in the work than the command is in the voice. The fifth is to obey manfully, lest one withdraw his strength from the command and pretend that he is weak and unequal to the precepts. The sixth is to obey humbly: not proudly, not haughtily, not contentiously; for unless obedience is humble, it will bring back not reward but punishment. The seventh is to obey perseveringly, because obedience that is broken off before completion is not true obedience; and he who perseveres to the end [unclear].

But the fruit of obedience is everlasting and is never bounded by any [unclear] end. To the same degree that we obey our superiors, Christ, almighty God, obeys our prayers. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, about persistence in prayer, for Christ does not hear in prayer the man who despises Christ's vicar in the monastery. Humility raises prayer up; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, the discoverer of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, shuts the gates of hell, makes one a fellow-dweller with the angels, and has the food of all the saints. But the monk who casts off subjection to obedience, to [death?]

*The fourth [degree] of humility*, our father Benedict says in the Rule, is this: if, under obedience, amid hard and contrary things, or even amid any injuries inflicted, the monk embraces patience with silent conscience. This degree too takes its beginning from the third, since no one is truly to be called obedient if he has been found impatient. For, as that divine Chrysostom has it, he cannot be acceptable to the king who has been subject to the enemy; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been enslaved to passionate and impatient flesh. For no one will be able to overcome a savage enemy unless he has learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, because no virtue makes lawful progress if patience abandons it. For, [as blessed Gregory attests?], it is of greater merit to receive adversities patiently for God's sake than to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: He who considers that tribulation has come upon him for his sins calls forth God's mercy upon himself for doing good more fully. Therefore let the cenobite learn to set the virtue of patience against all things that befall him, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. For he conquers who suffers; he who murmurs is overcome.
