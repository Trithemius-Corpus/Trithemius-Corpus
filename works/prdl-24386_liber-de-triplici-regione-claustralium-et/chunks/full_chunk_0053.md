The second degree is a will strong in uprightness: so that it may both flee bravely, against the assault of the flesh, the evil that it hates, and may attain, by doing it even with toil, the good that it loves.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in all riches. For indeed the will does not remain strong if it has not been devout in God’s commandments.

The fourth degree is a full will, inasmuch as it in no way divides its powers, but continually extends its whole self, through the denial of all things, toward its God. For this degree three things are necessary, so that good will may be made full in us. The first is complete denial of one’s own self, because the will is proved still to be weak if it is not mortified through every kind of self-denial. The second thing required for full good will is called perfect resignation; if anyone desires to grasp it, let him fervently ascend the third degree of humility. The third member necessary for the fourth degree of good will is called the total extension of the will into God; through this the soul, admitted into divine love and sweetly burning, is in a certain way transformed into the same, and is frequently admitted into the third region.

Finally, according to Bernard’s judgment, that transformation into divine love is love, affection, adherence, union, charity, or fruition; no one can reach it unless he has learned to mortify all his affections in the fervor of the spirit. For the spirit of a man who has his heart raised upward into God is called perfect will, and is united with God by a most sweet bond. In these degrees of good will, therefore, the faithful cenobite ought without interruption to direct his effort upward, and always to examine with prudent care how much he is advancing or falling back. For Saint Bernard says: Just as he who remains in a vicious will is contemptible, so he who does not strive to advance in the others is blameworthy. For where the effort to advance is laid aside, the danger of falling back is incurred.

*Article VII.*

Although a man does not know whether he is worthy of love or [hatred?], nevertheless each person can, by diligently examining his conscience, recognize what he ought to think about the condition of his state. The testimony of his own conscience reveals what sort each person is inwardly, hidden within himself.
