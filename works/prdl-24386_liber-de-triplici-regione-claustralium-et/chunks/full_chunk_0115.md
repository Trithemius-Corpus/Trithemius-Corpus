When, however, our monk, after having gone through his recollections, finds himself burdened by the aforesaid or by any other negligences or sins, however grave, let him take up the contrition possible to him for the past, a purpose of amendment and of firmly guarding against future things, and penitence for what has been committed. Let him therefore take the counsel of the above-mentioned chancellor, and, being compunct, say to the Lord:

*Truth I.*

Lord my God, I confess to your most holy goodness that in this way or that I have sinned against your will; for this I grieve vehemently, and because I have offended you, who are wholly good, I humbly do penance.

*Truth II.*

O Lord my God, who know all things, you know that, by your bounty, I have a good purpose, and I intend, with your help, to amend myself in the future according to my strength. Therefore sin is guarded against by prayers, by invoking God for help; by punishments, by inflicting penitence on the body; by flight, by avoiding the occasion of sin. Therefore, for the third truth, let him say:

*Truth III.*

Lord my God, I have a good will to make confession of my sins, according to your commandment and that of holy mother Church, according to place and time, from the heart, fully, without deceit.

Whoever has uttered these three truths to the Lord from sincerity of heart, without falsehood in mouth or mind, however much he may previously have sinned, is now secure, if in such a state, even without confession in the absence of a priest, he should die; yet in such a way that he must make satisfaction in purgatory for what, by doing penance, he did not complete. But whoever, because of an obstructing will to sin, in which he is actually taking delight, or because of a wicked purpose by which he scorns to avoid the occasions of sins, cannot truly say these same truths from a pure mind, is in a state of damnation, and not even the pope can absolve him.

*Indication of Future Precaution.*

The second thing prescribed to be observed by a monk in his recollection is called the indication of the future. Let him consider what were the occasions for him of past transgression, and he will more easily guard himself from the danger of future transgression. For the pit of sin is never better blocked up than if the occasion of sin is avoided. And Saint Bernard says: Indeed, an indication must be made for the night that is coming on, by distributing one’s exercises according to the rule of the common institution. In these every debt is duly paid if the spirit is subjected to God and the flesh to the spirit; and if anything has been omitted through neglect or left imperfect, let it not remain uncompensated in manner, time, and place.
