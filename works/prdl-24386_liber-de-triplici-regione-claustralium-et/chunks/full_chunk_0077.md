You withdraw from the one because you are multiplied in number. Return to the one if you desire to have a principle. For the one is the principle of all things; if you add anything to it in your mind, at once you withdraw from it. When you say “one,” you say “principle.” If you say “two,” you have already abandoned the principle. Therefore, so that you may always be able to contemplate your principle, take care at all times, having put off multitude, to make yourself one. For since all good things are from the first good, and every love is borne toward whatever is good, and is naturally kindled by the same, our love cannot rest in any goods as in its ultimate end unless, naked and pure, it strives finally to return to its own principle. For union comes about only by the power of the one itself, because for no other reason do we say that things more alike are more easily united to one another than because we see them to be one in quality. The mind, therefore, is believed to be able to be united with the one highest principle of things only when, having put off all multitude and been united to its own self, it is lifted up to God by most fervent love. Just as there is no union between things different in quality, so between God and a soul divided in itself there can be no adherence. For the union of one with one comes about through likeness of quality, and there will be adherence with God for the mind made simple and stripped of all things through loving affection. Thus the highest region of the spirit is founded in simplicity; in it the mind of the purified man is lifted up to God by the most fervent desire of love and is united by grace with its own immutable principle. Intellect and affection are the two eyes of the soul; the first of these is raised up to know God, but is not sufficient to come to the mountain of divine darkness, because it goes burdened with phantasms of sensible things. Affection, therefore, the simpler eye of the soul, enters the dark mountain without any images whatsoever; it contemplates God through love, separates itself from all creatures, thinks of nothing sensible, rests in mystical silence, naked and unoccupied. This is the single eye by which it makes the whole body full of light; this is the eye concerning which the bridegroom exults in the Canticles: “You have wounded my heart, my sister, bride; you have wounded my heart with one of your eyes.” For affection alone, through the fervor of love, enters into the secret place of divine contemplation without the guidance of the intellect, and therefore the bridegroom is wounded in one eye and not in both.

But whoever, through the exercise of devotion and purity, has deserved, by Christ’s granting, to come to this region of union and simplification, let him learn at all times to stand prudently in profound humility, lest through pride he lose the gift which he rejoices to have obtained. For Bernard, that profound and most cautious contemplator of divine things, says: It is necessary to deepen humility and to think humbly of oneself when striving toward higher things.
