Of these, many citizens of the lowest region, when they have come to the order, serve not God but the belly: they desire that more food, drink, and clothing be given them than necessity requires, and those who in the world could scarcely have bread and vegetables for food, now, having become monks, refuse eggs and little fish.

They importunately demand that large, varied, diverse, and delicate fish be given them; murmuring because of desire for flesh with the children of Israel in the desert, they cry out to their leader; they assert that they cannot eat eggs and peas. They always desire more delicate things.

*How the citizens of the lowest region are ill ordered toward contempt of the world.*

*Article VII.*

The kingdom of God suffers violence, and the violent seize it. Therefore whoever desires to enter the heavenly kingdom by the weapons of holy obedience, whoever wishes to live to God under regular conduct, must do violence to the pleasures of the flesh and strongly despise the world with all its vanities. But carnal monks, citizens of the lowest region, who are ill ordered both toward God and toward the goads, are also ill ordered toward contempt of the world.

And first, indeed, they are infected by the concupiscence of the flesh, because they are always burning with the fire of pleasure. In food and drink they are found excessive; in little beds and baths, delicate contrary to the rule. They prefer the desires of the flesh to the salvation of the soul; they do not seek the sweetness of inward conversion. They nourish their flesh too delicately, through which they continually blaze up in various desires. For no one can truly despise the world who neglects to restrain the appetite of the flesh even in the smallest things. If you eat to satiety, you do not truly despise the world. For the first commandment that is imposed on those who despise the world is established in abstinence. But if you fill your belly with food all the way to satiety, at once you fall either into lust or into heavy sloth. Therefore the concupiscence of the flesh draws back the mind of the disordered cenobite, so that he cannot despise the world.

Second, they are ill ordered toward despising the age because of the vain concupiscence of the eyes. For the monk who forgets the true honor of faithful souls takes delight in worldly pomp and altogether curious ornaments. Indeed, each person’s outward apparel reveals what sort of thing lies hidden within him before himself. To carry rings about on the fingers, corals on scarves, little scent-bottles in the hands, and pleats on cowls does not show a monk, but a soldier; it presents not a humble cenobite but a proud secular man.
