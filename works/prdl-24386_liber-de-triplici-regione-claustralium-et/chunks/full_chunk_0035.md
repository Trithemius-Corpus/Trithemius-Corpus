And just as the eagle is said to look upon the sun with undazzled eyes, so the monk, the servant of God, contemplates without interruption the end of the monastic institution. Just as a prudent traveler, as he proceeds pleasantly, keeps in view the lodging toward which he is making his way, so the good cenobite, in everything that he does, always with fervent desire chiefly longs for his end, which is God. But foolish is the traveler who, set upon the road, forgets the lodging toward which he is tending, and, wandering off elsewhere, knowingly entangles himself in various hindrances. And the monk who does not earnestly keep in view the end of his destination is indeed wandering blind upon the road.

Therefore the good monk, and lover of his own salvation, does not neglect his good striving in spiritual exercises, but always lifts up his affection toward higher things by exercising himself in holy works. Good striving, moreover, is to omit nothing of the things that God has commanded; for love of God to obey the pastor humbly in all things; always to lift up one's affection, withdrawn from earthly things, to the memory of heavenly things; and through the continual exercise of spiritual conduct to renew the holy purpose every day. Whatever good the devout cenobite does, let him always offer it to almighty God with faithful attention; let him despise all earthly things with a strong spirit; let him desire to please God alone.

Let the intention of the faithful cenobite always be that he wish to desire nothing other than the praise and honor of God; let his attention be diligent, so that he knows how to summon all the senses of the body and the affections of the soul to the service of God, in order that he may serve his Lord faithfully and seek his glory, not his own.

*On the Due Restraint of the Sensual Powers. Article II.*

How useful and also how necessary it is for us to restrain [them], especially among monks, I think no one is ignorant. Truly, whoever desires to reach, in this region, the proper end of regular conduct that we have described must tame, with prudent constancy, both his disordered natural habits and impulses, because no one can truly suffice to resign himself to another's authority who does not strongly restrain the powers of the sensual passions. For it is necessary for the one who desires to make progress to leave behind both the impulses and the prudence of the flesh with steadfast spirit, and to show himself a son of divine grace, not of flesh and blood. For Scripture says: “If you live according to the flesh, you will die.” Truly, those who sow in the flesh will also reap corruption from the flesh.
