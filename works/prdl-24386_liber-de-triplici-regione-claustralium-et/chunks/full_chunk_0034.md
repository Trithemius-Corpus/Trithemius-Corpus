And first indeed, the mortification of one’s own will requires in a monk the eradication of his own judgment; for whoever relies on his own judgment or counsel disdains humbly to be subject to another. For the root from which the defense of one’s own will arises is called attachment to one’s own counsel or judgment. But whoever, placed in a monastery, desires to cling to his own judgment, sins gravely against the Rule as a usurper of his own will. For it befits a cenobite rather to live under another’s command, if he desires to arrive at eternal life through the merit of obedience. Indeed, to love one’s own judgment is the greatest leprosy of the soul, which, according to Bernard, is the more pernicious the more hidden it is; because the more someone abounds in it, the healthier he seems to himself to be. Secondly, the mortification of the will requires the emptying out of one’s own affections, since no one is able to stand ordered among adverse and favorable things and truly subject to God who does not firmly restrain his carnal affections. Thirdly, for the mortification of one’s own will that poverty of spirit is required, lest in usable things, food, clothing, and the rest, the monk’s affection be held captive; for whoever loves these things too much shows himself, contrary to the Rule, to be a lover of his own will. For it is fitting that the true cenobite, inhabitant of this middle region, earnestly devote himself to the teaching of holy humility, willingly despise all worldly things for love of God, and love absolutely nothing except God. If he has anything for his use with the Rule permitting it, let him beware of attaching the affection of his heart to it; and let him be ready, at the pastor’s decision and for the usefulness of the brethren, to resign all things with a peaceful heart. Let him always choose the simpler and meaner things; let him give thanks to God in all things, placing his hope and his sufficiency in him alone, and let him say cheerfully: In you, Lord Jesus, possessing all things, for your love I willingly despise all things; concerning transitory things, nothing at all

Therefore let our devout cenobite always direct the eye of his intention, with faithful consideration, to this end of the institution of the monastic [unclear]. Let him never depart from its [unclear]. For whoever in action forgets the ultimate end wanders like an unskilled and blind sailor amid the storms of the sea, soon to perish. Therefore let the faithful monk imitate a sailor skilled through devotion, so that, just as he, always having the harbor before his eyes, strives with intent solicitude to steer the ship thither, so the mind of a good man may always be directed to God as to the shore and the ultimate end. Archers set up for themselves some fixed mark, and, lest their sight be scattered from the appointed limit, they close one eye. And just as the arrow of the shooter is directed to the mark, so the intention of the good monk is always ordered toward God; in it also the eye of lower action is closed, lest the sharpness of higher contemplation be scattered through vain things.
