*On the devout fitting of the powers for entry into the third region. Article six.*

When, by the degrees of humility, the powers of the soul, especially of the interior man, have been fundamentally purified and reformed, it is now said to be very necessary in monks that they be adapted more devoutly for progress into the third region. But this diligence in striving toward higher things is necessary for the cenobite, as we have said, because where the effort to make progress is laid aside, the virtue already acquired is wholly dissipated. No one is permitted to stand still in this second region, but all are commanded to ascend to the third through continual effort. Indeed, this continual motion casts down into the first region of the flesh all the slothful and lukewarm, but sweetly, without violence, impels the humble and fervent in desire always toward the third.

Whoever, therefore, has merited from the Lord through humility to obtain the reformation of his powers is now very near to the third region of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it powerfully, it is necessary that, by most diligently fitting the powers of his soul, he compose them for their several operations. The intellect, however, is then said to be rightly fitted for entry into the third region when it is illumined to know the highest truth according to its capacity. The memory is composed for the same when it is fully absorbed in holding and cherishing the highest truth. But in order that the cenobite may be able to attain the purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, dying to the world, with the mind pacified from every interior and exterior tumult, he rest, so that he may remain buried within the bosom of divine love and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union. The mind comes to this rest by four degrees, which are rooted in a good will in four ways.

The first degree of good will lays the beginnings of the virtues; it is called an upright will. For the soul which, consenting to the law of God, subjects itself to God through an upright will, even if it still seems weak, is ordered to the path of virtue. For virtue is perfected when the flesh resists, and that the will is upright is proved by the violence done to the spirit. Yet often a will is called upright and good even when it is found to be less strong. The good that it loves it cannot always accomplish; the evil that it hates, it grieves that through weakness it has committed. Through the weakness of the flesh it is very close to the lowest region, but from the uprightness of its intention and its effort to make progress it is continually kindled toward the freedom of the spirit. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks on many things.
