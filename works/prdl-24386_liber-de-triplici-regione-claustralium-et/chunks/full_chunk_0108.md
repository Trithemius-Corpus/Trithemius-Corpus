*The exercise of the monk after dinner until vespers. Consideration VI.*

After the reading, when you have returned to your cell, you ought always to put some prayer before your works, and in all the things that you do to ask for God's help. And when you are in your cell, do not be idle, but always do some good thing, especially what the obedience of the abbot has enjoined. Now pray, now write, read something good or meditate; for the cell makes an idle monk either a beast or a demon. But of these matters we shall speak more fully below in their proper place. Take care to spend your time usefully, since very soon you are going to render an account of all things to God.

*On dwelling in the cell and on the works to be done in it.*

There are three places in one of which the devout monk who loves God ought more frequently to be found: namely, in the church, in the cell, and, at its proper time, in the refectory. But wandering through the monastery is not permitted, because it is written of the evil, the reprobate, and the gyrovagues: “The wicked walk in a circuit.” But of the just man Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he shall sit alone and keep silence, because he has lifted himself above himself.” Now there are five principal works of the cell: the first is to attend to commands; the second is to read usefully; the third is to persist in suitable meditation; the fourth is to pray frequently; the fifth is to rest fittingly. Let us speak of these briefly in order.

*In the cell you must first attend to commands.*

First, then, when you are in the dwelling of the cell, learn to attend to commands, and not to prefer your idle devotions to obedience. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than the sacrifices of fools, and to listen is better than to offer fat.” Therefore, if something has been enjoined on you to do in the cell, do not transgress the paths of obedience on any pretext whatever, but strive diligently to carry out the work imposed on you for love of our Lord Jesus Christ and for the merit of holy obedience. Hence Hugh, the lover of quiet, says: “Be gladly in the cell, and always do something in it that builds you up and is enjoined by a superior, reckoning that it is not within your right to leave it without permission, except for the necessity of nature.” You also have on this matter the prohibition of our most holy father Benedict in the Rule, who forbids you to go out and run about without permission.
