But you, brother, when you are called to labor, take care to submit your intention to the regular institution, so that you love nothing other than the honor of God, the merit of obedience, and the usefulness of the monastery; intend nothing except the progress of the soul. And when you are engaged in labor, do not imprudently extinguish the fervor of the spirit; do not omit the exercise of spiritual conduct, but take care diligently to observe everywhere the four following things. In outward conduct be faithful, serviceable, and benevolent; inwardly, humble and devout; in speech, sparing and edifying; in manners, composed and mature. If you observe these four things in labor, you will always return to your cell a better man.

*Outwardly let the monk be faithful, benevolent, and serviceable.*

The first thing that must be diligently observed in the labor of a cenobite is that in laboring he should always show himself faithful to his monastery; that he should forestall the brothers, out of charity, in serving and ministering, or even in carrying heavier burdens; and that in all words and deeds he should show himself benevolent and tractable. And Saint Bernard, lover of God, says: A serious and prudent soul applies itself to labor, yet is not dissipated in it, but through it rather gathers itself into itself, since it always has before its eyes not so much what it does as what it intends in doing. For in every completion it intends the end; and the more truly it strives for it, the more inwardly, fervently, and faithfully it works with its hands, subjecting to itself the service of its whole body, in which the will creates use, and use, by exercise, supplies strength. The devout and faithful cenobite, who loves God in truth, does not spare himself in labor, but is accustomed to apply himself to the manual tasks imposed on him with such fervor, cheerfulness, and fidelity, as if he alone were compelled by his labor to feed all those established in the monastery. For the most prudent soul knows that it is spending itself wholly for God whenever it happens to labor for the bodily need of his servants. For just as spiritual things cannot long subsist in religious life without the cooperation of temporal things, so I do not doubt that one who ministers the bodily necessities to those serving God on account of God is to be called the servant of God. Therefore whatever a good cenobite can acquire for his monastery by his labors with right intention, let him know that he is presenting all that to the Lord. Yet let a monk always understand that he must labor in such a way that he by no means omits the monastic prayers, masses, and the other services of holy mother Church.

But manual work undertaken out of curiosity and one’s own complacency is deprived of the merit of obedience before the eternal judge; and, as idle and presumed without the permission of a superior, is held liable to account.
