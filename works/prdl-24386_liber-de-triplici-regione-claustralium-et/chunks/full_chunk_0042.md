First, the fear of God is described, which is called the beginning of wisdom. The fear of the Lord is handed down under a threefold distinction, namely servile, initial, and filial, which is also called chaste. From this threefold distinction three roots are said to proceed, according to which the beginner in the cenobitic life ought to order his meditations. For each root has its own proper meditations; and when one is rightly disposed, another, better one is always generated.

For from servile fear, assumed nevertheless not servilely but prudently and virtuously, there arise meditations of horror and of punishments; these, restraining the soul from sin, gradually introduce filial fear. For indeed, when by consideration of punishments the mind is turned to horror, boldness in sinning is restrained, because the servant who fears blows keeps the Lord's commandments. But this servile fear by itself does not suffice for meriting the paternal inheritance; nevertheless its initial effect disposes one toward filial fear, which is sufficient.

For initial fear is placed between the two, and is easily changed from servile into filial. This initial fear receives meditations concerning the last things, and it dreads both to incur punishments and to lose the sweet homeland. And this is set forth more fully in the Rule, where its roots are also described.

Filial fear, however, embraces its meditations in love, since it fears nothing else except that the beloved father be offended. Of this fear the wise man says: The fear of the Lord is glory and boasting, gladness and a crown of exultation. The fear of the Lord will delight the heart, and will give gladness and joy for length of days. And again: For him who fears God it will be well at the end, and on the day of his death he will be blessed. This fear is also called chaste, because not from fear of punishments but solely from love [unclear] it detests evils.

From this threefold fear we see three breadths of spiritual exercises arise concerning the degrees of humility, and to each fear its own exercises correspond. For servile fear performs its exercises in one way; initial fear applies itself to virtues in another; and filial fear proceeds in its works in a much different way. For just as the affections of hearts arise in varied fashion, so the exercises of a single fear take place in diverse ways. For in one way the publican, mindful of his crimes, feared the sentence of the judge; in another way Jerome said that he heard the voice of the trumpet calling the dead to judgment. For concerning that man the holy Gospel says: Standing far off, the publican would not lift up his eyes to heaven, but striking his breast he said: God, be merciful to me, a sinner.
