For just as this resignation of oneself receives its foundation from the second degree of humility, so too an inward and complete resignation to the will of God in all things is necessarily required. For the Lord says in the Gospel: “I have not come to do my own will, but that of the Father who sent me.” Perfect resignation, therefore, is that by which the human will is wholly subjected to the divine will and conformed to it out of entire love. For thus the Lord himself taught us to pray: “Thy will be done, as in heaven and on earth.” And that saying of the psalm-writer: “Into thy hands I commend my spirit.” No one ever lives securely in this world unless the human will is perfectly subjected to God.

Indeed, the perfect resignation of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his will to God, shows the straight way to the highest without any intermediary. Here there is the greatest tranquility of mind, the greatest security in the heart, since the soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will be able to fear no opposition of this world any longer, except sin alone. Whatever adversity comes upon it, with the Lord’s help it has learned to overcome; whatever convenience it desires, it understands that it possesses in the Lord alone. It despises all adverse things for love of him; it has placed all good things in love of him. For that devout man spoke prudently: “Give [unclear] all good things [unclear] has placed in love of him. Forsake cupidity, and you will find true peace.”

Finally, in order that this resignation of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it implores, so to speak, the support of a threefold assistance.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance, so that the will once denied may never be taken back. For against those who despise this assistance that holy Bernard says: “Why do you again take charge of yourselves, you who once, by mature deliberation, handed over your will to us?” For a good monk who has once handed over his will to God into the hands of the abbot does not take it back without the guilt of sacrilege.

The second thing required for the perfect resignation of the will is that it never grow slack. Hence the Lord says to the lukewarm soul in the Apocalypse: “I have a few things against you, because you have abandoned your first charity,” that is, your fervor.

Third, resignation of the will is perfected if it is never cut off. For the Savior says: “He who perseveres to the end will be saved.” Therefore, when you see a cenobite restless or moved to anger, know that the whole mass of the temptation proceeds from lack of resignation; nor will you ever take care of this disease of sickness unless you first persuade him to the resignation of the denied will.
