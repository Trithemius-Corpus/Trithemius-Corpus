There is also another mode of self-knowledge, which is taken up with respect to God, whenever the incomparable comparison between God and man is considered. On this mode Bernard, illustrious in contemplation, says: Never does the measure of human imperfection perceive itself better than in the light of the face of God and in the mirror of divine vision. For there, the more one is in the day and the more one sees what is lacking in oneself, the more one amends day by day whatever has strayed into unlikeness, and approaches, by likeness, him from whom one has been far removed by unlikeness. Let us therefore raise a suppliant mind to the mirror of divine perfection; let us submit our affection to that immutable good, so that in the wisdom of God we may be able to perceive our foolishness, and in his power to consider our weakness.

*On Keeping the Memory of One’s Sins.*

The root of living fear of God is the memory of sins and the very foulness of sin when pondered. For indeed, the person who truly understands the shamefulness of mortal sin is always terrified of the pit of this evil. In order that we may in some way recognize the gravity of sin, you must consider the seven things that follow.

First, then, consider the beauty of God’s justice, to which sin is wholly contrary and therefore most of all to be fled. For through sin the soul is utterly turned away from the highest good, and the love that is naturally in it toward its principle is changed into hatred. For just as all good things come from the first good, so every love, toward whatever good it is borne, is naturally kindled by that same source. For since our love cannot rest in any goods as in its end, it has, as the end where it is to be stilled, the same good that it had also as its beginning. Therefore, whenever sin is committed against the beauty of God’s justice, the soul is separated from its own principle. For since God is charity, he requires love in the soul; since he is peace, he requires peace; since he loves likeness, he hates unlikeness.
