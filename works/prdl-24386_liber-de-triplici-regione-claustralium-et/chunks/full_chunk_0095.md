The third chapter is called that of private humiliation, whenever a brother is rebuked by a senior for his negligences. For our most holy father Benedict commands in the seventy-second chapter of the Rule that a monk, accused in any place or at any time, as soon as he has perceived that the mind of his senior is moved, even slightly, against him, ought to prostrate himself at his feet and lie there humbly until his command. Here he is in no way permitted to excuse himself or to lay the blame on another; rather, the monk is commanded to be silent and humbly acknowledge himself. For he who is a true monk neither murmurs when justly corrected, nor excuses himself when unjustly accused. Likewise in the divine offices and the other conventual places, as soon as a devout cenobite has given offense in anything through negligence, humbling himself according to custom, he makes satisfaction there; and in doubtful matters he chooses rather to undergo the greater penance than, with the lesser, to wound his conscience or cause scandal to others. But whoever understands that it is still burdensome for him to submit to this humiliation at every time and place does not walk in the spirit, but still lives carnally; and unless he has made this yoke-bearing tributary to himself, he will never be a true and devout cenobite.

*The fourth chapter is the treatment of counsels*

We call the fourth chapter deliberative, when we summon the brothers to counsel for some difficult matter. How this chapter ought to be held, Saint Benedict makes plain in the Rule concerning the summoning of brothers to counsel. Therefore the humble and true cenobite, when he has been called to counsel, approaches humbly, with reverence and modesty; when asked what he thinks, he speaks few words; he does not at all disdain that his own counsel be rejected and others’ counsel approved. For what he thinks he says humbly, but always preferring the counsels of others to his own, and he commits both himself and his opinion to be examined by the one presiding, in true subjection. For he annihilates himself so deeply in humility that he always believes others more than himself, and trusts that good counsel is divinely inspired in those better than he; and he is accustomed to set his prelate before all in his mind as the vicar of Christ. Indeed, the monk makes the greatest progress toward the mortification of his own will who, in giving counsels, at all times submits his own opinion to the abbot with cheerful humility. But when the devout cenobite perceives that his own counsel is rejected or not approved, let him give thanks to almighty God for the said humility, and recognize that an occasion of pride has been taken away from him.

*After the daily chapter.*

If after the chapter of faults one must return to the church for terce, let the manner of the appointed prayer above be observed in each point. But if you pass from the said chapter to the refectory, again, with knees bent, as after matins, you shall pray to the Lord.
