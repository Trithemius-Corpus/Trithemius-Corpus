Before all things, take care that you admit no displeasure against your brother; for he who is frequently angry with his neighbor is plainly proved not to love God. For whoever loves God with all his heart does not admit displeasure against his faithful brother. A monk ought to be established in such great elevation of mind that, whatever his brother has committed against him, by weighing the nature of his soul he cannot be moved.

*Let the monk’s conduct toward those who do injury [be meek]*

Third, the monk’s conduct toward those who do injury ought to be meek; and he then at last acts well if regard for the common future blessedness and companionship is always kept before him. To attain this meekness of spirit, our cenobite should learn to recall to mind more often the ways of life of the holy fathers, and faithfully consider with how great joy they received injuries. First of all, as far as possible, let him strive to imitate our Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who not only endured the injuries and insults of the impious with a most meek heart, but also prayed to the Father for his persecutors, that they should not perish. Imitating this example of the Savior, the holy fathers were accustomed to receive injuries patiently and meekly for the love of God, and, what is more, to love the very men who injured them. To these things let the cenobite recall to mind the degrees of meekness, which are contained, briefly enclosed, in these verses: Fit yourself. Strengthen yourself. Being gentle, bear contrary things. Preserve. Restrain. Rest inwardly with yourself. Correct prudently. Be merciful. Give wisely. By placing yourself below, by taking up martyrdom. For humility, if it has possessed the mind of the cenobite, makes him love injuries. The degrees of humility are contained in these verses.

*The twelve degrees of humility.*

The first of the righteous degrees fears the Almighty.
The second puts reins upon the enticements of the flesh.
The third subjects himself reverently to the abbot.
The fourth, despising insults, patiently obeys.
The fifth reveals the inner chambers of his heart to the abbot.
The sixth holds himself vile and unworthy by his own judgment.
The seventh believes himself to be lower than all.
The eighth does nothing that the rule of the cell does not allow.
The eleventh speaks the few words of reason.
The twelfth always looks at the ground with bent head.
