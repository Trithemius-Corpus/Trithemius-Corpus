Woe to you, disordered cloistered men, who through a double concupiscence of the eyes descend to hell: while you desire to see in others what is not expedient, and present yourselves to be seen by the rest otherwise than you ought. What are the jewels of the age to you? What the curiosities of the world? These beauties, as the blessed Bernard bears witness, quickly effeminate the masculine soul; these worldly dignities quickly enervate a manly purpose. You are like those ragged boys who, because of a single narrow red band fastened to a torn sleeve, consider themselves very handsome and comely, and insolently mock others set before them, though excellently clothed, as if they were naked. These pompous cloistered men see others going about in humble dress, scorning the world in the spirit of poverty, and for that reason pleasing both God and men; but they see themselves despised by the wise and by princes, and sometimes not even admitted to conversation. Seeing these things, the proud, although they grieve heavily as if at an injury, nevertheless utterly refuse to lay aside their haughtiness.

Third, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered toward contempt of the world because of pride of life, since they are not only infected with vain complacencies of the flesh, but are also proud through swelling of mind. For those who follow the desires of the flesh vainly lift themselves above themselves; they despise others, however much better than themselves; they do not know how to endure equals; they do not acknowledge superiors. These men rejoice that a great retinue of servants attends them; in councils they strive to sit first; they desire to be seen by all as glorious; they go through public places pompously. Swollen with the spirit of pride, they boast that their monasteries possess, by foundation, insignia opulent in temporal goods, jewels, buildings, and curious privileges. With uplifted heart they despise poor monks or abbots of small monasteries; they do not blush to prefer themselves to better men; and the more they desire to hunt for glory in external things, the more they withdraw with the foolish virgins from true glory.

*How the citizens of this lowest region are disordered toward penance.*

*Article VIII.*

Since no one living in time and filled with many miseries can remain long in the same state, it is necessary that, by reforming himself daily with all zeal, he turn himself toward the end that is due, since for as long as anyone is permitted to live in this world, he is considered changeable in either direction. Therefore whoever, established in this changeableness, desires to come to the joys of immutability, let him humbly acknowledge his own frailty, and especially after the fall of sin let him flee to the remedies of penance, and let him fear to lie in crime even for a moment. For the life of a monk ought always to consist in saving penance, and in holy fear [unclear] the coming of the judge C. iii.
