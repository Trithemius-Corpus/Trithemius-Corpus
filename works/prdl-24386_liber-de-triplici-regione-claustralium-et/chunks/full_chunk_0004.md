And therefore all the exercises of monks are to be humbly submitted to the constitutions of the Rule, since whatever is prescribed either by us or by others for the instruction of monks is wholly directed to the observance of the monastic norm. For the sum of our whole religious life, which is acquired by the continuance of spiritual exercise, is contained in compendium in the Rule of monastic tradition, as in a root. Therefore every exercise of monks, whether spiritual or bodily, by which the mind is brought through love to humble obedience and self-denial, is conformed to the institutions of the holy Rule; the more it is recognized as presenting a good and humble monk as near to God, by so much the more it opposes the pursuits of the proud contemner. The holy mysteries of this exercise direct the mind of its cultivator, by an invisible teaching, toward the love of God, as toward its due end, along a secure path; they wonderfully acquire the grace of inward devotion, and make the monk despise all earthly things as nothing, and sweetly burn at all times in fervor of spirit. The animal man does not perceive this salutary exercise of devotion; the vain and curious monk does not understand this pursuit; the proud man does not admit this knowledge, which is to be preferred to all things. Happy is he who has learned to penetrate the secrets of this exercise; happy is he who has deserved to receive from the Lord this gift of holy compunction, which, once anyone has perfectly obtained it, he will fear no adversities of this world. But that exercise which, lacking its due end, does not direct the minds of cloistered men to the observance of the commandments of the Rule, the holy Spirit of discipline does not infuse, since where the obedience of subjection is despised, no gift is accepted by almighty God. For it is written: Obedience is better than victims, and to hearken rather than to offer fat. And although many little works of spiritual exercise composed by various authors are found, yet, because not all things suit all people, it has seemed good also to you to publish something in accordance with the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, by which we may more greatly and more nearly stir ourselves to the purity of regular observance. Accordingly, we divide the present little work, collected from the exercises of the excellent father Theodoric the abbot, into two principal parts: the first of which is set forth in consideration of monastic instruction; the second is occupied with the exercise of the spiritual life.
