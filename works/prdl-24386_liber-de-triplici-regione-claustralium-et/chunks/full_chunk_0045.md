The third degree of humility is to submit oneself in all obedience to a superior for love of God, and to show oneself an imitator of Christ by deeds, not words; concerning this the holy Apostle says: “He became obedient unto death, even death on a cross.” This degree takes its beginning from the second and raises its ascent as far as its own third degree. For just as the second mortifies one’s own will by denying it, so this one lays the foundation of holy work through humble subjection. The former subjects itself wholly to the divine will; this one, for love of God, also obeys men. The keeping of one degree without the observance of the other is not sufficient in a monk, nor is the will of a cenobite subjected to divine ordering if he disdains to obey the command of a superior.

Therefore the true cenobite places all his joy in the virtue of holy obedience. He thinks it the greatest gain to fulfill in all things the will of his shepherd; he believes that he incurs grave loss to his soul if he finds that he has committed even a little outside obedience. For he always considers with faithful devotion what the Lord says in the Gospel: “He who hears you hears me; and he who despises you despises me.” Hence that blessed Bernard says to the monks: Whatever a man commands in God’s place must be received no otherwise than if God himself commanded it in person. Therefore let the true monk prefer this subjection of obedience to every sacrifice; let him do absolutely nothing against its integrity; and let him continually say to himself within himself what is written: “Obedience is better than victims.” Hence the Rule too says: “Therefore all things are to be done with the will of the abbot.”

Now this degree, as we have said, arises from the second. He who, for fear of God, perfectly abandons his own will now seeks nothing else in this world than to follow that will in all things. But because God speaks once and does not repeat the same thing, a weak man cannot know the will of the Most High; for this reason he has committed himself into the hands of his prelate. Therefore let the cenobite obey, according to the Rule, the vicar of Christ, and without doubt he will fulfill the will of God.

This obedience, however, is owed strictly to prelates according to the Gospel and the monastic Rule; for although mutual obedience among the brothers is good, it must never be preferred to the command of a superior. Monks are commanded to show one another obedience with discretion, and, while preserving the commands of their superiors, to anticipate one another in mutual services. And, to insert an example from our own custom: all are to be obeyed according to rank. If a brother calls on you, obey gladly; if a senior commands something, then, setting aside the command of the former, obey this one. Again, if the prior of the convent interposes his command, all mutual obedience of the brothers will cease. But if the command of the abbot intervenes, no obedience to anyone else will have force to the contrary. For he is believed to act first in the place of Christ in the monastery, and therefore he is preferred to all.
