Rooted in this humility, the cenobite loves reproach and insult; he does not fear to be despised and cast aside most vilely, because, submitting himself wholly in the love of God, he places himself humbly, and hastens to insults as though to feasts, with the greatest cheerfulness. But so that you may understand the truth of this seventh degree, read the following considerations.

The first consideration directs the mind to hope in divine love, which arises from a certain pious confidence. For although a person does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless each person can in some measure recognize, from the disposition of his own heart, what he ought to think about his own state, good or bad.

Second, it must be considered that this good disposition of the heart and of an ordered will toward God is a sign of the love of God and of the approval of good works. For thus, when the Lord was born in the flesh, the angels sang: Peace on earth to people of good will.

Third, it should be noted that just as a good disposition of heart orders and summons the perfected mind to exercises directed toward the fear of God and the practice of virtues, so the negligence of an evil disposition inclines it especially to vices and sloth. But the consideration of this difference [unclear].

Fourth, it must be considered that an evil disposition of the heart impedes and nullifies the grace of God and merits in a person, because it makes the soul vile, base, and degenerate, and always disposes it toward graver and worst things. For indeed the root of evils of tongue and deed comes from an evil heart, as is clear in tales and many similar things.

The fifth consideration is that evil in deed and tongue is never properly examined unless first the evil of the heart is truly known. Each person, however, can examine his own heart better than others can, and consider what sort of person lies hidden within himself. Therefore for the cenobite, to whom the care of another has not been entrusted, it is enough to inspect his own heart diligently, and in no way to occupy himself in scrutinizing another's heart. But the pastor of monks, to whom the care of souls is entrusted, is also commanded to know the face of his flock.

Sixth, it should be noted from these things that it is very rash to examine another's heart, or to judge the inmost parts of brothers from words and deeds. The Lord knows those who are his, nor is it granted to a person to become a rash judge of another's heart without the rationale of office.

Seventh, it should be noted that a small evil of one's own heart, once known, would harm a person more in himself than a great evil, doubted from suspicion, concerning another's heart. For the former must be amended because it is certain and known; the latter must be piously excused because it is unknown.

Eighth, it must be considered that, since each person sees many imperfections in his own heart which he cannot recognize in others, on account of these obstacles to grace he ought to judge himself lower.
