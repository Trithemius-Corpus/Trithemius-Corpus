I truly do not know what I should answer to this question, so that I may not incur the danger which is seen to threaten on both sides. For if I tell the truth, I shall seem to violate the obedience of subjection; if I consent that monks are to be exposed to danger, I confess that I sin. Yet I shall say briefly what I think, although I am not unaware that the truth will give birth to hatred. Just as a monk, by the necessity of his profession, is bound simply to obey his pastor in all things that are according to the monastic Rule, so a prelate cannot compel a monk, without that monk’s own will, to things that are beyond the Rule, especially when danger to salvation intervenes. But to govern churches, to preside over women, to reside outside the monastery, is not according to the Rule [unclear]. Do not be angry with me, fathers, if I have spoken the truth; do not knit your brows if I have expressed what seems so to me. Nevertheless, you will do what seems good to you, even if I protest. But I beg you, do not expose your monks to danger to chastity; do not violently compel anyone to the governance of nuns and of parish churches, since you can find many who ambitiously seek these offices. Obedience is indeed good, but it must by no means be preferred to truth and the salvation of the soul. For when danger to salvation is at issue, it is better humbly to set obedience aside by making excuse than to suffer shipwreck of chastity. Woe to the prelates of monks who incorporate churches of secular clergy into their monasteries, who loosen their bosom to covetous desires by the torches of avarice, because, while they think they are doing good for posterity, they damn many through the occasion.

*Fourth, the governance of monks becomes disordered toward its subjects if it is found turbulent and anxious.* In a pastor of souls a pious and prudent discretion is very necessary, lest either zeal, with discretion lacking, be excessive, or the measure of compassion be slackened by excessive laxity. But a disordered rector sometimes, through indiscreet fury, presents himself as a tyrant to the monks; sometimes, through a fixed compassion, he grows, as it were, insensible to the gravest crimes. He does not know the just moderation of governance; he does not experience in himself the warmth of brotherly charity; he always thinks himself lord of the monks; he does not know how to be the father and companion of the humble. What is more savage than this governance? What more wicked than this power, where God, reason, and understanding do not govern the impious man? The judgments of those presiding do not depart from the rectitude of the law of nature. A disordered and anxious rector is infected by beastly passions of various kinds; he always suspects evil of the monks; and while he directs the manner of governance [unclear], anxious, he always fears with Herod that he will lose his kingdom.
