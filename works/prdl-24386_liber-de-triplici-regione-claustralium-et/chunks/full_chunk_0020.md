*The sixth degree of pride* takes its beginning from the fifth; it is called the arrogance of the proud. For indeed, when disorderly cloistered men, through a zeal for singularity, begin outwardly to appear different from others, they are inflamed into a greater madness of arrogance; and whatever is said by people in their praise, the fools judge to be true. O lamentable folly of the proud: since in all matters they believe no one else more than themselves, yet concerning themselves alone they believe themselves less than another. Whatever praise of themselves they hear from the unskilled, they ascribe not to the ignorance or goodwill of the praiser, but to their own merits. But whoever is pleasantly fed on human praises is held before the Lord to be empty of every virtue and full of arrogance.

*The seventh degree of pride* is born from the preceding one; it is called the presumption of the proud. Into this fall all the arrogant, who believe themselves individually to be something great. Concerning them Bernard says: He who thinks that he surpasses others, how shall he not presume more of himself than of others? He sits first in the community; in counsels he answers first; uncalled, he approaches; not ordered, he thrusts himself in; he reorders what has been ordered; he rejects what has been done by others. Whatever he himself has not done or ordered, he thinks was not rightly done nor handsomely ordered. He judges those who judge; he prejudges those who are about to judge. For one to whom God does not suffice in the heart seeks consolation in external things in vain.

*The eighth degree of pride* is called the defense of sins, because it is begotten from presumption. For a presumptuous monk always thinks himself better than all; if he has committed any sin, he either stubbornly denies it, or, if he has been convicted, excuses it in many ways. For how will he be able humbly to confess his fault, who does not acknowledge himself guilty? If you wish to chastise such a man for his offenses, you always make him worse. Therefore whoever, when corrected for any matter, has turned aside into words of malice, whoever opposes to argument excuses for sins, falls into this descent of death, opposed to the fifth degree of humility. Now the presumptuous man who is found to have fallen into this degree of pride tries to excuse his sin in various ways. For either he says, “I did not do it,” or “I did it indeed, but I did well,” or, if badly, “not very badly,” or, if very badly, “not with evil intention.” And if he has been convicted of malice, at once in his own excuse he takes up another’s counsel. But he who does not fear insolently to deny or defend even things that are fitting and manifest will never reveal hidden things according to the rule to his abbot. But when he comes to confession, he will cloak the truth with a feigned mind; so that he himself may be able to escape confusion, he strives to bring forward into the midst the negligences of others. Inwardly ill-ordered, beneath and presumptuous, in confession he cloaks his own faults; he confesses those of others.
