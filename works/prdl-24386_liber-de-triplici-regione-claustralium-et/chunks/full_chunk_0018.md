Nor is it any wonder if, toward others, he becomes outwardly a disordered cultivator of the lowest region, who, confused within himself by the various passions of the flesh, is inwardly scattered. For Scripture says: He who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good? The right order of the human condition established that the flesh is to be subjected to the spirit; the spirit is commanded to be subject to him from whom it received the form of being. But among carnal minds the order of reason is perverted, when the flesh rules over the spirit, and reason, which according to the force of understanding implanted in it ought to have governed all things, is suffocated by the pleasures of the flesh and does not hasten by command toward the rectitude of judgment.

Carnal monks, therefore, are disordered toward themselves through the vice of curiosity, because by curiously inquiring into another’s life they pass judgment, and do not amend their own life, defiled by all vices. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, yet they proudly reprove others when they offend in the smallest matters. They diligently consider the speck in a brother’s eye, and excuse the beam clinging in their own eyes as if it were nothing. They subject their affections to sensuality, and, the judgment of right reason being spurned, they live carnally like beasts; passion frequently stirs them to anger, and the fury of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With anxious mind they demand vengeance upon their brothers, which, often blinded by excessive fury, they bring back not without harm to themselves. But disordered citizens cannot long lie hidden in secret, for the more they remain unknown to themselves through elation of mind, the more through blind judgment they become manifest to others. For a proud and disordered man is said first to be ignorant of himself, because if he truly knew himself, he would in no way be proud. Since, then, disorder of oneself takes its origin from pride, we cannot describe this plague better than if we undertake to uncover its roots, descending by degrees toward hell. For just as the perfection of the spiritual life is comprised in the twelve degrees of humility, so the whole disorder of the monastery is contained in the twice-six degrees of pride.

The first degree of pride, then, is called curiosity, by which monks badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and morals, and continually inquire into the deeds or words of others. At every time they accuse the negligence of the brothers; they reprove the life of the pastor; and the more curiously they desire to know outwardly the affairs of others, the farther they withdraw from themselves. But the vice of curiosity is most destructive to monks, because, when it has once invaded the mind, stripping it of all virtues, it makes it a dwelling-place of vices. It is a very true sign of infirmity to examine the deeds of others and, beyond the reason of one’s office, to occupy oneself with the faults of others. For the monk who, according to the definition of his name, is instructed to care for himself alone, scrutinizes the deeds of others not without manifest danger to his salvation.
