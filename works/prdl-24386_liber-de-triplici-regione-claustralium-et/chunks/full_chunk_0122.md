The handling of another’s property against the owner’s will is theft, and you know that thieves will have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God. Therefore a monk, regularly professed to God and to his abbot, thereafter no longer has lordship over his own will; if afterwards he usurps that same will for himself, he commits theft and sacrilege. Nor will permission [obtained?] save the presumptuous man, if it was obtained by threats and insults. Your abbot, brother, is God’s vicar; to him you must render the obedience you promised, not as to a man but as to God, and show humble reverence in God’s place. For whatever is rendered by subjects to their ruler is without doubt referred to God, whose vicar he is. Therefore, since you have subjected yourself to your prelate for God alone and for no other cause, you should strive at all times to show yourself such toward him that he may freely command you to do or omit whatever seems expedient to him, and may in no way fear any insolences or disturbances of your mind. For if your abbot is compelled to fear you, his monk, the order of equity is violated; and where the disciple strives to be above the master, the Gospel is sinned against. Beware, therefore, lest you withhold reverence and honor from prelates. Beware lest by grieving them you offend the Lord; but take care to meet them, whatever sort they may be, with reverence, and to obey their commands in all things according to the Gospel and the rule. Believe me, brother, that the fault which is committed by a subject against a prelate is punished most severely either in the present life or in the future one. It is written: Do not touch my anointed ones. Whatever your prelate has commanded you, you ought to receive with such confidence that you believe God has inspired it in him.

*Let the monk’s manner of life be in charity toward the brothers.*

Second, the monk’s manner of life toward his brethren commands him to have charity, through which alone peace and concord are maintained in the monastery. Let our faithful cenobite always think upon and turn over in his heart the common joy of souls in eternal happiness, and let him continually direct the eye of his mind to the equal condition of souls, so that his thought may not withdraw from love of their future fellowship. If a brother has offended you in any way by word or deed, if he has been troublesome or hostile to you in anything, do not take up from this a disordered movement of sensuality; do not consider his present infirmity, but raise the eyes of your mind to the nobility of his soul, and weigh what fellowship in eternal happiness it is to have with you.
