The sixth degree of humility arises from true confession: that the monk should consider himself vile and unworthy in all things. For according to the Rule we believe him to be a true monk whom we perceive, from humble patience, to be content with every vileness and extremity in food and clothing. This vileness, however, is generally referred to ordinary things; but extremity is located in abject and more grievous hardships. For the former gladly embraces patience in meaner garments, foods, and whatever necessities of the body; the latter delights in serving the sick, the horrible, the leprous, and the abject, and in being put to shame, lowered, and despised in all things. Therefore the monk who is truly dead to the flesh and to the world should be content with every vileness and extremity; he should show himself a faithful worker in all things, and yet, because of the corruption of nature by which he offends in many things, he should believe himself vile and unworthy. Indeed, whatever good is done in this world, however holy and religiously one may live in this age, can suffice for no one to merit eternal life, unless the great mercy of God deigns to come to his aid. We receive the greatest things for the least; we await the kingdom of God not as owed to us from our merit, but as promised by grace. For the holy Apostle says: The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that will be revealed in us. For if we receive the justice of God without mercy, we are worthy of hell. All of us incur wrath by sinning, but we gain love by grace. No one is saved except through grace; no one is damned except through justice. Nevertheless, meanwhile, in order that we may obtain the grace of God, the zeal of our good will is required; without it grace works nothing in us toward merit. For although no one is holy by his own powers, nevertheless no one has done good well against his will. For the grace of God and the freedom of the will stand in such relation to each other that one without the other cannot suffice to save a human being, but both must necessarily come together for the performance of virtue. Since, therefore, without the grace of God we can do nothing good, we ought to judge ourselves vile and unworthy in all things, so that, while we submit ourselves to God in humility, we may always obtain greater things. And therefore this sixth degree is the merit of confession and the single foundation of all the preceding ones. For as long as the cenobite remains in this degree of humility, he will stand firm in the former ones.

The seventh degree of humility is not only to believe oneself lower than all others from the innermost affection of the heart, but also to desire with a simple mind that others believe and say this about him. This condition of holy humility is set on high, and in this time is loved by few monks. For the first condition of this deep humility seems to consist in this: that he ought to judge that he should be considered vile and abject by all.
