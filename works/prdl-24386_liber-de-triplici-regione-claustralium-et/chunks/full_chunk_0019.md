*The second degree of pride, therefore, is lightness of mind and the unstable occupation of a wandering heart.* Into this degree of disordered conduct carnal monks fall through various affections, which they have contracted sensually from the vice of curiosity. But the true coenobite must remain steadfast in his holy purpose, because when lightness of mind is admitted, the constancy of virtues is scattered in the heart. It is a sign of a disordered mind to run through various images of things and always to generate new forms of sensual affections.

*The third degree of pride is called vain gladness, which is produced from the lightness of mind of a disordered man.* Carnal monks fashion for themselves images of affections; when these present themselves as pleasing to sensuality, they rejoice, but when the opposite occurs, contracting heavy indignation, they slip into various temptations. But that is not true joy which takes its beginning from anywhere other than God; nor will gladness long endure which has not taken its course from the love of God. And therefore disordered men, through vain gladness, fall into open boasting, while [unclear], as we have said, arises from vain gladness, which is called foolish boasting. Into this carnal monks fall when, magnifying themselves before others, they exalt themselves. If they have done anything good, they boldly bring it forward in public, often falsely inventing even what they have not done. They desire to seem holy, learned, prudent, and noble, and they invent many things by cleverness of wit, so that they may appear glorious outwardly to men, while they are not unaware that inwardly they are empty of the merit of virtue. If you begin to praise anyone before them, at once they rise up in opposition; they say many vicious things against him, because, while they desire to show themselves alone praiseworthy, they do not allow anyone else to be praised before them.

*The fifth degree is singularity.* Into this degree of pride cloistered men fall who have presumed through boasting that they excel others either in merit or in knowledge. Hence, lest they appear like the rest in their manner of life, out of pride alone they show themselves singular in all things. This singularity of the proud is reprehended in a monastery, where the rule commands all to live in common. The proud, as Saint Bernard bears witness, when they prefer themselves to others for their virtues and wisdom, fall into such rash arrogance that they flatter themselves more over one fast while the rest are eating than if they had fasted seven days with the others. Finally, one little prayer of their own singular kind seems more advantageous to them than the whole psalmody of one night. During the meal they carry their eyes around the tables, and if they see anyone eating less, they groan and begin to grieve that they have been defeated, cruelly withholding from themselves what they had provided for necessary sustenance.
