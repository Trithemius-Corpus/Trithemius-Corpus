For blessed Jerome says: “The monk has the office not of one who teaches, but of one who laments: one who should mourn either himself or the world, and, fearful, wait for the coming of the Lord.” But the disorderly cloistered men of the lowest region despise this saving remedy; they consume the acceptable time in vanity; and those who do not fear to be ill-ordered in all things show themselves also very disorderly in doing penance.

First, indeed, they are ill-ordered for penance through carelessness of mind, when they neglect to understand how much sin displeases almighty God, against whom they do not fear to act in contradiction by the choice of a depraved will. As black night is set against day, so the sinner, turned away from the divine goodness, is regarded as hostile and adversarial. What a putrid corpse is wont to do in the sight of human beings, this, in its own way, sin does incomparably in the sight of the angels. But carnal monks despise the honor of God, and therefore do not notice the insult of sin. They neglect to amend venial sins, who as if lawfully commit mortal sins. For Scripture says: “He who neglects little things will fall little by little.” Hence the saying of Bernard admonishes us, saying: “Let no one, brothers, say in his heart: ‘These are light things; I do not care to correct them. It is no great thing if I remain in these venial and very small sins.’ This, brothers, is impenitence; this is the unforgivable blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. For no one can escape greater sins if he has scorned to amend lesser ones.” Nothing is worse for the mind than carelessness.

Second, carnal monks, lovers of the lowest region, are exceedingly disorderly for penance, because they do not notice the contagion of sin. For just as from a contagious disease a healthy person is very often infected, so from one sin a person is inclined into another. For one sin is always said to dispose toward another, unless it is amended as soon as possible through contrition. For the prophet says: “Jerusalem has sinned a sin; therefore she has been made unstable.” And elsewhere: “He who is in filth, let him grow filthy still.” We have, moreover, sufficiently shown this contagion of monks in the degrees of pride; for just as we said that one always arises from another, so we perceive that through carelessness a person becomes slippery from one sin into another.

Third, carnal monks are disorderly for penance because they contract a very bad sickness through the habit of sin, when they incur a destructive blindness of mind. For a vice often committed is slighted, and through habit is judged more lightly. Just as grace enlightens the mind, so frequent transgression blinds reason. For it is written: “Blind the heart of this people and make its ears heavy, so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand, lest they be converted and I heal them,” says the Lord. For when the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he despises. No sin is so great that it does not seem light and small through habit.
