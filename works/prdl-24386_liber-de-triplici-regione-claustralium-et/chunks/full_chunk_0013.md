This enemy of the Christian faith the disordered and carnal abbot strives, as far as he can, to imitate: he resists the commands of so necessary a chapter. For the authority of the chapter alone is what maintains the observance of regular discipline in the order; if it is despised, no integrity of monastic life will remain. But the disordered abbot, a dweller in the lowest region, invents innumerable reasons against regular observance; he falsely alleges that the conditions of his monastery are contrary; he complains that ancient privileges are being suppressed. He disdains that his monastery be subjected to anyone. But woe to disordered men, through whom even one monastery should happen to be lost from this most holy congregation.

Finally, this plague of avarice makes an abbot not only disordered toward his subjects, but also contrary to himself and utterly turned away from God. For covetousness is the root of all evils; when it seizes a servant of Christ, it makes him a minister of the devil. Therefore the abbot who has subjected his mind to this vice shows himself to be not a shepherd but a hireling. He is always gaping after earthly profits; he cares nothing for the advancement of souls; a book of sacred reading is rarely or never seen in his hands; registers of rents are turned over more often. He joins field to field; he is always carrying together gold and silver to be buried in a chest; and while he desires too much that his monastery abound in temporal goods, he prepares prey for plunderers. For frequently the treasury of temporal lordship devours the monies of monasteries, which the excessive greed of monks brings into monasteries. Woe to you, avaricious shepherd of cloistered men, who bear greater anxiety for brute animals than for the souls committed to you. The horse grows sick and at once you prepare a remedy; a soul perishes and you think no danger threatens. The ox and the ass fall ill and you suffer with them; a brother is swallowed up by sadness and you do not come to his aid. You, therefore, hireling and not shepherd, who with a stubborn heart despise the infirmities of your brothers, who do not aid those set in bitterness, do not presume that you can escape the hand of the Lord, whose sheep you permit so wickedly to perish.

*The third way a ruler is disordered toward his subjects is when those whom he ought carefully to have kept within the enclosures of the monastery he places outside, not without danger.* For indeed, when a weak cenobite is exposed to the rule of nuns or to secular churches, the integrity of regular observance is violated; because he who, still remaining in the lowest region, has not learned to subject the flesh to the spirit, does not know how to preside over others without danger to himself. But the disordered shepherd who, on account of the advantage of temporal property, does not fear to expose his monks to so great a danger, is guilty of all the souls perishing by such an occasion, and before the inward judge is bound to make restitution for all.
