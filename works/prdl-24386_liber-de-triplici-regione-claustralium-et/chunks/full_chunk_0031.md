*Here begins the second region of the soul, which is called rational, in which human beings are engaged amid many labors.*

The second region of the soul, therefore, is called rational; in it the mind is exercised in continual labor for the inquiry into supreme truth, and the farther it separates itself from the lowest region, the more it approaches the highest. And this region is set forth as resembling, in certain ways, the ethereal heaven, in which the position of the stars and their continual motion are found. For just as motion itself is disposed in heaven by the order of the first mover, with [unclear] as cause, so continual labor is employed in the middle region, so that each intellectual power may perfect its own object and in no way withdraw from the order naturally assigned to it. The powers of the soul of this kind are three: memory, intellect, and will.

Of these, memory gathers the likenesses of things and phantasms, and extends itself toward the difficult. The intellect, by discerning those phantasms themselves, considers them, and here investigates the true. The will embraces what has been approved by the intellect in due order, and this is occupied with the known good. When these three powers of the soul rightly preserve their order, they lead the spirit in faith to heavenly things.

This second region, moreover, is compared to the ethereal heaven, in which, just as there is continual motion and the position of the stars, so in this middle region there is continual motion and labor against the contests of vices. And just as the motion of the stars, by the order of its office implanted in it by nature, accomplishes its task, so also in this region the equity of rectitude is required to come about, so that each power of the soul, while it is subjected to its own object, may be perfected to the ordained end assigned to it, so far as can befall wayfarers. For not simply can any of these powers be perfected to fullness in this region, since no one in this pilgrimage is so perfect that he cannot be more perfect in his own order. For even if memory, in its own object, which is the difficult, could behold the gathered likenesses and phantasms of all creatures, nevertheless it would not be satisfied unless it could apprehend their beginning, which is God. But this is altogether impossible for this region, in which we can form no phantasms or likenesses concerning God. The intellect also, the counsellor, so to speak, of the blind will, extends itself toward the true; but however much it considers its own object, it is not satisfied until it can apprehend all truth and come to knowledge of the eternal, supreme, and ineffable truth without the opinion of fantastic speculation.
