To the reverend and highly deserving Jacob Zuwilf, abbot of the monastery of Amorbach of the Order of Saint Benedict, Johannes Marquardus Marquardi sends greeting.

When last autumn, most honored father, as I was about to enter Swabia, I passed through Pforzheim, a town famous for its most learned talents, not so much to greet a few old friends there as to win new ones, the noble Landschad, illustrious by the happy splendor of his birth, whom I was accompanying, addressed Johannes Suebelinus, priest of the foundation, as they call it, of the Holy Spirit, since he had been his former teacher in letters. Suebelinus received us both with great courtesy. And although I was perhaps not known to him by face, afterward through letters, as happens, we fell into conversation; our studies brought us together, and from that agreement friendship was born.

He led me into a rather private study, where, as we looked about for a while, there presented themselves to us the flowery meadows of rhetoricians in both languages, the most pleasant groves of poets, the fertile fields of historians, the green searchings of philosophers, no thickets of sophists, the clearest streams of the Old and New Testament. Finally, from a bookcase there was brought out a little book by Johannes Trithemius on the intelligences. When I had examined and thoroughly searched it, by the Muses, what knowledge of things, how many predictions of future events, and how much, besides, it seemed to prophesy for these times full of dangers! I was captivated both by the discovery and by the omen, and then by the novelty of the matter, and I obtained from my friend permission to copy it. Then, when I compared my copy with one and another writer of histories, the numbers of the years corresponded admirably, and the truth of the histories agreed.

And so I judged it inhuman and uncivil that so great a treasure should be possessed by only a few, although I know that this share of happiness is begrudged to the people by many who will perhaps cry out that what is holy must not be given to dogs. Yet, most candid father, fearing none of those fault-finders, provided that I act under the title of your name, I shall do a thing pleasing to many, and especially to good men. For how rare is the man who will not read it more eagerly when he sees your name written before it on the page?
