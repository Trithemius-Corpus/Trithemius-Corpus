We are glad that the end of the first book of our Polygraphy is at hand; by it, made readier for what remains, we judge that the powers of our talent must be tested. For newly invented things sometimes trouble the beginner, because he whom all follow has no one preceding him. The first beginning of any invention is more difficult; afterward, when it has been made firm, it brings greater confidence to the author. Therefore, completing the first book of our invention with the favor and help of God, we have divided the whole into 384 minute divisions, assigning to each, in order, as is seen, its proper grades of periphrases. And these form a quite beautiful Christian harmony: sixteen way-stations, with nine thousand two hundred phrases duly joined throughout. Nothing is lacking here for students of these inventions of ours, so that in mystical, secret, and hidden matters, even to be announced very remotely in the safest manner, they may have the fullest knowledge and ability.

All things that are contained in the circle of the art proceed from a point; their atoms constitute a circular line, straight by the multitude of light. Whatever is outside the line belongs, by common reckoning, to the day, not to the night. For night, always enclosed, never goes beyond the limits of the minute divisions of the paraphrases. Therefore, whenever you have crossed the straight line of the day and have not fulfilled the purpose of the nocturnal syntax, turn the head back to the tail; when a joining has been made, let there be a mutual bond of both. Arrange the shadow so that it does not confuse the sun by similarity of word. And do not fear shame if, in the first calculation, the day has not comprehended the shadow; rather, carefully consider the minute divisions of the lines of the day, count one with another, until the necessary proportion follows according to your wish. You will also be able to make a leap from the first into the second, knowing that in the first creation of days there was made, from evening and morning, one day. Therefore, when evening has been completed, bring the day back into the following one, and, when the sign has been given through the point, fix the mystery known at once to the binary and the monad.

But in order that you may be able to avoid this difficulty, distinguish the great revolutions of our whole circle by arithmetical degrees; where day is repeated for night, impose the mark of Archimedes upon the flying one, lest an untimely ternary block the return to unity for the binary. For for this reason we have set minute divisions before each grade by an artful invention, so that we might relieve the person working in these matters from the labor of counting the paraphrases. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in these things which we have handed down as the principles of a new art, believe us: you will find greater things hidden than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these matters, know one thing, and not the last: that by the prudent operation of metatheleos, even [when you wish?], you will make one most skilled in this art ignorant, the seeing blind, the learned unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, our invention would give to each of the individual men of eighteen thousand worlds an individual way of safely and secretly writing and communicating very widely whatever secrets they wished in Latin.

*The end of the first book of the Polygraphy is here, of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James the Apostle at Würzburg, on the twelfth day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1508.*
