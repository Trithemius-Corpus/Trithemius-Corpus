The Northmen, however, together with the Asiatic Dacians, having once come forth from the Scandinavian islands, which are now called Gothic, roamed through Gaul for more than forty years and devastated it most severely. In order to provide for their secret affairs, as Bede, the English monk, testifies, they devised from the letters of the Greeks the following new and handsome manner of writing. For they adapted the numerals of the Greeks, comprised in ten letters up to twenty-four, to Latin characters, and under a foreign form prudently concealed our letters, namely in the way in which we use numbers in place of letters: that is, for a we put 1, for b, 2, for c, 3, for d, 4, and so in sequence to the end of the alphabet. A handsome invention indeed, and sufficiently secure, which neither a Greek nor a Latin will easily understand. For the whole alphabet is not found in it, but only nine letters, and one numerical sign, namely g, which among them signifies six, and in this manner of writing f, the sixth letter of the Latins. Moreover, deferring to our German language, in which we were born, we have placed at the end the double w as the twenty-fourth letter of our nation, which, according to the Greek numeral, we formed thus: ꝟ. This manner of writing can also be varied in many ways, when the letters are changed by transposition of the number. This can be done in the full Greek letters according to the wish and judgment of any industrious writer. Nor will the method of forming these characters be very difficult, especially for one who has had a little practice. The characters written above are also sufficiently suited to the German language, since with them we can denote all things that occur to us, in whatever speech we are skilled. Whenever iota is placed by itself, so that it may be known to denote the letter k, the tenth of the alphabet, it is marked above thus: ī.

The opinion of very many holds that the Germans once had letters from the Greeks, and they affirm firmly that they used the Greek language, especially in their sacred rites. For they say that there is a great consonance between the Greek language and our own, although the long interval of time has utterly extinguished that agreement. That most learned man, once my teacher, Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, most reverend bishop of the church of Worms, had collected some thousands of Greek words which have the same meaning in both languages, Greek and German. And Caesar testifies in his Commentaries that the ancient Druids, the priests of sacred rites in Gaul, used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. But when the Romans were devastating the world, they at length violently imposed the use of their own letters, together with their dominion, upon many nations.
