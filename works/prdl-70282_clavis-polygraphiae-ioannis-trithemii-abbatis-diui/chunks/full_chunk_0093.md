Johannes Trithemius, abbot [unclear], sends greetings to the reader.

Since all the letters of the Greeks and the Hebrews are numerical, I have often wondered that the painstaking Latinity of the ancients did not also make its own letters serve the reckoning of numbers. The Hebrews, by a duplication of certain letters, established twenty-eight signs sufficient for every number. The Greeks, however, remedied this lack of letters with like foresight by adding four characters. Therefore, since we have twenty-four letters, why should we not also make ours numerical, since we can similarly find four to be duplicated? Let us give four as an example, and it will be quite enough for every number: m and final nj; likewise long s and final or closed s; likewise closed v and open u. When we add these to the twenty-four usual letters, we have twenty-eight characters sufficient for all numbers. Nor will it be a matter of great labor to grasp this method of numbering for those who already know letters, if once they commit it to memory.

For this invention of ours may be useful to many, since with few letters it comprises many thousands in number, all of which can be distinguished and understood at first sight, without any calculation, more clearly than by the figures of algorism, especially in large numbers, as seems to many who are less experienced in the art of calculation not to be possible so easily. For as soon as you have looked with your eye upon a number, however large, you will be able both fully to understand it and to express it without circumlocution or division of speech. Since this is easy, it is fitting that we set down the letters themselves reduced to number. I wrote this on 16 March, in the year 1509.

Behold, reader, you have the method by which you can sufficiently mark and write every writable number with a few letters; their extension, as you see, proceeds to infinity. But, as it was once customary to say in a proverb, “A weary ox plants his foot firmly.” Whoever wishes to be content with former things should follow the ancient method of numbering. So that those who do not know it may understand it more easily, it will not be tedious for me to give in what follows the form which Hunibald, the historian of the Franks, and also Bede, Rabanus, Helpericus the monks, and many of the ancients used in chronicles and ecclesiastical calculations, as follows.
