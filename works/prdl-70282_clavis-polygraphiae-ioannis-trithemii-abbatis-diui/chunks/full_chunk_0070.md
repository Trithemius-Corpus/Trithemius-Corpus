With the aid of our Lord Jesus Christ, the savior of faithful souls, who lives in the sacred triune unity with the Father and the Holy Spirit, three in persons and one God in substance, incomprehensible, almighty, and everlasting, from whom, in whom, and through whom proceeds every good, beautiful, and true thing, we have brought this second book of the *Polygraphia* to an end. To him be praise, honor, power, and glory: he who, through the innocence of his death, freed our race from the destruction of eternal perdition, and in perpetual mercy reopened the entrance to eternal life to all who believe in him with a pure conscience. Line by line we have set down three hundred and eight paraphrases, avoiding a more lengthy wandering [unclear]. For it seemed enough to us at the beginning of this lucubration if we had given those who wished it a reason to inquire further, since both time and method failed us for those matters. Farewell, friendly reader. From Peapolis, on the penultimate day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord one thousand five hundred and eight.

*The Second Book of the Polygraphia of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot, Ends with its Conclusion.*

*The Third Book of the Polygraphia of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapolis, of the Order of Saint Benedict, Begins.*

Why did [unclear] from the beginning of human creation confuse the unity of language? Was it not the division of bodies and the variety of minds? For as Truth testifies in the Gospel: from the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For the tongue is the instrument for bringing forth outwardly those conceptions which the mind has thought within itself in its inner parts. Yet there are several modes of this ministry of the mind’s secrets to sense, whose properties and use are varied by practice and by art. Practice, introduced at will, is common to many; but art to few. Finally, no one will readily estimate how varied even the very practice of speaking, unregulated, has become among the Germans alone in six hundred years, unless he has more than once reread Otfrid, the monk of Weissenburg, in the poems of the German language. The sea flows and ebbs; men, once dying, do not return again to present life. Customs succeed customs, and the vigor of mortal minds always falls away toward softer things. Who today, among the Germans, the ancient emulators of the Greeks, retains knowledge of the old idioms? In the chronicles and old histories of the Franks, Hunibald, Doracus, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed into their compositions many things said both in old verse and in prose of the German language, which today are understood by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own idiom among the East Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhenish people, Mosellans, people of Cologne, Saxons, and Thuringians undergoes notable variation every day?
