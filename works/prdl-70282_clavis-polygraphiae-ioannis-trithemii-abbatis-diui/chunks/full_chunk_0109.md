But care must be taken that at one time and all at once no more than one word be taken from one alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, not skip over some alphabet and confuse the order of the narrative. By day understand the open narrative, but by night the hidden, secret one, which should not be very long but brief. The examples that follow in the text, concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen, pertain to both narratives, the hidden and the manifest. As often as “the leaf is moved by the wind,” and the rest, we advise that you count the secret narrative with reference to the open one, so that both may end together at the close of each period. But if anything should remain over from the secret matter, let it be enlarged with such moderation that both narratives end at one point, lest a confusion of eclipse be produced. The remaining points that follow in the first lesson of the explanation of the Polygraphy are all implicitly referred to the things we have said. It must also be noted that under the order of the alphabets no word is vacant, but each represents the letter set before it at the head. So that the reader may not lack full knowledge of this matter, let us explain the sense by an example. Let us suppose the case that a worthless man asks, through you, to be advanced with some friend of yours; but you, in order to cut off importunate requests and yet not promote an unworthy man, determine to write more secretly to your friend through the petitioner himself, so that he may not understand: “Beware of that fellow, because he is a thief, a most wicked scoundrel.” Go to the first alphabet and write the word Conditor, which signifies C; in the second, clemens, which [unclear]; in the third, discernens, which represents [unclear]; in the fourth, mundana, signifies e. Now you have, in the hidden narrative, “beware.” Proceed consequently also through the other alphabets, [unclear] such a discourse: “The gracious Creator, discerning worldly things, may he impart seraphic pleasantness to those who ask, with all his saints in heaven, amen. The gentleness of the most glorious prince, everywhere reverently to be remembered by the saints, [unclear] by the best precepts us, most abject, [unclear] of the director of good things, to abandon, to await with every affection the glory of perpetual rest [unclear]. Therefore, O vain mockers, receive with instruction, [so that?] you may be able to enter the most blessed rejoicings of the archangels of the orthodox rewarder, most blessed in comeliness. Therefore flee back from the foulest honors of this pilgrimage, since you are most holily about to enter the most blessed delights of the kindly savior of the ages.” In this foregoing public narrative there is contained this sentence lying hidden in secret, namely: “Beware of that man, because he is a thief and a wicked, most vile fellow; but be silent, I pray.” Proceed in the same order as long as it is in all other cases whatsoever, and one must begin from the head as often as necessary, until you have enclosed the hidden narrative of your mystery openly. But the words that are placed outside the alphabetical order designate no letter, nor do they serve the hidden narrative, but they have been interposed because of a lack of synonyms, so that the sequence of the Latin discourse might be completed. For we were not always able to have so many words with the same signification, especially among pronouns, conjunctions, prepositions, and interjections, that in place of the twenty-four wandering letters we could have made an alphabet, as is clearly evident after the eighth alphabet, where we placed two words outside the order, lest we leave the discourse defective. For thus we placed: “With all.” Thus: “When you live.” Of these the writer may take the two he wishes, so that the Latin discourse may be completed. Let him do likewise in the other cases, whenever he finds similar ones. We have adopted double w on account of our native speech, since we frequently need it in our German language. For he who is ignorant of the Latin language may write his hidden secret in German under a Latin discourse without any difficulty.

*Now follow the rules or cautions to be observed in the operation of this book.*

The first rule or caution warns you that the secret of your mind which you wish to announce to an absent friend, one who knows the art and has the book of Polygraphy, you should write as diligently as you can on a slip of paper, in the Latin language or in German, or in any other language you wish, provided it can be comprised in Roman letters.
