*Explanation of the fourth book of our Polygraphia.*

In the fourth book of the Polygraphia, in similar fashion, we have placed words fabricated by us in alphabetical order, but in one thing alone we deliberately differ from the preceding book. For in the preceding book the words contain no property in themselves other than that they represent the letters set before them at the head. But in the fourth book, which we now have in hand, each word contains its own force, and nevertheless designates the letters placed before it. Within it has what they show without, because the second letter of each word always serves the hidden mystery, which we shall make plain by the following example. Ecorach banasar dulay semalas, chorion suriel anephor schabarbas, busarat uimalo tropon tumelech amasar. For understanding the mystery of this, take only the second letter from each word, and you will find this hidden meaning: Beware this man. For the second letter in Ecorach is c, in banasar a, in dulay u, in semalas e. Join these four letters and they will make this word: Caue. And so one should proceed in the rest up to the end of the book; and if any hidden mystery remains to be written, one should return to the beginning as often as necessary, until the secret has been completed in full. In this very secret manner of writing, if someone has been practiced by long use, he does not need the book, but will easily compose on the spot words suited to his purpose. But one who is less practiced in composition necessarily requires a copy of the book from which he may write. The reader will not need a copy of the volume, since without the book, by means of the second letters everywhere, he can easily find the hidden secret. Yet in great, difficult, or dangerous matters, this manner of writing will not be trusted with sufficient safety, because by the diligence of a skilled man the hidden mystery could be discovered by chance. This manner of writing can also be changed by an intelligent man, so that the signifying letters are placed in the third or fourth position of the word, with the words altered.

In the fifth book of the Polygraphia we have handed down various modes of writing secretly through the transposition of common letters, arranged in such a coordination that under their covering we shall be able safely to write and report everything to an absent friend. Now, to speak in our customary manner, the progression of the transposition of letters is almost infinite, since innumerable things arise from one alphabet by metathesis. First we have described a straight table containing twenty-four alphabets; through understanding it, as many alphabets will be able to be composed as there are stars numbered in the firmament of heaven. For as many letters as there are in that table, so many times, by the art, hundreds of thousands of alphabets arise in order. After this we distribute a reversed table, which will arise into another as often as you change the first letter at the head. The first letter in the straight table is b, in the reversed one z. As often as you put any other varied letter in their place, so often you will find a table new in every respect, and so on to infinity. Then we expand the first straight table, placing at the head of each transposed black letter, in red, the letter that it represents, so that we might provide the reader with an easier method of writing. This is the method of writing: from the first black alphabet take one letter of the hidden sentence, from the second another, from the third a third, and so in succession up to the end.
