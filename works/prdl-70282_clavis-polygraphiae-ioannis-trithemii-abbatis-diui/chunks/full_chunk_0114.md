You now see, reader, what a remarkable transposition of the letters of the alphabet this table produces, since there is no one who, without knowledge of it, could penetrate this secret. For this manner of writing surpasses every common transposition of letters, since each letter is always changed from one series of the alphabet into another. From the reversed table also, which we have set out in order with a similar distribution, let us place such an example by way of introduction: Rdkt, st[unclear]nyb, tevqj, sn[unclear]f, fdrgh, vfd. Its hidden meaning is this: Beware this man, because he is evil. And note that, under the example of the direct table already given, we have drawn the hidden series from the beginning through the whole of it, and then, by continuing in like manner through the reversed table, we again make a circle, as you see, to the beginning of the direct table. Hence it must be considered that one series of writing can be drawn through both tables, just as we have already done in the demonstration of the first example; or, if you prefer, through one only, as we have shown sufficiently in the reversed table. Nor will it cause difficulty for the reader to whom you send your hidden message, for he will easily understand everything if he has had even a little experience in the art. But the strongest instruction, which increases knowledge, is practice frequently repeated in any art.

After the setting out of the tables, certain *orchemata* follow, in which there is not only a transposition of all the letters, but also an ordered leaping of one over another. For *orchema* in Greek is called *transilitio*, “leaping across,” in Latin. And first, indeed, we marked the leap of the letters in the expanded form; afterward we reduced it into an *orchema*. In the operation of this *orchema* there is a remarkable leap. In it there are twenty-four alphabets. In the first no letter intervenes. In the second, one. In the third, two. In the fourth, three. In the fifth, four. In the sixth, five. In the seventh, six. In the eighth, seven. In the ninth, nine. Finally in the eleventh, ten, and so consequently up to the twenty-fourth alphabet, in which twenty-three letters intervene; afterward a return of likeness is made to the beginning. But the manner of writing by *orchemata* for the mystery is similar to that observed in the tables of transposition of letters: namely, that one who is about to write according to the rule of the *orchema* should take from each alphabet only one black letter, which the writing in red shows from the head. For understanding this, let us give an example: Prdh. ifyqyn. bdyen. zhkt. sxsol. inq. Its meaning is this: Beware this man, because he is evil.

When in writing you have come to the end of the *orchemata*, and the begun secret of your heart has not yet been completed, you must know that you are to return to the beginning as often as needed, until the mystery has been completed through everything. And know that from one *orchema* of letters as many alphabets are composed as an inquirer may have wished, however studiously, to have. Finally, this method of writing by *orchemata*, that is, by leapings-across and leaps, is the safest of all, because the transposition of the letters arises not uniform but varied in many ways. And it should be noted that a true and perfect knowledge of the little tables and the *orchemata*, if you are industrious and studious, will give you understanding of all writings that are written in any manner by transposition of letters. And it is a great secret, which few have known, that all meanings hidden in any transposition of letters whatever can be discovered.

*Brief Explanation of the Sixth and Last Book of the Polygraphy.*

In the sixth and last book of the *Polygraphy* we have assembled certain characters of the ancients, which they used in place of letters, so that, if anyone wishes to take from it an alphabet of his own for himself, he may be able to do so, although one moderately skilled may, if he wishes, devise for himself much more beautiful characters. All the synthemata of this book are sufficiently open and clear, and therefore need no explanation, or only a brief one. Yet those which stand forth as newly invented by us require a definite declaration, lest perhaps they seem to have been set down without reason. First of all, we made all the letters of the Latins numerical, after the custom of the Hebrews and the Greeks, thinking it shameful for us if we should be found inferior to them in this respect. For what is it that we have doubled certain letters so that they might suffice for an infinite number, when the Jews, compelled long ago by the same necessity, did this very thing? And the Greeks, because of a lack of letters, admitted certain signs into number, since they could not double letters.
