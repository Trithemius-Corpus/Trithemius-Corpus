*On the signs and wonderful but excessively laborious method of writing of M. T. Cicero, and afterward of Saint Cyprian, bishop and martyr.*

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, wrote a book of signs of no small size, which Saint Cyprian, prelate of the Carthaginians, martyr, enlarged with many signs and words, adding vocabulary necessary for Christian uses, so that the work itself became not only more [unclear], but also [unclear] for the faithful.

The codex is rare, found by me only once, and bought at a cheap price. For in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1496, when for love of books I was inspecting several libraries, I found the aforesaid codex in a certain monastery of our order, neglected through excessive age, cast away under dust and despised. I asked the abbot, a doctor of law, at what value he held it. He replied that he would prefer to it the little works of Saint Anselm, recently printed. I went to the booksellers, since the matter happened in a metropolitan city; I bought the requested little works of Anselm for a sixth part of a florin, gave them to the abbot and the rejoicing monks, and rescued the codex, already brought nearly to destruction. For, out of love of parchment, they had resolved that it should immediately be scraped.

Almost two years after this, when I went up to Strasbourg on business of my order, and was admitted by Johann Geiler of Kaysersberg, the distinguished preacher of the place, into the library of the greater church, I found a whole psalter written out in these same signs of Tullius and Cyprian, most fittingly written in gold at the head. But the title had been placed outside thus by someone ignorant of the mystery: “Psalter in the Armenian language.” I brought in a learned man, showed the falsity, and advised that it be retitled: “Psalter written in Ciceronian signs.” Whether he did so or not I am uncertain, since afterward I did not return to that library. That you may see, reader, the form of Cicero’s notation, we have determined to append a few signs.

You need, indeed, a great memory for the method of writing, and an entirely immense labor of reading, where any character either signifies an entire word, or a final syllable, or some part of speech suitable for the composition of the whole. Therefore, all the signs being omitted, let us draw out an alphabet from a few.

Many other new alphabets could also be formed from Cicero’s signs; we leave the drawing-out of them to the judgment of anyone who wishes to do it. For the book is very full, and contains many signs, notably differing from one another, all of which serve writers.

[Greek-letter alphabet.]

This was the alphabet of Iaimiclis, the great king of the [Articori?], surnamed Megalopius, a very wise man, which he used in secret matters; it too can be changed in many ways. To this, out of love for our prince, we have appended the following, in which, if it is more often used over time, writing will become easier.

*How also by numbers and in many other various ways we can safely write any secrets whatever.*

These things are childish, I admit, since everyone according to his own capacity and judgment can devise more numerous and more elegant alphabets than we have recorded in this volume. Therefore we knowingly omit many things which in a similar way are childish, common, and [unclear].
