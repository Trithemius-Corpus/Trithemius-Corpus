In the sixth book, which is the last book of our Polygraphy, after introducing a mental narration, we have set out in order almost all the minute details of the preceding signs and degrees from the foregoing matters, and with arithmetical distributions we have compressed the functions of the letters into the mystery of a cabalistic operation. One thing lies hidden here in the bark, and another is found in the common operation of the signs. For just as the spirit in the blood gives life to the human body, so the vigor hidden in the art commends the operation. Therefore whoever needs knowledge of this invention should open the eyes of his understanding, so that he may comprehend what is the property of each operation. For we desire all things to be pure for good men in such a way that, if it could be done, they would nevertheless never be made manifest to the wicked. The cause compelled us to fit letters of the Latins to numbers, lest, if we were too obscure, our mind and intention should be clear to no one. Yet that novelty, so that it should not become public without reasonable cause, had to be hidden mystically under unknown signs and marks of numbering. For evening shadows are always stronger by night than by day, and just as night, in its proper order, begins the day, so light shudders at the shadows. What nature denies, no one happily dares. We ask the reader not to condemn this laborious work of ours before he understands it, but, as a judge without rashness, to seek what it is, prudently removing the bark until he comes to the hidden kernel. The work is good and natural, and can be turned to good use or bad, according as the desire of each person’s will moves him. The sword is a good instrument; it is turned to good use whenever it is carried for defense without injury to one’s neighbor. But if it is unlawfully extended to the wounding of anyone, through abuse a good thing is degraded into evil. Therefore, so that the good may have something by which they may prudently provide for their affairs, the Polygraphy is written by us; and lest it furnish the wicked an opportunity for sinning, the kernel is hidden in the bark under various enigmas. He who is good does not misuse good for evil, but rather, if he can, turns evil into good.

*What the three causes of this work of Polygraphy are, and at the same time its advantages.*

We have undertaken the labor of the present work, both rare and difficult, for no other reason than to provide for the usefulness of those in need. I mean those in need not of food, not of drink, but of help in preserving secrets without danger. We therefore judge that it will be opportune if we briefly note the advantages and conveniences that can come to those who use these inventions of ours. For since in our household affairs we have no business such that we need these teachings for concealing secrets, we have labored assuredly for those whose empire had to be defended. The first advantage is that, when understanding of this work has been completed, it gives the needy man an easy way by which, through the skill of the art, you will make known the secret of your mind safely, secretly, and without any suspicion on the part of any observers, to one who knows, however widely the work may extend to any distances of places by regulated speech. What lies hidden in these things, the work of Steganography brings out; it requires neither letters nor a messenger to carry the secrets to the reader. It would be otherwise in matters admitting the light, according to the common custom of all our people.
