With the sheet written in this way, place it before you, so that for the individual letters of the secret you may suitably fit a word. For, as we have shown, in this practice of writing a whole word is taken. We gave an example a little earlier in the foregoing.

*II. The second caution* directs you that, as has been said, you should not take more than one word at a time from any alphabet in one narrative of public Latin speech; otherwise you would both confuse the open meaning and wholly draw away from understanding of the mystery the friend to whom you had written.

*III. Thirdly,* take the greatest care that through negligence you do not pass over any alphabet from which you do not take one word appropriate to the secret. Likewise provision must be made that whenever one word or more occurs outside the sequence of the alphabet, you take the whole upper line, or the lower, and not both.

*IV. Fourthly,* care must be taken that whenever the discourse of the open speech has been brought to a complete sentence, the following sentence should take its beginning from a larger, thickened letter, according to the form of the preceding example, in which, according to the sense of the Latin discourse, we took capital letters at the beginning and small letters in the rest, in the common manner. When the secret of your mind is long, so that it exceeds the narrative of the open speech, if much remains you should begin again from the beginning; if little remains, the discourse may be brought back at pleasure from any place whatever. For no one even moderately skilled in this craft will be able not to know where you have begun again. Nor is there need to fear the harmony of the speech, since it will always be varied by the manifest narrative together with the hidden one; whenever a beginning is made from the start, a new face of the discourse will follow. A continuation can also be made from the end of the first book through the second.

*V. Fifthly,* we advise that whenever you wish to write in earnest by this art, you prepare for yourself a small book in which you write your secret mystery in manifest discourse. Whoever sees or reads this little book will not think that any secret lies hidden, nor, if he has a suspicion, will he be able to find anything of what you write.

*VI. Sixthly,* it should be noted that if, throughout the whole *Polygraphia*, you transpose the order of the words in each alphabet by changing the arrangement, and if you write two volumes according to it, one for yourself and the other for any friend, you will write between yourselves, with complete security, whatever you wish, nor is there any man living on earth who could penetrate your secrets. This transposition, however, can be made in infinite ways.

*VII. Seventhly,* we forewarn you that one must write polygraphically in one way to a man ignorant of the Latin language, and in another way to one skilled in it. For the former all words must be written out in full, without abbreviation, distinctly, completely, beautifully, and altogether legibly, without separation, without division, without confusion, lest, deceived by error, he think one word to be two, or suppose two to be one. The things that are set down must be heeded.

*VIII. Eighthly,* provision must be made that the person to whom you write mystically by this art should have a book wholly like yours; otherwise you would write in vain and fruitlessly to one who does not understand.

*IX. Ninthly,* if you wish this craft to remain forever secret and hidden, write out the *Polygraphia* anew, but do not place the letters of the alphabet before the words; no one will easily suspect the method of the secret. But so that no difficulty in the mystery may occur to the writer or reader, let the letters of the alphabet be written on a small sheet in the order corresponding to the words, and let it be fitted movably to the headings of the same words. The letters can also be engraved on a copper plate, so that they may be moved from one order to another.

*X. Tenthly,* it should be known that although we do not use the Pythagorean y in German and Latin words, nevertheless we have admitted it on account of the Greek language, so that we might have the twelvefold number doubled. Likewise we have received the doubled w as the twenty-fourth letter, which for us is altogether necessary in the German language.

*XI. Eleventhly,* let the person who receives letters written from the *Polygraphia* to be read know that intelligence is needed in the reading. And therefore, whenever he takes up a sheet or little book written polygraphically, let him go to a secret place, and, opening the book of the *Polygraphia* which he must have with him, let him consider each word through all the alphabets, and what letter each one represents; with chalk upon a table, or with ink on paper, let him note it very carefully. And when he has examined the whole letter in that way, let him put the letters together into words, and he will easily understand what lay hidden in secret.
