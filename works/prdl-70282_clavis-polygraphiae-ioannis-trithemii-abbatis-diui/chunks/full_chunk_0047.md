*The second book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Peapolis, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

The human method of numbering attempts by art to imitate nature, which admits no reckoning of the monad. Therefore, although the instruction of the preceding book of Polygraphy, through extension and the metathesis of particulars, suffices, as it were, for the universals of this art in infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a definite consideration has moved us to join a second to the first.

There is in each a different condition of light, but night in both preserves inviolate the order of its course. The reader who is prudent, from our small discoveries, not polished quite to the rule, will easily be able, if he is strong in excellent talent, to discover greater and more polished things. Whatever visible thing is placed in the whole circle of the world still offers ready support to the ministry of our new art. Air, fire, water, earth, springs, rivers, trees, thickets, flowers, grasses, words, names, accident, substance, stones, sand, dung, filth, things, terms, honorable things, modest things, woods, metals, hard things, liquid things, existing things, non-existing things, history, fable, beasts, brute animals, human beings, draught-animals, each and all things that speech can express or the mind can think, provide the very broad field of our Polygraphy; they promise that the art, insofar as it can stand among mortals, is to be enlarged to infinity.

For just as unity, through the binary, generates the ternary as a perfect number in the quaternary, so the completion of this discovery, establishing its root through the quaternary in the twice-perfect binary, gives us an infinite number under the minute particulars of things and of the paraphrases of Polygraphy. For although among us all our things are terminable, yet very many things often occur which, because of the slowness of our understanding, seem infinite to us. Who will be able to count the drops of the sea? And who will penetrate the inscrutable heart of man? Just as a finite and circumscribable number can, by progression, be led into infinity, so the instruction of this art is enlarged into immensity by varied metathesis. Whatever is seen anywhere in the whole world is in all respects conformed to our instruction, nor is there anything known to us that is without mystery, if the method is understood by a master.

We, however, persuaded by reason, remain under the ministry of the binary, and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor is an occasion given us to raise our undertaking to weightier matters; if such an acceptable occasion is offered to us, we shall not refuse to make more excellent things manifest to those who desire them. For it is written: Where there is no hearing, do not ever pour out speech. Therefore, adding the second book of Polygraphy to the first, we keep the same form and method, placing three hundred and eight paraphrases in the light of day by simple progression, in honorable memory of the most chaste mother of God; if the time had been suitable, we could have given both more graceful things and, with sufficient expansive diffusion, broader things. But studious posterity will easily provide both: it will add brilliance to the matter and sufficient progress to the form. The first inventor of any art transmits greater things to posterity, because, since the beginnings of an art are hard and unfamiliar, no subtle discovery is found consummate from its origin.

From Peapolis, which is also Herbipolis, a city of the Eastern Franks, on the twelfth day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord 1508.
