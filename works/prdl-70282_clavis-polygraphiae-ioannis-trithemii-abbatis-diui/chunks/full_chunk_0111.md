Thirteenth, it should be noted that, to stop the mouths of those who speak unjustly, we show the truth of this new invention of ours to be possible by placing the words in the alphabets of the first book so that they cohere with one another, and in the manner of an exhortation and supplication offered to God they produce a fitting and Latin prayer. Under its narrative we can conceal whatever secrets occur to us, and write in such a way to a friend skilled in the art that, if all the men in the whole world were to come together into one place, they would never be able to find the secret lying hidden beneath the open discourse, provided it has been arranged correctly.

Fourteenth, it should be known that in this very invention of our Polygraphy there still lie hidden certain things far better than those written above, namely how you will be able, in a few days, not to say hours, to instruct one ignorant of the Latin language so that he knows how to write, read, speak, and understand Latin, not indeed all things universally, but in a certain manner whatever necessary thing may occur to him on any subject in his affairs. Likewise, how you may make known to your friend far absent, and knowing the practice of the art, all the conceptions of your mind safely, securely, and most secretly, without writings and without the messenger’s knowledge, sometimes even naturally without a messenger and without any superstition, danger, or fear. But reason has moved us that these things remain buried, hidden with the rest in their own concealment, since it neither pleases me nor is at all expedient to cast precious stones, once found, away, and to accomplish difficult things without the fruit of gratitude. Finally, there lie hidden beneath these no small mysteries of subtlety concerning medicines, herbs, plants, precious stones, rivers, the members of the human body, brute animals, saints, nations, cosmography, astronomy and the seven liberal arts, also concerning all these things that are held in human use, and concerning the universal world, both of visible and invisible things. And lest we be more lengthy than is right: all things that are in the world, through the enlargement of demonstrated likeness, serve the mystical teaching of the art of Polygraphy.

Fifteenth, note that all these things which we have transmitted as discovered in the Polygraphy are certain small introductions in comparison with those which still remain to be discovered and written in this same faculty; concerning these we pass over in silence, lest we seem to boast vainly. For we have given to those endowed with talent an occasion for discovering greater things than we have either written or perhaps have been able to discover. Let him who can add, for the completion of this invention, the things which he knows to be useful; but let him who cannot do so not rashly judge things he does not understand.

*An explanation of those things which seemed rather obscure in the second book of the Polygraphy.*

In the second book of the Polygraphy, apart from the order and sequence of the words, nothing new is handed down from the first, and for that reason there will be one and the same method of working in both. All the things which we said in the first book had to be observed, we carefully advise must also be observed in the second. Only in the narrative of the prayer is a manifest difference seen to have arisen, since the former is composed concerning our Lord, the Son of God, whereas this one is raised to his mother, the ever-untouched Virgin. In the second book, moreover, three hundred and eight alphabets are set in order before the words, under different subjects. For from the beginning the narrative of the discourse is directed to that Lady of ours; afterwards the Lord’s Prayer is expanded under twenty-four alphabets; then the angelic salutation is appended; consequently the Salve Regina; and last a brief prayer concerning Saint Anne, mother of the Virgin Mary, parent of God. In all books of the Polygraphy one must beware lest a single word be duplicated in any alphabetical order, that is, lest it be placed twice, or lest any be omitted, because it would make great confusion for the reader. Therefore one must take the greatest care that the copies be in every respect emended and corrected exactly, lest you fall into error while writing. But if, through the negligence of the scribe or the carelessness of the printer, you find some word duplicated under one alphabet, erase the last one and put in its place another suitable word which has not already been in the same alphabet.
