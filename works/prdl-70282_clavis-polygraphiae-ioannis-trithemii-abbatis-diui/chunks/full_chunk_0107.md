With these notes or characters we once saw an entire Latin psalter, beautifully written, in the library of the greater church of Strasbourg; to which some judge of matters, ignorant, had prefixed on the outside a title such as this: "A Psalter in the Armenian tongue." But he erred, since it ought rather to have been written thus: "A Latin Psalter written in Tullian [Tironian] characters." For each character represents one entire Latin word. Nor does the estimation deceive me, since I have by me, written whole in an old volume, the very dictionary of Cicero, emended by Saint Cyprian—incorrupt—which I have nowhere seen elsewhere. And so great is the abundance of notes or characters, with the words subjoined to them, that they altogether suffice for writing in the Latin tongue whatever you wish.

*Mystery.* Pharamund, the forty-third [king] from Marcomer, king of the Franks, when in the year of the Lord's nativity 424 he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, etc.
*Explanation.* The kings of the Franks, from the time when they went out from the Scythian borders, in the year 430 before Christ's nativity, with their people to the mouths of the Rhine—where now Guelderland sits—waged continual war with the Gauls for nearly nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject [to themselves], as we wrote in the first volume of our annals. Of these [kings], the aforesaid king Pharamund, that he might safely send messengers through the kingdom, devised new letters proper to himself, which no one could understand without his voluntary instruction. And he used not one alphabet only, nor few, but many new alphabets.
*Mystery.* Clodius the Long-haired, son of Pharamund, and after him king of the Franks, who subjugated all Gaul to himself, the Romans being destroyed whom he found in it, used [the cipher-arts] of his father's invention in secret writings.
*Explanation.* This king devised proper and new alphabets, and gave single [ones] to each of his dukes, and retained the knowledge with himself alone.
*Mystery.* Charles by name the Great, the sixty-third king of the Franks, who first of the Franks bore the diadem of the Roman empire, etc.
*Explanation.* He is read to have been born the elder son of king Pippin, duke of Brabant, in the county of Namur, bordering on the Liège-men—although some report him born in the village of Ingelheim, not far from Mainz—[and] he too invented various modes of writing, and, Nanno the grammarian ministering [his] support, devised diverse alphabets, by which he communicated secrets, each in a singular manner, to the several prefects of the kingdom.
*Mystery.* The Normans, once gone out from the borders of Gothia, when they devastated Gaul with cruel invasion, having called owls from Greece, prepared a safe hiding-place for their secrets, etc.
*Explanation.* The Normans, ranging through Gaul (Bede the monk in England testifying), that they might safely write their secrets among themselves, substituted Greek numeral [letters] for the Latin letters, from a, the first, up to the four-and-twentieth, [with] a point such as w or gu, according to the custom of the Gauls. But this mode of writing is altogether most beautiful, in which all the letters are Greek, and according to the Pelasgian order of numbering designate our Latin [letters]: as a = 1, b = 2, c = 3, d = 4, e = 5, f = 6, g = 7, and so consequently up to w, which is 24. This writing neither a Greek will understand, nor a Latin, unless he has first learned the manner of the industrious art.
*Mystery.* Our Bede, a monk in England, not ignorant of what the Normans did, described many [things] like these of the ancients' inventions, by which they made an obscure way lucid through mysteries. He himself [described] arthrology [finger-counting], etc.
*Explanation.* This Bede the monk, in his little book on the inventions of the ancients, recounted many various modes of secretly writing and noting, by which they safely announced their secrets to friends, and spoke without voice to those present. He first of all committed arthrology [and] dactylology [finger-speech] to letters, by the use of which the one most securely discloses to the other whatever secret he wishes—which no one else standing by will note, and much less understand. That this very [thing] can be done in many other ways, Bede himself taught; concerning which, since we have written nothing in the Polygraphy, we here deservedly refuse to say more. A proper place for these very arduous matters we long ago assigned in the Steganography, which we do not yet think to publish. And who could express all the modes of the ancients' secret writing? For some had as familiars, in the ministry of the aforesaid art, "owls" [bubones] called Philophoti [light-lovers], others Mysophoti [light-haters], others called both into practice by [the] art; again others transposed the known letters of the common alphabet; others, under the guise of barbarians, devised proper characters for themselves.
