Having returned after these things to Gaul, he repaid us evil for good, and shamefully violated the covenant of Christian friendship. For when he was asked by Lord Germain de Ganay, a common friend, later bishop of Orléans, what he had found with me and seen at Sponheim, he wrote back falsehoods for truths and lies for benefits, condemning and rashly judging things which he was in no way able to comprehend with his understanding. For since he believed that the palm of learning would be conferred upon him by envy and lying, he wrote back to the aforementioned most learned man a letter full of falsehood, lies, wrongs, and many insults; in it, making mention of a steganography he had not understood, he proclaims me, by false, lying, and exceedingly injurious rash presumption, devoted to wicked arts, a magician and necromancer. To his most mendacious injuries and blasphemies, by God’s mercy, I shall shortly reply in such a way that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bovillus is impious, cruel, rash, and in this matter a liar. I steadfastly affirm, I truthfully declare, and I confidently swear upon my soul that I have never had any commerce with demons, or with wicked or pernicious magical or necromantic arts; but that each and every thing which I have either written or promised that I would write is pure, sound, natural, and in no respect whatever contrary to the Christian faith. Hence I do not fear to submit my promises for examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as there shall be need; and I by no means intend to resist their determination. That I have inserted this history of Bovillus’s rashness into this preface of mine had a reasonable cause: namely, that all may understand that the work of steganography, which until now remains buried in darkness, was done with mature counsel; not because I fear the rash judgments of Bovillus, but lest I seem to give more such persons cause for suspecting similar things about me. Indeed, so far as concerns me, I would rather appease my rivals by silence than irritate them by letters, or provoke them to madness by writings. For I judged it better that all the things wonderfully discovered should remain in perpetual silence than that I should incur, even through men’s false estimation, the mark of pernicious magical or necromantic superstition. Let steganography therefore lie hidden in darkness; and let it not be made common to the lying Bovillian company, which is accustomed to judge concerning unknown matters and to corrupt the reputation of a good man by the mere lust of depravity. Nevertheless, since friends urge it, so that together with Bovillus our rivals may understand how rash and lying an injury he has done us, while steganography remains within its own bounds, we consent to bring into the light the following work, which we call Polygraphy, divided into six books. Let those who wish read it, and let friends read this volume without ill temper; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves be judges, without envy, whether they are natural or superstitious.
