At the urging of the aforesaid prince and lord elector, Philip, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, I, Johannes Trithemius, now abbot of the monastery of Saint James at Würzburg, but then of Saint Martin the bishop in Sponheim, in the diocese of Mainz, began to write eight books of mysteries in steganography. But while I was already continuing my night-work on the third book of that same work, there occurred a matter which, briefly if I can, I am about to relate.

In the year of the Lord’s birth 1499, in the second Roman indiction, when Arnold Bostius, a Carmelite of the town of Ghent, asked me by letter what new thing I was publishing and with what studies I was occupied, on the second day of Palm week I wrote back to him through merchants, as to a friend, that I had under the hammer a new and wondrous work of steganography, setting out in order what was contained in each of the books. But before my letter reached Ghent, Bostius departed this life to Christ during the Easter days. The prior of that place received the letter, opened it, read it, and, astonished, as I suppose, by the novelty of what was promised, shared it with many others who asked to read and copy it. From this it came about that this letter of mine, in a short time made known through almost all Germany and Gaul, and often copied by many, turned many very learned men also to amazement and wonder, to such a degree that they hesitated what, consistently with truth, they ought to think of me. Among these there were some who steadfastly asserted that my promises in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, shamelessly devised by me out of empty desire for glory. Others said: “This abbot Trithemius promises great and wondrous things; if he can do them, he can do so only by the ministry of demons, since they seem to far exceed the limits of nature. But if he cannot, who will doubt that he is a liar, deservedly to be rejected by all good and learned men?” But let both be far from me, since I promised natural things. Yet of all those who rashly judged my unknown matters, the most rash, the most unjust assessor of things, at last burst forth in public: Charles de Bovelles, a Picard by nation. After he had travelled through the upper parts of Alsace and had come to me at Sponheim for the sake of lodging, intending to return to Gaul by way of Trier, I treated him with such courtesy as I could and, for as long as he was with me, showed him all the benefits of hospitality with a cheerful countenance. And since, as commonly happens among friends, especially those devoted to the study of letters, I had shown all my things for my most welcome guest to see, among other things the aforesaid work of steganography, not yet at that time completed, also presented itself. Bovelles saw it, read it cursorily, and, his mind occupied elsewhere, said that he admired the invention and praised it; nor did he take care how it might be understood. Hence, since he did not seek the key to its understanding, he deserved to hear or perceive none of the things that were contained in that night-work of ours.
