Thereafter, for nine hundred and fifty years, waging almost continual war with the Romans and Gauls, as Hunibald is the authority, they finally subjected almost all Europe to their command or kingdom. Yet, once they had received the letters of the Romans, they did not destroy them, but rather, being themselves mingled among the Germans and Gauls, they learned their language, and preserved their letters and the form of their writings. Vuasthaldus, a writer of the histories of this people, wrote books in his native speech on the deeds of kings, dukes, and princes over nearly seven hundred and fifty-eight years, with the characters appended, as Hunibald says, which he mentions in his volume that he published in eighteen books on the origin of the Franks.

Unless the fault of the scribe has deceived me, I have, as I think, suitably reproduced the alphabet of Wasthaldus; in it some characters differ entirely from the Greek, while some also seem partly to resemble them. Yet I do not know what I should judge: whoever their inventor may have been, although he did not preserve the form of the Greek letters, he nevertheless observed the order of the letters. The codex, falling apart from excessive age, could scarcely be read; hence I fear that I have rather been deceived by some interpolator than found the true and certain opinion of Hunibald.

But let us pass on to the things that remain, since if Hunibald's statement is true, it will have been well written by us; but if it is someone's fiction, it will neither harm us nor do injury to the reader. Another fictitious alphabet follows, found in the codex of Hunibald, to which this was inscribed:

*The alphabet of the Franks, which Doracus invented.*

Whoever wishes to use the characters written above should know that they are to be written beneath the lines, in the manner of the Greeks, not above as the Latins do. Likewise it must be observed that, of the double letters, the first are written at the beginning and in the middle of words, but the second always at the end.

*From the grammarians of Otfridus, monk of Wissembourg.*

We have read that Charles, king of the Franks, surnamed the Great, taking pity on the barbarism of his nation, with Nanno, Theobald, Albinus, and Berenger as helpers, attempted to bring the German language under rule. And although, as Turpin bears witness, he had begun so arduous a work, he did not complete it, being delayed by frequent incursions of wars, but, overtaken by death, left it unfinished; nor did another arise after him who would recall concern for this matter to his mind. But Otfridus, the aforesaid monk, formerly hearer and disciple of Rabanus Fuldaensis, under Louis the Pious, son of that Charles, having mastered that grammar, albeit imperfectly, wrote many things in our German language, which seem to sound with a remarkable weight of regular instruction, in meter both grave and elegiac; moreover he also composed some things in prose to Louis, king of Germany, son of Louis the Pious and brother of the emperor Lothar; also to Orgarius, archbishop of Mainz, to the monks of Saint Gall, and to others. Having obtained fragments of this grammar, I too have extracted the present characters of the alphabet from many, lest they utterly perish. Moreover it is established, on Otfridus' testimony, that Charles devised several alphabets of his own, by which he could safely use his very broad kingdom in secret matters with the individual prefects. From these, with Otfridus supplying them, we commit a few things to these letters.
