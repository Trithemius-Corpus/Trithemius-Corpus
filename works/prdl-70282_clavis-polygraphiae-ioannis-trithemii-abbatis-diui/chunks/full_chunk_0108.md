The methods of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many, and altogether innumerable; to make mention of them all is neither our wish nor within our power. Of invisible characters, however, there are three kinds more commonly used among mortals today. The first is called Dermatic, by which a certain artificial writing is made on the backs of messengers, visible only by the washing of a certain fluid; no one can penetrate it who does not know the secret of the art. The second is called Hyphasmatic, by which one writes on cloth with certain substances; when dried, it cannot be seen at all until, moistened in cold water, it is perceived. The third kind is named Alleotic; it is manifold and varied, both in form and in material, and has almost innumerable methods of writing secretly. Its writing is made on paper or without paper, with [unclear], with [unclear], and with various other things. Letters likewise are written in various ways and hidden in places, as under [unclear], under [unclear], in [unclear], in [unclear], in [unclear], [unclear], in [unclear], in [unclear], and in other almost infinite things of the same sort. But because none of these things at this time serves our purpose, we must therefore hasten to those things which reason requires. There is another kind, Dactiletron, not of writing, however, but of signs, by which, in very subtle ways, one can communicate to another the conception of his mind. This itself is varied by so many differences that there is no one in the world who could enumerate all the species of so great a mystery. By the principal method, together with many others descending from it, one may, by art, while sitting at table or in any other place, securely and most safely communicate whatever one wishes to a person who knows the art, without anyone’s suspicion. Finally, how without letters you may disclose the secret of your mind to one absent, even when the very person who performs the office of messenger does not know it, remains written among the inventions of steganography. For indeed, since kings and princes encounter many affairs, knowledge of which they sometimes do not trust to give safely enough by customary letters to their deputies far away, it will be worthwhile for each of them to have some special methods of writing and announcing, by which they may most safely make their secrets known to friends, especially in these times, in which, with license to sin prevailing, nowhere in the world is secure faith found.

Messengers of princes are seized everywhere by savage robbers; they are plundered, beaten, and for the most part killed. Nor do the impious robbers spare even the letters of kings, closed and sealed with rings. Therefore it would be necessary for your commonwealth, O most glorious Emperor Maximilian, that you put on the skin of Hercules and brandish the club in your hands, and purge the world committed to you by God of these savage monsters. There will never be peace in the kingdom unless the [unclear] are removed from the midst. He who disturbs the peace of the kingdom shows himself to be the king’s enemy. These are the robbers who beset the roads of travelers and seize others’ property; the earth is full of them today, and nowhere is the road safe for those who walk.

*Brief Explanation of Those Things Which Seem More Obscure in the First Book of the Polygraphia*

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphia, nevertheless, lest its composition perhaps seem profound to simpler readers, we briefly explain those things which are thought more difficult. We have distributed the words of the Latin language, in order, more than nine thousand of them, under almost four hundred alphabets, in such a sequence that if from each alphabet only one word after another is taken from the beginning to the end, cohering mutually with one another they will produce a fitting Latin discourse. For it will be a very suitable method of writing most secretly whatever we wish, since each word represents the letter placed before it at the head. Nor should it trouble you, reader, that a multitude of words in the open contains a brief meaning in secret, since in this childish arrangement of ours there lies hidden a higher method of writing whatever you wish, much more broadly and much more secretly, which public reason has forbidden us to make plain to all. But whoever is endowed with talent and applies the necessary powers to inquiry will find, hidden in these teachings, subtler things wonderfully unknown before; he will plainly understand wonders. Nevertheless this suitable method of writing will suffice for all things, since the narration, though repeated so many times, is varied.
