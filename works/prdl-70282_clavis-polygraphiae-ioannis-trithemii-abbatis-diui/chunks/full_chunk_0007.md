*Preface of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James of Würzburg, in the Polygraphia to Emperor Maximilian.*

We have read that many wise men of the ancients, philosophers, kings, and princes once devised various and manifold methods by which, when sending their messengers to more distant places, they might safely entrust to them the secrets of their counsel, and whatever secret mystery happened to need to be confided: so that they might become fit for carrying these things through, and safe from every attack of wicked men, lest either the simplicity of the cabalists should lay bare the mystery, or the inborn curiosity of the [bacucei] should bring harm upon apostolic matters.

Great among these, Caesar Augustus of the Romans, desiring in difficult matters to use the dark service of messengers, changed the breathings of the conceived utterance from clear forms into obscure ones; and by the [scheme?] of transposition, with a fine invention, he made their barbarousness, though resisting his wishes, conform to his nod. And this receptive and almost perpetual order, nevertheless subject to will and to the reckoning of time, although it keeps messengers safe on the road from the attack of the [bacucei], yet, by not keeping the regulated limits of its own system, will make all who look on anxious with suspicion of the matter.

That famous Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the chief of mathematicians, took a white bird suited to his arrangements, bare as to head and feet, and fastened it round about to a staff cut into the form of a tetragon; and, after his assistants had been set in due proportion, he entrusted the work to the winged instrument, until the image of the white bird appeared changed into a magpie. When this had been duly performed, he loosened what had been fastened with his own hand; and when he had allowed it to fly away, at once a great separation of appearance took place among the intimates of the inner chambers; and soon there arose the too-vagrant monster of an image, previously monastic and twisted, at the sight of which no one could think what it was. Nor did the disagreement of the owls come to rest before the image of the master had skilfully bound the magpie again with a similar tetragon.

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator, using talent and art, changed the small elements of phrasing into signs, and placed the industry, or character, of his will in the place of the customary phrase. In this very innovation, indeed, he used such abundance that his invention could suffice for nearly all things in the world. To these fore-announcers of mysteries the discipline of the art gave a wondrous agility, so that, contrary to the nature of all solid things, they use the steps of feet; and they serve the good will of the master not by small divisions or moments, but by whole degrees. A king can always use their obedience very safely, though not without great labor and the manifest suspicion of the [bacacei].

But Cyprian, the most holy bishop and martyr of the church of Carthage, wishing to provide for the advantage of Christians, enlarged to no small extent the very teachings of the Ciceronian system, and supplied what had been lacking for our customs.
