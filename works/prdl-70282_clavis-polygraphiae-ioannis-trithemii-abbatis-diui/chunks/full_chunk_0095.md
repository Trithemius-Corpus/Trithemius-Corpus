They also began to sink more deeply into the pleasures of the flesh, and, taking beautiful wives, with many children begotten from them, they fell notably away from natural simplicity. They invented games and songs; they began to play the cithara; whatever they maintained pertained to the influence of Venus, they devised with greater zeal, following too much the motion of the flesh.

They called the third intelligence of Jupiter Zachariel; receiving the dominion of the world after Anael, it made men restless with desire for rule, to go hunting, to make tents, to adorn themselves with various garments. While he held dominion, in accordance with the quality of Jupiter, who deprived his father of the kingdom, there arose a great division between the good and the wicked: the former invoking God, the latter given over to the flesh and the world. Under that rule Adam, the first man, died, leaving to all posterity the testament of dying once.

But the fourth mover, or angel, of Mercury, Raphael, influencing worldly things after Zachariel, began in the one thousand fifty-fifth year of the world, at the end of the month of February, and lasted until the one thousand four hundred tenth year of the world; under him the use of letters is said first to have been invented. The letters then, however, as they report, were different from those which the world has today, devised in the form and likeness of trees, plants, and animals; yet with time these were afterward altered in many ways, and took on an easier and more cultivated manner of being formed. For the fictions of the poets said that Mercury was the patron of all literature, promising that great hidden mysteries of things lay beneath their fables.

After this, when the human race had multiplied and had been spread everywhere over the whole earth, the unity of letters did not long remain; but the separation of bodies and minds far from one another discovered different and various modes of writing, concerning which a long narration would be needed from us. Plato the philosopher, in the Phaedrus, relates that a certain philosopher named Theotones first invented letters and the use of writing, together with the system of weights, numbers, and the game of dice, and taught Thamus, king of the Egyptians.

Concerning the rest, however, and the ancient men who are said to have handed down special letters to almost individual nations and peoples, which remain in use among them down to the present day, it is not my purpose on this occasion to set out or describe a narrative. For all our learned men know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said first to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: αβγδεζθκλμνοπρστυφψχω. [unclear] ε, κ, ω, λ, μ, β, ζ, σ, τ, ψ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is reported to have added four: [unclear], θ, φ, χ. And afterward Pythagoras added the letter v, so that there should be twenty-one; and κ and ϖ were subsequently invented by others. Gordanis, bishop of the Goths, gave his people letters proper to them. When the unity of language had been confounded, the simplicity of characters was also varied in many ways. From this it happened that the use of letters, in the course of time, claimed such license for itself that not only individual peoples, but also individual men, fashioning new characters for letters at their own judgment, prepared hiding places for their mysteries.

Yet every writing in unusual characters will not be free from suspicion on the part of those who see it. Notwithstanding this, many kings, tyrants, and princes of old devised for themselves a private mode of writing, separated from the common one, caring little for the suspicion of others, provided that what they wished to remain secret was not perceived. Moved by imitation of them, and wishing to bring to a perfect end this work of the Polygraphia, so far as can be done, in this sixth book, which is also the last, I have determined to call back from the dead certain characters of the ancients which have long since ceased from mortal use, lest they perish utterly. And first indeed we shall set down the ancient manner of writing of the Northmen.
