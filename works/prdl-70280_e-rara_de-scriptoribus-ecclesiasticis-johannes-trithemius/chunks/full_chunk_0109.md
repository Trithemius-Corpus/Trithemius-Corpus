He flourished under King Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Lanfranc, archbishop of the church of Canterbury in England, formerly abbot of the monastery of Caen, of the order of Saint Benedict, an Italian by nation from the city of Pavia, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, especially in dialectic, in the opinion of all the most learned man of his time, renowned no less for holiness than for knowledge, left excellent monuments of his talent. Of these I have found only the following:

On all the epistles of Paul the Apostle: fourteen books, [unclear].
Against Berengar: one book. “Lanfranc, by the mercy of God, [unclear].”
The Deeds of Count William: one book.

Of the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Peter Damian, formerly a monk of the divine father Benedict, bishop of Ostia and cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, was a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, gentle and acute in intellect, calm and composed in speech, quite fit for persuasion; he shone forth as distinguished and renowned no less for holiness than for learning. Following the example of the great bishop Gregory, out of love for solitude and quiet he left his episcopate, after he had governed it vigorously for some years, and returned to his monastery; thereafter he spent his life with his brothers in tranquility of mind. But when he was reproved by the supreme pontiff with a charitable rebuke as to why, being a useful and suitable man, he had abandoned the episcopate out of love for private salvation, he published apologetic writings addressed to the three Roman pontiffs succeeding one another: Nicholas II, Alexander II, and Gregory VII. In these, humbly excusing his deed by authority, reason, and example, he calmed their minds.
