*On Ecclesiastical Writers, fol. 46*

Constantinus, a monk of the monastery of Cassino, of the order of Saint Benedict, African by nation, a man very devoted to the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, especially in philosophy and medicine, most learned, fully furnished with Greek, Latin, and Arabic eloquence, wrote many little works on medical matters, which are held in esteem among physicians. He also translated many volumes of the ancient physicians from Greek and Arabic into Latin, which he prefaced with his own prefaces and letters. Of his little works:

Universal Diets, five books: “What first compelled ...”
Particular Diets, three books: “We have completed in the first book ...”
On Medicinal Weights, one book: “Medicinal weights ...”
On Urines also, one book: “I have seen that [unclear] ...”

As for the rest of his volumes, which he wrote and translated in great number, I do not recall that any have come into our hands. They are better known to physicians.

Ambrosius Ansbertus, monk and priest of the order of Saint Benedict, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, gentle in talent and sweet in speech, produced many commentaries on the sacred books, by which he transmitted the fame of his name to posterity. Yet few have come to my knowledge. For I have found only the following:

On the Song of Songs, one book: “The gift of wisdom with Solomon ...”
On the Psalter also, one book.
On the Apocalypse of John, ten books.
On Cupidity, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.

He flourished under Emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 890.

Paschasius Ratbertus, abbot of Corbie, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, nobly furnished with Greek and Latin eloquence, sharp in intellect and orderly in speech, wrote not a few little works in verse and prose. Of these I have found only the following: to Placidus, abbot of our order, a distinguished work:

On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book: “Whoever among Catholics rightly ...”
Life of Saint Adalhard, bishop, one book.
On the Lamentations of Jeremiah the prophet, one book.

He also translated some things from Greek into Latin and composed many others. He flourished in the times of Emperor Charles the Fat, in the year of the Lord 880.

Ericus, monk of the order of Saint Benedict, of the monastery of the city of Auxerre, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and very learned in the disciplines of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, was an outstanding teacher in homilies delivered to the people. He wrote lucidly in meter:

Life of Germanus of Auxerre, six books.
Homilies to the people, one book.

Several sermons and letters. He also composed many other things in both styles, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished around the times of Emperor Charles the Fat, in the year 880.

Otfridus, monk of the monastery of Weissenburg, of the order of the divine father Benedict, German by nation, formerly a hearer and disciple of Rabanus Maurus, abbot of Fulda, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and exceptionally learned in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, and distinguished poet, excellent in talent and eloquent, transmitted [unclear]. Following the example of the emperor Charlemagne, he also endeavored to reduce the barbarism of the German language to grammatical rules, which he accomplished in part. Hence those things which he wrote in his native speech cannot easily in our age be read and understood, even by a man however skilled in the German language. Of his little works the following are extant. To Luitbert, the eighth archbishop of the church of Mainz, on the Gospel, a great and distinguished work, which he entitled:

The Grace of Theotista, five books: “The summit of dignity ...”
