Gregory, the third pope of that name, presided over the Roman and universal Church after Gregory II for eleven years: a pious, merciful, and most holy man, and very learned both in secular letters and in divine Scripture; distinguished for Greek and Latin eloquence. In a synod held at Rome he excommunicated the emperors Leo and Constantine, who despised the images of the saints, and deposed them from imperial rule. He also wrote, among other things, to the same emperors:

A *Commonitorium*, one book.
*To the Same*, one book.
*Against the Same*, one book.
*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.
And certain other works.

He died under the emperors Leo and Constantine, in the year of the Lord 742, in the tenth indiction, on the fourth day before the Kalends of December.

John, monk and priest, surnamed Chrysoras, by nation a Damascene from Syria: a man learned in divine Scripture and in secular letters, and no less renowned for his life than for his knowledge. Stirred and provoked by the writings of Pope Gregory III, with which he attacked the error of Emperor Leo, he too wrote in Greek, among his own people:

*Against Emperor Leo*, two books, in the Greek language.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 730.

Willibald, first bishop of Eichstätt, by nation a Scot, brother of Saint Walpurga, virgin and abbess of Heidenheim; a monk and disciple of Saint Boniface the martyr, of the Order of Saint Benedict; a learned and most holy man, renowned in talent and speech. At the request of Saint Lullus, archbishop of Mainz, he wrote for the edification of the faithful, in a sufficiently distinguished style:

*Life of Saint Boniface, Bishop*, one book. “Illustrious, therefore, and truly blessed...”

I do not know whether he published anything more.

He flourished under Pippin, father of Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 760.

Zacharias, pope, by nation a Greek, after Gregory III presided over the universal Church for ten years: a most learned man, who, because of his learning and holiness of life, is rightly judged worthy to be compared with the best pontiffs. He translated the four books of the dialogues of blessed Pope Gregory the Great from Latin into Greek, and enlightened many who were ignorant of the Latin language by the reading of them. He also wrote from his own talent, in the Latin language:

*Letters to Various Persons*, one book, very elegant and useful.

Among these there are extant several to Saint Boniface, archbishop of Mainz. He died under Pippin, two years before the martyrdom of the same Saint Boniface, in the year of the Lord 752, in the fifth indiction, on the Ides of March.

Alcuin, or Albinus, monk and deacon, abbot of the monastery of Saint Martin of Tours, by nation an Englishman, formerly a hearer of Saint Bede the priest: a man most learned in divine Scripture, and in the knowledge and skill of secular letters second to none in his time; excelling in verse and prose. Summoned from Britain by the emperor Charlemagne, he was held in such familiarity by him that he was called the emperor’s beloved master; under his instruction the emperor himself took pains to be initiated in all the disciplines of the liberal arts. He wrote many excellent volumes, among which are listed:

*On the Holy Trinity and Faith*, three books. “To the glorious lord Emperor Charles...”
*On the Questions of Fredegisus*, one book. “To the most desired son Fredegisus...”
*To Eulalia, on the Nature of the Soul*, one book. “Dearest in the love of Christ...”
*Sentences*, one book. “To the most beloved son Guido...”
*On Genesis*, one book.
*On the Song of Songs*, one book.
*On Ecclesiastes*, one book.
*On the Gospel of John*, seven books.
