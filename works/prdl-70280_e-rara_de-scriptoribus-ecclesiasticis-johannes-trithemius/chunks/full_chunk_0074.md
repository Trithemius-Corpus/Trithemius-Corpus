The moralities of the Virgilian contents, a book.
On the immensity of the Son of God, a book.
On the Passion of the Lord, a book.

But he is also said to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge. For he was splendidly trained in both Greek and Latin speech. It should be noted, however, that the title of the book on Adam without A is *The Book without Letters*; namely, on Abel without B, on Cain without C, and so on with the rest according to the sequence of the letters. But in the book whose title is *Exposition of the Virgilian Contents according to the Moral Philosophers*, with wonderful industry he sought gold in mud, so to speak, and found it, when he inclined the poet’s work toward natural-philosophical reasoning. He wrote this work to a certain deacon. Another work, of mythologies, he wrote to Catus, a Carthaginian priest; and *On the Obscure Expressions of the Ancients* to Chalcidius the grammarian, already an old man, when he himself was still young, and teacher and rector of a school of humane letters. He flourished in the times of Thrasamund, king of the Vandals, persecutor of the Catholic churches, under the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Ferrandus, deacon of the church of Carthage, to whom there survive some little works of the above-mentioned Saint Fulgentius, was a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and venerable no less for the holiness of his way of life than for his learning. He is reported to have brought forth certain volumes of lively intelligence, of which only the following have come to my knowledge:

To Count Reginus, *On the Seven Rules of Innocence*, one book: “From the labors of social life.”
To Saint Fulgentius, one letter.
A book of letters to various persons.
He flourished with Fulgentius, under the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Anastasius II, pope, Roman by nation, sat after the holy pontiff Gelasius for one year and ten months. He was indeed a learned man, but, led astray by Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, he not long afterward paid the due penalties for his error. For when he wished to obey the necessity of nature, struck by God, the wretched man poured out his entrails with torches, as is clear in distinction 19, *Anastasius*. And this was the second infamous pope. He wrote to the emperor Anastasius:

*On the Peace of the Church*, one letter: “The beginning of my pontificate.”
He is also said to have written other letters, but they have not come into our hands. He died, struck by divine judgment, when he had secretly wished to recall from exile Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, condemned and cast out by the Church, under Anastasius Augustus, in the year of the Lord 498.

Paschasius, deacon of the Roman church, a learned and holy man, father of the poor, wholly intent upon the giving of alms, was of such virtue and holiness that Pope Gregory writes in the fourth book of the *Dialogues* that a demoniac was healed after his death by the touch of his dalmatic. Saint Germanus, bishop of Capua, saw his soul in the punishments of purgatory, tormented for nothing other than that in the election of Pope Symmachus he had himself rather chosen Laurence, and in this opinion, though with peace, had persevered until death. On Gregory’s testimony he wrote a distinguished volume:

*On the Holy Spirit*, three books, against the errors of the Greeks.
And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Symmachus, pope, Sardinian by nation, a learned and holy man, obtained the pontificate after Anastasius, not without discord between the parties of Laurence, and sat for fifteen years and six months. A man merciful and generous to the poor, he loved the clergy, enriched the churches, and drove the heretics from the city. Having held a synod at Rome of 120 bishops concerning the crimes charged against him, he cleared himself canonically, and drove Peter, bishop of Ravenna, with his accomplices into exile. He wrote certain letters and little works not to be despised. Of these I have found:

Decree of the first synod, one book: “On account of the frequent ambitions.”
Decree of the Roman synod, one book: “When in one place at blessed Peter’s.”
Acts of another Roman synod, one book: “The fourth synod held at Rome.”
On the excesses of clerics, one book: “Your fraternity indeed well.”
A book of letters to various persons.
