Honorius of Autun, priest and scholastic of the church, a man most devoted to the Scriptures and very learned, and nobly instructed in the secular sciences, subtle in intellect and clear in speech. He wrote many excellent little works. Of these he himself, in the book *On Illustrious Men*, which he entitled *On the Luminaries of the Church*, listed the following: a very necessary work on Christ, on the Church, and on the future life, which he entitled *Elucidarium*, three books, “You say that you wonder why.” *On Free Will*, one book. *The Seal of Saint Mary*, one book. *The Mirror of the Church*, or *Of Sermons*, one book. *A Stumbling Block concerning Priests*, one book. *A Summary of Histories*, one book. *Gem of the Soul*, on the divine offices, six books, “A battle-line in the camps of the eternal king.” *On the Image of the World*, two books, “The world, therefore, is so called as if [unclear].” On the *Song of Songs*, one book, “At the beginnings of books there are three.” *On the Luminaries of the Church*, one book, “Gelasius, bishop of the city of Rome.” *On the Body of the Lord*, one book. *On the Hexaemeron*, one book. *On God and Eternal Life*, one book. *On the Soul*, one book. *The Ladder of Heaven*, on the degrees of visions, one book. *The Key of Physics*, on the natures of things, one book. On the whole Psalter, one book. Homilies on the Gospels, one book. *On the Pope and the Emperor*, one book. A dialogue from the little works of Augustine, one book. *Refreshment of Minds*, on feasts, one book. *The Food of Life*, on the saints, one book. Letters to various persons, one book. He also published many other works, whose titles I have not yet been able to find. He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Sigebert, monk of Gembloux, of the order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man from his youth most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and in secular letters second to none in his time in the territory of Liège, practiced in both verse and prose. He wrote many volumes in both styles, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. Yet this is to be blamed in him: that, adhering to Emperor Henry, a rebel persecutor of the Roman Church, he wrote letters and little works against the most holy pope Gregory VII, which did not befit his profession. What he wrote, however, he declared openly at the end of *Illustrious Men*. When he was placed in the monastery of Saint Vincent at Metz to instruct boys, he wrote in heroic meter: *The Life of Saint Theodoric, Bishop*, one book. *The Passion of Saint Lucy*, in meter, one book. *On the Translation of the Same*, one book. *The Life of King Sigebert*, one book.
