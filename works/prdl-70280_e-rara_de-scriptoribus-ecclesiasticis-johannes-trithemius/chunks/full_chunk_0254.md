Henry, surnamed the Prudent, of the Carthusian order, prior of the house of the Valley [of God?] near Bruges in Flanders, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, brilliant in intellect, devout in life and conduct, singularly devoted to God, wrote for the instruction of religious certain distinguished little works, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these there is a very celebrated work, especially suitable for devotion, divided into three parts according to the distinctions of states: of beginners, of those advancing, and of the perfect; in the manner of a dialogue, in which the interlocutors introduced are: an angel, a monk, Jesus the heavenly father, and Mary, the loving mother-in-law of holy souls; and he entitled it:

*Tetralogue of Devotion*, three books.
And certain other works.

He died in the new house of Saint Sophia of Constantinople near Buscum Ducis, in which for some years he held the office of prior, under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1484, in the second indiction.

Franciscus Diedus, a noble Venetian patrician, a man excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, distinguished in learning and eloquence, a very celebrated interpreter of pontifical and civil law, an ever-loving and tireless guardian of the Venetian commonwealth, wrote certain distinguished little works both in verse and in prose, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among these the following are reported:

*History of Saint Roch the Confessor*, one book.
Various orations and letters.
And certain other works.

He died at Verona under Emperor Frederick III and Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1483, in the first indiction.

Rudolph Agricola of Groningen, a Frisian by nation, a man most learned in secular letters, not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, a philosopher, rhetorician, and the most celebrated poet of all his time, skilled in Greek and Latin speech, not ignorant of the Hebrew language, excellent in intellect, eloquent in speech, formerly the teacher of Johannes Camerarius of Dalberg, bishop of Worms, a man likewise most learned: in the Heidelberg school, by teaching and writing, he easily obtained the first place among all the doctors, even those of any faculty. He wrote certain most excellent little works of his intellect, by which he consecrated his name to immortality and left it to posterity. Among these the following are extant:

*Life of Francis Petrarch*, one book.
A poem on Saint Anne, one book: “Anna, mother of the highest [unclear].”
A most subtle *Dialectic*, three books.
Various letters and orations, one book.

He also published various most elegant poems, by which he showed his intellect to be almost divine. He translated the Psalter from Hebrew, and certain works of Plato from Greek. Beginning to translate the volumes of Dionysius the Areopagite, he was overtaken by an untimely death and left them unfinished. He also wrote other works.

He died at Heidelberg, his mind most fully extended toward God, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1485, in the third indiction.
