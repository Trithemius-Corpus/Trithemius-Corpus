Alexander of Hales, English by nation, of the Order of Minors, teacher of Cardinal Eustace, was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and second to none in his time in secular philosophy; subtle in intellect, a true [unclear] disciple of philosophy. He entered the order of the poor of Christ, which he wondrously adorned by his learning and holiness. At the command of Pope Innocent IV he wrote a distinguished work, divided into four volumes and usefully comprising many things, which he entitled:

*A Summa of Theology*, four books: “As Boethius says.”

*A Declaration of the Rule of the Minors*, one book: “To the reverend father in Christ.”

The rest of his works I have not seen.

It is said, however, that he expounded both the Old and the New Testament, and discussed many things subtly concerning the concord of divine and human law, and composed various other things, which have not come into our hands. He flourished under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

John of La Rochelle, of the Order of Friars Minor, master of sacred theology at Paris, learned in the divine Scriptures and magnificently taught in Aristotelian philosophy, who subscribed his name together with Alexander of Hales in the Declaration of the Rule of the Minors to the minister general, published certain excellent little works, by which he transmitted the fame of his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these the following are reported:

*On the Sentences*, four books.

*Summa of virtues and vices*, one book.

Also *On the Soul*, one book.

He flourished under Frederick, the second emperor, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Henry, archbishop of Embrun, cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, commonly called Hostiensis, was a man most expert in both laws; because of the excellence of his natural ability and learning he was held in esteem by Pope Alexander IV. He wrote certain distinguished little works, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have seen only the distinguished work known to everyone:

*On the titles of the Decretals*, five books: “Alpha and O, one in essence.”

Also a concise *Summa*, one book.

The rest that he composed have as yet by no means come to my knowledge.

He flourished while the empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Walter, bishop of Poitiers, of the Order of Friars Minor, was a man learned in the divine Scriptures, nobly taught in the disciplines of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech; in delivering sermons to the people he was of excellent industry. He wrote certain works not to be despised; of these the following are reported. For at the command of Pope Alexander IV he arranged in style, very usefully, throughout the cycle of the year:

*Sermons*, two books.

*On the Sentences*, four books.

*Quodlibets*, one book: “It is asked in what way virtue.”

He flourished while the empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Walter, bishop of Maguelone, born from Lille, a town of Flanders, was a man exercised by long study in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, outstanding in intellect and clear in eloquence, who in his time, because of his learning and erudition, was of great reputation. At the request of the provost of the clergy of the church of Lille, he wrote:

*On the Psalter*, one book.
