On penitence, to the [prior?] of Bourges, four books: “By the grace of God, [unclear] of Bourges...”
On mystical [equivocations?], one book.
On the six wings of the Cherubim, one book.
On the lament of nature, one book.
A summa of virtues and vices, one book.
On parables, one book.
On the art of preaching, one book.
The Anticlaudianus, one book.
On the natures of animals, one book.
Several sermons, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.
Finally, he is said to have published notable commentaries and readings on many books of the Bible, especially on the prophets, on the Gospel, and on the Apostle. Concerning these I have not been able to find anything at all. Certain marvelous things are told about him, to which I neither lend support nor rashly refuse belief.
He flourished under the emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Wilhelm, a monk of the monastery of Affligem of the Order of Saint Benedict, German by nation, for some time prior in the same monastery, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures, learned through long practice, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, capable in verse and prose, published in both kinds of writing certain little works not to be despised, by which he perpetuated the memory of his name. There are extant by him:

Useful sermons, one book.
The visions of a certain nun, one book.
On the life of Saint Lutgardis, one book.
But other works which he composed have as yet by no means come to my knowledge.

Franciscus Accursius, Italian by nation, Florentine by homeland, son of the very learned Accursius, himself a man excellently learned in civil law and much instructed in the other disciplines of secular philosophy, whose works are extant in great authority among the professors of this faculty, made his times famous by teaching and writing, and gave his name to immortality. Of his little works I have as yet been able to see nothing. Various works of his are reported:

Glosses on the laws.
Disputations also, one book.
And many other things.

He flourished under the emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Martinus de Fano, professor of both laws, the most celebrated interpreter of all in his own time, teacher of Johannes Andreae of Bologna, subtle in intellect, distinguished in eloquence, and very acute in pleading judicial cases, wrote excellent volumes in his faculty, by which he also made his name known to posterity. Of these I have found only:

On actions, one book: “I indeed, Martinus...”
On the judgment of cases, one book: “Since to very many [unclear]...”
Of the others I have seen nothing.

He flourished under the emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Albertus Patavinus, of the Order of the Hermit Friars of Saint Augustine, formerly hearer and disciple of Giles of Rome, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, was of excellent talent in declaiming sermons to the people. By his preaching he recalled many from iniquity to the path of justice, and made his fame memorable to posterity. Of these the following are reported:

On Genesis, one book.
On Exodus, one book.
On Leviticus, one book.
