[of the Lord?] in which we write these things: 1493.

Johannes de Arundine, a German by nation, bishop of [Usbitensis?], of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, vicar in pontifical matters of David of Burgundy, bishop of Utrecht; a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, an outstanding preacher of sermons, subtle in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct. He wrote many excellent volumes, by which he made his name known. Of these the following are reported:

A notable lecture
On the Book of Wisdom, one book.
On Paul’s Epistle to the Romans, one book.
Sermons on the Blessed Immaculate One, one book.
Sermons for the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book.
And certain others.

He flourished at Utrecht, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1490. And I believe that he is still alive.

Georgius Valla of Piacenza, a man most learned in every respect, a very celebrated rhetorician and poet, excellent in intellect and eloquent in speech, who, now adorning all Italy with his learning, teaches both languages most brilliantly at Venice. He wrote many excellent volumes, by whose elegance and usefulness he may deservedly feel that learned men everywhere are indebted and bound to him. Of these the following are extant:

On orthography, one book.
On the quantity of syllables, one book.
On the figures of orators and poets, one book.
On Cicero’s On the Universe, one book. Plato’s book called Timaeus.
On the same author’s On Fate, one book. Cicero’s work On Fate.
On the book of the Topics of the same author, one book. A particularly good observation.
On the Rhetoric to Herennius, one book. “Long ago I had looked ahead.”
On Juvenal, one book.
On Horace’s Poetics, one book.
On the method of disputing, one book.
On things to be sought and avoided, one book.
A book of letters to various persons.
Various orations.
And many other works.

He translated from Greek into Latin Aristotle’s little work On Virtue; also two books of the same author’s Ethics; likewise the work of Nemesius, the Christian philosopher, On the Nature of Man; also the little book of Athenagoras, a very acute philosopher, On the Resurrection; likewise the book of dialectic of Bristo; in addition, the treatise of Galen the physician On the Sects of Physicians; the same author’s Introduction to Medicine; also four books of the Problems of Alexander; and many other works.

Johannes Camerarius of Dalburg, bishop of Worms, a German by nation, a very celebrated jurist and most learned both in divine Scriptures and in secular letters; once a pupil of Rudolf Agricola, that most learned man; skilled in both languages, a very distinguished rhetorician and poet, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, and notable for much experience of affairs, magnificently ennobling the nobility of his lineage by his character and learning. He has written and is writing, both in verse and in prose, certain little works of excellent talent, which he has not yet allowed to come to light, because, occupied with the affairs of the commonwealth, he has not been able to complete them quite as he had intended. Yet some of these have become known to us:

A Sapphic poem
On the death of Rudolf Agricola, one book.
Also On Coinage, one book.
An oration before Pope Innocent VIII, one book.
