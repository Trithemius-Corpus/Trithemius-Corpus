*Book*

Proba, a most elegant woman, wife of Adelphus, Roman proconsul; a woman most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine Scriptures; burning with remarkable zeal, she surpassed her sex by her learning. Perfectly trained in Greek and Latin letters, and excelling in verse and prose, since she held Virgil in her mind word for word, she conceived the idea of describing the New and Old Testament from his verses, as though he had intended to show this meaning in his poems. And so, vigorously undertaking the work stored up in her mind, carefully going through all the books of Virgil, taking now this, now that verse, sometimes a half-verse, and at times smaller parts of verses, with remarkable industry and everywhere preserving the measure of the feet, she described the catholic volumes in the poet’s verses; and she entitled this work, in almost seven hundred verses, with a Greek word:

A Virgilian cento, one book.

Whether she wrote anything more, I do not know. Nevertheless this work is counted among the apocrypha, because even if a devout woman undertook a pious study, still Virgil never judged that his little verses should be referred to the honor of Christ or to our faith. For the interpretation of this matter is not Virgil’s own, but a fictive one assumed by Proba. She flourished, as some think, under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 430.

Eudoxia, wife of Theodosius the Younger, a woman most devoted in the Christian religion and most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters: returning from Jerusalem, she brought with her the relics of the most holy protomartyr Stephen, and honorably placed them in the basilica of the blessed martyr Lawrence. Since she was very learned, excelling in verse and prose, she is said, in imitation of Proba, wife of Adelphus, to have turned the poems of Homer back to the Christian faith, likewise prefixing to the little published work this title:

Homeric centos, one book.

But this too is apocryphal. She also wrote certain other things. Since Saint Jerome says in a certain letter that he had read Homeric centos and Virgilian centos, they must necessarily have been composed before his death. She flourished with her husband Theodosius, who took up the empire in the year of the Lord 423.

Primasius, an African bishop, and, as some hold, once a disciple of blessed Augustine, a man indeed learned in the holy Scriptures, excellently trained in the disciplines, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, is said to have composed many little works, which among the ancients were held in great esteem. Of these I have found only:

Against Heresies, to Bishop Fortunatus, three books.

On the Apocalypse of John, to Castorius, five books. On the book of the Apocalypse of blessed John, to [unclear].

The other things that he composed have not yet come to my knowledge. He flourished under the emperor Theodosius II, in the year of the Lord 540.

Eunilius, bishop of a certain city in Africa, [unclear], certainly a man very learned in the sacred Scriptures, in my judgment sufficiently trained in secular discipline, profound in understanding, sweet and ornate in speech, is said to have compiled many little works; but I have seen only the distinguished work that he wrote to the above-named Bishop Primasius, which he entitled:

On the Parts of the Divine Law, one book. To the holy and most blessed Bishop Pri[masius].

Of the rest I have seen none. He flourished at the same time as Primasius, in the year of the Lord 540.

Roterius, bishop of Alexandria, a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, an outstanding theologian and computist; in the times of the emperor Leo, after a sedition had been stirred up, the successors of the heretic Dioscorus cruelly killed him as an instigator on behalf of Timothy, and they set up that same Timothy in his place, as an idol for the Alexandrian bishop. Among other things he wrote to Saint Pope Leo.
