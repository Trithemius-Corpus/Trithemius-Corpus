Usuard, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, ready in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, by order of the emperor Charlemagne after Eusebius, Jerome, and Bede gathered from the writings of the ancients the deeds and passions of the holy martyrs, confessors, and virgins into a single volume of no small size, which we call:
A Martyrology: one book.
And certain other works.
He flourished under the same emperor Charles, in the year of the Lord 770.

Paul, a monk of the monastery of Cassino, of the order of Saint Benedict, an Italian by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly taught in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, was held in great esteem by the emperor Charles on account of his knowledge of letters. Formerly, before his conversion, he was a deacon of the church of Aquileia and the chancellor of Desiderius, the last king of the Lombards; and, captured with him by Charles, he was led into Gaul and at length became a monk. He wrote not a few volumes, both in verse and in prose; of these I have found only the following. At the request of [Adelperga?], daughter of King Desiderius, he added:
To the chronicle of Eutropius: two books.
The deeds of the bishops of Metz: one book.
The Life of Pope Gregory the First: one book.
The Life of Saint Arnulf, bishop: one book.
The Life of Saint Benedict, in verse: one book.
The deeds of the Lombards: six books.
Many hymns in various metres: one book.
Letters to various persons: one book.
By order of Charles he arranged histories and readings for the whole year, suitable to the several feasts of the saints. And he digested and wrote many other works.
He flourished in the times of the same Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 780.

Hadrian, pope, the first of that name, a Roman by nation, sat in the pontificate after Stephen III for twenty-three years; a learned and most holy man, held in the highest honor by the emperor Charles. He established many things for the benefit of the Church, and at the command of the emperor Charles condemned the Felician heresy in a synod of 350 bishops at Frankfurt. Since he was studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, he abridged thirteen books of letters of Saint Gregory the pope into two, selecting the more useful portions. He also wrote from his own vein to Leo the Younger and Constantine his son, emperors:
On the veneration of images: one book.
To the emperor Charles: one book.
Letters to various persons: one book.
He died while Charlemagne was emperor, in the year of the Lord 795, in the fourth indiction.
