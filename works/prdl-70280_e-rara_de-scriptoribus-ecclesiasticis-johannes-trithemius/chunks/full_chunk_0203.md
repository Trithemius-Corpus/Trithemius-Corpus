*On Ecclesiastical Writers*
Folio 100

Thomas de Kempis, a German by nation, a canon of the order of Saint Augustine, of the Mount of the holy martyr and virgin Agnes near Zwolle in the diocese of Utrecht, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, devout in life and conduct, and an especial lover of blessed Mary ever virgin. He wrote little works, not to be despised, for the instruction of simple brothers. Among them the following are reported:

On contempt of the world, three books, “He who follows me shall not.”
Sermons to novices, two books, “Behold how good and pleasant.”
Sermons to the brothers, one book, “He who has not renounced.”
Dialogue of novices, one book, “I ask you, dearest father.”
On the solitary life, one book, “Dearest, since you were in.”
On the three tabernacles, one book, “It is written in the prophet.”
On true compunction, one book, “Weep with me, all you friends.”
The little garden of roses, one book, “With the holy you will be holy.”
The valley of lilies, one book, “I am the flower of the field and the lily.”
The alphabet of a monk, one book, “Show me your ways, Lord.”
Consolation of the poor, one book, “Be comforted, be comforted.”
The epitaph of a monk, one book, “The way of the just has been made straight.”
What a monk ought to be like, one book, “Listen, religious monk.”
Manual for beginners, one book, “Let the little children come.”
Instructional book for youths, one book, “Son, from your youth receive.”
Another work on contempt of the world, one book, “The Lord rules me, and nothing.”
Letters to various persons, one book, “Dearest brother, gladly.”
And certain other works.

He flourished in the times of Rupert, Bavarian king of the Romans, and of Pope Gregory XII, in the year of the Lord 1410.

Nicolaus, monk of the monastery of the Holy Cross in Austria, of the Cistercian order, a German by nation, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures and rich in the reading of the ancients, excellent in talent and distinguished in eloquence. He wrote certain useful little works, not to be despised, by which he transmitted the memory of his talent to posterity. Among them the following are reported: a work not to be despised in praise of the most blessed mother of God and virgin Mary, useful to preachers, which he entitled:

The Image of Saint Mary, two books, “The name of the virgin Mary: Lu.”
Various sermons, one book.

The rest, though many works which he composed are reported, have by no means come down to us.

Bartholomaeus of Urbino, of the order of the hermit brothers of Saint Augustine, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, a diligent investigator of the works of the holy fathers. He wrote volumes not to be despised, among which the following are reported. From the little works of Saints Ambrose and Augustine he gathered into one work, with singular zeal, according to the order of the alphabet, and gave it the title:

The Milleloquium of Augustine, one book.
The Milleloquium of Ambrose, one book.
Also, On Spiritual Warfare, one book.
And very many others.

He flourished in the times of Rupert, king of the Romans, and of Pope Gregory XII, in the year of the Lord 1410.

Franciscus de Platea, of the order of the Friars Minor of Saint Francis, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and notably learned both in law and in secular philosophy, excellent in talent and an outstanding preacher of sermons, is said to have composed many splendid little works, by which he transmitted the memory of his name with praise to posterity. Of their number the following are reported: an outstanding and splendid work which he entitled:

On Restitutions, one book, “Whether restitution is of necessity.”

*Book*

On censures, five books.
Various sermons, one book.

The other works that he composed have not come to our knowledge by reading.

Petrus de Candia, Cretan by nation, of the order of the Friars Minor, first bishop of Novara, then archbishop of Milan, afterwards cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, and finally supreme pontiff named Alexander V, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and splendidly learned in scholastic philosophy, a very celebrated preacher of sermons, who once presided over a public school in the university of Paris, and by teaching and writing gained great and immense praises. Among his most elegant little works there survive clear and useful commentaries:

On the Sentences, seven books.
Various sermons, one book.
And certain other works.

He died in the last year of Rupert, king of the Romans, and was buried in the convent of the Friars Minor at Bologna, in the year of the Lord 1410, in the third indiction.
