Hormisdas, pope, a Campanian by nation, presided over the Church after the pontiff Symmachus for nine years. He was a good, learned, and energetic man; having assembled a synod at Rome, he condemned the Eutychian heresy, and the Manichaeans, springing up again, he banished into exile after burning the books of their errors. Among other little works of his talent he wrote:

A synodal decree, one book.

To Justin Augustus, one letter: “Meanwhile, those things which pertain to the unity of the Church...”

To the clergy of Second Syria, one letter: “When the letters of your affection had been read...”

Several letters to various persons, one book.

He died under Justin the Elder Augustus, in the year of the Lord 521, in the fourteenth indiction, on the eighth day before the Ides of August. John succeeded him.

Evodius, bishop of Ticinum, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature, of remarkable eloquence in both Greek and Latin, was sent by Pope Hormisdas to Constantinople in matters of the faith to Anastasius, the heretical emperor. After receiving many insults, he was expelled from the city together with his companions, Anastasius saying: “Why do you offer commands to us, who are under the dominion of no one? The Roman pontiff ought to obey us, not to give orders.” Evodius wrote to Pope Symmachus:

On the Catholic Faith, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 510.

Jordanes, from a monk bishop of the Goths, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures and competently instructed in secular letters, gentle in disposition and plain in speech, an investigator of times and events and a notable writer, is said to have composed certain distinguished volumes. Of these, very few have come to my knowledge. For I have found only:

Histories, two books. In the first of these he reviewed the deeds of the Romans; in the second, the origin and deeds of the Goths down to the end of their kingdom.

He flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 530.

Faustus, a monk and disciple of our most holy father Benedict, Italian by nation, a man venerable in knowledge and morals: when he was seven years old, he was offered by his parents to Father Benedict. At length, after the complete formation of his regular profession, he was sent with blessed Maurus into Gaul to build a monastery. There, after living for many years in great holiness, when Saint Maurus was dead he returned to Monte Cassino. At the request of the brethren, he wrote in quite clear eloquence to Boniface, the second pontiff of the Roman city:

The Life of Saint Maurus the Abbot, one book.

He flourished under the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord 600.

Justinian the Great, emperor, sister’s son of Justin the Elder, reigned after him for thirty-eight years. He was a man of great and excellent talent, and rightly obtained a place among ecclesiastical writers, because he both published a very distinguished volume against the heretics, and, through the agency of John the patrician and the consuls Theophilus and Dorotheus, with admirable brevity corrected the Roman laws, which lay scattered in more than two thousand books. For, reducing all the constitutions of the emperors which were contained in many volumes to twelve books, he called it the Code.

Again, reducing the laws of the individual magistrates and judges, which, as we have said, extended to almost two thousand books, to the number of fifty books in three volumes, he named it the Code of the Digest or Pandects.
