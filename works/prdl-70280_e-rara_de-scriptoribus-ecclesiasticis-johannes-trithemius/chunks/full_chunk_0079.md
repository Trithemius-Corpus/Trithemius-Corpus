Cassiodorus, monk and abbot of the monastery near Ravenna, a man very learned in divine Scripture and in secular letters, easily the most erudite of all in his time, shone forth no less renowned for holiness than for knowledge. He was first chancellor of Theoderic, king of Italy, and a senator of Ravenna, and consul of the city of Rome; afterward, despising the vain honors of the world, for love of God he became a monk. Then, directing the study of humane letters to the exposition of holy Scripture, he composed many most elegant treatises. Of his works the following are reported. In the person both of himself and of King Theoderic he wrote:

Letters to various persons, 28 books.

On the Nature of the Soul, 1 book. “When now of the work undertaken...”

On the whole Psalter, 1 book. “Blessed is the man,” etc. Very beautiful.

Instruction in Divine Readings, 2 books. “Since the pursuits of secular letters...”

On the Song of Songs, 1 book. “Solomon, inspired by the divine...”

Instruction in Secular Letters, 9 books. “Our intention is concerning the art...”

He compiled the Tripartite History, 12 books. Very useful at the beginning of the book.

Catalogue of the Roman Consuls, 1 book.

On Etymologies, 1 book.

The Fore-Noted Priest, 1 book.

On Figures and Tropes, 1 book.

Memorial of the Scriptures, 1 book.

On Orthography, 1 book.

Expositions on the Apocalypse, 1 book.

On the Canonical Epistles, 7 books.

On the Acts of the Apostles, 1 book.

He also composed many other things which have not come to the knowledge of our reading. He flourished in the times of Justin the elder down almost to the end of the reign of Justin the younger, being more than 95 years of age, in the year of the Lord 575.

Arator, subdeacon of the most holy Roman Church, a man practiced in divine Scriptures and nobly learned in the literature of the secular disciplines, powerful in verse and prose, subtle in intellect, clear and polished in speech, wrote a very distinguished volume, composed in meter, to a certain abbot Florian:

On the Acts of the Apostles, 3 books. “That Judaea, polluted by the crime of its own...”

To Abbot Florian, 1 letter. “You who by merits...”

To Pope Vigilius, 1 letter. “With watery walls of wars...”

And certain other things which I have not seen.

This Arator, a very distinguished poet and notable theologian, offered the book on the Acts of the Apostles which he had composed in elegant meter to Pope Vigilius before the confession of Saint Peter, on the seventh Ides of April, in the presence of many bishops and learned men; and, at the pontiff’s command, he recited a large part of it publicly before all. As the hour was passing, Pope Vigilius handed the book to the venerable Surgentius, the primicerius, to be placed in the archive, so that he might complete its reading at a free time. All the learned men earnestly begged his holiness to order the book to be read before them in its entirety. When he had ordered this to be done in the church of Saint Peter ad Vincula, a great crowd of religious, nobles, and laymen assembled; and as Arator himself recited in distinct words, both books were heard four times, since on one day only half of a book was read because of the continual repetitions which the listeners demanded with manifold favor. Where in the present times is there such study of letters? Where is the love of saving knowledge? Our religious men, and nobles, and also clerical pontiffs listen not to books but to revenues; they cultivate not letters but avarice. Now Antimachus reads many poems, and as individuals withdraw, scarcely a single Plato is found to remain. But we are now speaking out of season about these matters.

Arator flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

*Fol. 36*

Victor, bishop of the [Tunnunensian?] church in Africa, a man learned in divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, was banished into exile in Egypt by the emperor Justinian and by Eutychius, bishop of the city of Constantinople, because of his defense of the Three Chapters. From there, summoned again to Constantinople, when he resisted the emperor and the bishop, the detractors of those same Chapters, he was sent to be guarded in a monastery of the same city; and, as they say, he remained in that same condemnation and died. Among other works of his talent he wrote, from the beginning of the world down to the first year of the reign of Justin the younger, a very useful History concerning deeds and ecclesiastical wars:

History, 1 book.

For the Defense of the Three Chapters, 1 book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.
