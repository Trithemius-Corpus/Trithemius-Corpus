Clement, the sixth pope, by homeland a Lemovician, formerly called Petrus Rogerii, abbot of [Fiscaniensis?] of the order of Saint Benedict, sat in the chair of Peter after Benedict XII for ten years and six months. He was a man learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular literature; an outstanding doctor of canon law, venerable no less in conduct than in knowledge; in delivering sermons to the people he was of excellent talent. He published certain distinguished little works, in which he showed the liveliness of his talent. Of these they report:

Various sermons: 1 book
On the canonization of Yvo: 1 book
On the approval of King Charles: 1 book
Many collations: 1 book
Letters to various persons: 1 book

And certain other things.

He died at Avignon, and was buried, under the emperor Charles IV, in the year of the Lord 1351, indiction 5.

Gregory of Rimini, prior general of the order of the Hermit Friars of Saint Augustine, was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and very learned in scholastic philosophy, distinguished in talent and eloquence. In the school of Paris he won from all men great glory for his learning, since he shone forth most famously in life and doctrine. He wrote little works of no contemptible reading; of these they report the following as the more important:

On the Sentences: 4 books
On the Epistles of Paul the Apostle: 14 books

And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Charles IV, emperor, and Pope Clement VI, in the year of the Lord 1350.

Philippus de Pergamo, prior of the monastery of Saint Mary de Avancia in Padua, of the order of our holy father Benedict, was a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy, excellent in talent and distinguished in eloquence. He wrote some little works of holy doctrine, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I found only one large volume:

On the Ethics of Cato: 1 book
He also wrote sermons for speaking: 1 book

The rest that he composed have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading.

Johannes de Beka, a German by nation, cleric of the church of Utrecht, was a man very devoted to and learned in the Holy Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy. He published certain little works of his talent that are not to be despised; of these I have seen only one work, written in a simple and open style, addressed to lord Johannes, bishop of the church of Utrecht, and to Guilhelmus, duke of Holland, concerning the deeds of the bishops of Utrecht and the counts of Holland. Bringing it down from the time of Saint Willebrord, the first bishop, as I have seen, to the year of the Lord 1346, he wished it to be entitled:

Chronicle of the Church of Utrecht: 1 book

Of the rest I have seen nothing.
