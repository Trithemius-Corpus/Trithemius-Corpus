*On Ecclesiastical Writers*
Fol. 3

Letters to Paul: five books. “I believe you, Paul, [unclear].”
Letters to Lucilius: twenty-two books. “Do therefore thus, my Lucilius.”

These are the writings of Seneca which at present I have been able to find. He is said, however, to have written some other little works, which have not yet come into our hands. He was indeed a most ardent lover of wisdom, and a zealous searcher after it, and of so tenacious a memory that, when two thousand names had been read before him, he immediately recited them from memory. Moreover, when a certain poet had produced two hundred verses and, after collecting them on paper, recited them in order before Nero, Seneca immediately, using his memory in place of the volume, repeated those same verses one by one, beginning from the last and proceeding in reverse order as far as the first. He is read to have retained in memory the names also of all his servants. He flourished at Rome in the times of the apostle Paul, under the emperor Nero, in the year of the Lord 50.

[Marcellus?], son of Marcus, prefect of the city of Rome, a certain disciple of Simon Magus, sufficiently instructed in secular letters, converted to the faith of Jesus Christ by the preaching of blessed Peter the apostle, composed many things for the usefulness of the church of God. For at the request of Christians he wrote:

On the Confounding of Peter and Simon: one book.
To Saints Nereus and Achilleus: a book.
On the Acts of Peter and Paul: a book.
On the Arts of Simon: a book.
On the Death of Saint Petronella: a book.
On the Passion of Saint Felicula: a book.
On the Passion of Saint Nicomedes: a book.

He also published other things concerning the deeds of the saints, which have not come to our knowledge. He flourished at Rome in the time of Saint Peter, in the year of the Lord 50.

Linus, the first bishop of the city of Rome after blessed Peter, a Tuscan by nation, a man of most holy life, shining with many miracles both in life and after death, sat in the pontificate for eleven years, three months, and twelve days. Since he was a very learned man and beloved of God, he wrote in the Greek language, concerning the holy apostles Peter and Paul, pastors on one day:

Their Martyrdom: one book.
And certain letters.

At last, punished with capital sentence by the prefect Saturninus, whose daughter he had freed from a demon, he was made a martyr, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of October. He flourished at Rome under the emperor Galba, in the year of the Lord 70.

Philo, a Jew by nation, an Alexandrian, of the race of priests, is placed by blessed Jerome among the ecclesiastical writers for this reason: because in his little works he is very often occupied with praise of Christians. When he had been sent to Rome as an envoy of his nation, he is recorded to have enjoyed the friendship of blessed Peter the apostle; and for this reason he also adorned with praises the followers of Saint Mark, his disciple, at Alexandria. He was, moreover, a man most learned in every respect, and was regarded as another Plato, so that it was commonly said: “Either Plato imitates Philo, or Philo imitates Plato.” For so great a similarity of thought and eloquence between them is reported. He wrote many and most elegant books, of which the following are given below:

On the Life of the Wise Man: one book.
On the Conversion of Life: a book.
On the Confusion of Tongues: a book.
On the Nature of Quadrupeds: three books.
On the Things Which We Attest by Sense: a book.
On Learning: a book.
On the Heir of Divine Things: a book.
On the Unequal Division: a book.
