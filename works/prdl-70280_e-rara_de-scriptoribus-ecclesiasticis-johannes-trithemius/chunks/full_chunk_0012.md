*Book on Ecclesiastical Writers, fol. 1*

*The letter of the most eloquent man and reverend father in Christ, lord Johannes of Trittenheim, abbot of Sponheim, to the most reverend father in Christ, lord Johannes von Dalberg, Bishop of Worms.*

To the most reverend father and lord in Christ, lord Johannes von Dalberg, by divine providence Bishop of Worms: Brother Johannes, called abbot in Sponheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict, [offers] the little that he is.

The ancients were formerly accustomed, most reverend father, if they had forged any new work, to dedicate it to distinguished men, so that it might receive perpetual strength and firmness, and so that the venomous tongue of rivals, beaten back by the light of majesty, should nowhere have power to fasten the tooth of a wicked bite. Nor did the opinion deceive them, which proceeded from humility, since kindness in learned prelates must always be praised toward their subjects: it rewards the pursuits of good men and restrains those of the wicked.

We too, imitating as far as we can this example of antiquity, are accustomed to submit the works that we write, dedicated, to prelates of churches and to more learned men, so that, approved by their authority, they may be able to go forth safely into public. For those things go forth into the light without fear which are supported by the testimony of a bishop. Indeed no one, unless foolish, readily accuses what he knows has been approved by the judgment of a superior. The innocence of the sheep rests safely when the compassion of the good shepherd watches over it.

Since, therefore, the examples of the ancients are incitements to virtues for us, it is fitting that we imitate the holy customs of those whose praises we sing, so that, while others profit both from them and from us, the order of the ecclesiastical hierarchy may remain inviolate. And although I am too insignificant to trust that anyone will take delight in my little works, nevertheless I have resolved humbly to offer what I have into the Lord’s treasury, in which the widow’s two small coins are rightly preferred to the riches of Croesus.

Accordingly, when recently, at the insistence of friends, I had brought together with much labor a Book on Ecclesiastical Writers, as the matter itself bears witness, lest I wander from the tracks of the ancients, I decided to submit this very labor of mine to some most learned man, outstanding in ecclesiastical dignity, through whose authority and goodwill it might pass by the teeth of the envious without a bite. And when much thought arose in me as to whom I might entrust this little night-work with greater favor, many presented themselves to me as I wavered, from whose learning and goodwill no small hope, once promised, was strong. Among them you, most reverend father, occurred to me as more powerful, more eminent, more learned: you surpass all in learning; you exceed everyone in erudition.

In you the lofty nobility of birth shines forth; episcopal authority stands out; probity of morals is seen; every kind of knowledge of the good arts is praised. Could we find such a man anywhere besides you, in whom all these things come together at once? Among the Germans I know no one equal to you, most reverend father, in whom lineage, dignity, and such great erudition shine. I do not speak in secret; let the Almighty be witness. Nor am I a flattering praiser: all Germany knows you to be a man most skilled in every respect.

The learned honor you; nobles and princes respect you. You are to us what Plato is said to have been to Antimachus: approved by your eloquence, a man is deservedly judged learned. We have learned by public report, which does not lie, how profound a knowledge in sacred letters abounds in you, how discerning an interpretation of law, and finally how great are the charm, sweetness, and grace of your speaking and delivering orations, so that we do not hesitate to compare you with the most learned men of whom we have made mention in the catalogue. The most brilliant orations which you are said to have delivered bear witness to this; your letters bear witness; many epigrams bear witness. Indeed the most illustrious fame of your name has become known with praise not only among our Germans, but among Italians and Frenchmen.

Hence it came into my mind that I should inscribe to you, so great a bishop, the work on ecclesiastical writers placed under your authority, and dedicate it to your name, so that under the shadow of the pontifical summit I might rest secure.
