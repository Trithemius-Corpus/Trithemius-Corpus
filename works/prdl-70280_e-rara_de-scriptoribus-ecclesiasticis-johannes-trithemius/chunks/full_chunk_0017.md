Josephus, a Jew by nation, a Jerusalemite, a priest by religion, son of the priest Matthias, a philosopher, rhetorician, and distinguished writer of the times, whom I would not place in our catalogue unless the Church read his writings and blessed Jerome had done so before, after the destruction of Jerusalem was captured by the emperors Vespasian and Titus and brought to Rome. Because of the excellence of his talent he was splendidly honored by the Senate and people of Rome. Although he remained a Jew, nevertheless he often praised the race of Christians, and in the eighteenth book of the Antiquities set down a notable testimony concerning our Lord Jesus Christ. His volumes were held in such authority among the Romans while he was still alive that they were placed in the public libraries; and after his death he himself was granted a bronze statue by the Senate and people. Of his little works I have seen only the following.

On the Jewish War: seven books, "Since the war which ..."
Antiquities of the World: twenty-four books, "To write a history ..."
Against Apion: two books, "Sufficiently, as I think ..."
On the Power of Speaking: one book.

He flourished at Rome under the emperors Vespasian and Titus, and remained among the living down to the times of the emperor Trajan, in the year of the Lord 100.

Ignatius, third bishop of the church of Antioch after the blessed [unclear], a man of outstanding holiness and a singular, most fervent lover of our Lord Jesus Christ, used to say, as Dionysius bears witness: "My love is crucified." Since this most blessed prelate was a learned and zealous preacher of the divine word, he wrote some elegant letters full of fervor, in which he showed himself to have been a lover of holy martyrdom.
