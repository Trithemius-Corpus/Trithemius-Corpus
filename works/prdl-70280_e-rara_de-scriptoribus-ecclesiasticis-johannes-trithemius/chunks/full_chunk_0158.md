Although there are many other things which he composed, they nevertheless have not come to me. He flourished in the times of Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Nicolaus of Naples, a man skilled and most learned in civil law, and nobly instructed also in other secular letters, whose authority among professors of this faculty has in no small measure consigned him to immortality. But I have been able to find only the titles of the following:

On the Code, 9 books.

On the Infortiatum of the Digest, 14 books.

Concerning the other works which he is said to have composed, I have as yet been able to find nothing certain. He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Hubertinus of Casale, of the Order of Friars Minor, formerly a hearer of John of Parma, minister general; a devout man and sufficiently learned in the Holy Scriptures. When he saw that his order was falling away from its first institution, he began certain new separations under the guise of reform, and obtained their confirmation from the supreme pontiff. Yet at the petitions of the guardians and ministers they were at once revoked by the same pope, since they seemed to have been undertaken to the grave scandal and schism of the order. Therefore Hubertinus, being moved, entered the Carthusian order. He wrote a very renowned and distinguished volume, useful for religious and devout persons, which he entitled:

The Tree of the Life of the Crucified, 5 books. “To the universal true faith of Christ.”

Sermons and letters.

And certain other things.

Certain Catholic doctors warn us that Hubertinus, in the aforesaid volume, in certain places, especially where he takes up the Canticle of Simeon, must be read cautiously, because concerning certain articles he thinks otherwise than the Church, the mother of us all. Yet the author himself is to be excused, because it had not yet been determined, etc.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Jacobus of Ravenna, originally from the province of Lotharingia, a man most learned in civil law and the sacred canons, and easily the most skilled of all in his time both in divine Scripture and in secular letters; a professor of sacred Scripture, and a distinguished philosopher and dialectician, who, as Cinus says, converted what had been handed down by the elders in simple meaning into a dialectical mode of argument. He wrote many little works in both laws, among which are reported:

On the Code, 9 books.

On the Old Digest, 23 books.

On the New Digest, 12 books.

On the Infortiatum of the Digest, 14 books.

Various disputations, 1 book.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1400.

Alanus of Lille, German by nation, a man most learned in divine Scripture and second to none in his time in secular letters; a theologian, philosopher, and most celebrated poet, who for a long time gloriously presided over the ecclesiastical school at Paris. There, shining clearly in all divine and human knowledge, he merited being called universal. He wrote in verse and prose almost innumerable little works, by which the memory of his name has been made immortal among posterity. But few of them have come to my knowledge. I find only the following under their titles:

On the Sentences, 3 books.

Quodlibets also, 1 book. “To the most reverend father and lord.”

The Eye of Sacred Scripture, 1 book. “Every science rests on its own principles.”

On Doctrine also, 5 books. “Since many for adorning.”

On the Five Books of Moses, 1 book. “In the five books of Moses,” 1 book.

On the Song of Songs, 1 book.
