There was another declaimer of the Order of Preachers, who, according to the custom of the moderns, composed sermons for the people under the name Peregrinus, much inferior to ours in eloquence and later than he in time. But whatever Conrad wrote in this way echoes Ciceronian eloquence, which the other could not do.

He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Arnold, abbot of Bonneval, of the Cistercian order, once a very close friend of the blessed abbot Bernard, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant also of secular writings, ready in talent and clear in eloquence, and no less distinguished in conduct than in doctrine, made his name known to posterity by writing. For when William, abbot of Saint-Thierry, died and left unfinished the work on the life of Saint Bernard, and when that saint himself also was already holding the heavenly realms, Arnold wrote and completed:

Life of Saint Bernard: 4 books. “The deeds of illustrious men do not...”

And some letters. He flourished under Frederick I, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Richard, monk of Cluny, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, by everyone’s report, most learned in secular letters in his time, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, who, by writing and composing various treatises, transmitted his name with great glory to the knowledge of posterity. I do not recall that any of them have so far come into my hands. Yet his notable and famous work is reported:

Histories of the Times: 1 book.
Letters to various persons: 1 book.

He flourished in the times of Frederick I, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Anfredus of Auxerre, monk of the Cistercian order, once a hearer and disciple of Peter Abelard, but, lest he should also become an imitator of his error, betook himself to the instruction of Saint Bernard of Clairvaux; under his discipleship, distinguished in doctrine and religion, he composed little works of reading not to be despised, by which he also made his name known to posterity:

Against Peter Abelard: 1 book.
On the Apocalypse of John: 1 book.
Letters to various persons: 1 book.

He flourished in the times of Frederick I, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Peter, cleric of the church of Reims, called de Riga, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and among all the teachers of his time most learned in the sacred letters, a notable grammarian and philosopher, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote not a few volumes in both styles, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these I have seen his notable metrical work on the Bible, which he entitled Aurora, as follows:
