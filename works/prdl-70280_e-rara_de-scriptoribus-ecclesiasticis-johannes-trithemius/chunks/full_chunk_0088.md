And since, in the attribution of the homilies, according to custom they wrote the name of the author as the venerable priest Bede, being unable to proclaim him otherwise while he was alive, the title once assigned at the beginning has prevailed down to the present day, so that he is called Bede the Venerable rather than Saint Bede. For while he was living it was not permitted to name him otherwise, even if he was holy. There are those who invent other reasons for this title “venerable”: some from the inscription of the epitaph, others imagining that Bede was blind. But they are wrong, for Bede was not blind, nor do we know that such an epitaph was inscribed on his tomb. And indeed, were I not consulting brevity, I could very easily refute these ravings.

He died under the emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 732, in the fifteenth indiction, in the seventy-second year of his age, on the day before the Kalends of June.

*Gregory, the second pope*, a Roman by birth, sat in the chair of Peter after the pontiff Constantine for sixteen years and nine months. He was certainly a learned and most holy man, who ordained Boniface, an English monk by nation, as bishop of Mainz. He wrote certain works full of learning, among which the following are reported:

*To the Questions of Justinian*, one book of responses.
*Against the emperor Leo*, one book.
*To John, bishop of Constantinople*, one book.
*Letters to various persons*, one book.

He died under the emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 731, in the fourteenth indiction, on the third day before the Ides of February. Gregory III succeeded him.

*Boniface*, the first archbishop of Mainz, a Scot by nation, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, when he was a boy of five years was offered by his parents to a monastery in England called Mischele; there, devoting himself to letters and morals, he became a learned and most holy man. After he had been ordained priest, from love of pilgrimage for Christ he came into Germany, and there, preaching the word of God, he was ordained bishop of Mainz by Pope Gregory [II?]; by his successor Gregory III he was raised to be archbishop and primate of Germany. Since he was a learned man, he taught the people committed to him not only by word but by writings. Of his works I have found only:

*Letters to various persons*, one book.
*The Life of Certain Saints*, one book.

He died while preaching in Frisia, crowned with martyrdom, in the times of the emperor Pippin, in the year of the Lord 755, in the eighth indiction, in the thirty-sixth year of his episcopate, on the Nones of June. Blessed Lullus, whom he had ordained archbishop after himself, caused his body to be brought back to the monastery of Fulda, situated in Buchonia, and honorably buried.

*Verus*, bishop of Seville in the province of Baetica, where Isidore had once held the episcopate, was a man learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, sharp in intellect, brilliant in speech, distinguished no less by life than by learning. For the edification of the faithful, especially of the church of Orange in Gaul, he wrote:

*The Life of Bishop Eutropius*, famous for miracles and virtues, one book.

And certain other things. He is said to have flourished in the times of the emperor Pippin, in the year of the Lord 760.

*Walafrid*, abbot of Saint Gall, as is said, a German by nation, was a man devoted to the divine scriptures, most learned in all secular letters in his time, distinguished in talent and eloquence, strong in verse and prose, a monarch of regular discipline, father of many monks. For the edification of all the faithful he wrote with devotion:

*The Life of Saint Gall* in prose, one book.
The same afterward in verse, one book.
*On the Divine Offices*, one book.
Several letters to various persons.

And he is said to have written some other things, which have not come into our hands. He flourished in the times of Augustus Tiberius, around the years of the Lord 700.
