*On Ecclesiastical Writers, fol. 39*

Sermons to monks, one book, “I fear in Christ, venera...”
Decree of the three councils of Arles, three books, “Concerning the rest I have found nothing.”

In the third sermon, which is on the monk’s battle against vices, he acknowledges both himself and those to whom he speaks as professed monks of the order of the Rule of our most holy father Benedict. He flourished in the times of the emperor Constantine, son of Heraclius, and of his sons, in the year of the Lord 670; under them he presided gloriously over the synod of Arles.

Audoenus, who was also called Dado, bishop of Rouen, a Gaul by nation, brother of Saint Ado the monk, a learned and most holy man, founder of the monastery of Rebais, since he was learned both in the holy Scriptures and also in secular letters, wrote in a very elegant style to Rupertus, bishop of Paris:

The Life of Saint Eligius, bishop of Noyon, three books, and certain other things.

He flourished with great holiness, virtues, and signs in the times of the above-mentioned emperor Constantine, in the year of the Lord 660.

Theodorus, seventh archbishop of Canterbury, consecrated by Pope Vitalian, a Greek by nation, from Tarsus in Cilicia, a monk of our order in the city of Rome, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, second to none in his time in secular letters, highly skilled in Greek and Latin speech, excellent also in verse and prose, renowned no less for holiness than for knowledge: after holding a synod in England, he made many provisions for the English church. Among other monuments of his talent he wrote an outstanding work on the kind of penance to be imposed on those who had offended in many crimes. The title of this work is:

The Penitential of Theodore, one book; some letters to various persons.

He died, not without a reputation for holiness, under Justinian the Younger, in the year of the Lord 690, in the third indiction, in the seventy-eighth year of his age, and the twenty-second of his episcopate.

Leo, the second pope, a Sicilian by nation, sat in the chair of Peter after Agatho for only eleven months. A man most learned in divine and secular writings, sufficiently instructed in the Greek and Latin languages, subtle and eloquent, no less holy than learned, he translated from Greek into Latin the sixth synod, which had been celebrated in the city of Constantinople in the time of Pope Agatho, his predecessor; in it Macarius, bishop of Antioch, was condemned with his accomplices, Cyrus, Sergius, Honorius, Pyrrhus, and Peter, bishops, who taught that in our Lord Jesus Christ there was only one will and operation. He also wrote:

Many letters to various persons, one book, and certain similar things.

He died under the emperor Constantine, son of Constantine, in the year of the Lord 677, in the fifth indiction, on the fifth day before the Nones of July.

Adamnan, priest and abbot of the monastery of Iona, which Saint Columba once built on the island of Scotland, a man very studious and learned in divine writings, not ignorant of secular letters either, cautious in mind, sweet in speech, distinguished in life and conduct, not without a reputation for holiness, was the father of many monks. Among other things he wrote, in a sufficiently composed style, a volume:

On the Places of the Holy Land, one book, and certain letters.

He flourished under Justinian the Younger Augustus, in the year of the Lord 690.
