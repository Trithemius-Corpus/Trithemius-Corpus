Anselm of Laon, scholasticus, very devoted to the divine Scriptures and skilled through long practice, and nobly learned in the disciplines of secular letters, acute in intellect, scholastic in speech, distinguished no less in life than in knowledge, explained the Scriptures of both Testaments in a new kind of exposition, with interlinear and marginal gloss from the writings of the Fathers. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1110.

Hugh, priest and monk of Saint-Victor at Paris, of the order of regular canons of Saint Augustine, abbot there, as they say, a Saxon by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, inferior to none of the ancients in secular philosophy, who was regarded in his time as a most celebrated teacher, like a second Augustine; subtle in intellect, adorned in eloquence, and venerable no less for his way of life than for his learning. He wrote many, almost innumerable, little works, of which few have thus far come into our hands:
