Heinricus de Dolendorp, of the Order of the Brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned by long exercise in the divine Scriptures and perfectly instructed in the disciplines of worldly philosophy, which they call Aristotelian; acute in intellect and very capable at debating and unraveling scholastic questions. He wrote, for the instruction of readers, certain excellent volumes. Of these I have found only four books:
On the Sentences.
Of the rest I have found nothing.
He flourished in the times of Charles IV, emperor, and Urban V, pope, in the year of the Lord 1370.

Gallus, abbot of the monastery of Aula Regia in Bohemia, of the Order, as they say, of the Cistercians, German by nation, a man skilled by long exercise in the divine Scriptures and rich in reading of the ancients; an outstanding contemplator of divine things, subtle in intellect, clear and orderly in speech, and also distinguished in the regular conduct of life. For the edification of his brothers he published certain excellent volumes, by which he sent his name on to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there is extant a great and notable dialogue between father and son, which he entitled:
The Pomegranate: three books. Son, when you approach service.
Sermons to the brothers: one book.
Today there is great use of this volume, The Pomegranate, among truly devout religious, because it is suitable for beginners, for those making progress, and for the perfect.

Richard, archbishop of Armagh and primate of Ireland, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy and canon law; eminent in intellect, scholastic in discourse, and of excellent industry in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote some little works of his intellect, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. When he once preached to the people on the matter of mendicancy, he put forward nine conclusions; offended by these, the mendicant friars appealed before the people to the Apostolic See. Therefore, when both the archbishop and the friars appeared at Avignon before Pope Innocent VI and the cardinals, in the year of the Lord 1357, on the eighth of the month, Archbishop Richard publicly set forth, read, and pronounced:
his Propositions: book [unclear].
He also wrote Sermons: book [unclear].
Questions on the Sentences: book [unclear].

He flourished in the times of Charles IV, emperor, and Innocent VI, pope, in the year of the Lord 1370.

Matthew of Kraków, Polish by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy; ready in intellect, scholastic in speech. Raised to the master's chair in the Prague school, he earned great praise from everyone for his learning. He wrote many excellent little works, though only a few of them have come into our hands. I have seen his work, not to be rejected, addressed to Heinrich, bishop of the church of Warmia, on predestination, namely that God made all things well; the speakers in this dialogue are father and son. He entitled it:
Rationale of Divine Works: seven books. I should like it to be explained how.
Also On Contracts: one book. On the transfer of temporal things.
