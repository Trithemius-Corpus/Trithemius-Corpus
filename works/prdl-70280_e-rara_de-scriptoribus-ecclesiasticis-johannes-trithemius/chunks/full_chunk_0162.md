William of Monte Laudinium, as they report abbot of the monastery of Poitiers, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man exceptionally learned in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, of the greatest Aristotelian philosophy, and not ignorant of pontifical law; subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech. He published several little works of great authority, by which he made the memory of his name known to posterity. Of these the principal are reported:

On the Sacraments: one book.
A Summa on Cases: [unclear] book(s).
Various Declarations.

He also published many other little works, especially in law, and arranged various glosses of canons; concerning these I have so far been able to find nothing.

Guido, priest of the church of Ferrara and afterward, as they report, bishop, a man long practiced in the divine Scriptures, rich in reading of the ancients, and nobly learned in secular letters, a philosopher, a distinguished poet, wrote several little works, both in verse and in prose, of not contemptible reading, by which he made his name known. Of these I found only a volume written in verse to Pope Clement V, on the New and Old Testament, imitating Peter of Riga, which he entitled:

The Pearl of the Bible: one book. Its noble beginning: "Of the earth."

Of the others I have seen nothing.

He flourished in the times of Henry of Luxembourg, the seventh emperor, and Pope Clement V, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Peter of Saxony, a German by nation, of the order of Friars Minor, learned in the holy Scriptures, not ignorant of secular philosophy, and nobly learned in canon law, wrote certain excellent volumes of his intellect, which in his time were held in esteem. Of these there is extant a distinguished work useful to confessors:

A Summa of Cases: [unclear] book(s).
Sermons on the Season: one book.
Sermons on the Saints: one book.

The rest which he is said to have composed have not come to my knowledge.

Orusianus, an Italian by nation, a Florentine by homeland, formerly a hearer and disciple of the physician Thaddaeus, a man indeed learned and educated in philosophy and medicine, but by no means fortunate; for that reason, bidding farewell to the world, he entered the Carthusian order and profitably changed the art of the flesh into medicine of the spirit. He had previously written some little works in his faculty, by which he made his name known. Of these there is extant a commentary:

On Galen's Tegni: one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished in the Carthusian order in the times of Albert, Roman emperor, in the year of the Lord 1300. And thereafter he is reported to have written various treatises pertaining to spiritual edification.
