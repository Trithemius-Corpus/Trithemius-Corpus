John Duns, a Scot by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor, once at Paris a hearer of Alexander of Hales, an Englishman; a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and most learned in Aristotelian philosophy, and so profound that his writings are penetrable by few, and for that reason also less used. He published certain well-instructed volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there are extant:

Cupientes: On the prologue.
Also Quodlibets: “All difficult things,” he says.
On the knowledge of God: “Because the highest knowledge for us.”
Questions on the Metaphysics: 1 book.
Sermons on the season: 1 book.
“There shall be signs,” etc. On this Gospel.
Sermons on the saints: 1 book.

He is said to have written commentaries on the Gospel and the Apostle, and to have composed various other treatises, which have not come to my knowledge. He died in the time of Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1308, in the sixth indiction, and was buried at Cologne among the Friars Minor.

Simon, a monk of Haselgem, of the Order of Saint Benedict; a man most studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, subtle in talent, distinguished in eloquence. The counsel of our divine lawgiver Benedict, by which the monk is commanded to love the divine Scriptures, he so turned into practice that nothing in this life was sweeter than to read sacred letters and to turn over diligently the little works of the holy fathers with zealous study. He abridged:

The Moralia of Pope Gregory: 10 books.
He wrote sermons on the Song of Songs: 1 book.
The vision of a certain convert: [unclear] book.
Several sermons: 1 book.
Also letters to various persons: 1 book.
And many other things.

He also collected excerpts from the sermons of blessed Pope Gregory on Ezekiel; also from the Conferences of the holy fathers, from Richard of Saint Victor on the contemplation of the twelve patriarchs, and from various sources various things not to be despised.

Peter of Bella Parthica, a Burgundian by nation; a man most ready in doubtful questions of law, both civil and canon. He wrote many excellent volumes on the laws, by which he transmitted the memory of his name to posterity. Of these the following are reported:

On the Code: 9 books.
On the Institutes: 3 books.
On the Old Digest: 14 books.
On the New Digest: 1 book.
One hundred disputations: 12 [books].
Several counsels: 3 books.
And certain other things.

He flourished at the same time as Dinus Mugellanus, under Emperor Albert and Pope Boniface VIII, in the year of the Lord 1300.
