She also wrote other things.

In all her little works Catholic doctrine shines forth, which either confirms faith or instructs morals; nor did she ever say or write anything that could be called into doubt. And although she was ignorant of the Latin tongue, nevertheless, by revelation of the Spirit of God, she dictated everything in Latin and fittingly, with notaries taking it down. We have seen many letters of Conrad III and Frederick I, emperors, and of the bishops of Mainz, Trier, Cologne, Bremen, Jerusalem, and almost innumerable others, who, moved by the fame of her sanctity and revelations from diverse regions of the world, commended themselves in writing to her prayers and sought answers to various questions. She died under Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1180, in the eighty-second year of her age, in the thirteenth indiction, on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of October.

[unclear] Innocent, Pope III, by nation a Campanian, formerly called Lotharius, after Celestine, the third pontiff, presided over the Church for eighteen years: a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, since from his boyhood he had devoted himself to letters in the school of Paris, and in life he proved no less upright than learned. He left not a few little works of his talent. Of these, under the name of Lotharius the Levite, or also of Pope Innocent, the following are current:

On the utility of the human condition: books 3. “Why from the womb of the mother...”

On the seven penitential psalms: book 1. “There are chiefly three things in which...”

On the office of the Mass: book 1.

On the cloister of the soul: book 1.

The old decretals: book 1.

Several sermons: book 1.

Letters to various persons: book 1.

He flourished at the time of the discord that was between Philip, brother of Henry, and Otto, duke of Saxony, in the year of the Lord 1200.

[unclear] William of Auxerre, as they say a bishop, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, ascending the theological chair in the school of Paris, shone with renown for a long time, and among all the doctors of that time was celebrated with the greatest fame and reputation. He wrote a very distinguished and useful volume, or:

A Summa of Theology: book 1. “As the Apostle says: Faith...”

On the divine offices: book 1.

He is said to have written many other things also, which have not come to my knowledge.
