He first transferred to the University of Basel the doctrine of those Parisians who are called Realists; there he planted, strengthened, and increased it. He was also one of the chief initiators and founders of the Tübingen school. Finally, after freely resigning several benefices, he was called to a canonry and the office of preaching in the distinguished church of Basel; and at length, after a few years had passed, leaving everything for the love of Christ, he took the habit of the holy Carthusian order. He wrote many excellent little works, by which he made himself useful to those then living and memorable to those to come. Of these, we have obtained access to only a few. The following, however, are extant:

An Introduction to Grammar, one book. “Since grammar is [the art] of speaking correctly.”

A Dialogue on the Art of Punctuation, one book. “Student: Greetings, master.”

On Exponible Propositions, one treatise. “So that from youth in the art of logic.”

On the Art of Resolving Sophistical Arguments, one treatise. “Since for every science.”

An Explanation of All the Books of Aristotle’s Logic, one book. “To investigate and to discover.”

An Explanation of the Books of the Same Author’s Physics, one book. “The book of the Physics.”

An Explanation of the Six Books of the Same Author’s Metaphysics, one book. “Because first philosophy.”

On the Things Required for a Worthy Priest, one treatise. “Man to the image.”

A Resolver of Doubts Occurring Concerning the Mass, one treatise. “The greater the office.”

A Summary of the Passion of Jesus Christ, one treatise. “At night Jesus, being sorrowful.”

On the Ascension of the Lord, among the sermons of Meffret, one sermon. “Arise, O Lord.”

On Meffret’s Sermons concerning the Immaculate Conception, one treatise. “Since the Lord and Redeemer.”

Very many sermons, both to the clergy and to the people, one book.

Various orations and letters, one book.

Also several other things of which no mention is made here.

Moreover, he divided many books by various authors into treatises and chapters, placing summaries before each, by which what the writer’s intention is in them can clearly be known. Among these are Cicero’s books On Duties, On Friendship, On Old Age, and On the Dream of Scipio. Likewise the books of Aristotle’s Logic. The books of the same author On the Soul, and the books of the Metaphysics. Likewise the books of Ephrem the deacon. The books of John Chrysostom On Compunction of Heart. The book of Saint Augustine On Contrition of Heart; the book of the same author’s letters. And almost all the books of Saint Ambrose the bishop. He is still living in the Basel Charterhouse, and is composing various works; by his example and writings he calls many to holy studies, under Maximilian, most merciful king of the Romans, and the supreme pontiff Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1493.

Angelus de Clavasio, an Italian by birth, of the order of the Friars Minor of Saint Francis, a man devoted to and learned in the study of the divine scriptures, and a very celebrated jurist; outstanding in talent, renowned in eloquence, devout in life and conduct. He is also said to have produced certain excellent little works, by which he spread his name far and wide. Among these there is extant a notable summa on cases, very useful and necessary for simple confessors, and nevertheless for learned men who are occupied, now already dispersed everywhere, which he titles from the author’s name:

The Angelic Summa, one book. “Although the angelic spirits.”

Additions to the Same, one book.

And certain other things.

He completed this summa under Frederick III, emperor, and the supreme pontiff Sixtus IV. And I hear that he is still alive while Maximilian governs the kingdom of the Romans and Germans, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1493, the twelfth indiction.
