He is distinguished today, an old man and advanced in age, at the court of the count of Württemberg, in the highest esteem; and he is said to be writing certain things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

Georgius of Gemmingen, a German by nation, provost and canon of the renowned church of Speyer, a learned and noble man, professor of both laws and a most celebrated interpreter, and in the other disciplines of the liberal arts excellently skilled, an outstanding philosopher and rhetorician, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, excellent in verse and prose: he wrote certain distinguished little works, in which, laudably exercising his talent, he also made his name known to posterity. Yet he conceals what he writes, so that he may avoid the appearance of empty glory, or may correct what he has written with the file of a more mature examination. Of these, however, the following have become known to us:

On the method of duel, one book.
On engines of war, one book.
On the care of soldiers’ horses, one book.
Certain orations.

In addition, he has produced many letters, both to me and to others; from these, how much he is worth, both in learning and in eloquence, and also in experience of many matters, is easily perceived by the person who reads them. He is distinguished, in manly age, in the city of Speyer, intent upon sacred letters and writing various things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, in the year of the Lord 1495.

Johannes Venetus, of the Carthusian order, of the house of Saint Andrew the Apostle de Litore, near Venice, a man zealous and learned in the divine scriptures, excellent in talent, devout in life and conduct, and an outstanding cultivator of regular observance: he wrote, for the instruction of the simple, little works not to be despised, by which he made his name known. Of these there is an excellent work, which he entitled:

Know Thyself, three books; “Since nothing [unclear], nothing...”
On Patience and Humility, one book; “Brothers most dear in Christ...”
The Mirror of the Dying, three books; “After the manner of good parents...”
Also The Crown of Old Men, one book; “While the human mind...”
Various Sermons, one book.
Several devout letters.
And certain other things.

He flourished at Venice under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV; he completed the works written above in the year of the Lord 1482, in the thirteenth indiction. And I am uncertain whether he is still living.

Johannes of Mylbach, a German by nation, a man zealous and learned in the divine scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy either, distinguished in talent, scholastic in speech, who formerly taught theology gloriously for a long time in the school of Erfurt, and instructed very many disciples in that faculty: he wrote some distinguished little works, in which he magnificently exercised his talent and commended knowledge of himself to posterity. Among these he regularly lectures on:

On the Epistles of Paul, fourteen books.
Various Sermons, one book.
In Praise of Saint Jerome, one book.
Various Questions.
And certain other things.

He is distinguished at Erfurt, an old and feeble man, a prebendary in the monastery of Saint Peter, in the year of the Lord
