*Book C*

He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1480; and under Pope Innocent VIII he died three years before this.

Nicolaus of Kreuznach, German by nation, from the diocese of Mainz and the county of Spanheim, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, distinguished in life, an acute disputant and an outstanding preacher of sermons, taught theology publicly for many years in the university of Vienna, and both among the scholars there and with Emperor Frederick III obtained great praise as the reward of his learning. He wrote certain excellent little works, by which he made his name known to posterity; among them these are reported:

Questions on the Sentences, four books.
Various Sermons, two books.
Many Collations and Orations, one book.
On the Conception of Saint Mary, one book.

And many other things, which, since he kept them hidden until his death, have not yet been able to come to the knowledge of our reading; yet they will come day by day. He died at Vienna in Austria under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1491, in the ninth indiction.

Petrus Scotus, German by nation, a native of Strasbourg, canon of Saint Peter there, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and very learned in both laws, and also highly educated in the other humane arts; a most celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, and poet; excellent in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct. He wrote certain modest and most elegant little works, both in verse and prose, by which he commended himself to posterity. On Saints John the Baptist, John the Evangelist, and John Chrysostom he wrote a beautiful and delightful work:

In elegiac verse, one book.
Praises of Jean Gerson, one book.
Most elegant Letters, one book.

He died at Strasbourg and was buried in the church of Saint Peter, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1491, in the ninth indiction, and in the thirty-first year of his age. Brought to perfection in a short time, he fulfilled many times; he was taken away, lest wickedness should change his understanding.

Nicasius of Voerda of Mechelen, German by nation, a Brabantine by homeland, deprived of his eyes from the third year of his age and therefore naturally wholly ignorant even of all letters, displayed, as it were, a great miracle in our time, a second Didymus of Alexandria, when he became most learned in every doctrine and science, both divine and human. For in the university of Cologne, where he died, he taught law publicly; the books of both laws, which he had never seen, he learned by hearing, retained in memory, and recited openly. In the university of Leuven he first received the degree of master in arts; afterwards he directed schools for some time in Mechelen; then, having returned to Leuven, he was made licentiate in sacred theology; by a special apostolic dispensation he was ordained priest; he preached publicly, heard confessions, ministered at the altar, and, though blind, recited the Gospel before everyone, using his mind as his book; only at the altar did he not celebrate the divine offices by himself. Finally, in the academy of Cologne, by the consent of all the doctors, he was made doctor of canon law, and also a cautious interpreter of the imperial laws. He published a very celebrated work, which he read extraordinarily in that same university of Cologne, with many taking it down:

On the Institutes, four books. “Because those things which are well begun...”
On the Sentences, four books.
Various Sermons, one book.
Diverse Questions.
