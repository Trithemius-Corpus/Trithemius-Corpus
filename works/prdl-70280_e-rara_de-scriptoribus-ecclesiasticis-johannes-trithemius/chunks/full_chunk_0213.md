Antonius of Genoa, of the Order of the Hermit Friars of Saint Augustine, a man learned in the divine scriptures, a professor of canon law, a most celebrated interpreter, outstanding in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote certain little works of reading not to be despised, by which he transmitted his name with praise to posterity. But I have not deserved to see any of them, except that which is entitled:

A compendium of moral figures, one book. To religious men in Christ.

The rest have not come to me.

He flourished in person at the general Council of Constance, under Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1418.

Johannes de Imola, a Bolognese by homeland, a very celebrated doctor of both laws, excellent in talent and distinguished in eloquence, whose learning and eloquence splendidly illumined all Italy in his time, is said to have published many distinguished volumes in both laws, whose authority among professors of this faculty is raised up with the highest praises. Among these, the following especially are reported:

On three books of the Decretals, three books.

On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, one book.

On the Clementines, one book.

On the Digest Infortiatum, fourteen books. Before we come to...

On the Digestum Novum.

And certain other things.

He died at Bologna in the times of Emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugene IV, in the year of the Lord 1426, in the fourteenth indiction.

Antonius of Parma, of the Camaldolese Order, prior general, a man devoted to and learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular literature, excellent in talent, composed in eloquence, and sufficiently fit for delivering sermons to the people, wrote several distinguished little works for the guidance of preachers of the word of God, by which he made his name known. Of these the principal ones are reported:

Sermons for the Temporale, one book. When [unclear] time had drawn near.

Sermons on the saints, one book.

Sermons throughout Lent, one book.

And certain other things.
