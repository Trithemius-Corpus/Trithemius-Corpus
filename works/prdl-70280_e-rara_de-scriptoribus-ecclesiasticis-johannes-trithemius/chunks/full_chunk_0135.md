Jordan, of the order of Preachers—after Saint Dominic, as they say, master general—a Teuton by nation, a man exercised and sufficiently erudite in the divine Scriptures, a distinguished preacher of the word of God, and venerable no less for sanctity than for knowledge; by his example and mellifluous teaching he instructed many unto piety. He is reported to have written some little works, the reading of which is judged not useless to preachers and devout brothers. Of these they report, [composed] from [his] office of priorate, to the brothers of his order:
Several exhortatory letters: 1 book.
Sermons to the people: 1 book.
On the institution of the order: 1 book.
He flourished under Frederick II, emperor, at the same time as also blessed Dominic, in the year of the Lord 1220.

Ubertus de Bobio, professor of both laws and a most sagacious interpreter, subtle in talent and luminous in scholastic eloquence, who among the ancient professors of this science was held among the principal doctors, transmitted his name with glory to the notice of posterity by teaching and writing, giving to the rest an example of learning and an aid to progress. For he wrote a notable Summa which he entitled:

And not a few others, which, however, have by no means come into our hands.

Bernard Dorna, a Provençal by nation, once hearer and disciple of Azo of Bologna, a most renowned man, a most learned professor and interpreter of both laws, who, provoked by the example of his most erudite teacher, accommodating himself wholly to letters, wrote various little works, by which he dedicated his name to immortality. Of these there is extant a volume
On the conceptions of [legal] bills:
A distinguished [work]: 1 book.
And others which I have not seen. He flourished in the times of Frederick II, emperor, in the year 1240.

Accursius, an Italian by nation, sprung from the city of Florence, once hearer and disciple of Azo, a man most skilled in both laws, and not meanly learned in the other disciplines of secular letters, subtle in talent and renowned in eloquence—how great his authority is at this time among the professors of law, no one even moderately learned is ignorant. He wrote many illustrious little works, and was the first to gloss the whole civil law:
On the Old Digest: 24 books.
On the New Digest: 12 books.
On the Inforciatum Digest: 13 books.
On the Code: 11 books.
On the Institutes: 3 books.
He published, besides, not a few others, which are most well known to the students of law.
He flourished under Frederick II, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1240.

Ubertus de Bonacurso, an Italian by nation, a man skilled in both laws and not ignorant of secular philosophy, prompt in talent and open in eloquence, who among the ancient expounders of law was of no obscure repute. He composed in that faculty certain illustrious little works, of which I do not recall that any has come into our hands. But there is reported [as] his:
On the preludes of cases: 1 book. ["When, by imperial mandate…"]
And certain [things] on judgments.
But we believe that both these and his other works are not unknown to skilled jurists.
