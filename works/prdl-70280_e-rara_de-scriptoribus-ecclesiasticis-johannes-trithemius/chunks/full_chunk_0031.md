*On Ecclesiastical Writers*, fol. 2

Letters to Severus: two books.
A Symposium in hexameter verse: one book.
An Epitome against the Gentiles: one book.
Entitled On Grammar: one book.
On Paradise, in hexameter: one book. “There is a happy place in the beginning.”
He is said to have written not a few other works besides, but they have not come into our hands.
He flourished under the emperor Constantine, to whom he was a very close friend, in A.D. 320.

Eusebius, bishop of the church of Caesarea in Palestine, surnamed Pamphili, formerly a disciple of the very learned eunuch Dorotheus, a most zealous restorer in the divine Scriptures: although he was an Arian, when he came to the first council of the fathers held at Nicaea, he laid aside his error and, following the judgment of the fathers, at their command composed the creed which is called Nicene; as testimony to his own most pure faith he offered it to them to be read and confirmed. In it he powerfully crushed the errors of the Arians, and nothing was either added to or taken away from his composition, but to this present day it is publicly sung in the church of God. After this, persevering in the orthodox faith, he was most holily brought to his end. He wrote many excellent volumes in Greek. Of these the following are current among the Latins, namely:

Against Porphyry: thirty books.
On the prophet Isaiah: ten books.
Expositions of the Gospel: twenty books.
On Evangelical Preparation: thirteen books. “When what Christianity is.”
Ecclesiastical History: ten books. “The successions of the holy apostles.”
An Epitome of the Chronicle: one book. “Moses, of the Hebrew nation.”
Theophanies: five books.
On the Disagreement of the Evangelists: one book.
Topics: one book.
Apology for Origen: six books.
And On Pamphilus: three books.
On the whole Psalter: one book.
Martyrology: one book.
He also composed many other works, which have not come to my notice.
He flourished under the great emperor Constantine, to whom he was a very close friend because of his outstanding learning and the holiness of his life, in A.D. [320?].

Rheticius, of the Aedui, bishop of Autun, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures, in his time a teacher of renowned reputation, is said to have written not a few little works, especially commentaries on the divine books; but we recall that few have come to our notice, namely:

On the Song of Songs: one book.
Against Novatian: one book.
Saint Jerome censures his exposition on the Song of Songs as inept.
He flourished in Gaul under Constantine the Great, in A.D. 320.

Jacob, surnamed the Wise, bishop of Nisibis, a city of the Persians, a man of great learning and incomparable holiness, one of the number of the confessors under Maximian [unclear], was present at the Council of Nicaea; by the addition of homoousion he condemned the most impious Arius. This man of most holy conduct often freed the flock committed to him from many dangers by his prayers. He wrote for the edification of the faithful, in the Syriac language, not a few little works. Of these there are current:

On Faith: one book.
Against All Heresies: one book.
On General Charity: one book.
