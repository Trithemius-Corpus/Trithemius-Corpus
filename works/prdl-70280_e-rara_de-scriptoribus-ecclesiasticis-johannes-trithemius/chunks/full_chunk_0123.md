Engelbert, abbot of the monastery of Admont, of the Order of Saint Benedict, German by nation, a man very studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and sufficiently instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, distinguished in talent and speech, and no less venerable in conduct than in learning, made his name known by writing and expounding various works, and both by word and by example did things by no means insignificant for the edification of his brothers. For he wrote:

Elegant sermons, one book. *On Vices and Virtues*, one book.

He also published several other things, which have by no means come to my knowledge.

Peter, priest of the church of Troyes, surnamed Comestor or Manducator, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and excellently taught in secular letters, practiced in verse and prose, wrote certain distinguished little works in both styles, whose authority has long since been approved by the Church through usage. He was called Comestor or Manducator because, by citing the authorities of the Scriptures rather frequently in his sermons and little works, he seemed, as it were, to have eaten them into [the belly?] of his memory. Among the other volumes of his talent he wrote a notable work addressed to William, archbishop of Sens, briefly and usefully explaining all the historical books of the Bible, which from usage is now entitled:

*The Scholastic History*, twenty books. “Of imperial majesty is ...”

*In Praise of Saint Mary*, one book. Sermons on the season, one book.

Sermons on the saints, one book. And very many other things. He flourished under Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Peter the Parisian dialectician, called Abelard, French by nation, a man most learned in secular philosophy and nobly instructed in the divine Scriptures, delighting in novelty of terms and attempting to search into things too high for him, overcome by glory, fell into several errors. Through the action of the holy abbot Bernard, he was condemned together with his depraved doctrines in the council of Sens. From this time secular philosophy began to defile sacred theology with its useless curiosity. This Peter wrote several little works, which, if he had not mixed errors into them, could have been of use to posterity. Of these there exists a volume shadowed over with dialectical obscurity, varied in many opinions, which he wished to entitle:

*His Theology*, one book. *Know Thyself*, one book. And certain other things. He flourished under Conrad, the third emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Adelphus, abbot of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures through long practice, not ignorant of secular letters, an indefatigable cultivator of regular observance, so that by word and example he might show himself a useful shepherd to the sheep of Christ committed to him, is said to have fashioned several little works for their benefit, in which a rich vein of talent recommends ornate speech. Of these there exist elegant works:

Sermons to the brothers, one book. *Against the Saracens*, one book. The rest which he is said to have composed have not come to my knowledge.

William, abbot of Saint-Thierry, afterward, as is said, a monk of Clairvaux under the blessed father Bernard of the Cistercian Order, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and also distinguished by holiness of life and conduct, for that reason especially beloved by him, published little works not to be despised in reading. Of these few have come to my knowledge. He began to write, but, forestalled by death, did not finish:

*The Life of Saint Bernard the Abbot*, one book. “About to write the life of your servant ...”

*The Mirror of Faith*, one book. *The Enigma of Faith*, one book. *Sentences of Faith*, one book. To Theophilus, *On the Nature of the Soul*, one book.

*On the Sacrament of the Altar*, one book.

*Meditations for Novices*, one book.

*On the Song of Songs*, incomplete, one book.

*On Paul’s Epistle to the Romans*, one book.

And certain other things. There are some who say that this William, while he was still abbot of Saint-Thierry, wrote that widely known letter to the Carthusians, whose beginning is: “To the brothers of Mont-Dieu, an eastern light ...”; it is ascribed by many to blessed Bernard. He flourished under Conrad, the third emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140. And after he had completed one book on the life of Bernard himself, he died; Arnold, abbot of Bonneval, added four other books to it.
