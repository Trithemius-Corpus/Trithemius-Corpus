Guigo, fifth prior of the Charterhouse, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures through long study, and not ignorant of secular letters either, mild in talent and distinguished in speech, and no less venerable in conduct than in learning, is said to have composed several little works, by which his name was given to immortality. Among these there is extant an excellent work, in lucid style, On the Life of Hugh, bishop of Grenoble, one book, written [unclear];
On the Institutes of the Carthusians, one book;
and certain other things. He corrected with diligent study, according to the line of truth, the letters of the most blessed father Jerome, corrupted by the excessive fault of copyists. He died under the younger emperor Lothair, in the year of the Lord 1137, in the fifteenth indiction, on the sixth day before the Kalends of August.

Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris after being a scholastic, a man most devoted to the divine writings, nobly learned, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, transmitted his name by writing to posterity with such great glory that, down to the present day, the school of theologians everywhere, exercised in his little works, names and holds him as Master with singular veneration. For with sagacious study he wrote, from the sayings of the holy fathers, a commendable work:
Sentences, three books. “Desiring something from.”
On the whole Psalter, one book. “Since all the prophecies the Holy Spirit.”
On all the letters of Paul, fourteen books. “The beginnings of things must be sought.”
Various sermons, one book.
He also composed not a few other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Eckard, first abbot of the monastery of Saint Laurence which is called Aura, in the diocese of Würzburg, in the Bircinian Forest, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, among the Germans in his time, inferior to none of the learned in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote little works not to be despised in reading, by which he displayed the liveliness of his intellect. Of these I have seen only an excellent work, put together in a double style in imitation of Boethius, very useful and necessary for the instruction and consolation of monks and of all the faithful who fear God, which he entitled:
The Lantern of Monks, five books.
