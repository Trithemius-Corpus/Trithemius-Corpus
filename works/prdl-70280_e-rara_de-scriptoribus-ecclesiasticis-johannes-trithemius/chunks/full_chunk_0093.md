Hilduin, abbot of the monastery of Saint Denis at Paris, of the order of the divine father Benedict, archchaplain of the Sacred Palace, to whom Rabanus, abbot of Fulda, dedicated commentaries on the four books of Kings, was a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and very skilled in secular letters, excellent both in verse and in prose. He wrote to the emperor Louis, in both styles:

The Life of Saint Denis, 2 books.
And certain other works.

He flourished under the emperor Louis, son of Charles, in the year of the Lord 840.

Hincmar, archbishop of Rheims, formerly a monk of Saint Denis at Paris, was a man exceptionally learned in the divine Scriptures, second to none in his time in secular letters, distinguished for wisdom and honorable conduct, and most eloquent. He wrote several little works of considerable authority, of which I have found only the following. In the person of the emperor Charlemagne he wrote, with great learning:

To the Church of Ravenna, 1 book.
The Life of Saint Remigius, bishop, 2 books.
To Walo, bishop of Metz, 1 book.
Of many letters, 1 book.
Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished under the emperors Charlemagne and Louis his son, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 800. And he wrote many doctrinal works.

Amalarius Fortunatus, archbishop of Trier, a German by nation, a monk of Luxeuil, and cardinal of the Roman Church, was a learned and most eloquent man, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence. Sent by the emperor Charlemagne to Constantinople to the emperor, he confirmed peace and friendship between them by his remarkable industry and eloquence, as is contained in the deeds of the Treveri. He wrote, among other things:

On Ecclesiastical Offices, 1 book. Enough, and indeed more than enough.
To Louis on the same subject, 4 books.
On the Mysteries of the Mass, 1 book.
Letters to various persons, 1 book.

He died under the emperor Louis, in the year of the Lord 813, in the sixth indiction, around the sixteenth year of his episcopate.

Angelomus, monk of Luxeuil, of the order of Saint Benedict, was a man endowed with a noble talent for expounding the divine writings, so that the ancients, in comparison with him, seem not to have expounded what he expounded. He was also not ignorant of secular writings. At the command of Drogo, bishop of Metz, who was the son of Charlemagne and brother of the emperor Louis, he wrote a most excellent work, and one of such a kind that I do not remember having read its like elsewhere in the holy Scriptures:

On the Books of Kings, 4 books. Incipit: “Therefore, since by very many brothers.”
To Lothar, on the Song of Songs, 1 book. Incipit: “Before the imperial glory.”
On Divine Offices, 1 book.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Louis and Lothar his son, in the year of the Lord 830.
