Gregory, the first pope, from being a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, Roman by homeland: after the pontificate of Pelagius he sat in the chair of Peter for thirteen years and six months. He was most learned in the divine Scriptures, and indeed very learned in secular letters; prince of theologians, splendor of philosophers, and light of rhetoricians; upright and most holy in life and conduct. While he was explaining the divine Scriptures, the Holy Spirit several times appeared to him in visible form, and by invisible teaching disclosed to him the hidden mysteries of those same Scriptures. At Rome, at Saint Peter’s, he held a synod of twenty-four bishops, established many things for the utility of the Church, and promulgated a decree on the immunity of monasteries. He wrote several necessary volumes. Of these the following are reported: To Leander, bishop of Seville.

While he was still a deacon, on behalf of Pope Pelagius he wrote a very distinguished letter to Elias, bishop of Aquileia, who was unwilling to accept the three chapters of the Council of Chalcedon; this letter is not found among his letters. He is also said to have written very many other works: namely, on the Prophets, on the Books of Kings, also on the books of Moses, and on other sacred Scriptures. After his death, before they had yet been copied, his most wicked rivals, envying his glory and holiness, burned them with fire, as John the Deacon laments in the book of his life. The most blessed Pope Gregory died in the second year of the emperor Phocas, which was the year of the Lord six hundred and nine, in the eighth indiction, on the fourth Ides of March, shining with many miracles.

Leander, bishop of Seville, from being a monk, Carthaginian by homeland, from the province of Spain, born of his father Severianus: a most studious man, nobly learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, sweet in speech, most excellent in talent, and also holy in life and conduct and beloved of God. By his preaching and effort Hermingild, son of Leuvigild, the Arian king of the Goths, was brought back to the confession of the orthodox faith, and on the vigil of Easter, slain by his heretical father, received the crown of martyrs. His brother Richard, as soon as after his father’s death he had taken up the kingdom, at Leander’s urging and preaching, renounced heresy and converted the whole people of the Visigoths subject to him to the Catholic faith.
