Franco, scholasticus of the church of Liège, a German by nation, a man splendidly learned in the divine Scriptures and most highly trained in secular discipline, an outstanding philosopher, astronomer, and computist, and no less venerable for religion than for knowledge. He is said to have composed several treatises on the sacred Scriptures, which have not yet come to my knowledge. He wrote to Hermann II, archbishop of Cologne, a subtle and excellent work:

On the squaring of the circle, one book.
On ecclesiastical computus, one book.
And many other things. He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Bernard, a monk of Cluny of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man practiced in the sacred Scriptures by constant meditation, and not ignorant of secular literature, conspicuous also for religion and uprightness of morals, at the command of the venerable Abbot Hugh gathered all the customs of the monastery of Cluny into one volume in lucid language, prefixing this title to the work:

The Customs of Cluny, one book.
He also wrote other things. He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Bernard, a monk of Corvey in Saxony, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, a man nobly learned in the divine Scriptures and most skilled in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech. He wrote to Hartwin, archbishop of Magdeburg, in a lucid style indeed, but against Henry IV:

Against Henry IV, one book.
[unclear]: a volume. And other things which I have not seen. He flourished in the times of the same Emperor Henry, in the year 1070.

Ivo, bishop of Chartres, formerly provost of Saint-Quentin of Beauvais, of the Order of Regular Canons of the divine father Augustine, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and a most cautious interpreter of the canons of the holy fathers and of general councils, and no less revered for holiness than for knowledge; he magnificently reformed his order, long in collapse. After Burchard, bishop of Worms, he wrote from the canons of the saints a concise decretum, which jurists used before the times of Gratian, which he entitled:

Panormia, ten books.
Ecclesiastical Excerpts.
Letters to various persons, one book.
Ivo, to the [unclear] church of Chartres.
And certain other things. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1090.

Hildebert, bishop of Le Mans and afterwards archbishop of Tours, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and second to none in his time in secular teachings, acute in intellect and very eloquent in speech, once a disciple of Berengar of Tours, but an assailant of error, who endured many persecutions and tribulations, even imprisonment, for ecclesiastical liberty. He wrote in both styles, namely metrical and prose, certain distinguished little works, of which few have so far been able to come to our knowledge by reading. He wrote in elegant metre a very beautiful and useful exposition:

On the whole office of the Mass, one book. “I wished to write, since my...”
On his exile, one book. “Recently I was wealthy in...”
