But he also published some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the year of the Lord 590, under the emperor Maurice. He died under Reccared, the most devout prince of the Goths, renowned for miracles.

Priscian the grammarian, by homeland a Caesarean, a man most learned in secular letters, skilled in Greek and Latin eloquence, held in great esteem with Chosroes, king of the Persians, wrote certain distinguished volumes on the grammatical art, which among the ancients were held in great authority. Of these the following are reported:

To Julian the patrician.
On the parts of speech, two books.
On construction, one book.
On accents, one book.
On cosmography, one book.
On natural questions, to [unclear], one book.

He is also said to have written some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished, as some think, in the times of Chosroes, king of the Persians, and of the emperor Heraclius, in the year of the Lord 620.

Marcus, a monk and disciple of our holy father Benedict, and most dearly loved by him, a man learned in the divine scriptures and very skilled in secular letters, an orator and distinguished poet, renowned in morals and manner of life, wrote, after the blessed Pope Gregory, adding some things, running most elegantly in heroic verse:

The Life of Saint Benedict, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished down to the reign of Heraclius Augustus, in the year of the Lord 610.

Fortunatus, bishop of Poitiers, an Italian by nation, a man learned in the divine scriptures and most learned in secular letters, subtle in intellect, eloquent and polished in speech, excelling all the doctors of his age in verse and prose, having been freed from pain in the eyes by [Saint Martin?], came to Tours to his tomb to give thanks; and, having gone on to Poitiers, he was first ordained priest and then bishop there, and was renowned for great learning and religion. He wrote many useful and devout little works in elegant verse. Of these I have seen only the following. To Saint Radegund, daughter of the last king of the Thuringians and captive of the king of the Franks, afterwards abbess of the monastery of Poitiers, in hexameter verse:

The Destruction of the Kingdom of the Thuringians, one book. “[The condition?] of war, a sorrowful lot.”
To [Jovinus?] on Radegund, one book. “You who eagerly lick the Aonian [unclear].”
To Justin the Younger, one book. “Glory of the highest [Father?] and of the Son, and [unclear].”
To Sophia Augusta, one book. “To whom, marrying one equal in merits [unclear].”
On Radegund’s psalm, one book. “After the ashes of the fatherland and [unclear].”
In praise of Bishop Martin, one book. “When he was spreading apostolic light.”
On the venerable Bishop Leontius, one book. “Among whom [unclear] begot [a ray?].”
On praise of the same, one book. “Let every age know [unclear].”
Seventy-seven hymns, one book. “Let every age know [that] [unclear] has come.”
