He also wrote many homilies, sermons, and almost innumerable letters, which have not come into our hands. At length, after many labors and holy exercises, by the treachery of Theophilus of Alexandria and of the empress Eudoxia, he died in exile in Pontus, on the sixth day before the Kalends of February, in the year of the Lord 411, in the ninth indiction.

Severianus, bishop of Gabala, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and an admirable teacher in delivering homilies to the people, was of incredible eloquence. Hence he was frequently summoned to Constantinople by the blessed John, the above-mentioned bishop, and by the emperor Arcadius to deliver a sermon. To this man Saint John commended his people when he was compelled into exile, venerating not only his teaching but also the merit of his life. But Severianus, commending himself to the people more than was fitting, supplied kindling matter for discord between himself and John. But when the patriarch returned from exile, they returned to the bonds of brotherly love, as the homily given afterward in the church of Constantinople testifies. Of the writings of Severianus, a most learned man, few are held among the Latins, and still fewer have come into our hands:

On the Epistle to the Galatians: one book.
On baptism and Epiphany: one book.
In the church of Constantinople: one homily. “At the coming of the Lord and [unclear].”
On the death of the innocents: one homily. “Whither zeal tends, whither [unclear].”

There also survive among us certain other homilies of his, brief indeed but very useful. Concerning his other little works, although many are reported, I have so far found nothing. He flourished under Arcadius and Honorius, at the same time as John Chrysostom, in the year of the Lord 400.

Tychonius the African, defending the Donatist schism, a man who was quite practiced in the divine Scriptures, would have composed teaching sufficiently useful if he had remained in ecclesiastical unity. But because he charged true Catholics as though they were traditors and persecutors, he rightly fell into contempt together with his volumes. Of his writings the following volumes survive:

Against the nations: one book.
That the servants of God are hated by the world: one book.
Against the complaints of all: one book.

They say that he composed many other volumes besides, which have not come to my notice. He flourished under Constans, son of Constantine the Great, emperor, in the year of the Lord 360.
