Ambrosius, a priest, Alexandrian by nation, once a hearer and disciple of Didymus the Blind, learned the divine Scriptures magnificently as Didymus explained them, and composed various expositions on them, which among the Greeks at that time were held at a great price. Of these, the following are reported among us:

Commentaries on Job: [unclear] book.
Against Apollinaris of Laodicea: [unclear] book.
He flourished under the emperors Theodosius and Arcadius, in the year of the Lord 400.

Petronius, bishop of Bologna, a monk in his manner of life, very learned and eloquent in the divine Scriptures, revered no less for holiness than for knowledge, shone with many miracles in life and after death, as is read in his history. Among other things he wrote:

On the Life of the Holy Fathers: [unclear] book.
On the Ordination of a Bishop: [unclear] book.
And other works unknown to us.
He died under Theodosius and Valentinian, in the year of the Lord 385, in the fourteenth indiction.

Pachomius, abbot, a most magnificent founder of the monks of his time, founder of many monasteries, and a man of most holy way of life, shining with many miracles in life and after death, since he was a learned man, instructed in the divine Scriptures, wrote many letters for the edification of the brethren. Of these, the following are reported:

To Abbot Cornelius: one letter.
To Abbot Syrus: one letter.
To the superiors of the monasteries: one letter.
To the brothers working outside: one letter.
Also a Rule for Monks: one book.
He flourished under the emperors Valentinian and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 390.

Aurelius Clemens Prudentius, a Christian poet, a man very learned in secular literature, sufficiently skilled in the divine Scriptures, excellent in verse and prose, was held for fifty-seven years by the loves of the world. He read and expounded many small works of the poets, and from his own vein wrote many profound poems. At last, however, in old age, after rejecting the vanity of the world, he betook himself to the sacred Scriptures; in verse and prose he expounded many hidden things of both Testaments. He wrote many excellent small works. Of these, after the measure of the meter had been made, I found the following:
