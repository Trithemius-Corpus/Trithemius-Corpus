James of Lausanne, of the Order of Preaching Friars of St Dominic, and, as they report, bishop of a certain city, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and skilled by long practice, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, and quite distinguished in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote little works of no contemptible usefulness, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these they report:

On Genesis, one book: “In the beginning God created.”
On Exodus, one book: “I shall come to you,” etc.
On Leviticus, one book.
On the Book of Numbers, one book.
On Deuteronomy, one book: “As in the day [unclear].”
Sermons on the season, one book: “I am fixed with Christ to the cross.”
Sermons on the saints, one book.
Lenten sermons, one book. He also published many other things: lectures on the sacred books, conferences, various determinations, and treatises, which have not come into our hands.

Conrad of Alzey, a town of the diocese of Mainz in the Palatinate, three miles distant from Worms, a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently taught in secular letters, a philosopher, poet, and mathematician most celebrated among the Germans in his time, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, excellent in verse and prose. In both styles he wrote certain distinguished little works, by which he made his mind known. Among these there is extant a volume, adorned with beautiful variety in verses and rhythms, concerning the most holy and most pure Mary, Mother of God, and the redemption of the human race, entitled:

The Work of Figures, one book.
Letters to Various Persons, one book.
Poems also, one book. And many other things.

He flourished around the times of Emperor Charles IV, in the year of the Lord 1370.

Stephen Petringon, an Englishman by nation, of the Order of the Friars of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect and a most acute disputant, and very renowned in delivering sermons to the people. He published little works not to be spurned, by which he brought his name to the knowledge of many. Among these are:

Against the Wycliffites, one book.
A Repertory of Arguments, one book.
A Certain Notable Lecture, one book.
Sermons on the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book. And not a few other things.

He flourished in the University of Oxford, at the time when John Wycliffe was [unclear].

Bertram, bishop of Thessalonica, of the Order of Preaching Friars, a German by nation, vicar in pontificals of the bishop of Metz, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and among the most famous doctors of his time, outstanding in intellect and clear in eloquence, and also an excellent declaimer of sermons. He wrote to Cuno, archbishop of Trier, a distinguished work entitled:

On the Illusions of Demons, one book: “To the most reverend father in Christ.”
To the same, On Schism, one book: “The wise shall possess glory.”
Various sermons, one book.
And several other things.

He died under Emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Urban VI, in the year of the Lord 1387, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of February, in the tenth indiction. He was buried in his convent at [Coeluestinum?].

Thomas Lombe, of the Order of the Friars of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, an Englishman by nation, a man learned in the holy Scriptures by long practice and daily study, and well instructed in the precepts of the philosophers, subtle and keen in intellect; in the school of Oxford, after attaining the magisterial chair, he earned great praise for his learning. He wrote an excellent work:

On the Sentences, four books.
Against the Lollards, one book.
On the Incarnation of the Lord, [unclear].
Sermons, [unclear] books.
And various questions.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the University of Oxford, especially at the time when John Wycliffe in England was exhumed and publicly burned.

Philip Riboti, a Catalan by nation, of the Order of the Friars of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, and provincial prior of Catalonia, a man devoted to and learned in the holy Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular letters, venerable also in life and conduct, and an excellent lover and cultivator of the Carmelite Order. For the edification of the brethren he wrote little works by no means contemptible; in these, showing the fervor of a spiritual heart toward his religion, he also commended the memory of his name to posterity. Among these there is extant a volume not to be rejected, concerning the first institution, enlargement, and confirmation of his order, which he entitled concerning the deeds:

Of the Carmelites in Particular, ten books: “When the Israelite people.”
On Illustrious Men of the Carmelites, one book.
Sermons and letters.
