Algerus, from [being] a scholastic [a] monk of Corbie [Corvey] in Saxony, a Teuton by nation, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man exercised by continual study in the holy Scriptures and rich in the reading of the ancients, and moreover magnificently learned in secular letters, cautious in talent and eloquent enough, strong in verse and prose. He wrote, in a quite urbane style, a distinguished volume:
On the sacrament of the altar: book.
On grace and free will: book.
And certain letters. He is said to have written also several others, which have not come into our hands. ["Because the saints, from the body and…"; "It is to be known that free will…"]
He flourished in the times of Conrad the Younger, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1030.

Albert, monk of Metz, of the order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man skilled by continual exercise in the holy Scriptures, and not ignorant of the arts of humanity which we call liberal. That he might transmit his name to posterity, he wrote certain little works of no contemptible reading, of which there is reported a brief but useful work containing the history of the deeds of his time, which he dedicated
To the bishop of Metz: 1 book.
And certain others.
He flourished in the times of Conrad the Younger, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1030.

Eckebert, clerk of the church of Liège, a Teuton by nation, elegantly learned in the divine Scriptures, and instructed to perfection in the disciplines of secular letters, wrote both in meter and in prose some little works, by which he transmitted his name to the notice of posterity. Of these there is extant a volume composed in elegant meter, which he entitled
On rustic riddles: 1 book.
On the same matter, another, larger: book.
And certain others.
He flourished in the times of Henry III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Leo, pope, the ninth, from [being] bishop of the city of the Leuci which is called Toul, under the archbishop of Trier, sat in the chair of Peter, after Damasus, for six years; previously called Bruno, a learned and holy man. He wrote some illustrious little works, in which the gracefulness of eloquence and the sublimity of the sentences are deservedly commended. Of these are these:
To Constantine the monk-emperor:
On extirpating heresies: 1 book.
To the patriarch of Constantinople: book.
Against the errors of the Greeks: book.
To the bishops of Africa and Egypt: book.
Of letters, to various [persons]: book.
He dies under Henry, king the third, emperor the second, in the year of the Lord 1054, in the 7th indiction, shining with many miracles.

[Humbert,] most erudite in the [holy] Scriptures, and nobly learned in secular letters, renowned in talent and eloquence, whom Pope Leo IX, on account of [his] knowledge of letters, led with him to Rome and ordained a cardinal bishop; [and who,] sent by him to Constantinople to confute the heresies of the Greeks, so confuted and overcame Nicetas Pectoratus, a monk, in the presence of the emperor Constantine and the patriarch Michael, that he anathematized his book which he had written against the Latin church, and cast it with his own hand into the fire to be burned. And whatever he there said or did in disputing, he luminously set down, under the name of "a Roman and a Constantinopolitan," in the manner
Of dialogues: book.
He wrote also certain others.
He flourished under Henry the emperor, the second, in the year of the Lord 1050.
