*On Ecclesiastical Writers*, fol. 97

On the Epistles of Paul: fourteen books. “I have given you as a light to the nations.”

Various sermons: one book. And certain other things.

He is reported, besides, to have produced notable commentaries and lectures on almost the whole Bible, which have not come to the notice of my reading.

Jacobus de Theramo, an Aprucine canon, archdeacon of Aversa, a very celebrated jurist, a distinguished doctor of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, ready, clear and well-ordered in speech, whose authority among the professors of this faculty was great in his time, produced in that discipline certain excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have found only the dialogue on the redemption of the human race, commonly called *Belial*, which the author entitled:

*The Consolation of Sinners*: one book. “After through the tree of knowledge.”

On the Clementines he is said to have written: one book. And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Boniface IX, in the year of the Lord 1390.

Wilhelmus de Oppenbach, a German by nation, a distinguished doctor learned in the holy Scriptures, and nobly trained in scholastic philosophy, sharp in intellect, vigorous and well suited to disputing and resolving questions of the Scriptures, wrote in the theological faculty some not negligible little works, by which he made his name known in the University of Paris. Of these I have found:

On the Sentences: three books.

Disputed Questions: one book.

He is also reported to have written certain sermons and other treatises, and to have composed lectures in the schools, which have not come to my notice.

Heinricus de Langenstein, called de Hassia, a German by nation, a man learned in the aforesaid Scriptures, nobly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect, abundant and expansive, since, after spending many years in lecturing on Genesis and scarcely reaching the fourth chapter of the book, he completed as many volumes. For he was resolved on all matters, copious in speaking and writing, so much so that for him it was a greater difficulty voluntarily to reject noteworthy things that occurred to him than to find new things to say. As I have found from a certain letter of his, I would believe that he was a canon of the church of Worms. He first flourished in the University of Paris, where he earned the laurel of the doctorate; then, for the adornment of the new planting of the University of Vienna, he betook himself there, and by both teaching and writing acquired great glory for his learning. Of his little works the following are reported:

On the Sentences: three books. “In the beginning God created.”

A Prologue on Genesis: five books. “To the reverend father and brother in Christ [unclear].”

On the Schism of Antichrist: one book. “To you I will give the keys of the kingdom.”

On the Contemplative Soul: one book.

On Contemplation: one book.

The Mirror of the Soul: one book. “Seven times in the day I have spoken praise.”

On the Canonical Hours: one book. “Many great princes.”

Against the Astrologers: one book. “Divine wisdom in all things.”

On the Lord’s Prayer: one book. “Hail Mary, full of grace.”

On the Hail Mary: one book. “I believe in God the Father almighty.”

On Contempt of the World: one book. “To the reverend lord Johannes de [unclear].”

Against Brother Thelefforus: one book.
