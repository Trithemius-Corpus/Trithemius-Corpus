When he was still a boy, touched by the divine spirit, despising the things of the world and the affections of his parents, he sought the Agaliensian monastery, and for many years conducted himself fittingly there as a monk. He also built a convent of virgins in the little village of [unclear], which he endowed from his parents' resources. Then, made abbot of the Agaliensian monastery, he disciplined the monks' conduct, managed the property with discretion, and preserved his way of life. After this, by princely violence, he was brought back to Toledo, and there, after the death of his predecessor, was appointed bishop. He wrote many distinguished little works. Among them the following are reported: On the Holy Trinity, one book; On His Own Weakness, one book; On the Virginity of Saint Mary, one book; Notes on the Daily Office, one book; Notes on Sacred Matters, one book; Notes on the Sacraments, one book; On the Knowledge of Baptism, one book; On the Progress of the Spiritual Desert, one book; Hymns of Various Kinds, one book; Various Sermons, one book; Many Epigrams, one book; also On the Mass, one book; Letters to Various Persons, one book; and several others. When this most blessed prelate Hildefonsus had completed to the end the book on the virginity of Mary, the most pure Mother of God, whom he had always been accustomed to serve with all the zeal he could and with the greatest devotion, the most holy Lady of the world appeared to him, holding that book in her hands and thanking him for such service, which she affirmed was most pleasing to her. He, wishing to honor her more highly, established that her solemnity should be celebrated every year on the eighth day before the Lord's Nativity; and that solemnity has now prevailed, so that throughout the whole church of the faithful it is celebrated in honor of her most pure conception on the sixth day before the Ides of December, that is, 8 December. Hence the blessed Mother of God again appeared to him and brought him a priestly garment, which we call an alb, and a chair; concerning these many wondrous and glorious things are told. He flourished.

*Firmianus Lactantius:* formerly a hearer and disciple of the rhetorician Arnobius, a man abundantly learned in secular letters and nobly instructed in the divine scriptures, so that in the art of speaking he easily obtained the first place after Cicero. He taught rhetoric gloriously, first at Nicomedia, then at Rome under Diocletian, having been summoned by him. There, when through a scarcity of pupils he had fallen into poverty, he was at last made tutor of Caesar Crispus, the son of Constantine, already an old man, in Gaul. He wrote many distinguished volumes, of which these are reported: On the Divine Institutes, one book, beginning “Great and excellent”; On the Origin of Error, one book, beginning “Although in the first book”; On False Wisdom, one book, beginning “I would wish it to be declared to me”; On True Wisdom, one book, beginning “As I was thinking and with my mind”; On Justice, to Constantine, one book, beginning “There is no doubt with me”; On True Worship, one book, beginning “What was his duty”; [unclear], beginning “It is well”; To Asclepiades, one book, beginning “I have often observed, Donatus”; On Persecution, one book, beginning “[unclear] I am by no means at rest”; Letters to Probus, three books.
