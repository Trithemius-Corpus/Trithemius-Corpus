Petrus Blesensis, archdeacon of Bath, the close friend of the aforesaid bishop Iohannes Carnotensis, to whom he dictated some letters, was a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and also exceptionally learned in secular letters; acute and forceful in talent, he knew how not to flatter the vices of princes and prelates, but to rebuke them. On account of his learning and the integrity of his life he was held in esteem among kings, princes, and bishops. He wrote many little works, among which these are reported: *On Christian Friendship*, one book, “Because true things have been diminished.” *On Salutary Penance*, one book, “You asked me that I might [write] something for you.” *On Hastening the Passage*, one book, “Who will give water to my head?” *On the Quality of Penance*, one book, “The excessive austerity of your severity.” “You are divided and your heart.” *Against the Depraver*, one book, “Your little exhortation.” Letters, 184 in number, in two books, “Most urgently elected, reverend father.” And *On the Office of a Bishop*, one book, “As often as I recall the words.” *On the Danger of Prelates*, one book, “I give thanks to your grace, since.” *On the Life of Clerics of the Court*, one book, “Asked by you, bishop.” *Letters of Fortune*, two books. He also wrote many other things. He flourished under Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Alexander, the third pope, a Sienese by origin, after Adrian IV sat in the chair of Peter for twenty-one years. He was a learned and energetic man, who endured many injuries from the emperor Frederick; while he resolutely crushed the three antipopes raised up by him against himself, and at last overcame the emperor himself. As a sign of his subjection, he trampled his neck with his foot, saying: “It is written: You shall walk upon the asp and the basilisk, and you shall trample the lion and the dragon.” He wrote elegant letters to various persons, one book; and *On Ecclesiastical Power*, one book; and certain other things. He died in the times of Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1181, in the fourteenth indiction.

Crisolanus, archbishop of the church of Milan, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly learned in secular letters, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence, produced certain works of great authority, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. None of these has come into our hands. There is reported to be his *Against the Greeks* [unclear], one book; *On the Holy Trinity*, one book; letters, sermons, and other various treatises, which are unknown to me.

Hugo Eterianus, a Tuscan by nation, easily the most learned in the Scriptures among all learned men of his time, and exceptionally learned in secular letters, a most eloquent philosopher and rhetorician, perfectly instructed in Greek and Latin speech, whom all Greece admired as he wrote, most subtle in talent and second to none as a disputant. He wrote many excellent volumes both in Greek and in Latin, by which he transmitted his name with glory to posterity. Of these I have found only a certain work, brief indeed, but not to be despised, on the heresies which the Greeks heap upon the Latins, addressed to Cardinal Ardoinus, one book, “Your diligent prudence.” *On the Immortal God*, one book.
