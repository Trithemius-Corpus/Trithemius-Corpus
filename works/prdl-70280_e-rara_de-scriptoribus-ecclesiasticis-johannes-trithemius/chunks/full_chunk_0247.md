*On Ecclesiastical Writers*, fol. 122.

In the year of the Lord 1467, indiction 15.

Johannes Beatus, by nation a German, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular literature, an excellent theologian and subtle philosopher, sharp in intellect, distinguished in life, an outstanding disputant of questions, and notable in delivering sermons to the people, wrote certain excellent little works. Among these the following are reported:

On the Epistle of Paul to the Romans, one book: “Sacred Scripture divinely in ...”

A Preceptory of the Divine Law, ten books.

On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book.

Sermons on the Season, one book.

Sermons on the Saints, one book.

Various Disputed Questions, one book.

He also published a number of other things, which have not come to the knowledge of our reading. He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV in his convent of Tenen, in the year of the Lord 1476, indiction 9, on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of August.

Bartholomaeus Cepolla, a Veronese by nation, a very celebrated jurist and a distinguished cultivator of other disciplines of secular literature, acquired great praise for his learning by lecturing, disputing, and writing in regular course in the gymnasium of Padua. He wrote many excellent little works, and commended the memory of his name to posterity. Among these there is extant a work indeed most subtle, which he entitled:

Cautelae of Law and of Civil Cases, one book: “It is to be noted especially that ...”

On Simulated Contracts, one book.

On Urban Estates, one book.

On Rural Estates, one book.

Also On Military Affairs, one book.

And very many others.

He died in the time of Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1477, indiction 10.

Johannes de Regiomonte, by nation a German, once a pupil of the astronomer Georgius Burbachius, no less than Anaximander of Miletus or Archimedes of Syracuse, very learned in Greek and Latin eloquence, the glory of the Germans, endowed with a perpetual stipend by Matthias, king of Pannonia, and by the consulship and citizens of Nuremberg on account of astronomy and the other mathematical disciplines, gained great praise for his learning not only among his own Germans, but also among the Italians and the French. For, having been summoned to Rome by the supreme pontiff Sixtus to correct the Dionysian paschal computus, while he was preparing many things for posterity, he perished of disease, to the very great loss of scholars. He wrote certain excellent little works, of which the following are extant:

A Very Beautiful Calendar, one book.

Ephemerides, that is, a daily register, one book: “After we have sent out the index ...”

Against Gerardus of Cremona, one book.

Letters and Orations, one book.

And very many others.

He died at Rome under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1476, indiction 9.

Gerardus de Elten, by nation a German, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy, sharp in intellect, distinguished in life: after he had taught theology gloriously for many years in the gymnasium of Cologne, having held the vanities of the world in contempt, in his old age he entered the Order of Preachers for the love of God in the same city of Agrippina, and was soon appointed inquisitor and exterminator of heretical depravity. He wrote, among other things, as they report:

Questions and Sentences, one book.

*Book*

Disputed Questions, one book.

Sermons and Conferences, one book.

Various things also in philosophy.

And very many others.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and the supreme pontiff Sixtus IV, in the year of our Lord Jesus Christ 1475.

Alexander Tartagnus de Imola, a jurist both devoted to study and learned, and well experienced in the rudiments of other secular disciplines, excellent in intellect and of distinguished eloquence, wrote, for the usefulness of those who labor in the study of both laws, certain excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of their number the following are reported:

On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, one book.

Also On the Clementines, one book.

Also On the Code, nine books.

On the Old Digest, twenty-four books.

On the New Digest, fourteen books.

On the Infortiatum, fourteen books.

And certain others.

He flourished in the time of Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1470.
