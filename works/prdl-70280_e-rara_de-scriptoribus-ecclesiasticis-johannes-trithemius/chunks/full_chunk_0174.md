*Johannes Calderini*, an Italian by birth, from Bologna as his native city, formerly a hearer and disciple of Johannes Andreae, a professor of canon law and a very [unclear] interpreter, not ignorant of secular philosophy and the divine Scriptures, ready in talent and clear in speech, wrote not a few volumes in his own faculty; by these, proving his talent through diligent exercise, he transmitted his name to the memory of posterity. Among these I have found a great and distinguished work, divided into three volumes of no small size, which he entitled:
Repertory of Law: two books. A. This expression “a” sometimes.
On the Decretals: five books.
On ecclesiastical interdict: one book. Although many doubtful matters concerning.
Many opinions: one book.
Of the others I have seen nothing.
He flourished under Louis the Bavarian, the fourth emperor, and Pope Benedict XII, in the year of the Lord 1340.

*Paulus de Liazariis*, a hearer of Johannes Andreae of Bologna, a fellow-student of Johannes Calderini, a man most skilled in both laws, nobly instructed in the precepts of the philosophers, distinguished in talent and speech, who among the doctors of law in his time was not the least. He wrote, among other little works of his talent, a distinguished volume, as is said:
On the Clementines: one book.
And certain other things.
He flourished in the times of Louis the Bavarian, the fourth emperor, and Pope Benedict XII, in the year of the Lord 1440.

*Lupold of Bebenburg*, bishop of Bamberg, German by birth and, as they report, formerly a hearer of Johannes Andreae of Bologna, a very celebrated jurist, nobly learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, subtle in talent and distinguished in speech. He published little works not unworthy of reading, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity with great glory. Among these there is extant a distinguished work on the zeal of the Christian religion of the ancient princes of the Germans: one book.
On the rights of the Roman Empire: one book.
And certain other volumes on diverse subjects, which have not yet come to me.
He flourished in the times of Louis, the fourth emperor, and Pope Benedict XII, in the year of the Lord 1340.

*Nicolaus Mutinensis*, a man nobly learned in both laws, especially in the laws, subtle in talent, scholastic in speech, cautious in knowledge and counsel. So that he might leave monuments of his talent to posterity, he wrote certain excellent volumes in civil law, by which he had made himself useful to those then present and known to those to come. Among these are reported:
On the Code: nine books.
On the Old Digest: twenty-four books.
On the New Digest: twelve books.
On the Infortiatum: thirteen books.
And certain other things.
