*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

*Fol. 27*

... by an Arian prince; fearing lest, old and wealthy, he should suffer either exile or loss of his goods, he soon consented to the Arian impiety. But, as he deserved, a cruel end not long afterward finished his life. For after his impious betrayal, when Saint Gregory, bishop of Eliberis, had come to Corduba by imperial decree and refused to communicate with Osius, Osius, moved with anger, asked Clementinus, vicar of Constantius’s prefect, to drive Gregory into exile. The vicar said to him: “I do not dare send a bishop into exile unless you first depose him from the episcopate.” Osius, unable to brook delay, mounted the chair as judge and summoned Gregory by royal edict. When Gregory appeared before the multitude, and Osius, proud in his arrogance, was attempting to pronounce the sentence of deposition, Gregory appealed to Christ with all his strength, that he should not allow so hateful a presumption of his to go unpunished. And behold, while the impious Osius was sitting and wished to deliver the sentence, he twisted his mouth, distorting his neck as well, and, falling to the ground, suddenly expired. Thereupon fear seized everyone; afterward no one dared touch Gregory.

Osius, while still a Catholic, wrote:

To his sister, *On the Praise of Virginity*.

In the aforesaid council, *Sentences*, 18 books.

And several letters.

He flourished under Constantius, a very old man, by whom he was also deceived and perished, around the year of the Lord 390.

Dacius, bishop of Lémica, a city of the Spains, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, renowned in name and eloquence, who, together with Bishop Ursatius, because of the death of Priscillian, whom they had accused at Trier, was separated from the communion of the Church and thrust into exile; there he died. He wrote, however, some works not to be despised, of which these are reported:

*Against Priscillian*, 3 books.

*A Chronicle from the Time of Theodosius*, 1 book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the venerable emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 400.

Eporius, priest and monk, zealous in the divine Scriptures, but trusting too much in the purity of life, used to teach that one lives religiously by free choice and effort, and not by the grace of God, defending the Pelagian error. But, admonished by the Gallican teachers and corrected in Africa by Saint Augustine, he yielded from error; and the heresy which he had formerly preached he afterward sharply attacked. Therefore, giving thanks for his amendment, he wrote:

*A Correction against Heresy*, 3 books.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the younger prince Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 430.

Edulius, priest, a Scot by nation, from early youth a disciple of Hildebert, archbishop of the Scots, a man exercised in the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, left Scotland out of love of learning, came to France, then traversed Italy and Asia, and finally, leaving the borders of Achaea, shone forth in the city of Rome, renowned for wonderful learning. He wrote several works both in meter and in prose, of which I have found only the following. To Abbot Macedonius, a distinguished work according to the sequence of the whole Gospel, which he entitled:

*A Paschal Poem*, in meter, 14 books: “Paschal feasts, whoever...”

*On All the Epistles of Paul*, in prose, 14 books: “But first he unfolds the words...”

*On the Miracles of Christ*, 1 book: “From the quarter of the rising sun.”

*To the Emperor Theodosius*, 3 books: “Leader of the Romulidae, renowned...”

*On the Greater Volume of Priscian*, 3 books.

*On the Second Edition of Donatus*, [unclear] book.
