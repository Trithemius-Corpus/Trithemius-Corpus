Innocent, Pope IV, formerly called Sinibaldus, an Italian by nation, from the city of Genoa, sat in the pontificate after Celestine IV for eleven years. He was a man most learned in Holy Scripture and in law, a lover of religious, who held the general Council of Lyon, in which, with the consent of the fathers, he deposed Frederick from the empire; he made many statutes for the utility of the Church. He likewise wrote certain distinguished little works, on account of whose publication he is worthily numbered among ecclesiastical writers, and is to be transmitted with them to the knowledge of posterity.

Rolandinus de Romanciis, a professor of both laws, a very celebrated interpreter, and nobly learned in secular philosophy, who among the old jurists was not among the least. He wrote in that faculty certain distinguished little works, by which he transmits his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have found only a summa: On the order of crimes, one book. It begins: A little book.

Determinations of questions.

The other works which he is said to have composed have not come to my knowledge.

Bernard, a priest of Compostela, a familiar of Pope Innocent IV, wrote in law certain distinguished little works, which in these times are held in great authority among jurists. Of them I have been able to find a few, namely, a notable apparatus:

On the Decretals, five books. In the beginning of this book [unclear]. The peace-making king, with pious [unclear].

Cases of the Decretals, five books.

Of the rest I have seen nothing.

Guido de Suzaria, a doctor most expert in law, sufficiently instructed in the other disciplines of secular philosophy, subtle and ready in intellect, cautious in counsel, whose authority was great among the old experts in these matters. He is said to have fashioned certain distinguished little works in law, among which there is extant a notable summa which he entitled:

On the actions of cases, one book. On the ordering of cases.

Various declarations also.

And very many other things, which have not yet come into our hands.

Bartholomaeus Brixianus, a professor of canon law, a most learned interpreter, and learned in the other branches of secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote various distinguished little works both in law and on other matters, by which he gave his name to immortality. Of these the following are reported:

A repertory of the Decretum, three books. Since, with the old men supporting [unclear].

Disputations on the Decretals, five books.

Chronicle of Italy, one book.

Letters to various persons, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.
