Gregory, formerly a monk, bishop of Nazianzus, a man of such great learning, integrity, and eloquence that not even his adversaries dared in any way to contradict the things said by him; so much so that, for confirming an authority, it was enough that Gregory had merely said something. He was a cultivator and lover of the monastic life; when, because of the excellence of his learning and the holiness of his life, he was called to the episcopate, he fled and hid himself, preferring to be abject in the house of the Lord rather than to be exalted among the crowd with perishable honors. But almighty God did not will so bright a light to be hidden under the bushel of the aforesaid way of life, since it could be extended to the salvation of many. Found, therefore, by divine indication, he was led by the people to the episcopal see, and immediately, in excuse for his flight, there was dictated the solemn homily in which the governance of souls is shown to be the art of arts. On account of his distinguished learning and holiness of life this Gregory was called Gregory the Great before the blessed pope of the same name. He wrote many small works in the Greek tongue. Of these the following are extant:

On the death of his brother Caesarius: one book.
On the praise of Cyprian the martyr: one book.
On the praise of Bishop Athanasius: one book.
On the praise of Maximus the philosopher: one book.
A denunciation against the same: one book.
On virginity, in hexameter: one book.
On the Holy Spirit: one book.
Apologetic works, when he had fled: one book.
On the Nativity of the Lord: one book.
On the fruitful Epiphanies: one book.
On the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost: one book.
On himself, after returning from the country: one book.
On Jeremiah, in the presence of the emperor: one book.
On the reconciliation of a monk: one book.
On the devastation by hail: one book.
Against Eunomius: one book.
Against Julian the Apostate: one book.

He published many sermons and homilies, and various treatises on diverse subjects, which have not come to my knowledge. At length, worn out by old age, when he saw that he could no longer be adequate for governing the people, he ordained another bishop in his place and returned to the pursuit of his beloved solitude.

Eutropius, monk and priest, a man of most religious way of life, very devoted and learned in the divine Scriptures, and fully instructed in secular letters, is said to have composed some small works. Of these the following alone are known to us by title:

To two virgins, sisters: one book.
Chronicle, and up to his own times: one book.

He flourished under the emperors Valentinian and Valens. In the year of the Lord three hundred seventieth. There are also those who write that he was a disciple of Augustine and flourished in the year of the Lord 430.
