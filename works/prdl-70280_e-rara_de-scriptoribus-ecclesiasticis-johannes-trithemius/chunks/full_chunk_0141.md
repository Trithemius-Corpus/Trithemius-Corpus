Alexander, called de Villa Dei, a native of Dol, of the order, as they say, of the Friars Minor, a man certainly trained in every respect, a grammarian, philosopher, astronomer, and distinguished calculator, wrote not a few little works both in verse and in prose, by which he transmitted his name to the memory of posterity. Of these I have so far found only the following: a volume written in leonine verse on grammar, whose use today in boys’ schools is constant; I do not know whether it is so useful, because of the deficiency of weaker age and the sloth of teachers. He entitled this very work:

*Doctrinale for boys*, three books: “I prepare to write for little clerics.”

*On the ecclesiastical computus*, one book: “Who now at the end of times.”

*On the sphere*, one book.

*On the art of numbering*, one book.

He is also said to have written many other things: sermons, letters, and various treatises, which have by no means yet come into our hands.

He flourished with great religion and learning under Frederick II, in the year 1240.

Eustachius, also called Bonaventura, born at Bagnoregio, minister general of the order of Friars Minor, and thereafter bishop of Albano and cardinal of the holy Roman Church, formerly hearer and disciple of Alexander of Hales, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy: his teacher Alexander, contemplating the gift of his intellect by nature and the purity of his life, used to say: “In brother Bonaventure Adam does not seem to have sinned.” He wrote many, profound, and most devout little works, in which, bringing forth burning words, he kindles the reader’s affection in the love of Christ no less than he illuminates the intellect with holy teachings. For he easily surpasses all the doctors of his time in the usefulness of his works, if you attend to the spirit of divine love and Christian devotion speaking in him. He is profound, not wordy; subtle, not curious; eloquent, not vain; bringing forth flaming, not puffed-up, words. Hence he is read more safely, understood more easily by one who loves, frequented more usefully, retained more sweetly and fruitfully. Many put forth learning; many preach devotion; few, by writing books, have taught both. But Bonaventure both surpassed many and few, since his learning instructs devotion, and devotion instructs learning. Therefore, if you wish to be both learned and devout, be intent upon his little works, which are:

*On the Sentences*, four books: “Explored by profound rivers.”

*Breviloquium*, seven books: “I bend my knees to.”

*Soliloquium*, one book: “Tell me, I ask, O man, if after.”

*Itinerary of the mind into God*, one book: “In the beginning, the first principle.”

*The tree of life*, one book: “I am fastened with Christ to the cross.”

*Exhortatory work to a brother*, one book: “Since, my beloved brother.”

*On the reduction of the arts, etc.*, one book: “Every best gift, etc.”

*Rule of conscience, that is, a small good*, one book: “Behold, I have described it to you in threefold.”

*Itinerary of the mind into itself*, one book: “An evil thing occurs to consider.”

*Compendium of the Scriptures*, one book: “In the beginning of my conversion.”

*On the instruction of novices*, one book: “The question is about poverty.”

*Centiloquium*, four books: “Since according to venerable men.”
