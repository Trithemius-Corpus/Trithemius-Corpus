*On Ecclesiastical Writers, fol. 128*

He flourished under Frederick III, emperor, and Sixtus IV, pope, in the year of the Lord 1484. And I do not know whether he is still alive.

Dominicus Bolanus, a Venetian by nation, a man practiced in divine Scripture and excellently learned in secular letters, a philosopher and rhetorician not to be dismissed, sharp and forceful in talent, composed in speech, published some fine little works of his talent. In these he showed himself a devoted and faithful worshiper of the Christian name, and commended his memory to posterity. Of these I have seen only a notable little work against the detractors of Mary’s purity, which he dedicated to Nicolaus Marcellus, the seventy-first doge of the Venetians:

On the Conception of Saint Mary, one book, “With no small grief.”

Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished at Venice under Frederick III, emperor, and Sixtus IV, pope, in the year of the Lord 1485. I hear that he is still living.

Franciscus Niger, a Venetian priest, a man studious and learned in divine Scripture, a most learned professor of the arts and philosophy, a poet and orator of no slight worth, outstanding in talent and eloquent in speech, wrote various little works of his talent, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these there is extant a work not to be scorned, but to be extolled with great praises, addressed to Leonardus Botta, a Milanese knight, entitled Grammar, fourteen books, “From Annaeus Seneca in the letters.”

Letters of his are reported.

And certain other things.

He flourished at Venice under Frederick III, emperor, and Sixtus IV, pope, in the year of the Lord 1480. I do not know whether he is still alive.

Jacobus de Stralen, a German by nation, a man most learned in divine Scripture and excellently learned in secular philosophy, excellent in talent and distinguished in eloquence, who for many years taught theology gloriously in the school at Cologne, deserved great praise from everyone for his learning. He wrote many splendid little works, various lectures and questions, of which I have seen only:

On the Apocalypse of John, one book, “Jacob saw a ladder in his sleep.”

Of the others I have seen nothing.

He flourished at Colonia Agrippina under Frederick III, emperor, and Innocent VIII, pope, in the year of the Lord [unclear]. He was a very old man, being about eighty years of age. And I believe he is still alive.

Marsilius Ficinus, an Italian by nation, Florentine by homeland, a man most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of divine Scripture, a very celebrated philosopher and rhetorician, most skilled in both languages, the singular glory, ornament, and crown of Platonic theology, wrote many splendid little works in an ornate style, among which are:

[On the excellence?] of Plato, one book, “Just as in [unclear].”

Letters, several books, “Plato, father of philosophers, [unclear].”

And many other things.

From Greek, moreover, he translated many things into Latin. For Petrus de’ Medici, a Florentine, he translated fifty-six treatises of Plato. Also Mercurius Trismegistus, On the Power and Wisdom of God. The book of Xenocrates the Platonist, On the Doctrine of Plato. In addition he translated many other things too, which have by no means come to the notice of our reading. He flourished at Florence under Frederick III, emperor, and Sixtus IV and Innocent VIII, Roman pontiffs, in the year of the Lord 1490. And I believe that he is still among the living.
