Bruno, archbishop of the Ruthenians, formerly a monk of the Order of Saint Benedict, outstanding by birth, holy and in every way worthy of God, since, preaching the catholic faith in Prussia, he lawfully received the crown of martyrdom from the enemies of Christ. He wrote some excellent little works of his own talent, of which there survive commentaries:

On Genesis: two books.

And certain other things. He suffered at the hands of the Prussians under Saint Henry, who was the second king of this name and the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1008, in the sixth indiction. After afflicting him with many tortures, cutting off his feet and hands, they finally, by beheading him, made him a martyr of Christ.

Erhard, a monk of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures, learned by continual exercise, subtle in intellect and ready in speech, was of excellent industry in delivering homilies to the people. By expounding the divine Scriptures he made his name known; by frequent exposition of the same he became an outstanding teacher. He wrote, moreover, little works of reading not to be despised, among which they report:

Commentaries on Genesis: one book.
On Exodus: one book.
On Leviticus: one book.
On Numbers: one book.
On Deuteronomy: one book.
Also of homilies: one book.

He also published some other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Bernold, priest of the church of Constance in Swabia, German by nation, a devout man, zealous and learned in the holy Scriptures, and sufficiently instructed in the discipline of secular letters, clear in intellect and polished in speech, left some little works of his study, which nevertheless have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading. He is said to have composed, for the benefit of the ecclesiastical commonwealth, a volume which he entitled:

The Roman Order: one book.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Marbod, bishop of Rennes, very zealous in the divine Scriptures, learned by reading of the ancients, and nobly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote in both styles not a few volumes, which can no less please readers than profit them. Among these there stands out a notable work which he composed in elegant meter with a threefold sense:

On the Song of Songs: one book.
The Passion of the Theban Martyrs, in verse: one book.

And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Marianus, a monk of Fulda, of the Order of Saint Benedict, Scottish by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, a historian and distinguished computist, leaving his own country, came into Germany as a pilgrim for love of Christ. First at Cologne, at Saint Martin’s, he was made a monk and, from desire for solitude, enclosed himself; he stayed there for two years.
