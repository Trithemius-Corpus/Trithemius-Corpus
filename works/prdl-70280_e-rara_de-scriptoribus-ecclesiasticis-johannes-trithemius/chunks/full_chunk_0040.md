Philastrius, bishop of Brescia, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, was known to and intimate with blessed Ambrose of Milan. Saint Augustine mentions him in a certain letter written to Quodvultdeus. For the common benefit he wrote certain excellent little works, none of which I remember to have come into our hands. With blessed Augustine as witness in the same place, an outstanding work of his is reported, which he wrote:
On 156 Heresies: one book, “The Simonians from Simon ...”
I have not seen the rest.
He flourished under the most Christian emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 380.

Evagrius, a monk, disciple of Saint Macarius the Egyptian, afterwards bishop of Antioch, a man most learned in every respect, educated in both secular and divine writings, skilled in Greek and Latin eloquence, and even in the episcopate preserving the austerity of his former life, was of such abstinence that he was regarded as a marvel. He was the first to commend the life of the holy fathers to writing; from his own vein he published many little works, of which, however, very few have come to my knowledge. The following are reported as his:
To the Anchorites, very useful: one book, “The priest Evagrius to Innocent ...”
Also To the Cenobites: one book.
On the Eight Principal Vices: one book.
Exhortatory Pieces to a Virgin: one book.
He made the Life of Antony Latin: one book.
On the Life of the Holy Fathers: one book.
He also wrote many other things and translated from Greek into Latin works unknown to us.
He flourished under the prince Gratian, in the year of the Lord 380.
