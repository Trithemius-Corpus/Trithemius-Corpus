*On Ecclesiastical Writers, fol. 2.*

Beginning, therefore, with the disciples of the apostles, down to the last year of the emperor Frederick III, namely the year of the Lord 1493, I have drawn up a catalogue of illustrious men. In it I have marked down the names, dignities, writings, and times, not of all, but only of those whom I found, adding opposite them the openings of the titles of their volumes; those I had not seen I passed over with a space. I could not readily find the times at which certain men flourished; nevertheless I placed them in what seemed to me a suitable place. And let the knowledge of one who knows supply this defect in both respects, namely that he may reckon the times of individuals and restore those misplaced to their own places. I confess also that I deliberately passed over many whose writings are not easily found, nor their times. I also know many very learned men still living who are said to be publishing various little works; concerning them, since I have no certain knowledge, I prefer to pass over in silence rather than to ponder uncertainties. And, to use Jerome’s words: if any who still write about the living have been omitted by me in this catalogue, let them impute it not to me but to themselves, since they have hidden their writings [unclear] to come into the light. But I believe that, content with their writings and talent, they will not care about the losses caused by my silence. For of those whom I found, I wished to depict not their praises but their books. This task, indeed, would demand a rather lengthy volume. Go now, therefore, into the world, work completed by our labor; go, offer to lovers of books the treasure which you contain. Why do you fear? Why do you delay? Do not fear to come forth in public; do not dread the hatred of rivals, supported as you are by the patronage of so many saints. Come then, book, go forth, slight indeed in talent, but a displayer of great matters. Why do you tremble as if slothful and useless? Go forth confidently: a thousand will praise you, while scarcely one will blame you. The love of God, by which you were written, may it, I pray, mercifully excuse you always and everywhere, together with your author.

*The End.*

*The book of the most commendable man and reverend father in Christ, lord Johannes of Trittenheim, abbot of Sponheim, On Ecclesiastical Writers, happily begins.*

To the church of the Corinthians: one epistle.

Also to James the apostle: two epistles.

Besides, of the itinerary of his life: books, “Clement to lord James.”

Likewise, of Peter’s disputation: ten.

But these last two volumes are counted among the apocrypha, as is clear in XV, dist. *Sancta Romana*. Other epistles of his are also reported, but apocryphal. One: “When in the city of Rome.”

Clement, fourth bishop of the holy Roman Church from blessed Peter, as some think, by nation a Roman, a holy man beloved of God, adorned also with the beauty of morals, whose life and passion are contained in ecclesiastical writings. He sat in the pontificate nine years and ten days. He was banished into exile for the faith of Christ by [unclear] Constans. He died in the third year of Caesar Trajan, and in the 103rd year of the Lord. Dionysius the Areopagite, an Athenian, an eminent philosopher, converted to the faith by the blessed apostle Paul, was ordained, as some think, the first bishop of Athens, a man of exceptional sanctity and incomparable learning. After the death of the apostles, blessed Clement, bishop of the city of Rome, sent him with Rusticus the priest and Eleutherius the deacon to preach God to the nations in France, another bishop having been ordained in his place.
