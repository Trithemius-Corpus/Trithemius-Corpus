[Jacobus] de Vitriaco, bishop of Acre, and afterwards cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, bishop of Tusculum: a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not unacquainted with secular letters; distinguished in morals and life. In declaiming sermons to the people he was of excellent talent, and, gloriously preaching the cross against the Albigensian heretics, he marked many of the faithful against them by apostolic authority. He wrote little works not unworthy of reading, by which he made his name known to posterity:

The Oriental History, 1 book.
The Life of Mary of Oignies, 3 books.
Against the Saracens, 1 book.
On the Holy Women of Liège, 1 book.
Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.

In addition he composed some other things which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

Bonaguida of Arezzo, a man most learned in both laws in his time, of great authority among the old [teachers?] of this faculty, subtle in talent and clear in speech. He wrote, for the use of students of law, certain distinguished volumes, by which he made himself both useful to those then living and known to those to come. Of these there is extant a notable summa, which he entitled:

Introduction to Advocates, 2 books. “Since the office of advocacy ...”
The Pearl of Law, 1 book. “After the discovery of knowledge ...”
Declarations of Various Cases.

But he is also said to have composed certain other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Petrus de Vineis, German by nation, secretary and chancellor of the emperor Frederick II: a learned and eloquent man, but especially reprehensible in this, that, adhering to the same Frederick and favoring him, he foolishly barked against the Roman Church. By him at last he was worthily rewarded, when in certain matters he had offended him, being deprived of the light together with his eyes. He wrote certain little works of his talent; of these I have found only:

Letters [explona?] of the Emperor, 6 books. “The pontiffs gathered ...”
On Imperial Power, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the same Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.

Antonius, called of Padua, but Spanish by nation, born from the city of Lisbon, of the Order of Minors and a disciple of blessed Francis: a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, since before his entry into the order he taught theology gloriously for a long time at Toulouse, Bologna, and Padua, and left distinguished disciples after him. He was of such holiness that he shone with many miracles both living and dead. He was an exceedingly excellent preacher, so that the supreme pontiff saw fit to call him the Ark of the Covenant, and Saint Francis was accustomed to call him his bishop. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote very elegant and most ardent:

Sermons for the Season, 2 books. “There shall be signs,” etc.; “Isaiah says ...”
Certain Sermons on the Saints, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230. And because of the frequency of his miracles after death he was immediately enrolled by Pope Gregory IX, together with the saints of the Lord Dominic and Elizabeth, landgravine of Thuringia, in the catalogue of the saints.
