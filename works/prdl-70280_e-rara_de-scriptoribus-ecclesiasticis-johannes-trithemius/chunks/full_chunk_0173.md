Johannes de Blomendal, a German by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor, was a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy; outstanding in talent, distinguished in eloquence, and an excellent teacher in delivering sermons to the people. For the edification of the faithful he wrote volumes not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these I have so far found only:

Sermons on the season, one book, “Take your weapons, your quiver.”
Sermons on the saints, one book.
Sermons for Lent, one book.

He flourished under Louis the Bavarian, the fourth emperor, and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1330.

Guido de Monte Rocherii, a man very devoted to the holy Scriptures, rich in reading of the ancients, distinguished in talent, scholastic in speech, wrote little works of his talent not to be scorned, by which he also made the memory of his name known among posterity. Of these I have seen only that well-known work, published in print, which he composed for the direction of simple priests and wished to be entitled:

The Handbook of Curates, three books, “Since, as the prophet says.”

I could not find any others. He flourished in the times of Emperor Louis IV and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

Johannes de Lineriis, a philosopher and the most celebrated astronomer of all in his time, magnificently restored this discipline, which had by now been almost erased from human memory. In this faculty he had as co-workers and associates men most learned in mathematical science: Johannes de Saxonia, Johannes de Muris, and Bernard, distinguished philosopher-astronomers, who themselves wrote various little works. As for the little works of this Johannes de Lineriis, although he wrote many splendid things, I have seen very few, namely:

Canons of the Primum Mobile, one book, “Of any arc set forth.”
Tables of the same, one book.
On the Sphere also, one book.

Of the rest I have found nothing. He flourished in the times of Louis the Bavarian, the fourth emperor, and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1330.

Johannes Danck of Saxony, a German by nation, contemporary of the aforesaid Johannes de Lineriis and associate in his studies, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher and astronomer, among all of his time most celebrated, subtle and outstanding in talent, published many splendid and admirable little works, by which he transmitted his name with glory to posterity. But up to now I have found very few:

Canons on eclipses, one book, “The quantity of the eclipse of the sun.”
Canons of the tables, one book, “The time of the fourth aspect.”
On the Astrolabe, one book.
On ascensions, one book.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Louis IV and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord [unclear].
