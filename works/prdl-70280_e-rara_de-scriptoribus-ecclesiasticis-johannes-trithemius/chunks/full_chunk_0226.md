Georgius Trapezuntius, apostolic secretary, a man most learned in every respect, and a celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, and poet, who, appointed by Pope Eugenius to the public school at Rome, taught rhetoric and poetry for many years, and left behind many disciples most learned in every science. He wrote certain distinguished volumes, by which he made his name known to posterity, among which are extant:

A very fine rhetoric: 1 book.
A singular dialectic: 1 book.

Various orations and letters.

[Florianus?] de Sancto Petro, an Italian by nation, from Bologna by homeland, a man easily the most learned of all his time in civil law, and not ignorant of divine scripture or secular philosophy; acute in intellect, clear in speech, a subtle disputant, and a cautious compiler. He wrote in that faculty certain distinguished little works, by which he commended the memory of his celebrated name to posterity. Among these, they say, are:

On the Code: 9 books.
On the Old Digest: 24 books.
On the New Digest: 14 books.
On the Infortiatum: 14 books.

He flourished in the time of Emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1435.

Nicolaus Lackman, a German by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor of Saint Francis, minister of Saxony, a man studious and learned in divine scripture and not ignorant of Aristotelian philosophy; outstanding in intellect, and fit for disputation; of excellent reputation in declaiming sermons to the people. He wrote little works not to be dismissed from reading, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of their number, the following are said to be the more important:

On the Sentences: 4 books.
Various questions: 1 book.
Sermons on the season: 1 book.
Sermons on the saints: 1 book.
And many other things.

He flourished in the time of the Council of Basel and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1440.
