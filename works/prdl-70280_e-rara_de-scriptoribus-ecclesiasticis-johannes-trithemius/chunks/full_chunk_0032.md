Thus the whole work already indicated comprises twenty-five books. He flourished under the great emperor Constantine, in the year of the Lord 330.

Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia and afterward of Tyre, learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, a confessor and distinguished defender of the Christian faith and a diligent emulator of evangelical perfection, after publishing many volumes, was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ in the last persecution, or, as others affirm, under Decius and Valerian, at Chalcis in Greece. He wrote quite a few volumes in a polished and well-composed style, of which these are reported:

Against Porphyry: two books.
Symposium on the ten virgins: one book.
On the resurrection: one book.
On the woman with a divining spirit: one book.
On the departure of the soul: one book.
On Genesis: one book.
On the Song of Songs: one book.
On the last times: one book. “For it must be known, brothers.”
Also a Chronicle: one book.

He flourished under the great emperor Constantine, or, as others hold, under Decius and Valerian, in the year of the Lord 255.

Antonius, monk and abbot, Egyptian by birth, a most holy man and an outstanding dweller in the desert, whose praises the whole world resounds, provided many with a rule of religious and blessed life. Though he had not learned letters, nevertheless he was so learned in the divine Scriptures that none of the doctors would presume to prefer himself to him. He had memory in place of books, understanding and sense in place of letters, grace in place of the study of reading. In the Egyptian language he dictated various letters, of apostolic sense and speech, to different monasteries. Of these that distinguished one is extant:

To the Arsenoites: one letter.
To other monasteries: six letters.
