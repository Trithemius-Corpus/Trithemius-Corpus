Gilbert, bishop of Poitiers, formerly a scholastic of Paris, surnamed Porreta, by nation a Frenchman, a man much practiced in the divine Scriptures and splendidly learned in secular letters, subtle in intellect and confused in speech by the novelty of his terms, was accused by blessed Bernard of heresy in certain points; and, convicted in the council of Rheims celebrated by Eugenius, he is said to have renounced the error. There are those who excuse him, saying that the authorities introduced in his expositions were understood by some in a less than sound way. But we, agreeing with the Roman Church, confess that what has once been determined by it may be infringed by no one. Gilbert wrote many little works, of which they report the following:

On the whole Psalter, one book.
On all the epistles of Paul, fourteen books.
On the Gospel of John, one book.
On Boethius, *On the Trinity*, one book.
Books of Questions, etc.
Several sermons, one book.
And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Conrad III, in the year 1140.

Ivo, abbot of Mount Saint Cornelius, of the order of the divine father Benedict, learned in the holy Scriptures by continual practice, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters for his station, endowed with a noble talent for expounding the divine volumes, transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity by writing various treatises. But I do not remember that any of them has come into our hands. His notable work to Milo, bishop of the church of Terouanne, likewise a most learned man, is said not yet to have been seen:

On the Song of Songs, one book.
Several sermons and letters.
And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Lothar, the last emperor, in the year 1130.

Gilbert, bishop of Chartres, studious and very skilled in the divine Scriptures, and inferior to none of the doctors of his time in the disciplines of secular letters, sharp in intellect and sufficiently adorned in eloquence, distinguished in morals and life, and an especial lover and worshipper of the most blessed Mary, mother of God, wrote a notable little work on this prophecy: “The sceptre shall not be taken away from Judah.”

Against the Jews, one book.
On the Nativity of Saint Mary, one sermon, of approved custom.
Various sermons, one book.
He is also said to have written some other things, which have not come to my knowledge. I believe that he flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1100.

Odo, bishop of Cambrai, a man learned in the holy Scriptures and nobly instructed in the letters of the humanities, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, notably distinguished, after the manner of the ancients, in composing and declaiming homilies to the people. He wrote, in dialogue form, the disputation that he had had with a certain Jew on the mystery of the Lord’s incarnation, the first letter of his own name and that of the Jew disputing with him being noted:

To Wilbodo the monk, one book.
On the Canon of the Mass, one book. “There was a certain rich man.”
On the steward of iniquity, one book.
And he composed not a few other things, which have not come to my knowledge.
