Drevaldus, also called Albertus, a monk of the monastery of Fleury, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man distinguished on every side and very learned, made his name known by writing, since for the edification of simple brothers he composed many volumes on the Holy Scriptures in alternating style. Among the other little works of his talent, he wrote, in lucid speech, a history from the time of the translation of the body of Saint Benedict from the monastery of Monte Cassino to the monastery of Fleury down to the time of Odo, king of the Franks:

The Miracles of Saint Benedict, one book.
And many other things which I have not seen. He flourished in the times of Emperor Arnold, in the year 990.

Odo, first abbot of Cluny, of the Order of Saint Benedict, formerly a hearer of Remigius, the monk of Auxerre, a man zealous and learned in the Holy Scriptures, an outstanding restorer of the fallen order of the divine father Benedict, shone forth clearly no less by holiness than by noble learning. He was an excellent musician, arch-chanter of the church of Tours, who, when he had heard the fame of Berno of Gigny, a most holy abbot, entered the order under him; later appointed abbot of Cluny, he adorned the order with great holiness. Since he was an outstanding declaimer of homilies, he composed many discourses to the brothers. Among the other works of his talent he also wrote:

The Life of Saint Gerard, one book.
Praises of Saint Martin, one book.
A Concise Chronicle, one book.
He also composed various hymns and chants in honor of the saints, in sweet and regular melody. At the brothers' request also, he abridged the books of blessed Pope Gregory on Job and brought them together into one volume. He flourished in the times of Emperor Arnold, in the year 920.

Ratbod, the fourteenth bishop of Utrecht, a German by nation, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in talent, distinguished in speech, most holy in life and conduct, shone with many miracles while living and after death. Nanno, a most learned philosopher, had instructed him in all humane letters; he was an advocate in the palace of King Charles the Fat; afterwards he entered a monastery; finally, as we have said, consecrated bishop, he vigorously governed the church of Utrecht for seventeen years in the severe persecution of the Danes. He wrote some excellent little works:

Of which these are reported:
Praises of Saint Martin, one book.
Praises of Saint Boniface, bishop, one book.
On Saint Amalberga, one book.
Of homilies and sermons, one book.
Flowers on Saint Willibrord, one book.
He also composed a complete work on the translation of Saint Martin, and various chants in honor of the saints. He died under Louis, son of Emperor Arnold, in the year of the Lord 917, in the fifth indiction, on the third day before the Kalends of December, in the eighteenth year of his episcopate. He was buried at Deventer, where he had previously been accustomed to reside in his episcopate.
