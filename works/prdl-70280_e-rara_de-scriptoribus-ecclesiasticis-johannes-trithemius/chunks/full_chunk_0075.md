Symmachus, patrician, senator, and Roman consul, father-in-law of Boethius, a philosopher and distinguished orator, and a magnificent worshipper of the Christian faith, whom Theodoric, king of Italy, because of suspicion that he had aimed at liberty, banished into exile together with Boethius at Pavia, and not long afterward cruelly slew. Since he was a learned and most eloquent man, he is said to have composed some little works. Of these I have been able to find only one volume, brief indeed, but elegant:

Letters to various persons, one book: “Surely you laugh because concerning me.”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He died while Anastasius was emperor, killed by Theodoric, king of Italy, at Ravenna, in the year of the Lord 524, in the second indiction.

Avitus, bishop of Vienne, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and most skilled in secular letters, a theologian, rhetorician, and very famous poet, who in his time was valued no less for the purity of his life than for his knowledge of the Scriptures. He wrote not a few little works, both in verse and in prose, but very few have come to my knowledge. Of these the following are said to have been composed in elegant heroic meter:

On the Origin of the World, one book. On Original Sin, one book. On the Judgment of God, one book. On the Flood of the World, one book. On the Crossing of the Red Sea, one book. Letters to various persons, nine books. Many Homilies, one book. And many others. He flourished under the emperors Zeno and Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Godelbertus, priest, learned in the divine Scriptures through constant exercise, rich in his reading of the ancients, and most learned in secular letters; a profound theologian, a famous orator, a notable poet, subtle in intellect, adorned in speech. He wrote in heroic meter a very famous work, running most elegantly through histories and allegories, from the beginning of the world up to the birth of the Virgin. The title of this work is:

Allegories of the Scriptures, three books. But he wrote many other things. He flourished, as they say, in the times of the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Eugippius, priest and abbot, learned in the holy Scriptures, singularly devoted to reading the books of Saint Augustine, and sufficiently instructed in the letters of the secular arts; also renowned in life and conduct of monastic discipline. Some letters of Saint Fulgentius of Ruspe are said to be addressed to him. He wrote certain necessary little works. Of these I have found only the following. He excerpted a large volume, containing three hundred chapters, from the books of blessed Augustine the bishop, addressed to Proba. Its prologue begins thus: one letter, “To the lady deservedly venerable.” The book itself thus: one book, “What I have written to you, honorable one.” His Rule of Monks, one book. He flourished under Emperor Anastasius, according to Honorius the priest; but according to Sigebert he flourished under Tiberius Constantine, in the times of Pope Pelagius II, in the year of the Lord 580.

Benedict, the first abbot and founder of Monte Cassino, by homeland a man of Nursia, founder and chief of the monks of the West, a man of most holy conduct and illumined by the prophetic spirit. From his order afterward many most learned and most holy men became famous. He converted countless peoples from iniquity to Christ by word and example. From his order the Roman see has had eighteen pontiffs, more than two hundred cardinals, sixteen hundred archbishops in various churches, almost four thousand bishops, and outstanding abbots who became famous for doctrine, writings, or life, seventeen thousand.
