*On Ecclesiastical Writers, folio 27*

He wrote to me certain not inelegant letters, in one of which, rather fully, he wove together the whole history of his fortune; and, when questioned by me some time ago, he humbly confessed that he had made the aforesaid little works.

He died at Cologne and was buried in the greater church, under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1492, the tenth indiction, on the seventh day before the Kalends of September.

Christophorus Persona, a Roman, prior of Saint Balbina in the City, of the order of the hermit brothers of Saint William, was most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, highly skilled in the Greek and Latin languages, since from his earliest age he had imbibed those letters in Greece itself under Greek teachers, so that he seemed to have been born from Greece; subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech. Among other things he wrote:

Letters to various persons, one book.

And many other things.

He translated into Latin Origen against Celsus the philosopher, eight books, for Pope Sixtus IV. Also twenty-five moral sermons of Saint John Chrysostom, which he dedicated to Marco Barbo, cardinal of Saint Mark. But certain little works of Athanasius of Alexandria, with many others, he translated [unclear] and gave them to be read by Latins.

He died at Rome, carried off by plague, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1486, the fourth indiction.

Hermolaus Barbarus, a Venetian by nation, a patrician by birth, archbishop patriarch of Aquileia, and at last bishop cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, was a man devoted to the divine Scriptures, most learned in secular letters, a very celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, poet, astronomer, and geometer, highly skilled in Greek and Latin speech, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, devout and distinguished in life and conduct, and most worthy of every ecclesiastical honor. From adolescence he devoted zeal and labor to good letters and holy morals; he became such and so great that he left no second after him. Beginning to write in the eighteenth year of his age, he composed many very elegant little works, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of their number the following are reported: a very beautiful work:

On Celibacy, one book.

Plinian Corrections, one book.

Against Barbarous Philosophers, one book.

On the Married Estate, in verse, one book.

Questions of Geometry, one book.

The Agreement of Astronomy and Medicine, one book. His Lucubrations.

Precepts for Writing History, one book.

Compendium of Philosophy, one book.

Compendium of Galen, one book.

Letters to various persons, one book.

Multiple Orations, one book.

Of poems, more than twelve [unclear], one book.

And many other things.

The Plinian Corrections, a large work, which he began at Rome as Venetian ambassador and completed as patriarch, he dedicated to Pope Alexander VI; in it he healed nearly five thousand wounds of copyists. Once, in the year of the Lord eighty-six, when he was passing through Cologne on his way to Mainz, at the request of Theodericus [Flas?] of Neuss, a physician, he composed a little book on how far astronomy agrees with medicine. To Marcus Antonius Sabellicus, the historiographer, he published a book on the precepts for writing history. He interpreted Aristotle’s Rhetoric, the whole Dialectic, and also Dioscorides; he composed elegies, letters, metrical orations, up to twelve thousand poems, as we have said. He translated into Latin the responsal treatise of the patriarch of Constantinople on the Catholic faith, in reply to the questions of Mehmed, the great Turk; also Plutarch On Isis and Osiris, and the same author’s Why Oracles Ceased.
