John Beleth, once a distinguished theologian of the Paris school, learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in secular philosophy, ready in talent, scholastic in speech, not unknown among the doctors of his time, brought his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity by writing and disputing. For he wrote a not useless:

Summa on the divine offices, one book. In the primitive Church.
Also various sermons, one book.

He is said to have written besides not a few other things, but they have not come to my knowledge.

Theoderic, abbot of the Order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, no less distinguished in conduct than in knowledge, is reported to have composed some little works. Of these I have seen only, addressed to certain abbots:

Life of Saint Hildegard, four books. In the Roman commonwealth.
And certain letters.

He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Werner, otherwise Irnerius, an illustrious and notable man, professor and most renowned interpreter of civil law and an excellent cultivator of the other humane letters, who was deservedly called the lamp of the law, because he was the first to expound civil law with glosses and, without example before him, gave to others an example of illuminating the law. And from this time professors of both laws always flourished in abundance and began to explain the volumes of his instruction down to the present day. He taught in both, at Rome as well as at Bologna, for a long time; there he had very many and excellent disciples, who by their learning spread the fame and glory of their master. His glosses and outstanding monuments on the laws are extant, very well known among professors of this field.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year 1200.

Peter, abbot of Cluny, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man learned in the divine Scriptures by constant practice, gentle in talent, sweet and composed in speech, and sufficiently capable of persuasion, devout and praiseworthy in conduct, and a distinguished zealot for regular observance, making his name known to posterity by writing, composed certain devout little works for the benefit of readers. Of these the following are reported:

Revelations of many, one book. He received the letters of your affection.
On eremitic life, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.

He also published some other works, which have not yet come to my knowledge.

Peter Cantor of Paris, and afterward, as they report, a bishop, a man most deeply learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, presided gloriously for many years over the theological school at Paris and instructed many excellent disciples. He also wrote certain outstanding little works, of which I remember that a few have come into our hands. The volume entitled:

The Abbreviated Word, one book. He made the abbreviated word.
Another book of distinctions, one book. Abel is called the beginning of the Church.
Grammar of the theologians, one book.
Various sermons, one book.
