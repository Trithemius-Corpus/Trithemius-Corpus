*Letter*

*Responsive letter of the reverend father lord Johannes de Trittenhem, abbot of Sponheim, to the venerable and most learned man, Father Albert Morderer, giving the reason why he placed many professors of secular studies among ecclesiastical writers.*

Brother Johannes de Trittenhem, abbot in Sponheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict, sends greeting to Brother Albert Morderer, of the Order of Friars Minor of the Observance, his friend. You say, dearest Albert, that you wonder why I placed professors of secular letters, who wrote nothing ecclesiastical, among illustrious men and ecclesiastical writers, since Jerome, Gennadius, Isidore, Honorius, Sigebert, and the other assessors of learned men are found not to have done this at all; judging it unworthy that men devoted to secular disciplines, who labored in nothing for the edification of the universal Church, should obtain a place among ecclesiastical writers. I do not wonder that you wonder; the rest wonder too. Therefore, by answering you I shall satisfy all who hold the same opinion. And first, indeed, why others did not do what you wonder that I have done; but [now] why I did what they did not do, I shall briefly recount.

Indeed, I by no means judge professors of the good arts who remain in the Catholic faith, philosophers, orators, and poets, who write anything useful and honorable in their field, unworthy of the catalogue of illustrious men; for I know that they help the Church by their studies. For I do not think that anyone can be called sufficiently learned in the divine Scriptures if he has been ignorant of the discipline of secular literature.

But how will the theologian learn it, if there is no one to hand it down? Who does not know how greatly a Christian and faithful philosopher benefits the Church, who destroys the errors of the pagans, lays bare arguments and deceptive syllogisms, and confounds the doctrines of unbelievers? Read the histories of the ancients, and you will find that deeds support faith in words. How often orthodox philosophers, by disputing, destroyed the cunning of heretics, overcame pagans, and conquered schismatics! Why should I use many words? Every school of theologians could [unclear] from philosophy for those already studying.

For the rhetorician unlocks the figures of the Scriptures, makes tropes clear, unties knots, shows colors, explains doubtful matters, and makes the shadows of words clear. When he assumes the office of an orator, he will easily destroy the allegations of heretics and refute their errors. Who, finally, could worthily pursue the praises of a good orator, if he carefully weighs how much power he has in persuasion? Would that all theologians of our time cultivated oratory: how much fruit they could produce in the Church of God by their exhortations. A theologian who is an orator is said to have the minds of his hearers in his own power; by his persuasions they are easily moved toward those things that are holy and just.

But we do not reject Christian poets either, whom we do not doubt to come closer to our theory, who under the shadow of a real or fictional event show us, in sweet brevity, what is to be done or avoided. Nor are we unaware how great an injustice poets today suffer from certain undigested and raw theologians, who condemn all poets in one sentence, declaring them liars and foul-speakers, as if those who imitate the songs of ancient poets could not be truthful and good. Far be it that a good poet cannot be good and truthful, one who had the holy fathers Augustine, Jerome, Sedulius, Prudentius, Bede, and innumerable others as predecessors in this art. But it is not my office, nor the task of this time, to refute the sloth of these men.

Therefore, to close the letter, I thought that faithful and Christian philosophers, orators, poets, and cultivators of the other good arts, who wrote something useful, should be counted among ecclesiastical writers for three reasons.
