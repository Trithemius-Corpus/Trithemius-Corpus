Nor does he ever cease writing. He is still living under his king Maximilian, devoted to letters and cultivating his talent with liberal arts, in the year in which we write these things, 1493, indiction 12.

Sebastianus Titio, otherwise Brant, of Strasbourg, German by nation, a distinguished professor of both laws, and excellently learned both in divine Scripture and in the other disciplines of secular literature, not moderately skilled in poetry, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, outstanding in counsel and action, who by his learning and nightly studies wonderfully adorns Basel, the renowned city of Germany. He wrote, both in verse and in prose, certain little works of by no means contemptible reading, by which he made his name well known. Of these there are extant:

*On the titles of both laws*, book 1: “Since it is difficult.”

*A Sapphic Rosary of the divine Mary*: “Of the human stock.”

*Riddles in praise of the Virgin from the Old Testament*: “What things equal.”

*He proves the virginal conception possible*: “Come hither, O.”

*The amazement of Mary when the angel arrived*: “What do you wish for yourself.”

*The angel’s salutation*: “Sent from on high.”

*On the wondrous places of the blessed Virgin*: “May your, O Virgin.”

*The virginal compassion*: “Son of the Father.”

*Christ’s consolation to his mother*: “Why do you groan, O.”

*On Christ’s blows*: “Look upon what things I have endured.”

*On the ingratitude of those eating manna*: “By lot under this.”

*In praise of Saint Sebastian*: “Why should I attempt your.”

*Life of Saint Onuphrius*: “With our verses, I pray.”

*In praise of Saint Valentine*: “With praises, O prelate.”

*In praise of Saint Ivo*: “The beginning of the song.”

*Life of Saint Bernard*: “O would that for me.”

*In praise of the order of the Charterhouse*: “By the spirit of the holy.”

*On contempt of the world*: “You, whoever you will be.”

*Praises of blessed Christiana near Basel*: “Afterwards, because by merits.”

*Against slanderous advocates*: “The manners of advocates.”

*On the captivity of King Maximilian*: “Hear, O kings.”

*Elegy on the death of Emperor Frederick*: “A wondrous thing lately was.”

*To the divine King Maximilian*: “What does it profit, renowned king.”

*Of various epigrams*, book 1.

*Commendatory poems for various printed editions.*

Besides these, with wonderful art and talent he compiled, in the common and vernacular language, a little book which he called *The Ship of Narragonia*. In it he so elegantly expressed the cause and root of all follies, censured the morals of men, and handed on certain salutary remedies to each person, that one would rightly call that work not the book of fools, but rather a divine satire. For I do not know whether anything more salutary for the uses of the present age, or more delightful, could be read. They say that he is striving greatly so that this same work may soon appear also in Latin, both in verse and in prose. He is still living at Basel, being 35 years of age, and printers make very great use of his work and industry in embellishing their books. For he writes various things under Maximilian, king of the Romans; since, being singularly diligent, he has composed several very charming poems about him and to him, even secret ones, as they say, in the year in which we write these things, 1493.
