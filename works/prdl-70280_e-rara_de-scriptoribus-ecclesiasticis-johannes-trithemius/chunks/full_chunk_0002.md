To the learned and excellent man Master Johannes de Amerbach, Parisian master in the liberal arts, Brother Johannes de Lapide, monk of the Carthusian order, humble and unworthy professor of sacred letters and of the same study, wishes abundant greeting in the Lord.

Recently, most beloved brother in the Lord, you showed me a certain book, composed by the reverend and most learned father, Master Johannes de Trittenheim, abbot in Spanheim, entitled *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, asking that I look it over by reading it; if I should judge it worthy of your printing, by which, multiplied, it could come into the knowledge and use of very many, you promised that you would take pains for the matter to be completed more quickly. Wishing to accommodate your affection, to which it is not right for me to deny anything, and taking it into my hands with a willing and ready spirit, I read it, although I suspected that you had already decided what you were going to do. When it had been carefully inspected and examined, I found that no work more opportune for these times of ours could have come into my hands, nor anything more acceptable to all men of letters.

For many splendid volumes are found, whose titles or authors, since they are either unknown or uncertain, come to be reckoned among apocrypha; and from this they fall utterly into neglect and contempt, as no one who thinks rightly is ignorant. Furthermore, especially now among readers, very many printed books are found which we know have been unjustly taken away from their own authors and falsely ascribed to others. Whether this has been done through the ignorance of copyists, or from hatred or favor, or for the sake of some advantage, it is not for me to judge. Yet the result is that wrong is done to the former, the latter are vainly praised for the studies of others, and it offers readers various occasions for error. I [unclear] concerning honorable matters, whether they are dead or are still living, so far as it could be done, he gathered into one volume, assigning to each his several works. But since an office of this kind is very difficult and of immense labor, and requires an industrious man, a knower of deeds done, untiring in readings and in the turning over of books, no one has been found up to this present time who presumed to attempt it, except that virtue mentioned before, breathed upon and raised up by the divine Spirit, who dared to undertake it with his little study, in a singularly admirable manner and order, with true narration and elegant speech; he began it and happily completed it. To him, indeed, the whole company of learned men past, present, and future will by no means be able to render fitting and due thanks.

You have, therefore, excellent man, what I think of the book offered to me, and how highly I esteem it. For this reason, not only by my counsel but by my exhortation and request, may you wish to take care that, as soon as it can be done, you submit it to your press and complete it. For thus you will make all cultivators of the good arts, together with the compiler, indebted to you with everlasting praise and remembrance. Moreover, you will receive a fitting reward from the rewarder of all who labor for his glory, the Most High God. Farewell, most beloved brother. From the Carthusian house of Basel, the fifth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord 1493.
