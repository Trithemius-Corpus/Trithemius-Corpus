Johannes Plaeth, a German by nation, an ornament and pillar of the University of Heidelberg, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, nobly a doctor in Aristotelian philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, in life and conduct, so far as the secular estate permits, an excellent teacher in delivering sermons to the people, wrote, among other things, a great and distinguished volume, which he publicly read as an ordinary lecture in the aforesaid University of Heidelberg:

On the first [book], chapter [unclear], of the Decretals, one book, “Venerable fathers and lords.” Against the Bohemians, one book, “As the wise man says.” Questions on the Sentences, one book. Many sermons. Various questions.

And certain other things. He flourished under Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1430.

A Heidelberg man, learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular philosophy, an acute disputant, an excellent declaimer of sermons, formerly chaplain and secretary of Ludwig, Duke of Bavaria and Count Palatine of the Rhine, and often envoy in many difficult causes, wrote little works not to be despised in reading, by which he left to posterity the memory of his name. Among them they report the following:

Against the Hussites, one book, “When lately in the year of the Lord 1421.” Against the Feymeri, one book, “The law of the Lord [unclear] outwardly mortal.” On Contracts, one book, “As in the forum of the soul, where [unclear].” An oration before the King of England, one book, “Glory to God in the highest.” An oration before the Duke of Savoy, one book. Several letters, “My word is to you, O father.” Various questions, one book. Diverse orations.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugene IV, in the year of the Lord 1430.

Raymundus Sebeide, a Spaniard by nation, a man notably zealous in the divine Scriptures, who by teaching and writing in the University of Toulouse gave great proof of his learning. He is said to have written many excellent little works; of these I have seen only a large volume which he had publicly read in the aforesaid university. At its beginning he promises many glorious things, concerning whose truth some applaud themselves as if experienced; he entitled it:

On Creatures, or On Man, one book, “To the praise and glory of the Most Holy.” Disputed Questions, one book. The rest are unknown to me.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugene IV, in the year of the Lord 1430.

Franciscus Barbarus, a Venetian patrician, a disciple in Greek letters of Chrysoloras, the Byzantine philosopher, a man most learned in secular letters, not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, a famous philosopher, and a supreme orator in both languages. While still young he wrote certain little works not to be rejected in reading, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among them there is extant the work which he entitled:

On Wifely Matters, one book.
