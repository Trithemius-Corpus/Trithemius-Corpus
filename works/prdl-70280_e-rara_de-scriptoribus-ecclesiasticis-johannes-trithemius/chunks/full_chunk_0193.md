Nicolaus Rizonis, by homeland a Toulousain, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, and provincial prior in the kingdom of Sicily: a learned man, distinguished in life and conduct, an excellent preacher of sermons, subtle in intellect and lucid in speech. He published some little works on the Scriptures, whose [unclear] will not be contemptible to preachers of the word. Of these they report:

Sermons on the Temporal Cycle, book 1.
Sermons on the Saints, book 1.
A twofold Lenten collection, books 2.

Concerning the other things which he composed, nothing has yet come to my knowledge.

Henricus de [Kalkar?], by nation a German, of the Carthusian order, and prior of the house of Saint Barbara in Cologne Agrippina: a man having an intellect enlightened in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant either of secular letters, notable in life and conduct. He published little works not to be despised in reading, whose [unclear] is judged very useful for religious and devout brothers. Among them they report these:

Monastic Exercises, book [unclear].
A [Loquagum?] on Rhetoric, book [unclear].
A [Cantuagum?] on Music, book [unclear].
On the Origin of His Order, book [unclear].

And certain other things, pertaining especially to the cleansing of consciences. He flourished under the emperor Wenceslaus, in the year of the Lord 1390.

Richardus Maydescon, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, by nation an Englishman: a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and excellently taught in secular philosophy, which they call Aristotelian; bright in intellect, scholastic in speech, and a distinguished preacher. He wrote many commendable little works, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these I have found:

Against the Lollards of Wycliffe.
Sermons on the Temporal Cycle.
Sermons on the Saints.
The rest are unknown to me.

He flourished in the school of Oxford especially at that time when the heresy of John Wycliffe began to spring up in England; and he wrote various things.

Marsilius de Inghen, by nation an Englishman: a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and most nobly taught in secular philosophy; the first founder of the Heidelberg school, which Rupert, duke of Bavaria and count palatine of the Rhine, magnificently endowed when he established it, and Pope Urban VI confirmed on the model of Paris; also, as Wimpheling testifies, canon and treasurer of the church of Saint Andrew of Cologne. He published little works not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these, in the library of Heidelberg, they have today, written in his own hand:

Questions on the Sentences.
A Notable Dialectic.
Commentaries on Aristotle.
And certain other things.

He died under the emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Boniface IX, in the year of the Lord 1393, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of September, in the second indiction. He was buried at Heidelberg in the church of Saint Peter. There are some who write that he was a German; to them I freely grant their judgment. It is certain, however, that he was called from the school of Paris to Heidelberg for the beginning of the university.

Johannes, monk of the monastery of Castell, in the diocese of Eichstatt, of the order of Saint Benedict, by nation a German: a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular letters; excellent in intellect and eloquent in speech; a distinguished theologian, philosopher, and rhetorician. He wrote many excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these I have found only: a great and most famous work, divided into two volumes, which with immense and commendable study [unclear]

On the Rule of Saint Benedict.
A Breviary of the Bible.
Sermons on the Temporal Cycle.
Sermons on the Saints.
Sermons on the Passion of the Lord, 42.
Letters to Various Persons.

Moreover he published many other things, which have not yet come to my knowledge.

Conradus de Monte Puellarum, by nation a German, canon of the church of Regensburg: a man learned in the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect and bright in eloquence. First, receiving the rudiments of the liberal arts in the school of Erfurt, he then betook himself to the university of Paris, where, publicly lecturing to students on philosophy and sacred letters for eight years, he was adorned with the badge of the doctorate. He wrote to Lord Lupold, bishop of Bamberg, a great and noble work which he entitled [Concerning?] the Greeks:

Economics.
Monastics, to the duke of Austria.
Politics also.
Various Questions.

And many other things. He flourished in the times of the emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Urban VI, in the year of the Lord 1390.
