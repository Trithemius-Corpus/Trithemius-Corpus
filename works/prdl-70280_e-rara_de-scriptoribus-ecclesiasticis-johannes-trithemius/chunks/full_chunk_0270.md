Johannes of Kaysersberg, a German by nation, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy, outstanding in talent, distinguished in life and conduct, a very celebrated declaimer of sermons, who for many years has held the office of preacher in the cathedral church of Strasbourg. He has written some commendable little works, but they have not yet come to the notice of our reading. They report, however, that the following elegant works are his:

*Sermons to the People*, one book.
*Various Addresses to the Clergy*, one book.
And certain other works.

This man, at great expense and with long labor, collected the works of Johannes Gerson, many of which he brought to us from France; dividing them into three parts in a very fine order, and preparing a very useful table, he gave the whole to the press. He is still living in the German city of Strasbourg, and composes various works, under the most renowned Maximilian, King of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 1493.

Johannes Pico, an Italian by nation, Count of Mirandola and lord of Concordia, a man most devoted to every kind of good letters, and, as he was most noble by illustrious blood, so also by a truly divine talent; surrounded by a great retinue [unclear] of arts of every kind, with whom he exercises the arena of books and studies, Latin as well as Hebrew and Greek; worthy and most deserving, whose perfection the Muses [unclear]. He is the most learned philosopher of our age and a very acute disputant. He has written in a most elegant style some works which the age will both approve and especially admire, since they have not unworthily made the memory of his name and glory everlasting. Of him there are extant the following, which have already appeared:

*Heptaplus on the Beginning of Genesis*.
Also *Nine Hundred Philosophical Conclusions*.
*Apology*.
Also *On the Psalms*, one book.

And several other works which have not yet presented themselves to our sight. He is still living today among the Italians in the company of his more gifted young men, and he does not cease either to write loftier things or to benefit posterity, under Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, Maximilian, King of the Romans, in the year in which we write these things, 1493.

Johannes Capnion, otherwise Reuchlin, a German by nation, from the town of Pforzheim in Swabia, an excellent doctor of imperial law, a most eloquent counselor of the most illustrious Count Eberhard of Württemberg, and at the same time of the utmost integrity in law and faith; a most skilled investigator of all the most ancient philosophies and literatures. He was always most expert in the three principal languages, namely Hebrew together with Chaldean, and likewise Greek and Latin. He was also especially distinguished in French and in his own more polished vernacular tongue; and most learned both in the secrets of the divine Scriptures and in secular books. Indeed, in every kind of learning he is foremost in this age, as one who for many years has most earnestly penetrated the secret mysteries of the Greeks and Hebrews. Worthy indeed to devote himself to books alone, and not also to the noisy public court. For not only Italy, but not even our age, could offer us anyone equal to so great a man. He has written some works by which he has commended his memory to those now living and to posterity:

A dialogue addressed to Dalberg, chamberlain of the Vangiones, in which he explains all the secret, wonder-working words of Greek, Hebrew, and Christian philosophy; he entitled it *Capnion, or On the Wonder-Working Word*.
*On Rhetorical Preliminary Exercises*.
*On the Diversity of the Four Idioms of the Greek Language*.
A funeral oration on the death of Rudolf Agricola.
