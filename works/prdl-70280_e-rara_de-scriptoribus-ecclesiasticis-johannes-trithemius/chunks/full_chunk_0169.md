Francis Maronis, of the Order of Friars Minor, formerly a hearer of John Scotus, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in the teachings of the philosophers, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, composed in style, constant in study, and sufficiently acute and very well suited for debating and resolving questions of the Scriptures. He wrote not a few excellent volumes of his talent, in which he both showed himself most learned and transmitted his name with glory to posterity. Of these, the following are reported:

On the Sentences, four books: Around the prologue of the first book.
On formalities, one book.
Various questions, one book.
Sermons on the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book.

It is also said that he composed certain commentaries on the divine volumes and various other treatises, which have not yet at all come to my knowledge.

He flourished in the times of Louis the Bavarian, the fourth emperor, and of Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1320.

Bonaventure of Padua, of the Order of the Hermit Friars of Saint Augustine, once prior general and thereafter priest-cardinal of the holy Roman Church with the title of Saint Cecilia, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, ready in intellect and clear in eloquence, revered no less for his manner of life than for his knowledge, of great reputation in delivering sermons to the people; when he was most vigorously defending the liberty of the Church against tyrants, he was at last pierced by an arrow by the lord of Carrara and was crowned with martyrdom. He wrote very useful and elegant works:

Sermons on the season, one book: The desired one will come to all.
Sermons on the saints, one book: But far be it from me to glory.

And certain other things.

He flourished under Louis, the fourth emperor, in the year of the Lord 1320.

William Ockham, English by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor, formerly a disciple of John Scotus, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in Aristotelian philosophy, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence. He wrote certain little works on the Scriptures, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. Acting and writing against John XXII, who condemned the heresy of the Fraticelli, together with Peter of Picenum, whom the emperor Louis had set up as an idol and had caused to be called Nicholas V, and declaring him to be a heretic, he was excommunicated by him. Fleeing to the emperor Louis, likewise excommunicated, he said to him: “O emperor, I will defend you with the word.” And so he remained excommunicated with the excommunicated man until the end of his life, and at last was absolved at death. He was a most vigorous opponent of Scotus his master, and attacked almost all of his sayings. He wrote:

An Apology, one book.
On the Sentences, four books.
Quodlibets also, one book.
On the supporters of heretics, six books: In all curious matters.
On the sacrament of the altar, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Louis, the fourth emperor, and of Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1320. And he was buried in the Munich convent of his order, in the province of Noricum, which is now called Bavaria, of which the emperor Louis had previously been duke.
