Cornelius, after Peter the twenty-second bishop of the city of Rome, Roman by country, son of Castinus, was made pope after the glorious martyrdom of Fabian. He sat in the chair of Peter for two years, a learned and most holy man, until at last he too, crowned with martyrdom, passed, released from the flesh, to those above. He wrote several letters for ecclesiastical utility; of these I have found only the following.

To Fabius, bishop of Antioch:
On synods: one epistle.
To the same, on the Novatians: one book.
On Novatian: one epistle.
On the acts of synods: one epistle.
He wrote several epistles to Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, and other ones to others.
He flourished under Decius, the tyrant prince, in the year of the Lord two hundred and fifty-five.

Cyprian, bishop of the church of Carthage in Africa, a learned and most holy man, gloriously taught rhetoric for many years, and was a magnificent teacher in all secular literature. Then, persuaded by the presbyter Cecilius, from whom he obtained his surname, he was converted to the faith of Christ and distributed all his substance to the poor. And not long afterward, having been chosen presbyter, he was also ordained bishop. Since he was zealous in the divine scriptures, he usefully published many things for the edification of the faithful.

And he wrote the following volumes in the Latin tongue, namely:
On the grace of God, to Donatus: one book. “You advise well, Donatus.”
Against Demetrianus: one book. “While you bark against, and against.”
On piety and almsgiving: one book. “Many great things there are, brothers.”
On the praise of martyrdom: one book. “You have desired, Fortunatus.”
On jealousy and envy: one book. “To be jealous, because it seems good.”
On the good of patience: one book. “About to speak of patience, brothers.”
On mortality: one book. “Although among very many of you.”
On virginity: one book. “Discipline, guardian of hope.”
On the fallen: one book. “Peace, behold, most beloved brothers.”
On the sacrament of the chalice: one book. “Cyprian to his brother Cecilius, greeting.”
On the unity of the Church: one book. “Since the Lord warns.”
On the Lord’s Prayer: one book. “The evangelical precepts.”
On the vanity of idols: one book. “That idols are not gods.”
On the life of clerics: one book. “I had indeed promised you.”
On the two mountains: one book. “The proof of the chapters which in.”
To Antonianus, on Novatus: one book. “We have received your first letters.”
On the Jews, to Quirinus: one book. “The Jews into offence against God.”
To Pope Cornelius: eight epistles. “We have learned, dearest brother.”
On dice-players: one book.
To Julianus, his friend: one book.
Epistles to various persons: one book.

But he is said to have composed many other things which have not come into our hands. The little book On the Twelve Abuses of the Age, ascribed by very many to Cyprian, does not have the flavor of his style. At last he suffered under Valerian on the same day as the Roman woman, but not in the same year, namely in the year of the Lord two hundred forty-nine, on the eighth day before the Kalends of October.

Pontius the presbyter, hearer and disciple of blessed Cyprian, a learned and holy man and an excellent declaimer, after the glorious triumph of his master came into Gaul and at the city of Cimella obtained the palm of martyrdom for the faith of Christ. This Pontius, by the diligence of his honey-flowing preaching, converted the two emperors Philips to Christianity; he persuaded them, the first of the Roman princes, to renounce idols. He also wrote in an elegant style a notable volume, which contains:

The Life of Saint Cyprian: one book.

He flourished under the emperor Philip, in the year of the Lord two hundred forty.

Dionysius, thirteenth bishop of the city of Alexandria, once hearer and disciple of Origen, while he was still a presbyter held the public school under Heraclas and acquired great glory for his learning. For he was a man most learned in every science, a philosopher, rhetorician, and notable theologian. Agreeing with the doctrine of Cyprian and of the African synod on rebaptizing heretics, he wrote very many epistles on that matter to various persons; of these the following are counted as one in number:

On penitence, to Fabius: one epistle.
To the Romans: two epistles.
To Saint Pope Sixtus: two epistles.
To Philemon, presbyter of Dionysius: one epistle.
To Dionysius after he had been made pope: one epistle.
To Novatian the heretic: one epistle.
To the Laodiceans, on penitence: one epistle.
To Origen, on martyrdom: one epistle.
“Dionysius to his brother Novatian.”
