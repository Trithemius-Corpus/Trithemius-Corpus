For he was an outstanding declaimer, learned and eloquent, so much so that in a single disputation he overcame a certain heretic whom no one could refute. He flourished in the aforesaid monastery of Saint Florinus, which is called Schonau, in the diocese of Trier, under Emperor Frederick I, in the year 1160, and on account of his religion and learning he was held in esteem.

Elizabeth, nun and abbess of the sisters of the monastery of Schonau of the Order of Saint Benedict--for there are two monasteries, one of monks, the other of nuns, situated at the edge of the diocese of Trier, not far from the Rhine, in the Hircinian forest--sister of the above-mentioned Abbot Eckbert, was a virgin of most holy conduct, who had received many revelations from God and committed them to writing for the edification of posterity. Of these I have read the following:

The Way of God, a useful work, one book: “It happened at the beginning of the fifth.”

On the Eleven Thousand Virgins of Cologne, one book: “To you who [have] pious affections.”

Various Revelations, one book: “All who are going to read.”

To her brother Eckbert, one book: “You ask me, brother, for this.”

Letters to various persons, one book: “From the bishopric of Metz concerning the abbot.”

To Saint Hildegard, one book, and other similar works.

She died under Emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1165, in the thirteenth indiction, in the thirty-sixth year of her age, the twenty-fourth of her conversion, and the thirteenth of the vision of the heavenly splendor, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of July. She was buried in the church of the monks of Saint Florinus, in the middle of the choir.

Hildegard, abbess of the monastery of Saint Rupert the Confessor opposite the town of Bingen, on the other side of the river Nahe, in the diocese of Mainz, of the Order of the divine father Benedict, German by nation, from the county of Sponheim, formerly a disciple of the blessed virgin Jutta, sister of Count Megenhard of Sponheim, our founder, was a virgin of most holy conduct, illumined from her childhood by divine revelations. Her life, confirmed by many miracles, spread the fragrance of her holiness into distant regions, so much so that the Roman pontiffs Eugenius III, Anastasius IV, Hadrian IV, and Alexander III, after the writings of her little work had been read publicly before all, approved them, and again strengthened her by letters so that she should persevere in the undertaking she had begun. Moreover, all that she wrote she learned from divine revelation. Among these works are the following:

On the Rule of Saint Benedict, one book: “And I, a poor little woman.”

Thirty Questions, one book: “Charity which with abstinence.”

Life of Saint Rupert the Confessor, one book: “For as in the true vision.”

Life of Saint Disibod the Bishop, one book: “In a mystical vision,” etc.

Fifty-eight Homilies on the Gospels, one book: “A certain man who [unclear].”

On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book: “In the vision which [unclear] of my soul.”

Scivias, a large volume, one book: “I, a poor little form.”

The Life of Merits, three books: “O crowds of men being born.”

Simple Medicine, one book: “And it happened in the sixth year after.”

Divine Works, one book: “He who was, who is, and who.”

To the People of Cologne, one book: “I, a poor little [unclear] form.”

An Exhortation of Secular Persons, one book: “O crowds of men being born.”
