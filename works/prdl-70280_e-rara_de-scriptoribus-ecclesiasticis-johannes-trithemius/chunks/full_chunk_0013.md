But your learning encourages me, which your kindness and humanity render lovable to all, since it shrinks from no one’s letters and judges no one unworthy of its address. Therefore, weak though I am, I approach your highness; I humbly submit the present little work to you; I appoint you its judge and defender. Receive, I beg you, most reverend and most learned father, this lucubration of ours, such as it is; examine it, judge it, defend it, and, if you judge it worthy, pursue it with kindly favor. And you who turn over the writings of the ancients in continual reading, deign also, if ever there is leisure, to inspect their catalogue. But I have not yet communicated the present little work to anyone, until I may know it to have been approved by the testimony of your authority. Friends await it; the printers are waiting for it; but I judge the opinion of your paternity to be preferred to all. May your most clement paternity indulge me, I pray, because I have presumed to bring my foolishness to ears occupied with arduous matters; for I have such confidence in your humanity, gentleness, and kindness that I may in no way doubt the benevolent reception of our catalogue. And I shall count it among the greatest delights if I learn that you take pleasure in my little lucubrations. Farewell, most reverend father; hold me, your most devoted servant, together with the volume sent, commended to you. From Spanheim, the sixth day before the Kalends of May, in the year of the Lord 1492.

*Prologue of the same reverend father, lord Johannes of Trittenheim, to the Book on Ecclesiastical Writers.*

At the insistence of many I have often been asked that, when some opportunity should at last be found, I should complete the book which I once began on ecclesiastical writers, for the purpose of having knowledge of books. I confess that I put it off for a long time, lest, if I should presume thoughtlessly to undertake so arduous a matter, I might provoke the hatred of rivals through my own levity. But now, since the counsel of learned friends has been added to their request, who by their arguments have encouraged me to undertake this province, I could not resist their petitions any longer, especially since this little work of ours is supported by the patronage of such great men. For why should I fear the hatred of detractors, when I see that a refuge among the learned lies open to me? There will be, I do not doubt, those who judge this labor of ours worthy of praise; there will also be those who disparage it from a zeal of rivalry. What then? Shall we therefore abandon the exercises of good works, lest we provoke the hatred of rivals? Far be it. Whoever does not imitate the wicked offends them; but he who fears to offend the envious does injury to the good. And indeed to have displeased the wicked is praiseworthy, since they are accustomed to love nothing unless it accords with their morals. Therefore now, importunate friends and exactors, receive the book composed on ecclesiastical writers, which with diligent prayers you so greatly desired to be brought to completion. I have done what I could, and I have expended my ready powers on this work, although in many respects the capacity itself is less than the desire. For I, in the manner of those who are half-blind, entered the path of the preceding fathers under their guidance, and, gathering from their little works the several things suitable to my purpose, I have carried them together into this bundle. I have also inserted into our catalogue many writers found by long investigation, with great labors and expenses. Do not wonder, excellent reader, if in the notes and titles of books my opinion does not always agree with yours. Who could enumerate, much less reconcile, the opinions of individuals amid so manifold a variety of books? You will therefore grant pardon if you sometimes find a volume marked beforehand for another person, or in another way, than your opinion understands. For it was not possible for me everywhere to express the differences of individuals. For, as I have already said, I followed the footsteps of the fathers; and where they disagreed, I embraced what I believed to be true. But if you find something else established by more solid truth, I shall gladly yield to your opinion. It pleased me, however, to proceed by a new manner of writing, so that the reader himself may more easily be able to discover knowledge of the books from their beginnings.
