Johannes Soreth, a Norman by nation, of the Order of the Friars of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, the twenty-fifth prior general; a man most learned in the divine scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy; subtle in talent, sweet and clear in speech, devout in life and conduct, and singularly religious and holy; the ornament, glory, splendor, and reformer of the Carmelite order, such as past ages have rarely seen and future ages will more rarely behold; wholly given to God, wholly intent on reading and prayer; a despiser of the world; a lover, model, and mirror of monastic conduct; an imitator of angelic purity, guardian of virtues, and keenest enemy of vices. He wrote many commendable little works of his excellent talent, of which the following are extant:

On the Sentences, four books.
Various sermons, one book.
On the rule of his order, one book.
A larger commentary on the same, one book.
He arranged the Constitutions, one book.
He also wrote many other things.

He died at Angers under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus, in the year of the Lord 1471, in the third indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of August.

Theodore Gaza of Thessalonica, a man excellently learned in secular letters and not ignorant of sacred scripture, most learned in Greek and Latin, outstanding in talent and eloquent in speech, wrote many little works; he translated many things from Greek into Latin, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of his writings nothing has yet come to the knowledge of our reading. But among his translations are reported:

Theophrastus, On Plants, one book.
Aristotle’s Problems, one book.
The same author, On Animals, one book.
And certain other things.

He died at Rome under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1478, in the eleventh indiction.
