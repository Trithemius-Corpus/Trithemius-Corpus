He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Pileus of Modena, professor of both laws and a very celebrated interpreter, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, renowned in learning and cautious in counsel, held in the highest esteem among the older jurists, by writing and teaching various things in that faculty transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of his little works there is found a certain notable and not to be despised summa, which he entitled:

On the order of trials, one book: "When the name of Christ has been invoked, the matter..."

But concerning the rest that he wrote, nothing has come to my knowledge.

Johannes Bosianus, by origin a Cremonese, a very famous expounder of the laws, teacher of Azo of Bologna, the most renowned jurisconsult, a man of great learning and reputation, who appeared first among the expositors of law, and by his example gave many both the spirit and the form of study. He is reported to have written in that faculty certain distinguished little works, by which he gave his name to immortality. Of these there survives a brief summa on the Pandects, which is entitled:

Ventosa, one book.

Lecture on the Code, nine books.

And certain other things. He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Stephen the Englishman, once the ornament and rector of the school of Paris, studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and in Aristotelian philosophy second to none in his time, taught theology gloriously for many years, and was the first to begin to expound sacred Scripture inwardly and morally. From this arose the custom that masters of theology should open the divine books more clearly to their disciples in the schools by their lectures. This Stephen, as I have learned, wrote the following little works:

On the five books of Moses, five books.

On Joshua, one book.

On the book of Judges also, one book.

On Kings also, three books.

On the twelve minor prophets, twelve books.

He published besides many other things, which have not yet come to my knowledge.

He flourished around the times of the rule of King Henry VI, but the fifth emperor crowned by the pope, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Bagorotus, distinguished professor of both laws and very celebrated interpreter, not ignorant also of other secular letters, whose authority among the old jurists was not small, on account of his excellent talent for teaching, and by his zeal for writing also made his name known to posterity. Of his little works there survives a notable summa which he entitled:

Of cavillations, one book: "Since it becomes dangerous for me."

On the order of trials, one book: "By the prayers and insistence."

He is said to have written several other things besides, but they have not come to my knowledge.

Placentinus, an Italian by nation, cultivator and very celebrated expounder of both laws, attained great glory at Montpellier by teaching and writing; he once held a place among the chief doctors among the professors of this art, because of certain highly commended volumes which he composed. Among these, among other things, are said to be:

A summa of trials, one book: "When I was at Mantua."

On the Code also, nine books. I have found nothing concerning the others. He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Rofredus, by origin a Beneventan, a very renowned doctor in both laws, subtle in intellect, composed in speech after the scholastic manner, sufficiently capable, through long practice, of resolving doubts and questions of law, wrote in that faculty for students little works not to be despised, by which he consecrated his name and reputation to immortality. Yet of these we have been able to find only a few, which we have also set down: a useful volume containing:

Questions of civil law, one book: "If I were to consider my talent."
