[Aurelianus?], cleric of the church of [Réôme?]: very zealous in Holy Scripture, and nobly learned in secular letters; in his time an excellent and distinguished musician. He wrote to Bernard the archchanter, afterwards bishop, on the rules of modulations, which they call tones or tenors, and on the matter itself and its terms, a notable volume: *A Regular Tonary*, one book. Its title is: [unclear]. He also wrote certain other things. He flourished about the times of the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 900.

Egino, abbot of the monastery of Prüm in the diocese of Trier, of the Order of Saint Benedict, German by nation, a man most learned in divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, easily held the first place among the doctors of Germany and Gaul in his time. He wrote works of great authority, of which I have found the following. To Albero, first bishop of Metz, from the birth of Christ down to the nine hundred and eighth year of the same, chiefly containing the deeds of the Franks and Germans: *A History or Chronicle*, ten books. He wrote: *On Ecclesiastical Discipline*, two books, beginning, “In the first place one must inquire.” Of the rest I have not yet seen anything. He wrote the aforesaid work, as noted above, on ecclesiastical discipline and the Christian religion, at the command of Ratbod, archbishop of Trier; he compiled it from the decrees of the holier fathers and of general councils in two books, and lucidly divided it, in the customary manner, by rubrics into nearly nine hundred chapters. He flourished under the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 910.

Hildouin, from being abbot of the monastery of Lobbes, of the Order of Saint Benedict, first bishop of Liège, then, driven out, came to Hugh, king of Italy, to whom he was joined by a line of kinship; by him he was appointed bishop of Verona, and at last, after Lampert, was made archbishop of Milan. He ordained Ratherius, a monk, as bishop of the church of Verona; a man sufficiently learned in divine Scriptures. At the request of the brothers, while he was still abbot, he wrote: *The Deeds of the Abbots of Lobbes*, one book; *Sermons to the Brothers*, one book; and certain other things. He flourished under Hugh, king of Italy, in the times of Conrad, emperor of the Romans and Germans, in the year of the Lord 920.

Ratherius, from being a monk of the monastery of Lobbes, after Hildouin bishop of Verona; a man learned in divine Scriptures and very skilled in the arts of humanity, which they call liberal; simple in character, gentle in disposition, and scholastic in speech. He came to Hugh the king in Italy with Bishop Hildouin, and after Hildouin’s translation to the archiepiscopal see of the church of Milan, he was appointed bishop of the city of Verona. Not long afterwards, driven out from there by Hugh because he had admitted Arnulf, duke of Bavaria and Hugh’s enemy, into Verona, he was relegated to exile at Pavia. At length, returned to his church, and again, I do not know whether for the same reason, driven out, he composed various works in each place for his own consolation, which could no less please the understanding of readers than profit it. Of these they report the following: *On His First Exile*, one book; *Higher Concerning the Second*, with the prefixed [unclear], one book; another, which he calls *Inefficax*, one book; *On the Body and Blood of the Lord*, one book; *Against the Anthropomorphites*, one book; *Agonisticon, That Is, Pre-speeches*, six books; also *Confessions*, one book; *Life of Saint Ursmar of Lobbes*, one book. He says that he also wrote some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. And finally, as the monk Sigebert writes, after being made bishop of Liège, he was also driven out from there. He flourished in the times of Henry, first emperor of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 920.

Stephen, from being a cleric of the church of Metz, bishop of Liège after Franco; a man learned in divine Scriptures and greatly skilled in secular letters, and no less venerable for holiness than for knowledge. He wrote many works of no contemptible usefulness
