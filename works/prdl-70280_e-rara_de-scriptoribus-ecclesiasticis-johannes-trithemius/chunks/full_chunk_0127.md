Christianus, archchancellor of Frederick, the first emperor, German by nation, a man most learned in secular literature, eloquent and industrious, whom the Italians admired when he spoke in his own time, also not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, whom Leo the Tuscan, notary of Manuel, emperor of the Greeks, extols with praises. He wrote certain excellent little works of his talent, of which there are extant:

The Deeds and Life of Frederick, the first emperor.

Of Various Letters, one book.

Certain Speeches, and other things.

He flourished under the same Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1170.

Leo, Tuscan by nation, chief interpreter of the imperial letters under Manuel, prince of the Greeks, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, an excellent cultivator of secular letters, skilled in Greek and Latin speech. His office was to translate letters sent to the emperor, or to be sent to others, from one language into another. He published certain excellent volumes, and also transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have read only the distinguished work which he entitled:

On the Transgressions of the Greeks, two books: The intolerable [unclear] of the Greeks.

Against the Errors of the Greeks, one book.

He flourished at Constantinople in the times of Frederick, the first emperor, and Pope Alexander III, in the year of the Lord 1170.

John, archdeacon of a certain church of Horit[unclear], a man learned in the divine writings, not ignorant of secular letters, skilled in Greek and Latin speech, outstanding in talent, distinguished in eloquence. He wrote certain little works, not to be despised, for the edification of the faithful, by which he made his name known. Of these there is extant a distinguished and excellent work addressed to Stephen, a certain royal notary, against the Greeks:

On the Procession of the Holy Spirit, one book: Many things of the Greeks in rounded [unclear].

Also various letters.

But he is said to have written certain other things, which have not come into our hands.

Odefridus of Viterbo, priest, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular literature, outstanding in talent, distinguished, practiced in verse and prose. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which he left the memory of himself to posterity. Of these there is extant a large volume addressed to Pope Urban III, in verse mixed with prose composition, on the beginning of the world, on the whole Old and New Testament, on the ages of the world, the times, deeds, and all histories, which he entitled:

Pantheon, eighteen books: On the beginning of the world.

Concerning the others I have seen nothing. He flourished under Frederick, the first emperor, and Henry his son, in the year of the Lord 1185.

Eckebertus, second abbot of the monastery of Saint Florin, of the order of the divine father Benedict, in the diocese of Trier, German by nation, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, no less venerable in conduct than in knowledge, was the brother of the blessed virgin Heliçabet, abbess of Schönau. He had formerly been a canon of the church of Bonn; called by his holy sister to Schönau, he first became a monk, and then was made abbot. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these I have found the following:

Against Heresies, one book: The prophets had long ago [unclear] times.

On the Beginning of the Gospel of John, one book: The mysteries of the divine word.

On the Magnificat, one book: The Spirit of the Lord is upon you, Mary.

On “The Angel Was Sent,” one book: The Gospel sermon [unclear].

For Virgins Consecrated to God.

On the Death of His Sister, one book: A word is to me for you, O king.

Meditations, one book.
