How the citizens of this inferior region are disordered toward penitence.

Article VII.

Man, born of woman, living for a short time and filled with many miseries, cannot long remain in the same state. It is necessary that, by reforming himself daily with all zeal, he turn himself toward his due end; for as long as anyone is sent to live in this world, he proves changeable in the matter of virtue. Therefore whoever, placed amid this changeability, desires to come to the joys of immutability must humbly acknowledge his own frailty, must always after the fall of sin flee to the remedies of penitence, and must dread lying in wickedness even for a single moment.

For the life of monks must always consist in saving penitence, and must continually await the coming of the Judge with holy fear. For Jerome says: “A monk has the office not of teaching but of weeping,” one who should mourn either himself or the world, and wait in dread for the divine coming. But the disordered cloisterers of the inferior region despise this saving remedy; they waste the acceptable time in vanity; and because they do not fear to order everything badly, they show themselves very disordered even for doing penitence.

First, indeed, they are badly ordered toward penitence through negligence of mind, when they neglect to understand how greatly sin displeases almighty God, whom it is not lawful to contradict by the choice of a perverse will. Just as night is opposed to day, so the sinner is turned away from divine goodness and stands as its adversary. What a putrid corpse is accustomed to do in the sight of men, sin does in its own way, incomparably more, in the sight of the angels. But carnal monks despise the honor of God, and therefore they do not notice the insult of sin. They neglect to amend venial sins, as though they might lawfully commit mortal ones. For Scripture says: “He who neglects the least things falls little by little.” Hence Bernard warns us with his judgment, saying: “Let no one, brothers, say in his heart: These are slight matters; I do not care to correct them; it is no great thing to remain in these smallest venial sins. This, brothers, is impenitence; this is blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, which is unforgivable. No one can avoid falling into greater sins if he despises amending the lesser.” Nothing is worse than this negligence of mind.

Second, carnal monks, lovers of the lowest region, are exceedingly disordered toward penitence because they do not notice the contagion of sin. For just as a healthy man is very often infected by a contagious disease, so from one sin a man is inclined toward another. One sin is always said to dispose toward another, unless it is amended as quickly as possible through contrition. For the prophet says: “Jerusalem has sinned a sin; therefore she has become unstable.” And elsewhere: “He who is in filth, let him be filthy still.” We have shown this contagion of disease sufficiently in the degrees of pride: just as one is always said to arise from another, so we perceive that through negligence a man becomes liable to slip from one sin into another.

Third, carnal monks, disordered toward penitence, contract through the sickness of sin and the habit of sin a harmful blindness of mind. Every vice, often committed, is treated lightly, and through habit is judged less serious. Just as grace illumines the mind, so the frequency of sin blinds reason. It is written: “Blind the heart of this people and make their ears heavy, so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand, lest they be converted and I heal them,” says the Lord. For when the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises them. No sin is so great that it does not seem light and small through habit.
