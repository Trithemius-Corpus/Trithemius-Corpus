Secondly, the citizens of this dark region are disorderly toward their prelates, whose office they disparage, though they know that he has been appointed by God to preside in the monastery. On any easy occasion they murmur against him; they do not observe his commands; and those who long ago had submitted themselves with the weapons of holy obedience and for love of God now, in the manner of Sarabaites, despising obedience, usurp for their own will a dominion ruinous to themselves. They desire to have an abbot suited to their own depraved ways; they reject one who is holy and useful. If they find a lax and sluggish pastor, they intoxicate him with many poisons by flattering him; they call him an excellent and most loving father; they submit themselves to him not humbly but willingly, since they fear from him no correction of their depravity.

Thirdly, carnal monks show themselves disorderly toward their pastors when they withdraw from them the affection of filial love and despise obedience according to the Rule. Yet since a monk is bound to love his abbot sincerely as Christ’s vicar, he cannot hate him without the gravest crime. For truly, whoever does not love his pastor for the love of Christ will not be a true lover of God. For Christ, who wills even enemies to be loved, how would he permit his vicars to be hated? Therefore look to him who willed your abbot to preside over you in his place, and from then on there will be no cause of displeasure against him.

Fourthly, the disorderly citizens of the shadowy valley disparage the reverence due to their pastor; they not only scorn to render him the honor owed by the Rule, but, what is worse, they do not fear even to inflict contempt on him. For the Rule teaches that the abbot holds the place of Christ in the monastery, and all monks owe him humble obedience as to Christ himself, since the obedience rendered to superiors is shown to Christ. But perverse cloisterers, always ungrateful toward the prelate set over them, do not fear contumaciously to resist the divine ordinance; they despise the commands of obedience in the holy Rule; and they dishonor their abbots, to whom they ought to have shown all reverence, tearing them apart with hatred and detraction. Whatever he does, whatever he ordains, whatever he says, the disciple is so ill-disposed that, despising the office in his conduct toward him, he seeks nothing more, like a diseased sheep, than the confusion of his pastor. Of these, some judge and despise their abbot in secret; others contradict him to his face; and when they neglect to show reverence to their pastor, they throw the whole observance of discipline into confusion. Pride is always familiar to them, and foulness, the friend of the flesh; for those who voluntarily withdraw themselves from affection for their pastor fall into abominable temptations and the worst crimes. For whoever despises Christ’s vicar in the monastery hands himself over to the devil’s power to rage against him, and confounds the unity of the mystical body.

Fifthly, carnal monks are disorderly toward their pastor when they disparage his authority, thinking that nothing is lawful for him in the monastery unless they themselves have consented. With necks raised high they despise the virtue of humility; they spurn regular subjection; they scorn to yield to their betters. They wish to thrust themselves into all business and causes, to usurp for themselves the primacy of counsel, so that the abbot may dare to do or arrange nothing unless the matter first proceeds with their consent. But what does the blessed lawgiver Benedict say in the Rule? “After hearing,” he says, “the counsel of the brethren, let the abbot consider the matter within himself, and do what he judges more useful.” And again: “Let the brethren give their counsel with all humble subjection, so that they do not presume insolently to defend what has seemed good to them; rather, let it depend on the abbot’s judgment, so that all may obey what he has judged more salutary.” And again, concerning the appointment of a prior, he says: “We judge it expedient, for the preservation of charity and peace, that the ordering of his monastery depend on the abbot’s judgment.” This useful ordinance concerns the appointing and removing of all officials of the monastery; it requires not the consent of the brethren, but the counsel of those who fear God. Finally, in all things the Rule commands action according to the will of the abbot, and that nothing at all be presumed which has not been enjoined by him.
