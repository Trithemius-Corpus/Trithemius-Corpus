We see that today many among cloistered men live in a secular fashion: many seem to neglect the state of their profession, violating the observance of regular discipline in various ways, knowingly ignoring the goodness of God, and shrinking back from doing penance.

Third, many cloistered men of this lowest region are disordered toward God through careless neglect of the service due to him. They serve their own affections and fleshly desires in various ways, and always come to God’s service with a distracted mind. Of these, some run to choir lukewarm, without any devotion; others come unwillingly, compelled by necessity; some, scattered by outward thoughts, are present at the divine offices in body, but in mind wander outside through various workshops. Others are not afraid to seek out false occasions by which they may, as it were, lawfully absent themselves from choir. They pretend a severe headache; now they allege this, now that, deceitfully; and while they fraudulently withdraw the service owed to almighty God, they incur the reproach of eternal malediction due to the negligent.

Fourth, there are very many who are disordered toward God through the working of habit. Even if they sometimes avoid the malediction of the negligent, still they never draw near to the fruit of inward devotion. For they come to the service of God from habit alone, and are not moved to any good work by devotion. Others, placed under grave necessity, obey the commands of their superiors unwillingly enough, and bear temporal penalties rather than fear the judgment of God. These carry the cross of the Lord under compulsion with Simon, and, like Ephraim, a heifer trained for threshing, apply themselves to good works by habit alone. But they never do good well who come to the service of God with an improper intention. They also perform a just thing without the reward of justice, because in good and just works they do not assume a good will.

Fifth, the citizens of the lowest region are disordered toward God through the vice of ingratitude, which is the greatest crime of a disordered will. And indeed a man is often exposed, by his own deserving, to be scourged by various and very grave temptations when he is found ungrateful to the Lord for the benefits he has received. Therefore he deserves to lose the benefit he has received who neglects to show himself grateful to so great a benefactor. And what greater ingratitude can there be than to live irreligiously in religion, to break one’s vow, shamefully to violate the purpose solemnly undertaken? The monk who entangles himself in fleshly vices, who neglects to live according to the rule he has professed, voluntarily incurs the very great vice of ingratitude.

How the monks of this lowest region are disordered toward their prelate.

Article II.

Carnal monks are disordered in many ways toward their prelate. They tear apart his life with constant detractions; with a stubborn heart they disparage his pastoral office; they refuse, according to the Rule, to show their shepherd the affection of filial love. For the monks of this lowest region, blinded by various pleasures of the flesh, disdain to consider whose place the abbot holds in the monastery; and the one whom they ought humbly to venerate as Christ’s vicar they regard, because of weakness, as one like themselves and worthy of no honor. Under the snare of this disordered conduct the ancient enemy deceives carnal monks, whom he leads in various ways into displeasure with their shepherd. For the greatest power of raging against the monastic flock is granted to the devil when the one who presides is hated by his subjects. For just as a sick man cannot be healed if, by detesting the physician, he does not allow him to come near, so a monk cannot be saved who, because of aversion, is unwilling to disclose his temptations to his shepherd. For how will anyone be able to escape the savage teeth of the ravening wolf if he cannot endure the presence of the shepherd?

First, therefore, disordered monks criticize the life of their shepherd; they stubbornly accuse both his deeds and his words, because, when they consider that their ruler walks according to the traditions of the holy fathers, they fear being corrected in their own depravity. For among reprobate minds the life of the good is always drawn into suspicion; envy is always tormented by the virtues of others; and the monk who voluntarily subjects himself to vices by sinning [unclear].
