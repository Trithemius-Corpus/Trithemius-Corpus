At the “Ite, missa est.”

When at the end the “Ite, missa est” is sung, cause your mind to be lifted up with the holy angels returning into heaven, so that, while Jesus presides in majesty, you may deserve to behold by desire him whom, in the sacrament of the Mass now offered to the Father, you can no longer see on earth. Therefore kindle your desire by the image of desolation, in the manner of those who are unwillingly separated from their own friends.

Say, then, in your heart:

Meditation.

Behold, my Lord has returned to heaven; he has left me desolate on earth. What am I doing here among mortals, when I have one who is immortal? Why do I not follow him? Woe to me, for my sojourning has been prolonged; I have dwelt with the inhabitants of darkness; my soul has long been a sojourner. I desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ; but who will deliver me, unhappy man that I am, from the body of this death? O most loving Lord Jesus, my hope, O God and Savior of my soul, draw me after you; I will run in the fragrance of your ointments. O my sweet love, do not forget me to the end, but deign frequently to lift up my mind to you, so that, since I cannot see you bodily while set in this mortality, at least, lifted up in affection and desire in your most holy love, I may deserve more often to behold you in spirit in glory. O my salvation, O heart of my heart, most loving Jesus, do not abandon me or despise me; be a gracious helper to me, and direct me always in your most holy will, so that I may truly be able to please you.

After High Mass and the following hour.

After the end of the hour that immediately follows High Mass, return to your cell with the customary reverence described above; pour out prayers to the Lord with fervor of spirit, as we taught after Matins. Spend the remaining time in meditations. Consider the foulness of sin, how it separates from God and eternally condemns the one who is turned away and does not repent. Take care, therefore, to turn your will wholly to God with the firmest resolution, and to avoid the contagion of sin at all times with all your strength.

On the regular meal.

When the signal is rung for the meal, have compassion from the heart for the sick, the needy, and the poor who suffer want; and when you can do nothing else, pray for them to the Lord. Consider that among Christians there are many far better than you whom harsh poverty oppresses in this life, to whom not even coarse bread necessary for food is offered, nor even the thinnest clothing supplied for covering the body. But you, though you are a monk and a despiser of the world, are clothed in the best garments and sit at table like a prince; brothers better than you set food before you to satiety; in short, all necessities are supplied to you, an unworthy sinner, to the point of nausea by the servants of God. Consider, then, what end this abundance of things will have in a monk: for on entering religion you professed poverty, yet now in religion you feast more splendidly than the rich of the world. Take care, therefore, that you not live ungrateful for such great benefits of God, but according to the rule you have professed. Pray for those who contribute temporal substance to your monastery, that almighty God may mercifully grant them eternal life.

On reading at table.

When you are sitting at table, be composed while eating. Do not look this way and that, but endeavor to eat humbly and with thanksgiving the food set before you, as much as is fitting for the necessity of nature. Carefully consider what is read at table, so that, just as the body is refreshed with its food, so the soul may not be deprived of its own refreshment.
