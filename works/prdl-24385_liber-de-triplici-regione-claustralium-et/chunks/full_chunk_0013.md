Your ox and your ass fall sick, and you feel for them; your brother is swallowed up by sorrow, and you do not come to his aid. You, therefore, are a hireling and not a shepherd, you who with a stubborn heart despise your brother’s infirmities; you do not help him when he is set in bitterness. Do not think that you can escape the hands of the Lord, whose sheep you allow to perish so wickedly.

Third, a ruler is disordered toward his subjects when those whom he ought to have kept with solicitude within the enclosure of the monastery he places, not without danger, in external affairs. For indeed, when a weak member of the monastery is set over the governance of nuns or assigned to secular churches, the integrity of regular observance is violated; and he who, still remaining in the lower region, has not learned to subject the flesh to the spirit, does not know how to preside over others without danger to himself. A disordered shepherd, who for the advantage of temporal property is not afraid to expose his monks to such great danger, is guilty of all the souls that perish through such an occasion, and before the inward judge he is bound to make restitution for them all. It does not belong to the profession of a monk to govern secular churches; it is not expedient for a weak cenobite to preside over nuns.

But what then? Should a monk oppose the command of his shepherd if he wishes him to reside with nuns or in secular churches? The Rule commands that nothing be preferred to obedience. Truly, I do not know what I should answer to this question, so as not to incur the danger that is seen to threaten on both sides. For if I speak the truth, I shall seem to break the obedience of subjection; if I encourage monks to expose themselves to danger, I confess that I sin. Yet I shall say briefly what I think, although I know that the truth will bring forth hatred. Just as a monk, by the necessity of his profession, is simply bound to obey his shepherd in all things that are according to the monastic rule, so a prelate cannot compel a monk, without his own consent, to things that are beyond the rule, especially when danger to salvation intervenes. But to govern churches, to preside over women, to reside outside the monastery, is not according to the rule of obedience.

Do not be angry with me, fathers, if I have spoken the truth; do not knit your brows if I have expressed what seems right to me. Nevertheless, you will do what seems right to you, even if I protest. Yet I beg you: do not expose your monks to danger against chastity; do not violently compel anyone to the governance of nuns and parish churches, since you can find many who ambitiously seek these offices. Obedience is indeed good, but it must in no way be preferred to truth and the salvation of the soul. For when danger to salvation is at issue, it is better humbly to excuse oneself and set obedience aside than to suffer shipwreck of chastity. Woe to the prelates of monks who incorporate secular churches into their monasteries, who loosen the torches of avarice in the lap of desire; because they think they are benefiting posterity, they damn many through the occasion.

Fourth, the ruler of monks becomes disordered toward his subjects if he is found turbulent and anxious. In a shepherd, pious and prudent discretion is very necessary, lest either zeal for discipline be excessive, or the measure of compassion be relaxed by excessive laxity. But the disordered ruler who, through indiscreet zeal, shows himself a tyrant to the monks, or who, through lax compassion, is silent and insensible before the gravest crimes, does not know the moderation of just governance. He does not experience in himself the fervor of fraternal charity; while he always esteems himself above the monks, he does not know how to be a humble father and companion. What is fiercer than this rule? What is more wicked than a power where neither God, nor reason, nor understanding, but a beast holds command? For where God and understanding govern authority, the judgments of those who preside do not depart from the uprightness of the law of nature. But the disordered and anxious ruler is bestial and infected with various passions; he always suspects evil of the monks, and, while he perversely directs the burden of governance, he is always anxious to lose the kingdom to which he clings like Herod.

Fifth, a shepherd of cloistered men is called disordered toward his monks if, in dispensing the goods of the monastery, he is found either excessive or obstinate. He is to be called excessive if he is wasteful in distributing, or poured out in indiscreet generosity. But he is called obstinate if he denies what ought fittingly to be given.
