The nourishment of holy reading is never lacking to us as we eat, and it sweetly inflames us with divine compunction. For the true and inward worshiper of God, whenever even amid meals he hears something read about the bitterness of the Lord’s passion, the fervor of divine love, or the kingdom of the heavenly homeland, at once groans with an immense desire, is kindled in the ardor of love, and, compelled by exceeding sweetness, weeps. For indeed the mind that truly loves the Lord Jesus, as soon as it hears his name or beholds the image of the crucified, is dissolved in compunction and is lifted up in affection to his feet or to his embrace.

For thanksgiving.

When the meal is finished, give thanks to the Lord; and while you go to the church under the psalm, direct your mind upward and intercede for benefactors, living and dead. If after thanksgiving you add some special prayer for the dead, you do well.

On the regular reading.

Leaving the refectory for the cell, if it is not a fast day, complete the customary prayer; then read something good for edification, meditate, or do or write the work that has been assigned to you. But if it is a fast day, take up the volume of your study and enter the place of regular reading; sitting with the others, read in silence what you wish. But if you are asked by the president to say something good, answer humbly with a few useful words, without curious citations. Do not use a very long speech, nor bring forward the vain curiosities of secular people among them; do not entangle your brothers sitting around you in crooked or distorted words, but express the thought of your mind simply in few words, unless perhaps the president, for a definite reason, orders you to make a longer speech.

The monk’s exercises after dinner until vespers.

Consideration VI.

After the reading, return to your cell. You should always [unclear] some prayer before your works, and in everything you do ask for the help of God. When you are in your cell, do not be idle, but do something good, especially what the abbot has enjoined by obedience. Now pray, now write, read something good or meditate; for the cell makes an idle monk either a beast or a demon. But we shall speak of these things in their proper place below. Take care to spend your time usefully, for in a short while you will have to render account of all of it to God.

On dwelling in the cell and the works to be done there.

There are three places in which a devout monk who loves God ought often to be found: namely, church, cell, and, at its proper time, refectory. But he should not permit himself to wander about the monastery, for it is written of the wicked, the reprobate, and the wandering monks: “The wicked walk in a circle.” But of the just man Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of God from his youth; he will sit alone and be silent, because he has lifted himself above himself.” The works proper to the cell are these: first, to attend to commands; second, to read what is useful; third, to apply oneself to suitable meditation; fourth, to pray frequently; fifth, to rest fittingly. We shall speak of these briefly in order.

In the cell, first you must attend to commands.

First, when you are in the dwelling of your cell, learn to attend to commands, and do not prefer your idle devotions to obedience. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than foolish offerings and victims, and to listen is better than to offer sacrifice.” Therefore, if something has been assigned to you to work at in the cell, do not transgress the paths of obedience on any pretext, but strive diligently to perform the work imposed on you for love of our Lord Jesus Christ and for the merit of holy obedience. Hence Hugh, lover of quiet, says: Gladly remain in the cell, and always do something in it that may edify you; and when something is enjoined by a superior, reckon that it is not your right [unclear].
