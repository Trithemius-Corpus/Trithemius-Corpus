Therefore a monk must diligently examine himself and purge himself of his committed faults according to the ordinance of the Church. But he who, on examining himself, by his own knowledge finds his conscience clean from mortal sin, should offer humble praises to God; yet he should not neglect to subject himself to the following examination.

The second thing, likewise necessary for a good man, is to direct his intention chiefly to God in all works, and to order all things, according to his powers, to his praise and honor. But if, because the multitude and variety of works hinders him, a man cannot always actually keep God before his eyes, let him at least conform his habitual intention of living according to God’s most gracious will to this desire.

The third is that in all things he should obey God, holy mother Church, and his superiors, and not depart from the path of right and enlightened reason, since whoever despises this threefold obedience has denied the faith. It is therefore necessary to show obedience in due order to these three according to the reasons of their institution, so that the life of monks may remain blameless in their actions, without fear.

These three things, possessed with humble sincerity, make a good man and show the state of a conscience that hopes well in the Lord. But a man who lacks one of these is not perfectly good, even if he seems to be good. Yet if, repenting of his sins, he is able from then on to fulfill these three things, he is now made good and pleasing to God, however evil he may previously have been. Furthermore, for a good religious or spiritual man to be born from a good man, three other things are required, according to the lord of Castellum.

The first thing required for making a good religious is that purity of heart be cleansed from every rivalry or incitement of the flesh through the pursuit of discipline. This purity is never acquired better than when the world, with all its things, is despised.

The second thing by which a good man is made a good religious is called spiritual freedom in God. Once the phantasms of all things have been shut out, the soul can raise itself, unencumbered, upward to God in all its exercises, and continually lift itself without hindrance in thanksgiving with devout prayers.

In the third way a good man is made spiritually good when, through the desires of love, the spirit is joined to God by certain great bonds. The last two things make our cenobite spiritual and introduce him to the citizens of the third region of the enclosures. Therefore whoever wishes to have good confidence from the testimony of his conscience should humbly strive to exercise himself in the things set forth, because no one prudently presumes upon security when danger is not avoided. And what is so dangerous as always to live in negligence and sins, and to refuse to amend committed errors? The confidence of the just, arising from the testimony of a good conscience, is always excellent, and continually subject to God in humble service. But the impious and sinners, corrupted by their evil actions, raise themselves up against God and do not trust in the testimony of conscience, but rather presume vainly.

There follow the seven roots of divine fear, which are laid as a foundation in the first degree of humility.

Since this second region principally instructs the cenobite, in order, through the degrees of humility proceeding from the fear of God, it is necessary for him to know clearly the roots of that fear, if he wishes to arrive, by ascending, at the love of God. Therefore, so that we may be able to construct this spiritual house to perfection, we must set those roots in order in the foundation. We did not arrange them in the first degree of humility for this reason: lest, in speaking in order, we should confuse the reader by excessive length. There are therefore seven roots of the fear of God, through which the cenobite is led to compunction and, by advancing in true humility, is more strongly rooted.

The first root of fear, of the love of God, is intense meditation.
