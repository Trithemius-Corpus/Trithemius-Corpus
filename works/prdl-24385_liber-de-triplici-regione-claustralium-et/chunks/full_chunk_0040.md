Therefore the eighth degree of humility is set forth in the Rule thus: if a monk does nothing except what the common rule of the monastery or the examples of the elders encourage. In this degree, singularity in outward conduct contrary to the Rule is forbidden to monks; but inward singularity of greater charity and humility is forbidden to no one. Therefore let no one prefer his own inventions to the holy Rule; let no one set his own customs before the examples of the elders, since standing in the seventh degree is not enough for one who does not keep the common rule.

The ninth degree of humility follows from the Rule, by which excessive talkativeness is forbidden. It is fitting for monks to keep silence, especially while one sits among the elders. And indeed, one who speaks without being asked is deservedly regarded as a fool.

The tenth degree of humility forbids readiness to laugh and levity; whoever delights in laughter is shown to be foolish and a stranger to the fear of the Lord.

The eleventh degree of humility commands modesty and dignity in a monk, so that, when opportunity and permission to speak are granted him, he may speak with humble seriousness, without laughter, and as befits a monk. Let a monk’s speech be rare, brief, and reasonable, because it is not fitting for him to say useless things who wishes to please the Most High.

The twelfth degree of humility teaches a monk to compose the outward man, in appearance and bearing, from a humble heart, and to show by outward conduct also that he is devout within. And although this degree is last in the order of all of them, nevertheless in practice it begins to make progress and is first. Therefore Bernard, describing these twelve degrees of humility, begins from this one and, ascending to the first, descends, so to speak, in order. For no one easily makes inward progress toward compunction of heart and fear of the Lord who does not compose his outward behavior.

On the fitting of the powers of the soul for entry into the third region. Article six.

When, through the degrees of humility, the powers of the soul, especially of the inner man, have been purified, firmly founded, and reformed, he now says that it is very necessary for monks to be more devoutly fitted for entry into the third region. This diligence in striving toward higher things is necessary for cenobites, as is said, because where the effort to make progress is laid aside, acquired virtue is wholly dissipated.

No one is permitted to stand still in this second region, but we are all commanded to ascend to the third by continual effort. For indeed this continual movement casts all the slothful and lukewarm back into the first region, that of the flesh; but it gently impels the humble, fervent with desire, always toward the third, without violence. Therefore whoever has deserved, through humility, to obtain from the Lord the power and reformation of his soul is now very near the third region, that of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it with strength, it is necessary that he most diligently fit the powers of his soul and compose them for their individual operations. The intellect is said to be rightly fitted for entry into the third region when it is enlightened to know the highest truth according to its capacity. The memory is composed for the same thing when it is fully absorbed in holding and cherishing the highest truth. And if the will is disposed to love the highest good, which is God, it is well fitted for entry into the spirit.

But in order that a cenobite may be able to attain the purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, withdrawn from the world, the mind, pacified from every inward and outward disturbance, should rest, so that it may remain buried in the bosom of divine love and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union.
