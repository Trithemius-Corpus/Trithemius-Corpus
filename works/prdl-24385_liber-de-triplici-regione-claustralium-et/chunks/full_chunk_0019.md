The ninth degree of pride is described as murmuring or impatience, which for the most part rules carnal monks. But the man whose confession is feigned, when touched by one slight insult or penance, soon rises up into open pride. The deceit that was pretending humility is revealed; the false mind can no longer hide itself for long, but is compelled to bring into public view whatever lies hidden within. From then on, having lost control of himself, the proud cenobite murmurs at everything; he bitterly accuses the judgment of his pastor, however just it may be; he gnashes his teeth, is stirred up and grows angry; without reason, like a madman, he throws everything into confusion. But the proud monk, given over to murmuring, having despised the fourth degree of humility, falls into the tenth descent of pride, through which he runs toward hell.

The tenth degree of pride follows murmuring, and is called rebellion. Proud and carnal monks fall into this, who, still dwelling in the lowest region, devote themselves to murmurings. Hence Bernard says: unless divine mercy looks upon the murmurer, so that at last, being converted, which is very difficult for such people, he silently acquiesces in the judgments of others, he soon becomes brazen and shameless, and falls all the worse into this degree, the more desperately he fails to restrain the movement of his mind. He always advances toward worse things; and he who before had arrogantly despised his brothers in secret now, what is more serious, openly shows that he despises his master as well. But the chastisement of divine judgment does not allow this degree of pride to pass long unpunished, since monks who rebel against their pastors are often quickly consumed by a miserable death. For some, departing from the order, become apostates; others, because of grave crimes, foul the prisons; some, expelled from monasteries, wander in wretched exile; many, horribly struck by the Lord, hasten in despair to hell. For it is written: Whoever resists authority opposes God’s ordinance. And again the Lord says to the prelates of the faithful: Whoever despises you despises me.

What will you still say, rebellious monks? Will you cite laws to me, by which you corrupt the truth? But I dare to say what I wish you were ashamed to hear. Rebellious monk, in the strict judgment of God no presumption of law will save you, who now proudly despise the obedience of subjection. Law commands obedience; it requires humble subjection in the order.

Consequently the rebellious monk falls into the eleventh degree of pride, which is freedom to sin, when he despises not only his brothers but even his prelate, and corrections and exhortations. When rebellion against the preceptor is taken up, boldness in sinning is born; and one who despises the authority of correction now commits every crime as if lawfully. The love of God voluntarily restrains good cenobites from sins; fear of correction restrains the weak. But where neither the love of God nor fear of correction pierces the monk, the mind, through rebellion and the habit of sinning, is already drawing near to hell. For Scripture says: When the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises. What does he despise? He despises God, spurns men, and, despairing, does not know how to seek the salvation of his soul.

Therefore he falls into the twelfth degree of pride, who through boldness in sinning runs into evil custom. This is the first descent to hell; this is the path of hell; this is the swift road to the region covered with the darkness of death. Whoever once falls into this degree of the custom of sinning rises again only by the strong hand of God’s mercy, because when custom is not resisted, it produces a necessity of sinning. He always advances in malice toward the worse, and, always falling, he falls more deeply.

Behold, these are the twelve degrees of pride by which one descends to hell. Whoever desires to avoid them must always strive to keep himself in the observance of humility. In these twelve evils every unworthiness of vice darkens the cenobite, makes the monk disordered within himself, and by his conduct changes the man into a most savage beast. The end of this is destruction; and unless the remedy of penitence intervenes before the death of the flesh, through the steps of the evil degrees it drives the monk endlessly to Tartarus.
