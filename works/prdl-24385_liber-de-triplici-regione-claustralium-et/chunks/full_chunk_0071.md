If the name of a confessor, or of a virgin, or of a saint of any other condition has been recited, the devout lover of God will easily, according to the ardor of his affection, vary his prayer extempore to any saint whatever. For affective prayers are efficacious above all others.

*At the reading of the Rule.*

When the reader, according to custom, recites one passage from the Rule, let the devout monk say in his heart: Help me, most sweet Lord Jesus Christ, that according to this rule of your religious servant Benedict I may be able to live, and to remain faithfully in your most holy service at all times.

Then let him consider that, just as the Rule is read daily to the good and devout for their progress, so it is recited to the lukewarm, the evil, or the negligent, that they may be inexcusable, to the heaping up of damnation. Finally, let him know that he is bound to a faithful effort at all the things which the Rule contains, just as the tenor of his profession holds. For thus he says in profession: I promise obedience and the conversion of my conduct according to the Rule of Saint Benedict.

*When the names of the dead are recited.*

But when, after the reading of the Rule, the names of the dead have been recited, then for these and for all other faithful, commended to him and not commended, no less out of charity than out of duty, let the monk pray thus:

*Prayer.*

I beseech you, most sweet Lord Jesus, by the pain of your wounds, and by the shedding of your most holy blood, and by the power of your most innocent death: have mercy on these souls, and on all those commended to me in prayer, and on all the faithful departed, and grant them everlasting rest.

It is indeed a great work of mercy to intercede before the merciful Judge for the souls confined in the prison of purgatory, and to come to their aid out of charity by the remembrance of the Lord's passion. For I firmly believe, and with pious presumption I trust, that the prayer which is poured out for the dead from charity alone will return doubled into the bosom of the one who prays. For the devout man who here, by the fervor of divine love, praying singularly for all the faithful departed, suffers with them in charity, will at his death be honored with an inestimable reward. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.

*On the chapter of faults.*

Since nothing in human actions is perfect in every part, let the devout monk rejoice that he has found a remedy for his imperfection: for whatever is done amiss through human frailty in well-reformed monasteries is amended daily. For the chapter of faults, continued every single day, washes away public offenses; sacramental confession, daily repeated, remits hidden negligences through the grace of contrition. We discern four chapters in the monastery, of which we shall speak in order.

*The first chapter is the passive one, when a brother accuses himself or is accused by another.*

The first chapter is called the passive one: that in which our devout monk accuses himself of his negligences, or is accused by another. In this chapter the lover of God, who has denied himself to himself, both humbly accuses his own negligences and suffers himself to be accused by others, lest he be judged more harshly by the Lord after this life. For the holy apostle of God says: If we judged ourselves, we should certainly not be judged. And the prophet Nahum says: A double tribulation shall not rise up. Truly indeed, he who here and now is willingly afflicted for his sins for the love of God deserves, as one thus well purged, to be freed from future tribulation through the mercy of the Creator. If only all cloistered persons knew how much it would profit them toward the merit of everlasting life when they willingly endure chapter accusations, they would endure all accusations and injuries for the love of God with inestimable joy. For we merit more when we patiently suffer injuries and adversities for the love of God and fraternal charity than if we apply ourselves to good works.

Standing therefore in judgment, let the devout monk not fear any accusations whatever, but long to be accused by others in memory of the Lord's passion, knowing that the glory of highness is set before him, and is to be conferred in heaven upon humble patience.
