Since, almighty eternal God, you created us from nothing when we did not exist, and mercifully redeemed us when we had perished, I beseech you by your unchangeable majesty: receive these praises and prayers which I am about to say to you now and at all times, in that union of charity and love in which you sent your only-begotten Son into the womb of the most blessed Virgin Mary, and in which you received him as he suffered and died for us on the cross. Amen.

Note on the preceding prayer.

Do not think this manner of praying frivolous, nor devised by human curiosity. For if you believe that God was accustomed to teach his people of old many things by revelation, you will in no way contradict this most holy form. Rather, read the wondrous works of the Lord which he has shown to those who love him, and you will find that he revealed this manner and form of praying to Mechtild and to others. Know this also as something fully assured: that a prayer is most pleasing to almighty God when a devout mind has resolved to offer it to God the Father in union with the Lord’s passion. And this offering can be made, in mind or by mouth, not only before the canonical hours, but also before other prayers, Masses, and any good exercises or works. But if through forgetfulness it has not been made beforehand, it can be inserted during the prayer, or even added after its completion.

How a monk ought to conduct himself in the hours.

From the beginning of the divine praise the devout cenobite should use great diligence, lest his mind admit images or thoughts about transitory things; for unless vain thoughts are driven away at once in the beginning, they will disturb the heart’s attention throughout the whole office.

Therefore the faithful worshiper should gather himself wholly within himself, and set nothing before the divine service and the true rule.

While chanting the psalms, he should refer the very matter of the psalm to the fitting focus of his intention; he should prudently consider the meaning of the prayer, pronounce the words distinctly and in order, and lift his mind upward.

After each psalm or reading, let him diligently examine himself by reflecting, and test whether the compunction of devotion is still fervent in his mind. If he finds that the fervor is still present, let him strive to kindle it further with holy desires, and to summon himself to higher things. But if he feels that he has grown lukewarm in affection or distracted by thoughts, then, reproving himself, let him say these or similar words: Woe to me, wretch that I am, who praise my God so negligently. Behold, my Lord, suffering most bitterly for me through the whole night, had no rest; and I could not watch with him for one hour. Help me, Lord my God, that I may be able to chant psalms to you wisely.

Finally, under each psalm he should suitably stir himself to compunction of fervor by some burning words, so that through inward exhortation he may inflame his affection still more. Let him say: Would that, Lord God, I could love you above all things with my whole heart, and cling to you alone without ceasing. O good Jesus, kindle my heart in your most holy love. My love has been crucified; and what am I doing upon the earth? O Jesus, my sweet love, possess my heart, for you are the treasure of my heart, my king and my God.

After Matins.

When the office of Matins has been completed, and confessions have been made or heard, let him not neglect immediately to read the hour of the Holy Cross. When it is completed, let him add this little prayer.

Prayer.

O sweetest Lord Jesus, by the pain of your wounds, by the pouring out of your most holy blood, and by the power of your most innocent death, have mercy on me now and at the hour of my death. Amen.

Then let him say:

To the holy and undivided Trinity, to the crucified humanity of Jesus Christ our Lord, to the most blessed Mary ever virgin, and to Saint Anne, her venerable mother, with all the saints, be everlasting praise and glory for infinite ages. Amen.
