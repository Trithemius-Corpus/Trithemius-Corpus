*Blessing of rest*

I commend myself this night and at every time.

*Prayer*

In your wounds, most loving Lord Jesus; and I pray you, by the merit of your most holy Passion, have mercy on me and guard me from all the snares and illusions of the enemy, so that, with a pure mind and a clean body, I may be able to perform, with the dispositions granted me, this hour that is about to be kept, and to praise you with fervor of spirit.

*Discretion for obtaining rest*

The fifth and last thing to be remembered in recollection is discretion in obtaining rest: just as the cenobite should never wholly omit this evening recollection, so too he should not wish to prolong it too indiscreetly to the destruction of his own head. Therefore, when all things have been completed with discretion, let the fervent desire for God, with tears if he can, raise up the mind; let it not pour it out too deeply. But when he goes up to his bed, let him carry with him in memory something of the Lord’s Passion, or of the love of Jesus, or certainly of the glory of the heavenly kingdom, in which, as he sleeps, he may rest sweetly.

For Bernard says: When you are about to go to sleep, carry something with you in memory or in thought, in which you may quietly fall asleep; which may sometimes even help you to dream; which may also receive you when you wake, and restore you to the state of yesterday’s intention. Thus the night will shine for you like the day, and your night will be light in your delights, so that you may quietly fall asleep, rest in peace, and wake easily; and when you rise you will be ready to return to that from which you had not wholly departed. Nevertheless, discretion must be applied in all these things, lest either excessive meditation, hindering sleep, destroy the brain, or sleep, taken immoderately, wholly interrupt meditation. Rather, a monk must sleep and meditate moderately, so that at the appointed time he may be disposed to rise for the divine office, with a cheerful and well-ordered mind. Affection inflamed too much by meditation destroys the brain and the senses, and takes sleep from weak eyes. Thus, then, let meditation in bed be brief, where great strength is not present in the head either by grace or by nature.

*On the labor of the hands. Colide. E.*

Since our most holy father Benedict, in the Rule, commending monks who live by the labor of their hands, declares them true and blessed, it has seemed fitting to us to say something after the end of the day about this most holy exercise. Indeed, we read that this work is commended by the holy fathers in many ways; and we know that in it they advanced before God and men in no small measure. For the holy Apostle says: He who does not work, let him not eat. What Augustine, more learned among the most learned, thought about this manual exercise, he shows most clearly in the book On the Work of Monks. We also read that those ancient and most holy fathers who dwelt in solitude labored fervently at the work of their hands, so that they might not incur the danger of idleness, and, living by their own labors, after the example of the most blessed teacher of the Gentiles, might burden no one. Would that we too, brothers, might deign to take up this holy exercise of laboring with the same intention and good will as they did, so that after the end of our labor we may deserve to rest with them without end in the heavenly homeland.

*Four things are to be observed by a monk in labor*

A monk called to labor should strive to show himself such in all things that he does not lose the merit of bodily labor through dissolution of mind. For we see many among monks go out to labor wrongly, whom we see seeking nothing other than the comforts of the flesh.
