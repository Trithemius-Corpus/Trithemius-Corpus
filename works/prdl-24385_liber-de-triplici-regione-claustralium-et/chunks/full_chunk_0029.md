For it is useful and also necessary for us to restrain the powers of sensual passions; among monks I think almost no one is ignorant of this. Indeed, whoever desires to reach the due end of regular life which we have described in this region must subdue, with prudent constancy, his disordered natural habits and movements, because no one can truly resign himself to another’s command if he does not firmly restrain the powers of sensual passions. One who desires to make progress must leave behind, with steadfast mind, both the movements and the prudence of the flesh, and show himself a son of divine grace, not of flesh and blood. For Scripture says: If you live according to the command of the flesh, you will die. Indeed, those who sow in the flesh will reap corruption from the flesh. But flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God. But what does the Savior say to us? “A man’s enemies,” he says, “are those of his own household.” And who are these members of his household except the sensual powers that oppose the spirit? For concupiscibility and irascibility, our enemies, stir up within us all evils against the spirit and continually disturb the house of conscience with various passions. For as long as these sensual powers carry out their natural movements and courses in a man, they do not allow him to ascend to the freedom of the spirit. Concupiscibility enjoys the delights in the sense of the flesh, and, on the contrary, the irascible power detests the bodily hardships. Meanwhile, as long as this war of the flesh is waged in a man, he is regarded as a restless, carnal monk and a dweller in the lowest region. Therefore whoever desires to come to eternal life must firmly restrain these disordered powers of sense in all things.

Therefore the faithful cenobite strives to direct the concupiscible power toward hardships that are acceptable to God, and to reform all his affections according to God’s good pleasure. If he does this, he will pray confidently to the Lord: “My soul has longed to desire your justifications at all times.” And, sweetly weeping, he will cry out that other saying: “As the deer longs for the fountains of waters, so my soul longs for you, O God.” Then let him learn prudently to attach the irascible power to God, to set it against sensual delights, and to reform it diligently toward hatred of vices. Thus the soul, ordered toward its principle, will not fear to endure any hardships for the love of God, and by means of the irascible power it will begin to resist firmly all carnal desires. And then the devout cenobite, with his spirit lifting itself up, will cry: “My soul refused to be comforted.” And that saying of the Apostle: “I have counted all things as dung.” For the soul that truly loves God despises everything except him.

From these two powers every disordered affection arises; and good affections likewise are generated from them in the contrary way. For joy and hope take their beginning from the concupiscible power, and the irascible power brings forth sorrow and fear. Saint Bernard speaks of these thus: “The whole heart of man is governed by four affections; if we bring these back to God, the whole heart of man is converted.”

On the Faithful Cleansing of the Powers

Article Three

Since we are by no means able to restrain the sensual powers unless we also faithfully cleanse the intellectual powers, great and continual diligence is needed for this cleansing, because the cenobite who is weakened by the torpor of negligence is never cleansed from vicious affections. For one who sows with corrupted powers brings back nothing else from his errors but corruption. Therefore the cenobite, the inhabitant of this middle region, must correct his higher powers from every disorder, if he wishes to remain constantly in the pursuit of progress.
