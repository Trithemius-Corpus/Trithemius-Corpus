For although no one is holy by his own powers, nevertheless no one does good well unwillingly. Thus the grace of God and freedom of choice stand in relation to one another, and one without the other cannot suffice for saving a human being; both must necessarily come together for the working of virtue. Therefore, since without the grace of God we can do nothing good, we ought to consider ourselves worthless and unworthy of all things, so that, when we submit ourselves to God in humility, we may always obtain greater gifts. And therefore this sixth degree of confession is one of merit and the single foundation of all the preceding ones. For as long as the cenobite remains in this degree of humility, he will stand firm in the earlier ones.

The seventh degree of humility is not only to believe, from the inmost affection of the heart, that one is lower than all, but also to desire, with a simple mind, that others believe and say this about him. This condition of holy humility sets one on high, and in this time it is loved by few monks. The first condition of this deep humility seems to consist in this: that the monk ought from his heart to reckon himself lower than all. The second is that he should rejoice to be reckoned worthless and abject by all, and should desire this without pretense from the inmost affection of his heart. The third condition is placed in a higher dwelling: namely, that with the same affection the monk should delight to be treated most contemptibly and to be trampled on by all. Rooted in this humility, the cenobite loves reproach and insult; he does not fear being despised and most contemptibly abused, because, submitting himself wholly in the love of God, he humbly takes his place, and hastens to insults as though to banquets, with the greatest cheerfulness. But so that you may understand the truth of this seventh degree, read the following considerations.

The first consideration directs the mind toward hope in divine love, which arises from a certain pious confidence. Although a person may never know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless each person can in some way recognize, from the disposition of his own heart, what he ought to think about his own state, whether good or bad.

Second, one must consider that through a good disposition of heart and an orderly will toward God, love of God and approval by the good are signs.

Thus, when he was born in the flesh, the angels sang: Peace on earth to people of good will.

Third, it must be noted that just as a good disposition of heart always orders and summons the mind through holy exercises toward the fear of God and the practice of virtues, so the carelessness of an evil disposition inclines it especially toward vices and sloth. Reflection on this difference provides a conjecture.

Fourth, one must consider that an evil disposition of heart hinders and empties out the grace of God and merits in a person, because it makes the soul base, ignoble, and degenerate, and always disposes it toward graver and worst things. Indeed, the root of evils of tongue and deed comes from an evil heart, as is clear in Saul and many similar cases.

The fifth consideration is that evil of deed and tongue is never properly examined unless the evil of the heart is first truly known. Each person can examine his own heart better than another’s, and consider what kind of person lies hidden within himself. Therefore it is enough for the cenobite to whom care of another has not been entrusted to inspect his own heart diligently, and in no way to occupy himself with scrutiny of another’s heart. But the shepherd, or the monk to whom the care of souls is entrusted, must also know the face of his flock.

Sixth, from these things it must be noted that it is very rash to wish to examine another’s heart, and from words and deeds to judge the inner thoughts of the brothers. The Lord knows those who are his, and it is not granted to a human being, apart from the reason of his office, to become a rash judge of another’s heart.

Seventh, it must be noted that a small evil of one’s own heart, known in oneself, harms more than a great evil suspected doubtfully in another’s heart. For the former must be corrected, because it is certain and known; the latter must be piously excused, because it is unknown.
