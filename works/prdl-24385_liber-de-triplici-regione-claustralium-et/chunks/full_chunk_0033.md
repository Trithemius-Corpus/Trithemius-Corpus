Let him read the things that kindle the soul to the love of God through fear. Therefore let frequent reading on the last things that await us take its beginning from fear; then let the deeds of the holy fathers be read, so that the memory of the future may strike the mind with fear of God, and the conduct of holy examples may kindle the desire to imitate them. For the wise man says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin.” The conduct of the holy fathers is set before us as an example, and the destruction of the wicked is recounted for our instruction, so that rewarded virtue may stir desire, and punished iniquity may forbid license to sin.

Therefore let the devout cenobite call to mind the examples and virtues of the saints; let him consider the brief pleasure of the wicked, but their eternal torment, so that by fearing unhappiness he may deserve to escape it, and by persisting in virtues obtain that everlasting joy. The distinct condition of the good and the wicked is no slight matter, since it is described as truly enduring forever and is ended by no limit. O eternity, always to be feared by mortals: in it, after this life has been completed, there is no fear for the just, and no hope of deliverance for the wicked. Let him who can consider what eternity is, where there is no end; let him who dares imagine a limit; and when in eternity he finds nothing other than eternal, let him understand that all the time in which he lives in this world is nothing. For indeed, once the spirit has been driven from the flesh, it is necessarily compelled to enter this abyss of eternity; and the condition which it has once received according to its merits it will bear without end and will not be changed into another. For he who dies good in the charity of God will obtain eternal life according to his merits. But he who, evil and without the reconciliation of repentance, has once departed from this life, will remain with the demons in hell, to be tormented without end.

From these meditations servile fear arises in the mind, through which one gradually comes to love. But so that we may successively lead cenobites through these roots to charity, it pleases us to explain briefly, in order, the degrees of humility described in the Rule.

The first degree of humility described by Saint Benedict in the Rule is the fear of God, which is called the beginning of wisdom. Fear, however, is handed down with a threefold distinction: namely, servile, initial, and filial, which is also called chaste. From this threefold distinction three roots are spoken of, according to which the beginning cenobite ought to order his meditations. For each root has its own proper meditations, and when one is rightly disposed, another better one is always generated.

From servile fear, if it is taken up not merely servilely but prudently and virtuously, come meditations on terrors and punishments, which, by restraining the soul from sin, gradually introduce it to love. For when the mind is turned to horror by the consideration of punishments, boldness in sinning is restrained, because the servant who fears blows keeps the commands of his lord. This servile fear, however, is not sufficient for meriting the paternal inheritance; yet as an initial effect it is disposed toward filial fear, which is sufficient.

Initial fear is placed midway between the two, and from the third it is easily changed into filial fear. This initial fear receives meditations on the last things, and both fears to incur punishments and trembles to lose the sweet fatherland. And this place is set down in the Rule, where its roots are also described.

Filial fear, however, gathers its meditations into love, since it fears nothing except that the beloved father be offended. Of this fear the wise man says: “The fear of the Lord is glory and boasting, gladness and a crown of exultation. The fear of the Lord will delight the heart and will give gladness and joy and length of days.” And again: “It will be well with him who fears God at the last, and on the day of his death he will be blessed.” This fear is called chaste because it detests evil not from fear of punishments, but from love alone.
