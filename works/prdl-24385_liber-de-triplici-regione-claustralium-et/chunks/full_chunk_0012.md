Secondly, the pastor of souls becomes disordered toward his subjects if his mind is too boldly and excessively occupied with temporal affairs, if he has greater concern for secular business than for the souls committed to him. This excessive care for temporal things the holy father Benedict forbids in the Rule, and he rightly judges rational souls to be preferred to all worldly things. “Above all,” he says, “let the abbot not, by feigning ignorance or making light of it, neglect the salvation of the souls entrusted to him, and take greater care for transient, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always think that he has undertaken to govern souls, for which he will have to render an account.” Accordingly, concerning the necessities of the body, the loving father gives assurance, if one loves God and worships him with a pure mind. “And lest perhaps,” he says, “he complain of too little substance, let him remember what is written: ‘Seek first the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things shall be added unto you.’ And again: ‘Nothing is lacking to those who fear God.’”

But the disordered monastic pastor is seduced by greed; yet, blinded in mind, he does not perceive it. He casts away the fear of the Lord, and, like Ahab sold under sin, he runs to the aid of wickedness; he is deprived of the confidence promised by the holy father concerning the obtaining of necessities, and he is occupied outwardly more than befits a monk. Driven thereafter by the goads of greed, he despises the commandments of God and of holy mother Church; he does not keep feast days, but plunges himself wholly into earthly affairs; he thinks and meditates on nothing else. Like Saul, the fool transgresses the commandment of the Lord, and stupidly supposes that he can be cleansed by victims offered against obedience. Finally, fattened and swollen with secular affairs or cares, he generally slips into every kind of wantonness and laxity; he detests the observance of regular discipline, and, since he fears being restrained from evil pursuits by the authority of the chapter, he seeks the dissolution of holy union. Then, having become reprobate like Saul, he calls upon the counsel of the witch; he cries out that obedience to reform is damnable for his monastery; he desires to be released as quickly as possible from submission to the holy confederation, and to this end he tells many things falsely and mendaciously.

But when the authority of the annual chapter is despised with impunity, it remains that monastic observance should not be broken, since the love of God keeps the good regularly within discipline, and fear of correction restrains the wicked from evil works. Whoever tries to escape the unity of the chapter imitates the devil. Thus the emperor of the Bulgars forbade the Christians subject to him, under penalty of death, to hold an assembly in church, though he granted them freedom to believe whatever they wished. For the cunning man knew that the sincerity of the Christian faith could not long endure if the office of public exhortation happened to be lacking. The disordered and carnal abbot, an adversary of the Christian faith, strives as much as he can to imitate him, because he resists the commands of so necessary a chapter. For the authority of the chapter alone is what preserves the observance of regular discipline in the order; if it is despised, no integrity of monastic life will remain.

But the disordered abbot, a cultivator of the lowest region, invents innumerable reasons against regular observance; he falsely claims that the conditions of his monastery are contrary to it; he complains that ancient privileges are being suppressed; he deems it beneath him that his monastery should be subject to anyone. But woe to disordered men, through whom even one monastery may happen to be lost from this most holy congregation. Finally, this plague of greed makes the abbot disordered not only toward his subjects, but also opposed to himself and utterly turned away from God. For covetousness is the root of all evils; taking away the servant of Christ, it makes him a minister of the devil. Therefore the abbot who has subjected his mind to this vice shows himself to be not a pastor but a hireling. He always gapes after earthly gains; he cares nothing for the progress of souls. The book of sacred reading rarely or never rests in his hands; registers of revenues are turned over more often. He joins field to field, always burying gold and silver in his chest as he gathers it; and while he desires too much for his monastery to abound in temporal goods, he prepares prey for robbers. For often the treasury of secular lordship devours the monies of monasteries, which the excessive greed of monks brings into the monastery. Woe to you, greedy pastor of cloistered men, who have greater concern for brute animals than for the souls committed to you.
