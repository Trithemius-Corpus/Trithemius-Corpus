Obedience to prelates must be kept strictly according to the Gospel and the monastic rule; for although the mutual obedience of the brethren is good, surely it is not to be preferred to the command of a superior. Monks are ordered to render discreet obedience to one another, and, while preserving the commands of superiors, to anticipate one another in mutual service. To take an example from our own custom: obedience is owed to all according to order. If a brother calls on you, obey gladly; if another, a senior, commands you, leave aside the command of the first and obey him. Again, if the prior of the community intervenes with his command, all mutual obedience of the brethren will cease. But if the abbot’s command intervenes, no obedience to anyone else will have force against it. For he is believed to act first in Christ’s place in the monastery, and therefore he is preferred to all.

There are seven graded circumstances of the holy virtue of obedience, which blessed Bernard described. The first degree of obedience is to obey willingly, so that you have no will of your own at all, but believe your shepherd’s good pleasure to be wholly your own. The second is to obey simply, so that you do neither more nor otherwise than what has been commanded you, nor omit anything by doing less. The third is to obey cheerfully, so that you show serenity in your face and sweetness in your voice. The fourth is to obey swiftly, so that the hand is not slower in the work than the command is in the voice. The fifth is to obey manfully, lest one withdraw his strength from the command and pretend to be weak and unequal to the precepts. The sixth is to obey humbly: not proudly, not arrogantly, not contentiously; for unless obedience is humble, it will receive not reward but punishment. The seventh is to obey perseveringly, because that obedience is not true which is interrupted before completion; and only he who perseveres to the end is rewarded.

The fruit of obedience is everlasting and is never bounded by any end. Almighty God obeys our prayers only insofar as we obey our superiors. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, because of persistence in prayer, for Christ does not hear the prayer of one who despises Christ’s vicar in the monastery. Humility raises prayer; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, the finder of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, closes the gates of hell, becomes a fellow-dweller with the angels, and possesses the food of all the saints. But the monk who casts off subjection to obedience runs toward death.

The fourth degree of humility is patience in adversities. Our holy father Benedict speaks of this in the Rule: if a monk, under obedience, embraces patience with silent conscience in hard and contrary things, or even in any injuries inflicted on him. This degree also takes its beginning from the third, since no one is truly to be called obedient if he is found impatient. For as lord Chrysostom says, he cannot be acceptable to the king who has been subject to the enemy; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been marked by the passion of impatience. For no one will be able to conquer a fierce enemy unless he has learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, since no virtue perfects the one who reads if patience deserts him. It is also, as blessed Gregory teaches, a mark of greater merit to endure adversities patiently for God and to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: He who thinks that tribulation has come upon him for his sins calls forth God’s mercy more fully toward doing good. Therefore let the cenobite learn to oppose the virtue of patience to all accidents, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. He who suffers conquers; he who murmurs is overcome.
