All these things are full of mysteries; if they are examined with careful consideration, they will splendidly commend this supreme region of the spirit. For Mount Sinai signifies the height of this region; the smoking cloud represents the object of contemplation; Moses expresses the affection burning in love; the people show the crowd of thoughts and phantasms. Moses alone, the leader of the people, is permitted to ascend the mountain and enter the cloud, because the affection inflamed with divine love alone is raised to the summit of the light of contemplation. But the people are forbidden, under threat of death, to approach the mountain, because no intellect, no thought, no imagination or reasoning can in any way penetrate the secret of the divine majesty. The common crowd stands far from the mountain, stricken with fear, because when the mind alone enters the abyss of contemplation through the affection of love, the whole crowd of thoughts, failing utterly, is thrown into confusion. The intellect does not dare to approach its own affection as it ascends, but, standing far off and shaken with fear within itself, is forced to remain behind. Therefore, yielding in wonder before majesty, it says with the prophet to the Lord: “Truly you are a hidden God.” But Moses, standing on the summit of the mountain, is wrapped in the cloud: he does not grasp God by the light of the intellect, but passes into contemplation of him only through love and desire. Moses, and not the Lord, speaks to the fearful people, because the purified affection enters the dwelling-place of love, through which the powers of the spirit are enlightened.

By what modes or degrees one must ascend to this supreme summit of this region.

Although in several preceding places of this little work I have briefly handed down the manner of ascending to this supreme region of the spirit, nevertheless, as I think, it will not be useless to repeat blessed things more often, so that we may be able to retain them more firmly. For it seems to me of little profit to describe the beauty of contemplation unless we also know how to show those who desire it the way of coming to it. Therefore, whoever wishes to ascend through the desire of love to the pleasantness of this upper region should strive to exercise himself diligently in the labors of the middle region, because unless in that region he has been purged from sensual passions through the practice of holy compunction, he approaches this one not without danger. For undertaking this exercise, let him often recall to memory the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; let him weep from delight in divine love; and let him learn to run through the text of that Passion frequently in attentive meditation, and to stir himself to compassion for the Savior.

And because the body, which is corrupt, weighs down the soul, so that the mind cannot vigorously direct itself in this meditation on the Lord’s Passion, let him strive, if need be, to present to his thoughts images that are truly perceptible to the senses. Therefore, withdrawing from crowds into silence, let him begin to turn over in memory the order of the Passion; let him see himself with the eyes of the mind as present at each event with the suffering Lord; let him hear Jesus speaking in his imagination; let him go with him through all the places of the Passion. Then at each article let him bring his affections out of a desire for compassion; let him gaze with faithful eyes upon Jesus suffering, set before him; flowing with anguish of heart, let him grow accustomed in spirit to address him as though bodily present, and to embrace him, wounded at the pillar and on the cross, through the desire of love. For the mind can easily be kindled in love of the Lord Jesus if it truly grows accustomed to suffer with him through the tears of meditation. But I remember having written elsewhere more fully on the most effective exercise of the Lord’s Passion; therefore I have touched on it briefly here. For it is enough to have given beginners the first principles of meditation, since those who are experienced have higher things.

Therefore, when the affection has been purged from every passion and hindering concupiscence, the mind, with the help of the intellect, begins to think somewhat upon the goodness and beauty of the highest good; then it desires to behold the immutable good. But when, with the intellect lifting it up, it raises itself through admiration toward knowledge of that good, at once the operation of the intellect grows faint; nor can reason follow the affection any farther, since it sees that it can be raised only in another way, and only when wholly and completely stripped bare of all things. But so long as the mind has not been lawfully purified, the affection does not reach the true cloud of this mountain.

Contemplation, however, sometimes comes through the enlargement of the mind: when the soul stretches itself more vigorously by its own effort in holy meditation, meditation is changed into contemplation. And this mode of contemplation is accustomed to rise up through three degrees: teaching, by which we learn what we must do; exercise, by which we carry out what we have learned; and attention, by which we fulfill the task.
