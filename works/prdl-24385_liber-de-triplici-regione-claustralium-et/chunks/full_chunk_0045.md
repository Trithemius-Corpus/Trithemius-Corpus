His Passion was also contrary to custom, because it received no delay of time. According to custom he is not put in prison; nothing is deliberated in his case. Instead, once captured, he is immediately handed over to the judge to be condemned to death; scourged, spat upon, afflicted with many injuries, blows, and mockeries, he is nailed to the cross.

Third, it was contrary to the law of nature, and procured by the obstinate hatred of the heart. For nature forbids cruelty against the dead, and shudders to tear a slain body with new wounds. For our Redeemer, dead upon the cross, is pierced with a lance; and, contrary to the law of nature, the departed one is wounded by the impious.

Second, the Passion of Christ is said to have been ignominious in its manner, for the six causes that follow. First, because of the paltry price: the blood of incomparable worth is sold by a disciple for thirty pieces of silver, for which no one would deign to sell his little donkey. Second, because of the ignominious office: filled with wounds and half dead, he is compelled to carry his own cross, something found to have been done to no robber. Third, because of the unworthy place: he is crucified outside the city, in the place of Calvary, where robbers and wicked murderers are punished for their deserts. Fourth, because of the ignominious punishment: he is hung naked upon the cross, a penalty owed only to robbers and wicked men. Indeed, as Scripture says, the Redeemer of the world was condemned to a most shameful death. Fifth, because of the ignominious mockery: naked before the whole multitude he is hung upon the cross, and is blasphemed by all as though he were a fool and a madman. Of this the psalmist says: “All the day my shame is before me.” Sixth, because of the ignominious company: numbered with the wicked, he is hung in the midst of robbers.

What are you doing now, O Christian soul? Behold, your Savior hangs in the middle between robbers, and you are not afraid to sit in the first places in council?

Third, the most holy Passion was most bitter with regard to pain, for seven reasons. First, because of the delicate and lively nature of his flesh, which in its natural constitution was the best; therefore it was grievously afflicted in the oppression of pain. For delicate flesh suffers more than flesh coarse by natural condition. Since, then, the flesh of Christ was fine and delicate, in the garden it gave forth a bloody sweat. Second, because of the character of those who killed him: for Christ was not killed by strangers, but by his own kinsmen and closest people, as he had earlier complained through the prophet: “My people, what have I done to you?” For one suffers more tolerably from strangers and enemies than from one’s own people, from whom at least friends by nearness of blood feel compassion. Third, because of the bitterness of the Passion: he is afflicted most in those places where pain is felt more strongly because of the nerves. For raised upon the cross, he is fastened by hands and feet; the Lord of the universe is given gall and vinegar to drink. For the psalmist says of him: “They have dug my hands and my feet; they have numbered all my bones.”

Who could conceive in his mind the pain of your most holy heart, O good Jesus, when they began so cruelly to pierce your most pure hands and feet? Would that my soul may always show itself grateful to you; would that the remembrance of your most holy Passion may never depart from my memory. I shall easily be able to obtain from you the effect of my prayers if I have taken care to delight without interruption in the exercise of your Passion. Let each one think what he can: I prefer the memory of your Passion to all exercises, through which I trust that in my prayers I shall receive whatever accords with salvation.

Fourth, because of the long duration, the Passion of Christ was most bitter: from the hour of his captivity up to his death he was always in most bitter torment. “All the day,” he says, “I went about sorrowful, and there is no soundness in my flesh.” Fifth, because of the universality of the Passion: for in whatever ways Christ could suffer, he suffered singularly. For he suffers in his reputation, because, falsely accused, he is blasphemed by the impious. He also suffers in his possessions, because, naked and stripped, he is hung upon the wood.
