Sixth, the Creator has bestowed on us time for meriting eternal life, and has given the necessary nourishment of the body. Here too we are debtors to ourselves, since in this time we owe ourselves the acquisition of merit. Why then do we spend the days of salvation so negligently and without the fruit of merit, when we have nothing more precious than time? Behold, in this brief time we can merit the joys of eternal happiness, and yet we neglect to do every good thing. Woe to us, foolish and mad, who esteem nothing in this world more precious than time, and yet are accustomed to spend nothing more cheaply. For now this acceptable time is granted to us; now the days of salvation are bestowed, in which we ought to devote ourselves to holy works with the greatest fervor, and always keep the hour of death before our eyes.

Seventh, God has set heaven before us all, filled with joys, and has promised himself, with all things contained in it, as our reward. For this munificence of our Savior we ought to devote ourselves to holy works of mercy, and show ourselves imitators of God as far as our strength allows. The Lord exhorts us to this in the holy Gospels: “Be merciful, just as your Father God is merciful.” Likewise at the end of the world, when the day of judgment comes, the merciful, as the Lord himself promises, will go into eternal life, because whoever has shown mercy will obtain mercy. And lest anyone say to me, “As a monk I can bestow mercy on no one, since my religious state permits me to possess absolutely nothing,” we should know that among the necessary works of mercy, spiritual works are more important. If, as a monk, you wish to be merciful, pray to God for the wretched; impart saving doctrine to the ignorant.

The sum, then, of meditation on benefits rests on three exercises, in which it is principally contained. In the first, we ought to attribute all things to God, not to ourselves; in the second, by remembering God’s benefits, we ought to exercise ourselves in the pursuit of love; in the third, we ought always to give thanks from a pure heart for benefits received. But if by these exercises concerning benefits we wish to kindle our heart to the love of God, we ought to consider six conditions in the Lord’s gifts. First, consider that the gift of God is utterly noble; second, that it is especially fruitful and useful to you; third, that it is varied, manifold, and very frequently repeated. These three consider the gift as ordered in itself; the two that follow regard the person of the giver; the last marks the condition of the receiver. Fourth, therefore, consider the great affection of the giver toward you; fifth, his inestimable dignity; sixth, that you, the receiver, are lowly and contemptible, receiving great things from the greatest, and unworthy of all things. From these considerations of benefits, the love of God is accustomed to arise in the mind of the devout person, and, advancing by fixed degrees, to lift the spirit to contemplation.

On knowledge of oneself.

The fourth root producing divine love is called knowledge of oneself, very useful if it is referred to the preceding things. For if it is brought back to the mind in consideration how great a Lord has done such great things for us who are so little, we shall recognize that we are unworthy, vile, and wholly undeserving of all things. Four things ought to lead a person to knowledge of himself; from consideration of them we are especially fittingly moved to love God.

First, then, let the faithful person consider the beginning of his creation, and he will find that, when he did not exist, he was created from the goodness of God alone. Who could ask God, when no one existed? That highest, utterly ineffable good, O man, made you to his image when you did not exist; from goodness alone he made you a sharer in his goodness. What did you deserve when you did not exist? Or what advantage could your condition bring to almighty God, who, always subsisting unchangeably in himself, admits no accident? Nothing is new to God; nothing can be old to the highest and unchangeable good. He is always [unclear].
