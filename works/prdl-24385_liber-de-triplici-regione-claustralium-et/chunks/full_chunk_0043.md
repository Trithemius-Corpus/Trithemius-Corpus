The less the incomprehensible divine majesty can be grasped, the more curiously it is investigated by man. For the prophet says: “Truly you are a hidden God.” Meditation on divine perfection begets wonder; wonder causes reverence; from reverence arises fear of majesty, and from fear worship grows.

But the meditation of the highest goodness is divided, according to our purpose, into seven parts, which are not unfittingly adapted to the seven days of the week, or also to the seven canonical hours. And although that highest and most perfect goodness abundantly suffices for us as material for meditation, yet, because it belongs to few to be able to ascend to invisible things without visible ones, we wish to raise the minds of the weak through things known to things unknown. For the holy apostle says: “The invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made; his everlasting power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.”

First, therefore, the wondrous power of God is praiseworthy in the principal work of creation, since he, who was able to bring all things forth from nothing, created things so great, so solid, so varied, and so noble by his will alone. From this consideration arises wonder at the divine power, through which man is moved to the fear of God.

Second, the provident wisdom of God is praiseworthy, because, knowing all things, he orders the universe, and governs by his providence all things that he has created. He knows all things, understands all things; by his counsel he disposes all things; by his command he encloses all things. As a certain lover of the wisdom of the world says: God is a sphere whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. He is within all things and is not enclosed; outside all things and not excluded; beneath all things and not oppressed; above all things and not borne up. He includes all things, and because he is wholly everywhere, he is confined in no place; great and immeasurable, he is bounded by no quantity. He orders all things as he wills; stable in himself, always unmoved, he changes the vicissitudes of things; good, without passion of mind, he mercifully prepares eternal things for merits; angered without disturbance, he condemns the unjust. Great without magnitude, immeasurable without quantity, he has set bounds to things; governing the world, he disposes all things.

Third, the merciful clemency of God is praiseworthy in our redemption, because he so mercifully restored man, whom he had made in his own image and who had fallen so miserably into the pit of damnation. O what shall I render to the highest and incomprehensible majesty, I, a little worm, indeed nothing compared with my maker, who, when I did not exist, created me by his power, and, when I had perished, being made man for my sake and dying on the cross, redeemed me? What shall I repay you, mediator of God and men, Jesus, savior of the world, who, though you are the high and inestimable God, became man for my sake, most vile as I am, and willed to undergo things so vile and unworthy? Behold, my heart speaks to you; all my inward parts are bowed toward you, since no voice can express what you inwardly give your faithful to feel in their hearts concerning your most innocent Passion.

Fourth, in the work of our justification the kind patience of God is praiseworthy, because he not only mercifully bears with sinners, but even calls those who are to be justified to penance by promised eternal joys. Who could sufficiently praise this most indulgent clemency of the Father, who so mercifully justifies sinners returning to him, grants after a fall the grace of meriting, arranges many occasions for living well, supplies his sacraments to the penitent as certain vessels of grace? Finally, for compunction, instruction, consolation, and the working of grace, he pours the Holy Spirit into his faithful, making friends and sons out of enemies. He does not consider what sort of sinner a man was before; he seeks only that henceforth he remain faithful to him. Great is the clemency of divine compassion, which so mercifully receives the penitent to pardon, and even promises them great rewards after guilt.

Fifth, the deep justice of God is praiseworthy in the damnation of the reprobate, because he permits no evil to pass unpunished, depriving the sinner of the good which, in sinning, the fool does not fear to lose. Finally, to speak in our manner, the highest cleanness of God is shown praiseworthy in this: that it abominates all sin and vice like a putrid corpse. We also recognize that his wisdom is praiseworthy in the damnation of the wicked, drawing good things even from evils for himself [unclear].
