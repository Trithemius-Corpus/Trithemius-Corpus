Servile fear performs its exercises; initial fear applies itself to virtues; and filial fear proceeds much differently in its works. For just as affections come to the heart in various ways, so the exercises of one fear take place in different ways. For the publican feared the judge’s sentence in one way, through the memory of himself and of his sins; Jerome said that in another way he seemed to hear the sound of the trumpet calling the dead to judgment. And concerning the former the holy Gospel says: Standing far off, the publican would not lift up his eyes to heaven, but, striking his breast, said, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” But that blessed Jerome said: “Whether I drink or eat, I always think that I hear that trumpet sounding in my ears: ‘Rise, you dead; come to judgment.’” The compunction of both arises from fear, but both are not begotten from the desire of one and the same affection. For the publican, terrified by the guilt of sins and by the sentence of the just judge, feared, and therefore, pierced with compunction, said, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” Jerome, however, is not shaken by fear from consciousness of guilt, but, founded in charity and reckoning himself an unprofitable servant, reveres the abyss of divine justice.

The second degree is to abandon one’s own will for love of God, and not to love one’s own willing in anything against the rule. This degree arises from the first ascent of humility and lifts one up to the third. For truly, he who fears God, and truly dreads being separated from him and undergoing punishments, hates nothing more than his own will. For the faithful cenobite knows that every sin arises from self-will alone, and therefore he detests it as the gate of hell. For that blessed man truly said: Take away self-will, and there will be no hell. For nothing burns in hell except self-will; nothing loses the grace of God except voluntary fault. Therefore whoever truly fears God and dreads losing his grace begins to fear his own will as the ambusher of the soul. The consideration of the danger of self-will makes the cenobite despise his own willing and deny himself. And this denial of self-will and of oneself is perfected in three ways, as will be said in what follows.

First, he denies himself to himself who strives to live according to the teaching of eternal wisdom and not according to his own impulses, and to obey Christ, who persuades him to humility in all things. Therefore let the Savior say to the cenobite: If you wish truly to deny yourself to yourself and follow me, take away your eyes, lest they see harmful things; your ears, lest they hear vain things; and in place of the sweet things of this world, take bitter things. Learn to despise the pleasures of your body; to love tranquility and peace of heart; to receive all tribulations patiently and joyfully for love of me; and to desire to be seen by all as vile and contemptible. Learn accordingly to break your wills in all things, to mortify the desires of the flesh, to despise all earthly things, and, as far as can be done, to conform your life with all your strength to the way of life of your Savior. For he who says that he abides in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. Whoever has done these things has well denied himself.

Second, the denial of self-will is then rightly perfected when the advantages of others are set before one’s own. For the holy apostle says: “Not seeking the things that are your own, but the things that are others’.” For according to the saying of Bernard, the soul is laid down for the brethren as often as their advantages are preferred to one’s own benefits for love of God.

Third, the resignation of self-will receives the fullness of perfection when the cenobite, pierced with compunction and wholly handed over to God, desires out of love to serve him most gratefully without regard for reward. And this most holy resignation of self-will makes a man near to God and always rests in his love.

But just as this resignation of oneself receives its substance from the second degree of humility, so also it necessarily requires inward and complete resignation to the will of God in all things. For the Lord says in the Gospel: “I have not come to do my own will, but the will of the Father who sent me.” Perfect resignation, therefore, is that by which the human will is totally subjected to the divine will and, out of whole love, [unclear].
