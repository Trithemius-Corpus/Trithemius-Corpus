The ordered effect of this renewal is shown when the truth of the articles of the middle region is duly observed.

The first article, then, is the diligent intention of the cenobitic end; the second is the due restraint of the sensual powers; the third is the faithful cleansing of the powers; the fourth is the attentive reformation of the work of the lower region; the fifth is the ordered instruction in the progress of the true cenobite; the sixth is the adaptation of the powers for entry into the third region; and the seventh is the exemplary or experimental certainty of the aforesaid and of all things.

On the continual and diligent pursuit of the cenobitic end.

The first article.

Since the end is first in the intention of the wise man, and everything in its own kind is judged the more perfect the more closely it approaches its end, the monk who cultivates this middle region should always consider why he entered religion. If he finds his intention ordered, he should strive to continue it; but if he finds it corrupt, he should not fail to reform it according to the rectitude of the due end.

But note that in spiritual exercises the end is described as twofold, by which the virtue of monastic life is increased in merits. The first is charity, which is called the principal and ultimate end, to which all monastic exercises are ordered as to their first cause. Whatever good we do must always and principally be ordered to this end, because no work of virtue perfects merit unless charity always establishes the due foundation.

“God,” says John, “is charity; and he who abides in charity abides in God, and God in him.” And everyone who abides in God does not sin. The second end is dispositive toward the first and principal end, and is directed to it as a kind of intermediate fittingness; this is called secondary, or less principal. The monk must always attend to this too, and order his exercises through it to the ultimate end. For no one can lightly reach the end of principal charity if he neglects to observe the intermediate end.

This dispositive end toward the ultimate, so far as it pertains to cenobites, according to John Cassian in the Conferences of the Fathers, is to cast away from oneself entirely all the cares of the world, to mortify one’s own wills, to entrust every disposition of oneself to a prudent superior, and to take no thought for tomorrow. For in this middle region the cenobite must utterly reject his own will by taking up the denial of himself, and then resign himself totally into the hands of his prudent superior, so that within himself he may be clean from every impulse of his own will, and outwardly free from every secular occupation for serving the Lord. Indeed, unless the cenobite has come to this end, he is by no means a true monk, because he still sows corruption in corruptible flesh and in no way observes the virtue of his profession.

Next, the monastic denial of one’s own will seems principally to consist in three things, without which no one can truly be said to have denied himself. First, the mortification of the will requires in the monk the prior uprooting of his own judgment, because whoever relies on his own judgment or counsel disdains being humbly subject to another. For the root from which the defense of one’s own will first arises is called adherence to one’s own counsel or judgment. But whoever, while established in a monastery, desires to cling to his own judgment sins gravely in the Rule by usurping his own will. For it is fitting that the cenobite live by the command of others rather than by his own, if he desires to attain eternal life by the merit of obedience.
