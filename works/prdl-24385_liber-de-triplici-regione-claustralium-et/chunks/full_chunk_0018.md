The fifth degree of pride is called singularity, which is born from the swelling of boastfulness. Proud cloisterers are gathered into this degree: those who, out of boastfulness, have presumed that they excel others either in merit or in knowledge. Hence they do not appear like the rest in their way of life, but from pride alone show themselves singular in everything. But this singularity is reprehensible in a monastery, where all are ordered by the rule to live in common. Proud monks, as blessed Bernard testifies, while they prefer themselves to others in virtues and wisdom, fall into such arrogant rashness that they flatter themselves more for a single fast while the rest are eating than if others had fasted for seven days; one little private prayer seems to them far more profitable than the whole psalmody of a night. During meals they often carry their eyes around the table, and if they see anyone eating less, they groan; they begin to grieve within themselves that they have been outdone, cruelly withdrawing from themselves what was provided for necessary sustenance. Proud cloisterers are eager for their singular practices, sluggish for common ones. They lie awake in bed; in choir and at the regular reading they doze.

The sixth degree of pride takes its beginning from the fifth; it is called the arrogance of the proud. For when disordered cloisterers, through zeal for singularity, begin to appear outwardly before others, they burn into a greater madness of arrogance, and whatever is said by men in their praise, the fools consider true. O pitiable folly of the proud: although in all other matters they trust no one more than themselves, about themselves alone they trust themselves less than another. Whatever praise of themselves they hear from the ignorant, they ascribe not to the ignorance or good will of the one praising them, but to their own merits. But whoever is delightfully fed on human praises is regarded before God as empty of every virtue and full of arrogance.

The seventh degree of pride is born from the preceding one and is called presumption. From this begin those who singularly believe themselves to be something great. Of them Bernard says: He who thinks he surpasses others, how should he not presume more about himself and about others? He sits first at the banquet; in council he answers first; uncalled he approaches; unbidden he intrudes; he rearranges what has been arranged and rejects what has been done by others. Whatever he himself has not done or ordered, he does not think rightly done or properly arranged. He judges those who judge; he prejudges those who are about to judge. For one to whom God is not enough in the heart seeks consolation in outward things in vain.

The eighth degree of pride is called the excuse and defense of sin, and it is always born from presumption. For a presumptuous monk always considers himself better than everyone else; the sin he has committed he either stubbornly denies, or, if he has been convicted, he excuses in many ways. For how will he be able to confess his fault humbly, when he does not acknowledge himself guilty? If you wish to chastise such a man for his offenses, you always make him worse. Therefore whoever, when corrected for any matter, has turned aside into words of malice, because he opposes excuses for sins to the one reproving him, falls into this descent of death, which is opposed to the eighth degree of humility. The presumptuous man who is found to have fallen into this degree of pride tries to excuse his sin in four ways. For he says either, “I did not do it,” or, “I did it indeed, but I did well,” or, “If badly, not very badly,” or, “If very badly, not with a bad intention.” And if he has been convicted of malice, he immediately pleads another’s advice in excuse of himself. But he who does not fear brazenly to deny or defend even open and manifest things will never reveal hidden things to his abbot according to the rule. When he comes to confession, he will cloak the truth with a feigned mind, so that he himself may escape shame; otherwise he strives to bring the negligences of others into the open. Inwardly disordered, proud, and presumptuous, he conceals his own faults in confession and confesses others’. Always restless within himself, he examines the deeds of others, condemns all with one sentence, complains that no one lives rightly in the monastery, and thinks there is no charity among the brothers.
