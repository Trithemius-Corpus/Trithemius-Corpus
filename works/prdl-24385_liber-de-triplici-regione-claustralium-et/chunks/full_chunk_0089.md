Others, so that they may be able to run about here and there, as though by occasion, without the bridle of discipline, have taken pains to arrange various labors. Others, so that they may freely indulge in vain conversations and frivolities, procure labors useless to the monastery. Others, so that they may be able, as if by opportunity and without suspicion, to see and speak with people, secular men known to them and friends after the flesh, not after God, love external labors. Others, because through instability of mind they are unable to be at rest in the monastery, always seem to pant after labors. But the intention of all who labor in this way is not sincere, and therefore they do not attain the best reward of labor.

But you, brother, when you are called to labor, take care to submit your intention to the rule’s discipline, so that you love nothing except the honor of God, the merit of obedience, and the usefulness of the monastery; intend nothing except the advancement of the soul. And when you are at work, do not imprudently extinguish the fervor of the spirit; do not omit the exercise of spiritual conversation, but take care diligently to observe everywhere the four things that follow. In outward conduct be faithful, serviceable, and kindly; inwardly, humble and devout; in speech, sparing and edifying; in manners, composed and mature. If you observe these things in labor, you will always return better to your cell.

*Outwardly the monk should be faithful, kindly, and serviceable.*

The first thing to be diligently observed in the labor of a cenobite is that in laboring he should always show himself faithful to his monastery; in serving and ministering, and even in carrying heavier burdens, he should anticipate the brothers out of charity; and to all, in words and deeds, he should show himself kindly and tractable. Saint Bernard, the lover of God, says: A serious and prudent soul prepares itself for every labor; nor is it dissolved in it, but through it rather gathers itself into itself, since it always has before its eyes not so much what it is doing as what it intends by doing it. For the more it aims at the end of consummation, the more truly it strives toward it, so much the more fervently and faithfully it also works with its hands, subjecting to itself the service of its whole body; in this the will creates use, use creates exercise, and exercise supplies strength.

A devout and faithful cenobite, who loves God in truth, does not spare himself in labor, but with great fervor, cheerfulness, and fidelity is accustomed to apply himself to the works of the hands imposed on him, just as if by his labor alone he were compelled to feed all those established in the monastery. For the most prudent soul has learned to expend itself wholly for God, whatever bodily necessity may have made it labor in his service. For just as spiritual things cannot long subsist in religion without the cooperation of temporal things, so I do not hesitate to call him a servant of God who for God’s sake ministers the bodily necessities to those serving God. And therefore whatever a good cenobite, by his labors and with right intention, can acquire for the monastery, let him know that he offers all of it to the Lord. Nevertheless, the monk should always understand that he must labor in such a way that he by no means omits the monastic prayers, masses, and the other services of holy mother Church.

Manual work, however, undertaken out of curiosity and personal complacency, is deprived of the merit of obedience before the eternal Judge; and, as idle and undertaken without the superior’s permission, it is held subject to the strictest accounting. But the good cenobite, who desires to please God alone, in every stealthy intrusion of an idle word, deed, or thought, should say this saying of Bernard within himself: If you were now about to die, would you do this thing which you now do, think, or say? Since you would not do now, if you were about to die, what you are now doing, you are most foolish, for, being about to die in a short time, you so boldly permit yourself acts worthy of death. But if, since it is necessary, you desire to die well, never do at any time what you would not do if death were already present. For whatever you do now, after death you will find it, whether good or bad.
