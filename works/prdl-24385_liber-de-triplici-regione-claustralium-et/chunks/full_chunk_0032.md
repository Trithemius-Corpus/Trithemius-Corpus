On the rightly ordered formation of the true monastic spirit and the degrees of humility. Article V.

The monk’s first virtue is humility, and pride is the gravest crime in a cenobite. For a monk who is proud with a swollen mind becomes an imitator and servant of the devil, and is stripped of all virtues, if he had any, because no virtue can remain in a mind in which pride happens to dwell. Therefore the cenobite who desires to make progress in this middle region should learn to order himself through humility toward the true progress of monastic formation, because no one truly attains humility except one who devotes himself to spiritual exercises.

True progress in regular formation, excluding all adulterous Muses, arises from the degrees of humility, which are set down through their roots as twelve in the Rule. The roots of this progress come from the practice of sacred reading, which gradually leads a man to the fear of the Lord. From reading arises spiritual meditation; from it holy compunction is generated. The more useful mode of cenobitic meditation consists in the memory of the Lord’s passion and in the consideration of the benefits of God. But those who pass beyond this middle region and come to tranquility of spirit make their meditations more sublime through contemplation of the divine majesty and excellence. We shall speak of these in their proper place.

These two roots receive increase from the watering of continual prayer and grow strong into the firmness of a most sturdy plant. For no meditation makes progress without prayer, and study without it will never save.

From these three roots of spiritual progress arises the fear of the Lord, which is the first degree of humility; from it the other degrees are begotten, succeeding in order. But monks who neglect the saving study of the Scriptures and holy meditations with continual prayers by no means arrive at this first degree of humility. And he who cannot climb the first degree of humility, how will he be able to come to the others? For the second is never climbed if the first is neglected. Therefore he who neglects one climbs none.

The study of sacred reading must not be undertaken for the vanity of the world, nor for the pleasure of a seduced mind, because reading never advances toward compunction when the delight of the flesh or of vanity has corrupted it. A monk, through the study of sacred reading, cannot reach devotion unless he strives to change all curiosity into a simple spirit. Therefore whoever desires to advance from the reading of Scripture to compunction, whoever wishes to ascend to humility through the roots of fear, must not choose just any material for reading, but only that which can more greatly stir the mind to the love of God.

For this purpose the reading of the lives of the saints seems to me sufficiently suitable, because the mind of one lying low is often raised to the love of God when the life of his servants is recalled to memory. We gladly admit their miracles and revelations concerning the torments or joys of separated souls, when they conform to the Catholic faith, because we have learned by experience that sluggish hearts are often stirred by them to compunction. Finally, the passions of the holy martyrs arouse the fervor of devotion in no small measure; in them, when we bring to mind the unconquerable charity of Christ, pierced by the example of such constancy and touched as if by a certain fire of love, we sigh over our imperfection.

From reading the material of meditation is taken; and as the former has been, so the latter will be. The shameful songs of poets, the curious doctrines of philosophers, and the swollen writings of rhetoricians do not arouse the fervor of devout compunction in monks, but rather suffocate what has been conceived. Therefore, if you wish to devote yourself to good meditation, do not occupy your mind with readings other than holy and devout ones. Indeed, grapes are not gathered from thorns, nor is compunction generated from the pursuit of vanity. Let him who studies for the knowledge of this world read the things that suit his learning; but let him who seeks compunction [unclear].
