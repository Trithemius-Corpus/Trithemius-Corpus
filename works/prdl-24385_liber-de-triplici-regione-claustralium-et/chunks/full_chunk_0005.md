Letter of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the little work concerning the threefold region of cloistered persons.

To the reverend fathers in Christ, the lord presidents, definitors, and the other abbots of the annual chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict, of the union and observance of Bursfelde: Brother Johannes Trithemius, called abbot in Sponheim, of the same order and congregation, in the diocese of Mainz, offers reverence and charity.

I have obeyed your commands, most venerable fathers, and, as you instructed me, I undertook to correct the little work on the threefold region of cloistered persons, together with the spiritual exercise of monks, recently published by the venerable father lord Johannes, abbot of Bursfelde. Indeed, unless your authority had compelled me, I would never have dared to set my hand to the distinguished work of so great a father, who, it is certain, wrote most excellently, being far more learned and holier than I. For what was I to correct, where everything is found most approved? The good and devout man wrote in no other way than he wished and than was fitting.

Nevertheless, in order to satisfy both your command and the prayers of the author himself, I rendered the whole little work in briefer, though not fewer, statements, and, adapting it to accents, I brought it as far as I could into my own manner and style of writing. I kept the order and matter of the former volume, but I entirely changed the form and customary mode of speaking. I added many things that seemed useful to me in almost every place; I removed a few; I altered the words in individual passages, not the sense. That I dared to do this was due both to your command and to the urgent request of the aforementioned father. For you wished me to make a new work out of old material, to add whatever seemed suitable to me, to cut away what was superfluous, and to alter what needed changing. I did what I could; I obeyed your commands; according to the strength of my talent, which is slight, I completed the task enjoined on me by rewriting it.

Receive now, therefore, most beloved fathers, a work not mine but yours, not composed by me but rather rewritten. If it pleases you, praise the talent of the first father; but if, because of its worthlessness, it displeases you, correct my imperfection, since I, who had from another man’s expenditure the best material for a spiritual building, have through my own lack of skill constructed an inept structure. If I have become foolish, you compelled me. Therefore, whether the work pleases or displeases, it has been easier for me, by writing foolishly, to accept your command obediently than, contrary to obedience, to keep silence. Yet I trust not in my own powers but in another’s merits, for a work which charity has produced will displease no one who loves God.

I desire your paternities always to remain happy in Christ. From Sponheim, the eighth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1498.

Here begins the book on the threefold region of cloistered persons and on the spiritual exercise of novices.

When I call to mind the whole condition of monastic life in these days, I feel intense grief for its calamity, because the fervor of spiritual exercise, which once produced most abundant fruits in the order of our holy father Benedict, has now for the most part failed among monks. We see cloistered persons returning in their morals to the world; we see the institution of regular observance despised by many; and religion, which from the beginning had been instituted for contempt of the world, is by wicked monks being called back again to the pomps of the world. The crown has fallen from our head; the integrity of regular discipline has been corrupted; and the former fervor of holy conduct is perishing. We perceive that many monasteries of our order lie buried in the worst deformity, while we see very few reformed to the purity of the Rule.

The cause of this destruction of regular institution is negligence in spiritual exercises; for as soon as the spirit of fervor has lost its savor in the minds of cloistered persons, the flesh, previously dead through self-denial, has risen again, and the world, which before appeared dry and contemptible to devout hearts, has begun to bloom again in carnal minds. For truly the thought of the human heart, prone to evil from youth, unless it is daily stirred to fervor of spirit by the bridle of spiritual exercise, falls back again in its usual way into its former negligences and grows lukewarm from its holy purpose.

For just as a fallow field, deprived of the diligent care of cultivation, with thorns springing up, quickly returns to its former desolation, so the human mind, however pure or holy it may be, is quickly reduced to thickets of vices unless it is renewed to fervor of spirit by the daily exercise of devotion. Led by this consideration, the fathers of our observance of Bursfelde, devout cultivators, entrusted to us and to posterity, by word and example, that since through our own frailty we cannot remain constant in fervor of spirit, we should at least, by evening recollection after Compline, accuse our daily negligences.
