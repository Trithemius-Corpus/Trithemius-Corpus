O adorable Trinity, O venerable Unity, through you we have been created, true eternity; through you we have been redeemed, you highest charity. Protect, save, free, rescue, and cleanse all your people. We adore you, almighty one; to you we sing; to you be praise and glory for endless ages of ages. Amen.

Then, turning to the most blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of God, let him say:

Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with you. Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb, Jesus Christ. Amen.

Show yourself to be a mother; may he who, born for us, consented to be yours receive prayer through you. Mary, mother of grace, mother of mercy, protect us from the enemy, and receive us at the hour of death. Glory be to you, Lord, who were born of the Virgin, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, for endless ages. Amen.

At the first signal, the monk should go to the secret place of his inner cell; cleansing his conscience, he should examine it diligently, and in the three things that follow he should compose himself in preparation for prayer.

First, he should strive to draw his mind back from outward things, vain and perishable and contrary to the spirit, to inward things, and to think absolutely nothing about worldly or temporal affairs. Just as Abraham is read to have stood firm and driven the birds away from the sacrifice, so it seems necessary for us to act against worldly thoughts that fly importunately around us during divine things. The monk can more easily bring his mind to this forgetfulness of passing things if he often wishes to recall to memory the sixth root, set above in the second region, concerning the vanity of present things.

Second, with humility he should direct the eyes of his mind to knowledge of himself, knowing that by his own strength he cannot persist in holy fervor of spirit unless divine mercy deigns to help him. Finally, let the humble monk understand that he is not worthy to praise his Creator, and therefore let him ask help from the Lord and worthy merit for praise. But if he desires to know more about knowledge of himself, let the devout monk read the fourth root.

Third, for one who wishes devoutly to stand before the Lord God in prayers, it is necessary to lift the mind with fervor of spirit to heavenly things. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: God is spirit, and those who adore him must adore in spirit and truth. The most devout suppliant of God, Bernard, also says: The little novice must be taught in his prayer to lift his heart upward, to pray spiritually, to withdraw from images of bodily things, and to think nothing bodily.

But because it is not given to all to be able to raise the mind to God, invisible and beyond understanding, provision must also be made for the weak in an easier manner of raising themselves to God. Hence he also says: For a weak person meditating or praying, an image of the Lord’s nativity, humanity, passion, or resurrection is more safely set before him; in it the mind may be stirred to tears of compunction, so that the weak soul, because it has known only bodily things, may have means by which, according to the measure of its capacity, it may be moved toward divine things. For while through faith in the form of the mediator it does not divide God from man, it attains to God himself in man. For our Savior, the mediator of God and men, Jesus Christ, is God and man.

Therefore let the devout monk often turn over the first root of divine knowledge, so that he may be able to lift himself higher through love. For the more perfectly he knows God, the more ardently he loves; and the more he loves the Lord, the better he praises him. For praise is the first work of lovers.

But so that you may always be able to raise your heart freely to the Lord God in prayer, learn to love nothing in this world in a private way; for one who is affected by no thing at all wanders less often. For nothing so unloads the heart and makes it free, nothing makes it converse before the Lord so generously and without constraint, and nothing so quickly and lightly gathers a person back to inward things after various outward disturbances, as to be attached to nothing in all things and circumstances by any private affection, but to live humbly in all things resigned to God and to one’s superiors. In addition, whatever has been enjoined on one so disposed for the glory of God and the preservation of regular discipline, the monk undertakes all this with humble obedience, whether it is advantageous to him or troublesome.
