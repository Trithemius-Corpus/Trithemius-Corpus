Secondly, in the cell you must read profitably.

It especially befits a monk, while he is in the cell, always to read something good and to occupy himself profitably in all things. For sacred reading overcomes the temptations of the devil, calms the fires of the flesh, makes one despise the world, and raises the mind to heavenly things. For Jerome, lover of divine Scripture, truly said: “Love knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh.” Not all things are to be read by all monks; one who is still weak in spirit must be instructed only in simple teachings. And Hugh says to the monk who loves simplicity: Do not read in order to be considered a teacher, nor read curiosities that do not edify, for vain reading generates vain thoughts and extinguishes devotion of mind. Therefore simple things, not lofty things, devout things, not curious things, are to be read by the simpler monks, so that those who, because of slowness of understanding, are not learned in higher matters may not become dissipated in vain ones. But we do not forbid suitable studies to those who are stronger in judgment, for all things are clean to the clean, and nothing that is read harms the truly devout. Nevertheless let no one claim for himself a strength of spirit that he does not have, but let him place the matter in the discretion of the abbot, who may judge whether he is fit for freedom in reading.

Thirdly, in the cell you must attend at proper times to meditations.

As a flower springs from the root, so meditation is generated from reading. If the reading has been vain, the meditation too will be fruitless and useless. For the fruit of reading is meditation, and the offspring of meditation is prayer. Therefore you must attend at proper times to holy meditations, so that you may also learn sometimes to raise your mind in contemplation through the fervor of divine love. For meditation itself lifts up the mind and commits the study of reading to memory.

Fourthly, in the cell you must attend to frequent prayer.

The holy Apostle teaches us to devote ourselves to prayer without ceasing, for just as breath is always necessary to us for the life of the flesh, so continuous prayer is altogether necessary for the health of the mind. For as we are instructed for good by readings, so by prayers we are freed from evil. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Pray, that you may not enter into temptation. For prayer refreshes the wearied mind, extinguishes the fire of carnal desire, forms one’s character, and drives away demons. If you wish always to be with God, always pray. But if your prayer is pure, simple, and bare, poured out to God through fervor of love, it cannot fail to be fruitful.

Fifthly, in the cell you must rest fittingly.

He rests badly who commits many evils. He rests well who despises all the occupations of the world. Be quiet in the cell, so that you may not wander here and there with any thought, but remain pure and tranquil with your whole mind, so that you may deserve to obtain savory knowledge. For the saint says in his poem: Wisdom instructs a tranquil mind, free from cares, and will be the guest of a peaceful breast. And again he says: Those who are at rest can search the law, if hearts free from noise are empty and unencumbered. Therefore, so that the mind intent on holy studies may be helped, let divine love conquer carnal quarrels. Remain quiet, then, in the cell, and you will be able to know the truth by loving it. For the mind will not be able to be tranquil and capable of divine wisdom when the body is restless. Hence Hugh says: In the dormitory be quiet, lest another be disturbed because of you. But when you wake, or when you desire to sleep, let the memory of God’s goodness always come to you with thanksgiving, because he himself always watches over us, so that he may guard from every evil us who humbly serve him.
