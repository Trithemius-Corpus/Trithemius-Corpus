Second, mortification of the will requires the emptying out of one’s own affections, because no one can stand ordered amid adversities and comforts, and truly subject to God, who does not firmly restrain his carnal affections.

Third, for the mortification of one’s own will that poverty is required as hope, lest the monk’s affection be held captive by visible things, by food, clothing, and the rest; for whoever loves these things too much shows himself first to be a lover of pleasure against the rule. For the true cenobite of this [unclear], whose task is to devote himself fervently to the teaching of holy humility, must gladly despise all worldly things for the love of God, and love absolutely nothing except for God. If he has anything for use, with the rule allowing it, let him beware of setting the affection of his heart upon it; let him be ready, at the judgment of his shepherd and for the usefulness of the brothers, to surrender everything with a peaceful heart. Let him always choose the things that are simple and lowly, give thanks to God in all things, and, placing his hope and sufficiency in him alone, say cheerfully: “In you, Lord Jesus, I have all things; for love of you I gladly despise all things; I shall care nothing at all for passing things.”

Therefore let our devout cenobite always direct the faithful eye of his consideration toward this end of his monastic institution and intention; let him never withdraw from the sight of it, because whoever forgets the final end in his action wanders like an unskilled and blind sailor amid the storms of the sea, soon to perish. Let the faithful monk, then, imitate the sailor experienced in devotion, so that just as the sailor, always having the harbor before his eyes, strives with intent solicitude to steer his ship there, so the mind of a good man may always be directed to God, as to its shore and final end.

Archers set up for themselves some fixed mark, and lest their sight be scattered away from the target, they close one eye. And just as the arrow of the shooter is aimed at the mark, so the intention of the good monk is always ordered toward God; in that intention the eye of lower affection must also be closed, lest the sharpness of higher contemplation be scattered through empty things. For we must close ourselves, as though in unknowing, to the affection for earthly curiosities and to the understanding of carnal wisdom, if we desire always to be lifted up by the gaze of the mind toward our proper end.

And just as the eagle is said to gaze upon the sun with eyes that are not dazzled, so the monk, the servant of God, contemplates without interruption the end of the monastic institution. As the prudent wayfarer, while proceeding with delight, considers the dwelling toward which he is going, so the good cenobite, in everything he does, always pants with fervent desire chiefly toward his own end, which is God. But foolish is the traveler who, though placed on the road, forgets the dwelling toward which he is going and, wandering, knowingly entangles himself in the various hindrances of others. And the monk who does not diligently consider the end of his destination is certainly blind and wanders off on the road.

Therefore the good monk and lover of his own salvation does not neglect his good effort in spiritual exercises, but, always striving in affection toward higher things, raises himself up by exercising himself in holy works. Good effort is this: to omit nothing of what God has commanded; to obey the shepherd humbly in all things for the love of God; to lift up the affection, always drawn away from earthly things, to the memory of heavenly things; and through the continual exercise of spiritual conduct to renew his holy resolve daily. Whatever good the devout cenobite does, let him always offer it with faithful attention to almighty God; let him despise all earthly things with a strong spirit; let him long to please God alone.

Let the faithful intention of the cenobitic life always be that he desire and long for nothing other than the praise and honor of God; let his attention be so diligent that he knows how to summon all the senses of the body and the affections of the soul to the service of God, so that he may faithfully serve his God and seek his glory, not his own.
