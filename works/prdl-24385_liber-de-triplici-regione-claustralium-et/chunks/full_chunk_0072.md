Do you expect angels? And let all indignation be far from you; let murmuring be absent; let pride withdraw far away. For you have no cause for impatience. Either you are accused justly, or unjustly. If justly, why would you not endure it? It is better to be cleansed here in small matters than after this life in great ones. And whoever sins publicly is to be corrected before all. But if you are accused unjustly, remember that you are a monk, and you will find that you are bound to endure injuries. For when you are accused justly, know that you are to be cleansed by humble patience; when unjustly, you show yourself a true monk and merit more. Therefore, when you are accused, recall to mind the fourth and sixth degrees of humility, and if you are a true monk, pray even for those who accuse you. For a true lover of God loves all the more the one who accuses him, and on that same day always offers him either some prayer or some special service. If you are good and wise, accusation will not be able to displease you; but if you are evil and foolish, you are worthy to be accused at all times. For Scripture says of the wise man: “Rebuke a wise man, and he will love you.” But of the evil and foolish the prophet says: “They hated the one who rebuked in the gate, and they abhorred the one who spoke perfectly.”

The second chapter on action is that in which one accuses another.

We call the second chapter on action that in which we no longer accuse ourselves, but others. For the action of this accusation charity is supremely necessary; compassion too must be applied no less, and discretion. Therefore, when, by reason of charity, you wish to accuse your brother, you should always apply the fear of God; and, mindful of your fellowship in eternal life, at the beginning you should say: “Our beloved brother has committed this or that; I ask that, for the Lord’s sake, he may be willing to amend himself.” Whatever has to be said, let him put it forward in few words and with compassion. Let him state the deed simply; let him not increase the fault of the one accused by making it heavier; let him silently entrust to the president the judgment whether what he did was good or evil. For the man who, alone among many, makes his brother’s fault heavier by passing sentence on it very often brings back the suspicion of bitter zeal and displeasure. Therefore, whoever attempts to accuse another should beware lest he be moved against his brother by the zeal of bitterness; for when the charity of the accuser does not intervene, resentment does not amend the accused. The accusation of one who accuses never bears fruit in the hearing of the offender when love of God and brotherly charity do not commend it. Indeed, since almighty God is supremely good, he will not give the effect of compunction to your accusation in your brother’s heart when he finds that you have been moved to accuse not by charity but by duplicity. For where he has found the root of his charity, he will not deny good fruit. But if you perceive that you have been moved against your brother by some indignation, it is better to put off the accusation for a time than to wound yourself so cruelly with another’s wound.

The third chapter is that of private humiliation.

They call it the third chapter of private humiliation whenever a brother is rebuked by a senior for his own negligences. For our most holy father Benedict commands in the seventy-first chapter of the Rule that a monk, in whatever place or time he has been accused, as soon as he has sensed that the mind of his senior is even slightly moved against him, should prostrate himself at that senior’s feet and lie there humbly until his command. Here he is in no way permitted to excuse himself or to lay the fault on another; rather, the monk is ordered to be silent and humbly acknowledge himself. For whoever is a true monk neither murmurs when justly corrected nor excuses himself when unjustly accused. Likewise in the divine offices and in the other conventual places, as soon as a devout cenobite has offended in anything through negligence, he humbles himself and does according to the custom there, choosing rather to undergo a greater penance in doubtful matters than to harm his conscience with a lesser one, or to cause scandal to others. But whoever still understands this humiliation, to be undergone at every time and place, as burdensome and difficult, does not walk in the spirit but still lives carnally; and unless he subdues this Jebusite and makes him tributary to himself, he will never be a true and devout cenobite.
