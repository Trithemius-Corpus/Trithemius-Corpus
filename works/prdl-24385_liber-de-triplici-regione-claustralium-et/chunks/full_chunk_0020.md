But whoever, being disordered in himself, has once fallen into the first degree of pride, unless he strives to rise again as quickly as possible through penance, soon casts himself down into the second and then, in order, into all the rest. In these degrees of elation, then, the sum of the disorder of carnal monks in themselves is expressed; through them the disordered citizens of the lowest region are shown. And what we have said about disordered and proud monks must also be understood of the prelates of the lowest region.

How the citizens of the lowest region, the disordered carnal monks, are disposed toward the monastery’s goods.

Article Six

Whoever, being placed in a monastery, neglects to order his inward affections toward the purity of a holy purpose, often also exceeds grievously in outward things. Monks disordered toward God are, consequently, declared disordered toward their duties. For just as a good cenobite, united by love to the divine will, considers all things with a simple eye according to the right order of reason, so one who is turned away from God through disordered affections sins even in the good things that he uses badly. Therefore the carnal monks who inhabit the lowest region are badly disordered not only with respect to their souls, but also with respect to the monastery’s temporal property. For an outward disturbance of the body follows the inward confusion of the mind, and the one is shown by the other.

First, then, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered toward the monastery’s goods when they demand that these be granted to them in an improper order, or in any way usurp more than the usage of regular observance permits. For the Rule, which commands that all necessary things be given to monks, utterly forbids superfluous things.

Second, those use the monastery’s goods badly who, against the prohibition of the master, do not fear to give and receive, or who faithlessly commit deceit and fraud in what has been entrusted to them. For a monk who, at his own pleasure, without reasonable cause, against the Rule and without permission, in any way squanders the monastery’s goods, sins under the Rule like an unfaithful dependent and a falsifier.

Third, monks or abbots show themselves disordered toward the monastery’s goods when they devote them to unlawful uses. Theft and sacrilege are committed together when the monastery’s goods are turned to illicit uses. For the monastery’s property, dedicated to God, is commanded to be guarded like the vessels of the holy altar, lest it be squandered in any way through negligence. But one who forbids cloistered persons to spend goods uselessly must by no means allow them to be granted and paid out for sinful purposes. What will the monks and rulers of the lowest region answer to the lawgiver in the strict examination, when they have not feared to squander the monastery’s goods on parents, relatives, mimes, actors, and shameful people, as an occasion and reward of sin?

Fourth, abbots and carnal monks sin gravely in regard to the monastery’s goods when they heap them up too greedily, to the detriment of divine charity and their own salvation. For, inflamed by the torches of avarice, they think of nothing but profits and money; they violate and do not observe all feast days; they always desire to increase temporal goods; they do not notice the losses of charity. They grieve more over a beast dying than over a sinner perishing. Indeed all disordered cloistered persons are so blind that they consider avarice to be prudence and think riches to be reform. They join field to field, call gain piety, and where they see temporal goods abound, there they say the best discipline is observed. They refuse to spend even a little for the common good of the order, because they have lost the inward order of true charity. They raise useless, curious, and damnable buildings on high; they buy costly garments and ornaments less necessary for divine services; they clothe sacred things with gold and leave Christ’s poor naked. This greedy curiosity does not befit monks, since we are commanded to imitate the saints of old and are ordered to sell [unclear] for the support of the poor. But a covetous monk and an avaricious abbot are as far from the purpose of holy religion as the devil is from heaven.
