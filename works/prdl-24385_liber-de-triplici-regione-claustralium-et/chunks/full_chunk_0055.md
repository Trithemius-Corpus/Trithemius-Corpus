Preface to the third treatise of the first part, which describes the supreme region of the spirit of cloistered persons.

At last, with the Lord’s help, as I am about to write of that supreme region of the spirit, I am shaken by fear and shame together: for, small as I am, I fear to speak of the greatest things, and I greatly blush to teach what I have not sufficiently experienced. For what mind of any man, however much exercised, could recognize the state of a mind resting sweetly in God? Or what [unclear], even of the most eloquent orator, could explain the sweetness of inward contemplation? Indeed, the exercise of this third region surpasses human understanding for the greater part. In it the mind of a purified man, through the fervor of divine love, is carried above itself and, while its natural essence remains, is lulled and absorbed with wonderful sweetness in the abyss of divine contemplation. It is oppressed by so great a sweetness of inward majesty that it seems able neither to understand nor to express its own state. Once the operation of sense has plainly been left behind, it wholly surpasses even the intellect; and, as though leaving itself still further, without imagination, reasoning, or understanding, it is raised by affection above itself to that supreme summit of divine darkness.

But when it has utterly failed away from itself, it does not act but rests; nor, when returned to itself, can it understand what it has undergone, or reveal to anyone the manner of its elevation. For this elevation of the mind into God is so pure and so sublime that not only can one who has not experienced it not understand it, but even one who has experienced it cannot fully explain in words what it is like. But what shall I be able to say about the mountain of divine darkness, which I have not yet deserved to penetrate? For the well of inward contemplation is deep, and I am weak: I have brought neither a bucket fit for these waters, nor have I prepared steps suitable for climbing to the heights. Polished words do not befit a fool; nor ought an inexperienced and cold man to speak of the secrets of divine contemplation, when he still stands with the weak in the valleys. For only he speaks fruitfully of profound matters who is illumined by the ray of the twofold spirit.

Yet since I am a man placed under obedience to the annual chapter, I am compelled to accept the command of that synod and, according to my strength, to complete the work set before me. Therefore I enter the third region of cloistered persons under the guidance of the venerable father, lord Johannes of Bursfelde, a devout abbot, so that what he described in learned and truthful speech, I may seem to reduce, as far as I can, to my own manner of speaking, in obedience to the chapter.

Here begins the third region of the soul, which is called spiritual, in which the highest tranquility of the mind is found.

The third, or supreme, region of the soul is called spiritual; in it the highest rest and tranquility of the mind abide. And this region is compared to the empyrean heaven, in which there is the true rest of all and the true happiness of souls. The spirit dwelling in this region leaves behind what is beneath reason and, raised by love into God, ascends wondrously. For whatever it does in this most holy region is judged wholly divine. Here the human spirit, rapt above itself, receives the divine inpouring and, lulled in the darkness of divine contemplation, withdrawn from all creatures, sweetly rests. In this region almighty God directs the spirit of the one contemplating him; he perfects the simplified intellectual powers of the soul, recalled from every phantasm into the inner chamber, according to the objects of their own tendencies; and he leads the mind, stripped of every multiplicity, to himself, the one principle, making it one with himself, not by nature, but by gracious participation. The inhabitants of this supreme region are truly spiritual men, who, having despised all the things and affections of the world, love nothing in this world except God, desire nothing inordinately, but through inward affection absorbed by [unclear].
