Pressed by insults, the fourth patiently obeys.
The fifth reports the depths of his heart to the abbot.
The sixth, by his own judgment, is sure that he is [useless?] and unworthy.
Then the seventh believes himself to be lower than all.
The eighth does nothing that the rule of the cell does not permit.
The ninth does not speak until another asks him.
The tenth proscribes laughter and condemns guffawing.
The eleventh speaks a few words of reason.
The twelfth always looks at the ground with head bent.

*On the same, otherwise.*

[Fear] God, and do not do your own will; let another govern you.
Bear [hard things?]; conceal nothing in your heart, but also seek the lowest things.
Be refuse; do not wander alone; shut your tongue.
Let laughter be sparing, speech grave, gestures humble.

Note: *peripsema* ought to be read and written, not *peripsima*, as the ignorant read in the Epistle to the [Corinthians?]; and it means filings and the cleansing of all things.

*Thus the monk’s conduct toward secular persons is modest.*

Fourth, the monk’s conduct toward secular persons ought to be modest, so that neither his familiarity with them be excessive nor his withdrawal indiscreet. Hence Hugh says: “Toward all persons, as far as lies in you, beware of everything that can fail to be a good example for them,” just as it befits the faithful servant of God to promote in all things the honor of his Lord, and to guard against his dishonor. Do not be familiar with any woman, even a holy one. For if they are discreet, you will please them more by this; but if they are importunate, they are to be avoided all the more. Be [unclear]. Be obliging to the weak, humble in [services?]; be very truthful in words; let your words be as an oath, and edifying to others. Love poverty. Be chaste in all things, and everywhere reverent.

But in founding all virtues and good morals, set before yourself that most glorious and most perfect example of all brightness: the life and conduct of our Lord Jesus Christ, who was sent from heaven for this, that he might go before us in the way of virtues and teach us by his example to follow himself. Therefore, the more the monk takes care to conform himself to him by imitation in the way of virtues, so much the more like and nearer to him he will without doubt be in the glory of eternal felicity. Whatever you do, always look to him, and consider how he bore himself in all things according to the Gospel and according to your own estimation. For you know this very well: he bore himself most excellently in all things. Say therefore to yourself: If my Lord Jesus were present, what do you think he would do in this matter? Or what would he now say? Certainly he would both act and speak most excellently; I ought, according to my powers, to imitate him in all things.

*General conclusion of all spiritual exercises.*

Wishing at length to put some end to this work, we say that the completion of all spiritual exercises consists in two things. First, in knowledge and perception of one’s own imperfection, so that the monk may always be conscious of his weakness and remain rooted in humility. Second, in the desire and effort to come to a higher manner of life, so that he may always desire to advance in spirit to better things and may in no way withdraw from this effort. Every cenobite ought to stretch out his mind at every moment toward this effort of advancement; if he ever neglects it, he incurs transgression of his vow and very grave danger to his soul. But let the monk beware lest consideration of his own weakness lead him away from the effort of advancing; let him beware lest that effort itself despise consideration of imperfection. Rather, let these two cohere with each other mutually in such a way that neither ever withdraws from the other. But if excessive consideration of his own imperfection attempts to lead the mind into despair, let him recall to memory what the holy abbot Bernard said: “Let us breathe again, brothers, because although we are nothing in our own hearts, perhaps something can lie hidden in us in the heart of God.” These two considerations, of our weakness and of divine estimation, excite great devotion in us, as the psalmist said: “I am poor and sorrowful”; behold the first. “Your salvation, O God, has upheld me”; behold the second.

Furthermore, it is necessary for one who wishes to occupy himself usefully in these spiritual exercises to have a joyful and free heart, and one estranged from every anxiety or care of the world. For the Holy Spirit is the spirit of freedom and joy; he loves a heart free and joyful in God, and, so to speak, applauds what is like himself. For just as a bird bound on the ground cannot lift itself up to heaven by flying, so a mind occupied with perishable things cannot raise itself to heavenly things. Nor ought one to cast down the spirit by excessive repetition of these things; otherwise the brain too is confused, and one labors in vain by dry habit. Therefore let the cenobite learn, fervent in spirit, always to persist in these things with moderation, and continually to refer the cause of every exercise, through intention, to God, who is the beginning and end of all goodness. Let him faithfully direct his effort to fear and love of him, and fix his mind in deep humility, since he who is rooted in love is not easily abandoned by God.
