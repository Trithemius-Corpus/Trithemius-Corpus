But insofar as they defile the soul, let him despise himself as more worthless, even if he knows their public crimes. For since the roots of the heart are utterly unknown to him, he cannot know how the divine election concerning that person was ordained from eternity. Who would have believed that the thief crucified with God was elect from eternity, if he judged him according to his works? All things turn out for the good of the elect, since where he sees sin to have abounded, often by the will of divine election greater grace is found. For divine love regards and embraces its elect from eternity, and looks upon them as elect not according to the misery in which they now are, but according to their future glory. Therefore, cenobite, beware of slandering others; beware of judging others, if you desire to escape eternal death. For who are you, that you judge another’s servant? He stands to be judged by his God alone.

Now the disposition of this seventh degree, because of that clause “from the innermost affection of the heart,” has a threefold root of its origin: namely, truth, hope, and charity. For when the faithful cenobite sees that he does not have his being from himself, he truly thinks himself, insofar as he is from himself, to be nothing; and consequently, in the same respect, he considers his works, insofar as they are from himself, who is nothing, to be nothing, since he knows that nothing comes from nothing. Therefore the monk who, in this respect, regards himself and his works as nothing does not dare to depart from the truth, because he fears falling into the vice of falsehood if he believed himself to be something great. And therefore, withdrawing from himself, he fails in himself, and places his being, his life, and his power in God alone. Secondly, this disposition arises from hope of acquiring greater grace, because when a man through humility fails in himself, he comes nearer to the grace of God. Thirdly, the disposition of this humility takes its beginning from the love of God, and by continuing in it receives increase. For whoever perfectly loves God seeks nothing in all things except his honor, nor does he know how to exalt himself in anything, but for God’s sake desires to be subject to all. Therefore the more he is cast down by reproaches and injuries, the more joyfully he rejoices in the Lord. He rejoices that he is wretched, so that in himself he may proclaim the Lord glorious. Whoever truly loves his Lord seeks nothing else but his glory.

From what we have said it is clear that the perfect charity promised to those who ascend through the degrees of humility is displayed in this seventh degree. For the other five degrees that follow in the Rule add nothing to the perfection of the inner man who exists in the seventh degree, but rather compose outward things so that they may not dissipate the inner man. Finally, from the seventh degree we could set up a certain new ascent of humility, and in them lift all the other degrees higher. For filial fear resides more fervently in him who can make wider progress than others.

Now that the ascents of the heart up to the degree of charity have been known, let the cenobite consider with what zeal, what effort, and what perseverance he ought to exercise himself in these things, so that he may reach this seventh degree of love. But whoever does not always strive to advance in these degrees of humility should know that he sins against the Rule; and unless by repentance he recovers his holy striving, he incurs the sentence of damnation. For thus the Rule says: “But if at any time he has done otherwise, let him know that he is to be condemned by him whom he mocks.” If, then, anyone who makes less progress in religion is believed guilty before the divine judgment, when he abandons the effort to advance, what else does he deserve to receive but the sentence of damnation? For where the Rule gives an account even of lesser progress among those striving to advance, monks who despise every effort to advance are without doubt condemned by the sentence of the judge. Therefore, if you do not wish to incur the danger of damnation, take care always to keep the effort of advancing under the Rule.

Since we have said that the other five degrees of humility pertain to the guarding of the inner man, let us also explain them briefly. For the monk who neglects to compose his outward conduct according to the Rule will not stand well composed in inward things. For just as a body stripped of clothing is corrupted, so humility without conduct is emptied.
