Sixth, the generous munificence of God is praiseworthy in the glorification of the blessed, since he rewards his few elect, according to their merit, with so ineffable a recompense, and grants them so immortal a gift with the holy angels.

Seventh, the incomprehensible excellence of God is praiseworthy in himself: he orders all things according to his providence, and in himself remains most tranquil, immovable by everlasting motion or by time. The seventh contemplation of this consideration belongs to the Sabbath of eternal happiness, in which there is no longer the laborious activity of this life, but the blessedness of a happy, eternal dwelling and rest. For just as God, the creator of all things, is read to have completed all his work and rested on the seventh day, so not work, but eternal rest, will be laid upon the just in the heavenly Sabbath. Therefore, if we desire happily to come to those heavenly mansions, let us rest in God alone, in whom the rational creature is perfected by resting; and, once perfected through the attachment of love, it rejoices with the Lord in ineffable union and joy. Henceforth let pure devotion seek nothing outside God for praising God, since God himself, almighty, perfect, and most wise, is sufficient to all as the matter of his praise, and exceeds every praise by his infinite perfections. For Scripture says: “Praise the Lord as much as you can; he will still surpass it.” For he is greater than all praise; and therefore he is to be praised by us most humbly rather in himself and for his own sake than from creatures.

From the foregoing considerations there arises a manifold and varied affection of devotion toward God, through which a worthier effect of prayer is also generated according to the desire of the affection. For when the meditation of the heart is taken up concerning the majesty or power of God, reverent wonder is produced. But when we begin to meditate on his unspeakable wisdom, we accuse ourselves within ourselves of our foolish imperfection. So, in consequence, from the consideration of God’s mercy hope of pardon is taken up; from his patience love of heart is generated; from justice humility is born; from meditation on his munificence, gratitude. For just as a fire that has been kindled grows in size according to the quality of the wood, so, according to varied meditations on God, the ardor of devotion rises up in prayers among those who love God.

On the meditation of our reparation in the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ.

The second root of the fear and love of God is placed in meditation on our redemption, which is generated through diligent exercise in the life, conduct, teaching, and Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. Among all meditations, we believe none to be more useful to us than this, in which we know that our salvation and the sum of eternal happiness are contained. And note that the Passion of the Lord, as to its legal proceeding, was unjust; as to its manner, shameful; as to its pain, bitter; and as to its effect, fruitful; and therefore it must always be kept in memory.

First, as to its legal proceeding, it was unjust, because he who was born of a virgin neither contracted sin nor committed it, nor was deceit found in his mouth. For death is inflicted on us for sin; but he could not justly die, because he was always just and innocent, without any sin. Finally, he was killed contrary to human law, if a sentence of death is passed upon a man neither confessed nor convicted of a crime. For he was accused before Pilate the governor on three counts: that, calling himself a king, he forbade tribute to be paid to Caesar; third, that he made himself the Son of God. The Gospel speaks of the first two, and the accusation is shown to be false. For concerning the first, the contrary was plainly established, since Christ said that his kingdom was not earthly. The falsity of the second also did not escape Pilate, because he knew that Jesus had commanded that the things that were Caesar’s should be given to Caesar.
