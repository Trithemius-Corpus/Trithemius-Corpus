Thus indeed the Lord himself taught us to pray: “Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.” And that saying of the psalmist: “Into thy hands I commend my spirit.” Never is one safer in this world than when the human will is perfectly subjected to God.

Indeed, perfect surrender of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his will to God, shows without intermediary the right way to the highest good. Here there is the greatest tranquillity of mind; the greatest security is present in the heart, because the soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will no longer be able to fear any opposition of this world, except sin alone. Whatever adversity comes upon him, with the Lord’s help he has learned to overcome; whatever comfort he desires, he understands that he possesses it in the Lord alone. He despises all adversities for love of him; he has placed all good things in love of him. For that devout man wisely said: “Leave all things, and you will find all things; abandon desire, and you will find true peace.”

Finally, so that this surrender of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it seeks, to pass over the rest, the aid of a threefold support.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance: that the will, once renounced, never be resumed. For that holy Bernard says to those who despise this support: “Why do you again take charge of yourselves, who once, after mature deliberation, handed over your will to us?” For the monk who has once handed his will over to God in the hands of the abbot does not resume it without the crime of sacrilege.

The second thing required for perfect surrender of the will is that it never be relaxed. Hence the Lord says to the lukewarm soul in the Apocalypse: “I have a few things against you, because you have left your first charity,” that is, your fervor.

Third, surrender of the will will be perfected if it is never revoked. For the Savior says: “He who perseveres to the end will be saved.” Therefore, when you see a cenobite restless or stirred by anger, know that the whole mass of temptation proceeds from lack of resignation; nor will you ever cure this sickness unless you first persuade him to the surrender of his renounced will.

The third degree of humility is to submit oneself to a superior in all obedience for the love of God, and to show oneself an imitator of Christ by deeds, not only by words. Of him the holy Apostle says: “He was made obedient unto death, even death on a cross.” This degree takes its beginning from the second and raises its ascent to the third. For as the second mortifies one’s own will by renouncing it, so this one lays the foundation of holy work through humble subjection. The former subjects the whole self to the divine will; this one, for the love of God, obeys even men.

The keeping of one degree without observance of the other is not enough in a monk; nor is the will of a cenobite subjected to the divine ordinance if it disdains to obey the command of a superior. Therefore the true cenobite, placing all his joy in the virtue of holy obedience, thinks it the greatest gain to fulfill in all things the will of his pastor; he believes that he incurs grave damage to his soul if he finds that he has committed himself even a little outside obedience. With faithful devotion he always considers what the Lord says in the Gospel: “He who hears you hears me, and he who despises you despises me.” Hence that blessed Bernard says to monks: “Whatever a man commands in God’s place must be received no differently than if God himself commanded it in person.” Therefore let the true monk prefer this subjection of obedience to every sacrifice, let him do absolutely nothing against its integrity, and let him continually say within himself what is written: “Obedience is better than sacrifices.” Hence the Rule also says: “Therefore all things are to be done with the abbot’s will.”

But this degree, as we have said, arises from the second, because he who has first perfectly renounced his own will out of fear of God now seeks nothing else in this world than to follow that will in all things.
