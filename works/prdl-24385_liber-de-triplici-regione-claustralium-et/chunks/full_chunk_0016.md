The pleasures of the flesh confuse him and inwardly scatter him away from himself. Scripture says: “He who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good?” The right order of the human condition was established by making the flesh subject to the spirit; and the spirit itself is subject to him from whom it received the form of its existence.

But in carnal minds the order of reason is overturned, when the flesh rules over the spirit, and reason, which according to the power of understanding implanted in it ought to govern all things, is choked by the pleasures of the flesh and does not hasten, by its authority, toward upright judgment.

Carnal monks are disordered toward themselves through the vice of curiosity: they pry curiously into another’s life, words, and deeds, while they do not correct their own life, stained with every vice. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, yet they proudly rebuke others who offend in the smallest matters. They carefully consider the speck in a brother’s eye, and excuse the beam lodged in their own eyes as though it were nothing to them.

They subject their affections to sensuality, and, setting aside the judgment of right reason, live carnally like beasts. Passion often stirs them to anger; the fury of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With an anxious heart they are always demanding vengeance on their brothers, and often, blinded by excessive fury, they do not obtain it without [unclear].

But the disordered cannot remain hidden long in secret: the more they remain unknown to themselves through pride of mind, the more manifest they become to others through the blindness of their judgment. For every proud man is disordered and is said first to be ignorant of himself, because if he truly knew himself, he would in no way be proud.

Since, then, disorder of oneself takes its origin from the root of pride, we cannot describe this plague better than by attempting to disclose its roots as they descend by degrees toward hell. Just as the summit of spiritual life is comprised in twelve degrees of humility, so the source of all monastic disorder is contained in twelve degrees of pride.

The first degree of pride is called curiosity. By it monks who are badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and conduct, and continually inquire into the deeds or words of others. They accuse every negligence of a brother; they rebuke the life of the pastor; and the more curiously they desire to know outwardly what belongs to others, the farther they withdraw from themselves.

The vice of curiosity is most pernicious for monks: once it has invaded the mind, it strips it of all virtues and makes it a dwelling place of vice. It is a very sure sign of sickness to examine the deeds of others, and, neglecting the office of reason, to occupy oneself with others’ faults. For a monk, who according to the true definition of his name is commanded to attend to himself alone, does not investigate the deeds of others without manifest danger to his salvation. Whoever subjects himself to the vice of curiosity descends to the second degree of pride in conduct. For one thing always disposes a person toward another; no sin rules alone in proud monks.
