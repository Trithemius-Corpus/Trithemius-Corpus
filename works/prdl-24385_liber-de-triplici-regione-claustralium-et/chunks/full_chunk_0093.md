Since, therefore, you have subjected yourself to your prelate for God alone and for no other cause, you should strive at all times to show yourself such toward him that he may freely be able to command you to do or leave undone whatever seems expedient to him, and need not fear any insolence or disturbances of your mind at all. For if your abbot is compelled to fear you, his monk, the order of equity is violated; and where the disciple strives to be above the master, sin is committed against the Gospel. Therefore beware lest you withhold reverence and honor from prelates; beware lest you offend them by saddening them, but take care to meet them, whatever sort of men they may be, with reverence, and in all things to obey their commands according to the Gospel and the Rule. Believe me, brother, because the fault committed by a subject against a prelate is punished very severely, either in the present life or in the life to come. For it is written: Do not touch my anointed ones. Whatever your prelate has commanded you, you ought to receive with such confidence that you believe God inspired it in him.

*Let the monk’s manner of life toward the brethren be charitable.*

Second, the monk’s manner of life toward his confrères is commanded to have charity; through this alone peace and concord are maintained in the monastery. Let our faithful cenobite always think upon and turn over in his heart the common joy of souls in eternal happiness, and let him continually direct the eye of his mind toward the equal condition of souls, so that his thought may not withdraw from love of their future fellowship. If a brother has offended you in any way by word or deed, if he has been troublesome or contrary to you in anything, do not weigh the disordered movement of sensuality from this; do not consider his present weakness, but lift the eyes of your mind to the nobility of his soul, and consider what fellowship of eternal happiness it will have with you. To this end you ought to bring back to memory the chapter of the Rule on the good zeal of monks, so that you may more easily rouse yourself to brotherly charity. Before all things, beware lest you admit displeasure against your brother, for he who is often accustomed to be angry with his neighbor clearly proves that he does not love God. For whoever loves God with his whole heart does not admit displeasure against his faithful brother. Indeed, a monk ought to be established in such great elevation of mind that, whatever a brother has committed against him, by considering the nature of that soul he cannot be moved.

*Let the monk’s manner of life toward those who do injury be meek.*

Third, the monk’s manner of life toward those who do injury ought to be meek, for then at last it is done well if regard is always had for the common future happiness and fellowship. To attain this meekness of spirit, let the cenobite learn to bring more frequently to mind the lives of the holy fathers, and faithfully to consider with how much joy they received injuries. Let him strive first of all, as far as possible, to imitate our Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who not only endured the injuries and insults of the impious with a most meek heart, but also prayed to the Father for his persecutors, that they should not perish. Imitating this example of the Savior, the holy fathers were accustomed patiently and meekly to receive injuries for the love of God, and, what is more, to love those very injurers. To this end let the cenobite recall to mind the degrees of meekness, which are contained briefly in these verses: Be apt, strengthen, as a meek man bear contrary things. Preserve, restrain, rest wholly with yourself. Correct prudently, be merciful, give wisely. Pray by setting yourself down, by desiring martyrdom. For if humility has possessed the mind of the cenobite, it makes him love injuries. The degrees of humility are contained in these verses.

*The twelve degrees of humility.*

The first of these degrees fears the Almighty.
The second sets reins upon the enticements of the flesh.
The third submits itself reverently to the abbot.
