He too must be called obstinate who casts aside the fear of God, who trusts too much in his own judgment, and who in any business admits no counsels of the wise. And among shepherds of souls there are many who are disordered by this sickness of mind: they either spend the goods of monasteries or money improvidently, or preserve them with excessive stinginess. They prepare the most lavish banquets for their flatterers, but to others from whom they are estranged they deny even the smallest things. In the guesthouse or in their abbey, shepherds corrupted by disorderly conduct eat to excess and drink always the best wine; but to the poor monks, who by keeping watch and singing bear the burden and heat of the day, they scarcely grant even meager necessities.

Sixth, a prelate who is a monk is shown to be very disordered if, bold against God, he is lifted up in pride by making light of God’s judgments. For indeed, when a shepherd cannot be corrected by his subjects, he is sent on by the holy father Benedict to the judgment of God, as is found in many places in the Rule. “Let the abbot know,” he says, “that for all his judgments he must render an account to God.” And again: “Let the abbot who has been ordained always consider what a burden he has undertaken, and to whom he will have to render an account of his stewardship.” And again he says: “As soon as vices begin to arise, let him cut them off with charity, not with tyranny.” And in another place he warns, saying: “Let the abbot himself do all things with the fear of God and observance of the holy Rule, knowing beyond doubt that for all his judgments he will render an account to God, the most just judge.”

But the disordered ruler of monks, like a deaf asp stopping its ears, despises this warning of the most holy lawgiver; he scorns the patience of God’s long-suffering; he thinks nothing of the examination to come, until at last the judge, angered against him, says to his face: “These things you have done, and I was silent. Now therefore render an account of your stewardship. Give back to me the sheep of my inheritance, and restore to me without delay the souls once entrusted to you in the body. Return their number whole; for if even one of them has perished through your negligence, your soul shall never see my face for eternity.”

What then will you answer the supreme shepherd, invincible ruler, when not only the blood of those who have perished is demanded from you, but also an account of those who have made too little progress? Behold, now you walk in the worst security, despising God’s examination; you think that everything plainly permitted to you in the body is lawful for you, and you do not consider that one day you will give an account of all these things. But soon the Lord of all will come, before whom you do not fear to show yourself an unfaithful servant, whose honor you do not fear to claim for yourself. Unless in the meantime you do penance, unless you amend your life for the better, you will burn without end in hell with the demons.

Seventh, a shepherd happens to be disordered toward his subjects if, neglecting the common advantage, he seeks his own profit with a corrupt intention; if, presiding in a monastery, he serves not God but his own interests, while he rejoices that he is prelate in order to obtain the destructive liberties of the flesh, and fraudulently provides for himself, from the goods of the monastery, for the future. This disorder is especially seen in those who resign their office on some pretext, to whom, as the reward of their stewardship, as if they had been bishops, an annual pension is assigned from the goods of the monastery. This is not the manner of those who seek God; this is not the way of the old monks. When you resign the pastoral office for God’s sake, resign it simply. Do not demand anything for food and clothing, if you do not wish to sin with proprietors. You are a monk; you were a monk before you became abbot; after your office you will remain a monk. Why then do you demand a pension, since you know that you are a monk? Just as one commits the vice of simony by buying prelacies, so one sins against the Rule by demanding a pension for the resignation of an abbacy.

What then? Do we condemn those who, resigning the office of pastoral care without fault, have received annual pensions from the goods of the monastery? By no means. We do not forbid necessities; we deny superfluities. But he who is a true monk and lover of God is content, even after an abbacy, with the provision allowed by the Rule.
