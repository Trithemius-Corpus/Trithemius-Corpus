For, as we have said above, that highest good, God, is one; and our soul cannot draw near to it unless, stripped of all multiplicity, which it receives by attraction, imagination, and understanding, it strives to present itself wholly one to itself. For the soul naturally desires to reach the highest good; but the more it is divided in its operation through multiplicity, the less fit it is found to come to it. For multiplicity is opposed to unity, and the more the mind turns itself toward many things, the more it withdraws from unity. One, two, three, four are ten. You withdraw from the one when you are multiplied in number. Return to the one if you desire to have the beginning. For the one is the beginning of all things; if you add anything to it in your mind, you at once withdraw from it. When you say “one,” you say “beginning.” If you say “two,” you have already abandoned the beginning. Therefore, so that you may always be able to contemplate your beginning, take care at every time, stripped of all multiplicity, to make yourself one.

For since all good things are from the first good, and every love, toward whatever good it is borne, is naturally kindled by that same good, our love cannot be touched by any goods or rest in any final end unless, naked and pure, it strives at last to return to its own beginning. For union comes about only by the power of the one itself; and for no other reason do we more readily say that things more alike are united with one another than because we see that they are one in quality. Therefore the soul is believed to be able to be united with the one highest principle of things only when, stripped of all multiplicity and united even to itself, it is lifted up to God by most fervent love. Just as there is no union between things different in quality, so between God and a soul divided in itself there can be no adhesion. For the union of one thing with one thing comes about by likeness of quality; and the adhesion of a mind simplified and stripped of all things through the affection of love will be with God.

Therefore the supreme region of the spirit is founded on simplicity; in it the mind of the purified person is raised up to God by a most fervent desire of love and is united by grace to its immutable beginning. Intellect and affection are the two eyes of the soul. The first of these, the intellect, is indeed raised up to know God, but it is not sufficient to come to the mountain of divine darkness, because it proceeds burdened with images of sensible things. Therefore affection, the simpler eye of the soul, enters the dark mountain without any images whatever; separated from all creatures, it contemplates God in love, thinks nothing sensible, and, resting naked in mystical silence, is free. This is the simple eye that makes the whole body full of light, the eye of which the bridegroom boasts in the Canticle: “You have wounded my heart, my sister, my bride; you have wounded my heart in one of your eyes.” For the fervor of loving affection enters alone, without the guidance of the intellect, into the secret of divine contemplation; and therefore the bridegroom is wounded in one eye.

But whoever, by the exercise of devotion and purity, has deserved through Christ’s gift to arrive at this region of union and simplification should learn at every time to stand prudently in deep humility, and not through pride lose the gift he rejoices to possess. For Bernard, that profound and most cautious contemplator of divine things, says: “One who strives for higher things must be deepened in humility and think humbly of himself, lest, while he is vainly lifted up above himself, he fall from the height to which he seemed to have ascended.” Therefore, standing in this region, let him prudently return sometimes to the degrees of humility described in the second part; let him humbly learn to remain in profound resignation of himself, lest, if the soul [unclear] by denial of self, it fall from the highest mountain into a dreadful precipice.

For many, trusting in the jubilation of their sensual devotion, have been deceived, who climbed this highest mountain without the foundation of humility. Others, their heads injured and their senses destroyed, have become foolish, because, without having mortified their passions, they presumed to enter the darkness of mystical silence.
