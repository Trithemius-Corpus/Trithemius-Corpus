But he who does not have a firm effort to make progress toward the better certainly cannot truly be called composed, although to men he may seem to be mature. For feigned maturity quickly dissolves into open dissolution of mind, where it does not have the fear of God as the root of discipline. Therefore, so that our cenobite may always be composed within and without, let him strive to be truly rooted in the fear of the Lord. For he who fears God neglects nothing. And the love of God is not idle.

*What the conduct of a monk toward other people ought to be.*

Since, indeed, we have begun to speak of the maturity and composure that a monk must preserve before men, it seems useful to us now to say briefly how he ought to present himself to each person. For he is instructed to present himself in one way to his superior, and in another way he is commanded to show himself to his confreres and to secular persons. To the rector, as standing in the place of Christ, he ought to show reverence; to his confrere, charity; to those who trouble him, gentleness; to secular persons, modest piety. And so let the monk render to each one what is his, and he will be truly composed before God and men. What he owes to God, and what to himself, has been sufficiently said above; now let us pursue what has been set before us.

*Let his conduct be reverential toward the prelate, as standing in the place of Christ.*

First, the conduct of a monk ought to be reverential toward the prelate, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “He who hears you hears me, and he who spurns you spurns me.” For the monk who withholds due obedience and reverence from his abbot does injury to almighty God. For, as our most holy father says in the Rule, the abbot is proved to hold the place of Christ in the monastery. Whoever, therefore, despises the vicar of any king has beyond doubt despised the king himself. But tell me, good brother, why do you withhold such reverence from your prelate, whom you hear called the vicar of Christ? Perhaps you judge him wicked and utterly unworthy of such an office, and for that reason you think it lawful for you to despise him. Not so, brother, not so; but consider whose place this is, in which the vicar of Christ sits, and know that you must honor the one who sends, not the one sent. If you wish to be a disciple of Christ, always consider why you came, and you will never deny reverence even to an unworthy prelate. For why did you come? Certainly so that, according to the rule of the Gospel, you might deny yourself for God’s sake; that under another’s command you might serve as a soldier for Christ; that with patience you might await the eternal reward promised to you. Therefore, brother, do not, do not lose the crown of eternal happiness prepared for you in heaven because of the displeasure caused by one man, a crown which you ought not exchange for the whole world. Remember that you have nothing except from God, to whom you owe all that you are and all that you know, so that you may fulfill his will and live according to his ordinance. But since you cannot trust yourself to be able to understand the will of God, you ought humbly to obey your rector, as his sacred vicar to whom you have subjected yourself; nor ought you to scrutinize either his life or his command, since it is not lawful for you to judge your shepherd. Indeed, in your profession you swore that you must not carry out your own will, but that you wished to obey the abbot in all things according to the Rule. The abbot, therefore, is the lord of your will, not by his own presumption, but by the ordinance of God, whose servant and vicar he is. But the handling of another’s property against the owner’s will is theft; and you know that thieves will have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God. And so a monk, professed according to the Rule to God and to his abbot, henceforth does not have his own [unclear] of his will; if afterward he has usurped his own will for himself, he commits theft and sacrilege. Nor will permission obtained save the presumptuous man, if it has been obtained by prayers and insults. Your abbot, O brother, is the vicar of God, to whom you ought to render the obedience you promised, not as to a good man, but as to holy God.
