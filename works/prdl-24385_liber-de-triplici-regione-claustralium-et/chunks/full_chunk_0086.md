Would that I could give thanks worthily to you, the highest and unchangeable good. Would that in your holy service I could praise you with a pure heart and a clean conscience. But alas, through weakness, concupiscence, and evil habit prevailing over me, I, who ought to have clung to your most holy love with a pure heart and conscience, have offended you again and again by many negligences and faults; and as often as, according to the inward inspirations of your goodness, I have tried to rise to fervor of spirit, alas, so often I grieve that I have fallen again.

Good Jesus, into how many faults, how many negligences, how many guiltinesses have I fallen today alone; how many vain, how many useless, how many harmful thoughts have I admitted in so few hours; how many useless and harmful words have I spoken; how many negligences and offenses have I committed in my actions this day.

Woe to me, miserable man, who could not persevere even one very short day in your most holy service without impurity of heart and negligence; who today have committed so many faults and various imperfections. I confess to your goodness, O most gentle God, all my sins into which I have fallen from my earliest age. Before your feet, O sweetest Jesus, I lay all the negligences that I have committed today. I ask pardon, which I am not worthy to receive; I beg for grace, which I confess that I have so often received in vain. O most loving mediator between God and men, Jesus Christ, do not despise me, a miserable sinner, whom you willed to purchase with your own blood. I confess to you all the negligences into which I have in any way fallen today, and the faults that I have committed.

Here let the monk, prostrate in mind before the Lord, recall to memory the more important negligences, and, humbly asking pardon, lament his imperfection. But let him especially consider whether he has offended anyone by word or deed; whether he has said anything against a brother; whether he has committed anything against charity and reverence; or whether he has knowingly saddened anyone wrongly. And generally, in whatever he knows that he has sinned against God, against himself, against his prelate, against his neighbor, or against the rule or the statutes of the fathers, let him be eager to recall it for the examination of this chapter; and concerning secret matters in tomorrow’s confession, and concerning public and regular transgressions in the first chapter, let him faithfully and from his heart resolve to clear himself by accusing himself. Let him grieve over what has been committed; let him make satisfaction for what he has repented of; let him beware of what may be committed.

Then let him consider the grave danger of offending God, whether the sin be venial or mortal. For John Gerson, chancellor of Paris, says in his treatise On the Spiritual Life: Sin, even venial sin, is so much against God that, by his justice, he could condemn a man for it to eternal punishment. But that he does not impute it to man unto damnation proceeds not from justice but from his infinite goodness. And Saint Anselm warns us that a man ought not knowingly to commit the least sin against the will of God for the whole world. For the most blessed bishop Augustine says: No sin is so venial that it will not become mortal while it is pleasing. You will say: This opinion cannot be true, because if a sin is venial, it can never become mortal; but if it becomes mortal, it was not venial. But listen and understand. I grant that venial sin, in its own consideration, cannot become mortal; but one and the same sin, which can stand together with charity, becomes only venial in one person from mere negligence and weakness, while in another, from contempt and aversion from God in the same sin, it is without doubt mortal, if it is pleasing even insofar as it is sin and against God. Therefore blessed is he who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil. Whoever neglects the smallest sins that he could amend falls little by little into graver ones.

But how much sin displeases almighty God, the punishment that follows makes plain. For in sacred books and in the deeds of times past we read that sinners have so often been severely punished against the Lord that we rightly fear the tinder of sin. How much even the briefest thought of a spirit turned away displeases him is declared to us by the fall of the first angel. For as soon as he showed himself disordered by rising up against the Lord, he was cast out.
