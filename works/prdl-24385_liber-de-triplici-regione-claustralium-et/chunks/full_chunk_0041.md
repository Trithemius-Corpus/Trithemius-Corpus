With the flesh resisting, virtue is perfected; and that the will is right is proved by the vehemence of the spirit. Often, however, a will is called right and good even when it is found to be less strong. The good which it loves it cannot always accomplish; the evil which it hates it grieves that through weakness it has committed. By the weakness of the flesh it is very near the region of infirmity, yet by the uprightness of its intention and by the effort to advance it is continually kindled toward the liberty of the spirit. For the body which is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the sense that thinks on many things. O who could describe this battle between spirit and flesh, where the spirit itself desires to subdue the flesh to itself through a right will, and the weak flesh always resists the spirit through contrary desires?

The second degree is a will strong in uprightness, so that it both firmly flees the evil which it hates, against the assault of the flesh, and strongly lays hold of the good which it loves by doing it, even with labor.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in all riches. For a will does not long remain strong if it has not been devout in the commandments of God.

The fourth degree is a full will, inasmuch as it does not divide its powers at all, but wholly stretches itself out continually toward its God through the denial of all things. For this degree three things are necessary, so that the good will in us may be made full.

The first is full denial of oneself, since a will is proved still to be weak if it is not mortified through complete denial of itself.

The second thing required for a full good will is said to be perfect resignation; if anyone truly desires to apprehend it, let him fervently ascend the third degree of humility.

The third member necessary for the fourth degree of good will is called the total extension of the will into God. Through it the soul, moved toward divine love and sweetly burning, is in a certain way transformed into that same love, and is frequently admitted into the third region.

Finally, this transformation into divine love, according to Bernard’s judgment, is love, affection, adhesion, union, charity, and enjoyment. No one can attain it unless he has learned to mortify all his affections in the fervor of the spirit. For the spirit of a good will, having the heart lifted upward into God, is called a perfect will, and by it the soul is joined to most sweet God in covenant. Therefore in these degrees of good will the faithful monk must direct his effort upward without interruption, and with prudent zeal always examine how much he advances or falls short. For Saint Bernard says: Just as he who remains in a vicious will is condemned, so he who does not strive to advance in higher things is blameworthy. For where the effort to advance is laid aside, one enters upon the dangers of descending.

On the Example of All the Things Said Above, from Experimental Certainty

Article VII

Although a man does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless each person can, by diligently examining his conscience, recognize what he ought to think about the condition of his state. What sort of person each one lies hidden within, before himself, the testimony of his own knowledge makes manifest. Therefore, so that a monk may be able clearly to discern the condition of his state, he must, according to John of Kastl, observe three things in his conscience as a rule; without these no one becomes a good Christian, and no one is judged a good man or monk.

The first, then, which is required for a good man and for the testimony of a good conscience, is that no mortal sin lie hidden in the conscience. For the mind which is polluted by the guilt of mortal sin has the testimony of an evil state of conscience.
