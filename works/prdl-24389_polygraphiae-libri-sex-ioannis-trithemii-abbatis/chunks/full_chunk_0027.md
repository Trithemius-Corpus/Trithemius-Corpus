*Magnus, from these, emperor of the Romans.* On this method of writing, see Suetonius Tranquillus in the Life of Augustus, chapter 83, and in the Life of Julius Caesar, chapter 80. “There are extant,” he says, “letters to Cicero, and likewise to his friends, concerning domestic matters, in which, if anything had to be conveyed rather secretly, he wrote it by signs, that is, with the order of the letters so arranged that no word could be formed; if anyone wished to investigate these, he should substitute the fourth letter of the alphabet, that is, d, for a, and change the rest in the same way.” Moreover Isidore also mentions a letter of Augustus to his son, in which he writes thus: “Since countless things continually occur which one or the other of us ought to write, and which ought to be secret, let us have signs between ourselves, if you wish, of such a kind that when something has to be written by us, for each single letter we write the following one, in this way: b for a, c for b, and thereafter the others by the same method. But for the letter z one must return to double aa.”

*Archimedes of Syracuse.* Similar to this is what is read in Gellius, book 17, chapter 9. The ancient Lacedaemonians, when they wished to disguise and conceal letters sent publicly to their commanders, so that, if they were intercepted by enemies, their plans would not become known, used to send letters made in this manner. There were two rods, round, somewhat oblong, of equal thickness and the same length, shaved down and finished in like fashion. One was given to the commander setting out for war; the other the magistrates kept at home, under oath and seal. When there was need of more secret letters, around the rod they wrapped a long strap of moderate thinness, or as much as was sufficient for the matter, in a round and single winding, so that the adjoining edges everywhere of the strap that was being folded came together and held fast. Then they wrote the letters on that strap across the joined edges, in lines proceeding from top to bottom. That strap, thus written all over with letters, when unwound from the rod, they sent to the commander, who knew the device. The unwinding of the strap likewise rendered the letters broken and mutilated, and their parts and strokes were scattered into very different places. Therefore, if that strap fell into the hands of enemies, they could infer absolutely nothing from it. But when the man to whom it had been sent received it, taking the matching rod which he had, he wrapped it around it to the end, just as he knew it had to be done; and thus the letters, coming together along the same circumference of the rod, joined again and provided a complete and uncorrupted letter, easy to read. The Lacedaemonians call this kind of letter a scytale. See also Plutarch in the Life of Lysander.

There is also another, no less ingenious, method of concealing letters, described by Jerome Cardano in his books On Subtleties. You take two equal sheets of parchment ruled for writing, and in the lines themselves you make small holes scattered here and there opposite one another in both, but nevertheless according to the size or height of the letters. Some will contain seven letters, some three, some eight or ten, so that all the holes together contain one hundred and twenty letters, when all those which can be written in them are joined together.
