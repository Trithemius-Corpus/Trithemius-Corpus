To these the same author immediately adds: "From the very computation which I have noted above, a certain manual speech can also be practiced, both for the sake of exercising ingenuity and of playing a game. By it, when the letters are expressed one by one, a person may convey to another, who also knows this technique, even if he is placed far away, the words contained in those letters, so that they may be read and understood; or he may signify necessary matters more secretly by intimating them in this way, or deceive the unskilled as though by divination. The order of this game or speech is as follows. When you wish to indicate the first letter of the alphabet, hold one with the hand; when the second, two; when the third, three; and so the rest in order. For example, if you wish to warn a friend placed among ambushers to act cautiously, show three and one, and twenty, and ten and nine, and five fingers; for the letters of this order signify 'act cautiously.'"

Thus far the example of dactylogy according to Bede, which is also varied in many other ways. For, once it has been posited, as was also said before, that the sign of unity signifies the letter a, and so on in order, let them be arranged on the left hand: one in the thumb, two in the index finger, three in the middle finger, four in the ring finger, or the third from the thumb, and five in the little finger. Likewise on the right hand let two be understood in the thumb, three in the index finger, four in the middle finger, five in the third from the thumb, and six in the little finger. Once these have been established in this way, let us observe which numbers arise from addition, multiplication, or division of them with one another. For example, twelve arises from the addition of one to eleven, two to ten, three to nine, four to eight, six to six, seven to five. In multiplication, however, twelve is produced from the multiplication of four by three, or of two by six. In division we have twelve if we divide twenty-four by two. Further, when the fingers are merely raised, it signifies addition; but when we touch one with another, multiplication. But if we bend one and raise the other, division must be understood.

Therefore, wishing to demonstrate the letter m, which occupies the twelfth place, I indicate twelve either by addition, or by multiplication, or by division. First, by raising the two little fingers of each hand together with the thumb of the left hand; for the thumb signifies one, while one of the fingers denotes five and the other six; but five, six, and one make twelve, which number indicates the letter m. By multiplication in this way: let us touch the third finger from the thumb of the left hand with the index finger of the right, since three multiplied by four makes twelve; or let us touch the little finger of the right hand with the index finger of the left, because the same [unclear] six twice gives twelve. It could also be done in another way, namely by the contact of the index finger of the right hand and the middle finger of the left, which show six. These again, by contact of both thumbs, or of the right index finger and the left thumb, signify the multiplication of the former number by two; but twice six makes twelve.

By division thus: first we shall indicate twenty-four, the division of which number by two yields twelve. Division by two is represented by the gesture of which I spoke above, namely, if two thumbs are shown, one indeed raised and the other bent; likewise the thumb and little finger of the left hand, while the index finger of the right is inclined. For they signify the partition of six by three, that is, two; by these, twenty-four divided demonstrates twelve. So much concerning dactylogy, or the speech of the fingers, which can also be handled otherwise in infinite ways, by assigning to fixed places vowels, consonants, semivowels, double letters, liquids, and treating such matters as shall seem good to each person. Although these things may appear ridiculous, nevertheless they have very great use if by a certain analogy they are transferred to other matters.
