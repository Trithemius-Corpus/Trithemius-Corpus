And therefore, whenever he takes up a sheet or booklet written polygraphically, let him go to a secret place; and, having opened the book of the *Polygraphia*, which he ought to have with him, let him consider each word through all the alphabets, and let him most carefully note with chalk on the table, or with ink on paper, what letter each one represents. And when he has examined the whole letter in that way, let him put the letters together into words, and he will easily understand what has lain hidden in secrecy. *XII. Twelfth,* kings and princes should note this, who have many vicars in different places, to all of whom it is not prudent to write by one kind of hidden narration. Therefore, so that they may have their own secret manner of writing to each one individually, let them change the order of the words as many times as they wish to have separate methods. Thus they themselves will possess the writing methods of all, and none of their subjects will understand another’s method. For the variable methods of writing more secretly in this art are infinite. *XIII. Thirteenth,* it must be noted that, in order to stop the mouths of those who speak wicked things, we show the truth of this new invention of ours to be possible, placing the words in the alphabets of the first book so coherently with one another that, in the manner of an exhortation and supplication offered to God, they render a fitting and Latin prayer. Beneath its narration we can conceal the secrets that occur to us and write in such a way to a friend skilled in the art that, if all the people in the whole world were to come together into one place, they could never discover the secret lying hidden beneath the open discourse, provided it has been arranged correctly. *XIIII. Fourteenth,* it should be known that in this very invention of our *Polygraphia* there still lie hidden certain things far better than those described above, namely how you will be able, in a few days, not to say hours, to instruct someone ignorant of the Latin language so that he knows how to write, read, and understand Latin: not, indeed, absolutely everything universally, but, according to a certain method, whatever necessary thing comes before him in his affairs on any subject.
