But concerning the other ancient men who are said to have handed down special letters to almost individual nations and peoples, which remain in use among them down to the present day, it is not my purpose on this occasion to set down or describe the account. For all the more learned men among us know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said first to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: α. β. γ. δ. ε. ζ. ι. λ. μ. ν. ο. π. ϱ. σ. τ. ψ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is said to have added four: ξ. θ. φ. χ. And Pythagoras afterwards added the letter θ, so that there should be twenty-one. χ. κ. ω. were subsequently discovered by others. Gordanis, bishop of the Goths, gave his people letters proper to them. When the unity of language had been confounded, the simplicity of characters too was variously altered in many ways. Hence it came about that, in the course of time, the use of letters arrogated to itself such licence that not only individual peoples, but also particular men, fashioning new characters for letters at their own discretion, prepared hiding-places for their mysteries. Yet every writing in an unusual character will not be free from the suspicion of those who see it. Notwithstanding this, many kings, tyrants, and princes of old devised for themselves a manner of writing set apart from common use and familiar to themselves, taking thought for the suspicion of others, provided only that what they wished to remain secret was not perceived. In imitation of these, I, [unclear], wishing to bring this work of Polygraphy, as far as possible, to a completed end, have resolved in this sixth book, which is the last, to call back from the underworld certain characters of the ancients which have now passed out of human use, lest they perish utterly. First, then, we shall set down the ancient manner of writing of the Northmen.

*Concerning the alphabet and the remarkable manner of writing of the Northmen, at the time when they were laying waste Gaul by their incursion.*

All the letters of the Greeks and Palestinians are numerical, as was said before, while of the Latins, which we Germans also use, only seven are referred to numbers, namely c, d, i, l, m, v, and x. But the Northmen, after they had once come forth with the Asiatic Dacians from the Scandian islands, which are now called Gothic, wandered through Gaul for more than forty years and devastated it most grievously. In order to take counsel for their secret affairs, as Bede the English monk testifies, they discovered from the letters of the Greeks a new and beautiful manner of writing, as follows. For they adapted the numbers of the Greeks, comprised in ten letters among the twenty-four letters, to Latin characters, and prudently hid our letters beneath a foreign form: that is, in the manner in which we use numbers in place of letters, when, for example, for a we put 1, for b 2, for c 3, for d 4, and so on consecutively to the end of the alphabet. It is indeed a beautiful and quite safe invention, which neither a Greek nor a Latin will easily understand. For the whole alphabet is not found in it, but only nine letters, and one numerical sign, namely g, which among them signifies six and in this manner of writing denotes f, the sixth letter of the Latins. Moreover, yielding to our German language, in which we were born, we have finally added w, double v, as the twenty-fourth letter of our nation, which, according to the Greek number, we have formed thus: kΛ.
