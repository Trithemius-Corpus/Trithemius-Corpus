Having obtained fragments of this grammar, I too have extracted the present characters of the alphabet from many, lest they perish entirely. It is clear, on Otfrid’s testimony, that Charles devised several alphabets of his own, which he might use securely in secret matters with individual prefects throughout his very wide kingdom. From these letters, with Otfrid supplying them, we commit a few here.

Charles the great king, most Christian emperor, fought for thirty years with the Saxons, whom at last, overcoming them by the sword, he converted to the Christian faith. But fearing lest they should again, as they had done several times, apostatize from the faith, he established certain secret investigators, to whom he gave judicial power, who, traversing all Saxony, should secretly and diligently inquire into the faith and morals of the people; and whomever they found to have apostatized from the faith, or to be robbers, adulterers, blasphemers, contemners of the Church or of its priests and commandments, or disturbing the Christian commonwealth with notorious crimes, or recalling or soliciting the people to paganism, they should, without delay, by imperial and royal authority, with impunity hang by the noose, or kill by other means as they could. In order that this institution might remain forever unshaken, he gave these same men the power of substituting also other suitable men under fixed conditions, who, enjoying the aforesaid authority, should exercise with impunity the office of inquiry and of death upon the guilty. Finally he prescribed for them secret laws, hidden signs, and at the same time a form of oath, by which they might proceed justly in judging and punishing, and, being mutually known to one another, remain hidden from others, and perpetually preserve in the land of Saxony the necessary judgment in greater secrecy. They also used certain alphabets among themselves for a time, which, however, at length entirely fell out of use. Yet the office of this sort of inquiry remains down to the present time, whose ministers are commonly called Feimeri.

Although these characters do not appear sufficiently beautiful and elegant, nevertheless it has pleased me to join them with the foregoing new inventions of mine, for the honor and reverence of antiquity, since there is no one, even of little industry, who could not devise prettier ones for himself at his own discretion. Although continuous practice in writing will be able, by easy compensation, to amend this misshapen crookedness and, when often brought into habit, to make it pleasing. If anyone should wish to fashion for himself a new alphabet according to his own discretion, let him know that two things must especially be observed, which nevertheless I do not always find preserved in the preceding examples. The first is that he form characters of easy tracing, and the simpler they are, the better they will be. The second is that he order the individual letters in such a way that, joined into words, they present a beautiful and elegant appearance. I add a third, not promised: if you wish the writing to remain secret, make some letters of the alphabet double, in the manner of the Hebrews.
