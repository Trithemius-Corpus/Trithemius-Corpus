*The second kind, Hyphasmaticon.* The very learned Theodorus Bibliander has “hyphalmicon,” for this reason, as I think: because in this sort of concealment none of the letters appears before the paper has been immersed in water. Thus, when we write letters with alum, water poured over the paper will make it darker than the letters, which otherwise could not have been read.

*Letters are also written in various ways.* Herodotus, near the end of book seven: After Xerxes had resolved to undertake an expedition against Greece, Demaratus, who was at Susa, when he had learned this, judged that it should be reported to the Greeks. But since he could not do it otherwise, for it was dangerous lest he be detected, he devised this thing: taking a pair of writing-tablets, he scraped the wax from them. Then on the wood of them he scratched the king’s plan. When he had scratched it in letters, he covered it again with wax, so that the tablets would present no business to the road-guards for the person to whom they were being carried. When these had been brought to Lacedaemon, the Lacedaemonians could not interpret them until, as I understand it, Gorgo, daughter of Cleomenes and wife of Leonidas, warned them, herself interpreting the matter, ordering the wax to be broken off; for they would find letters in the wood. So the Lacedaemonians, listening to her, found the letters, read them over, and then sent them on to the Greeks.

Gellius, book seventeen, chapter 9: “There is also,” he says, “in the records of Greek affairs a certain deep and hard-to-grasp hiding-place, devised with barbarian cunning.” There was a man named Histiaeus, born in a not undistinguished place in the land of Asia. At that time King Darius held Asia under his rule. This Histiaeus, when he was in Persia with Darius, wished to announce certain secret matters to a certain Aristagoras by clandestine writing. He devised this remarkable concealment of letters: from his slave, who had long had diseased eyes, he shaved the hair from the whole head as though for the sake of treating him, and marked his bare head in the forms of letters. With these letters he wrote out what he had wished. Afterwards he kept the man at home until his hair grew back. When this had happened, he ordered him to go to Aristagoras. “And when you come to him,” he said, “say that I have commanded him to shave your head, just as I myself recently did.” The slave came to Aristagoras as he had been ordered and brought his master’s instruction. And he, thinking that this order was not without purpose, did what had been commanded; thus the letters were carried through.

Herodotus writes almost the same things; in that author it is also read in book one: When Astyages was harsh toward the Medes, Harpagus, insinuating himself with each of them individually, persuaded the leading men that Astyages ought to be removed from the kingship, with Cyrus chosen. When these matters had been arranged and prepared by Harpagus, then at last, wishing to reveal his intention to Cyrus, who was staying among the Persians, and being unable to do so otherwise, since the roads were guarded, he devised a thing of this sort: having gutted a hare, which he had skilfully obtained, so that nothing at all was cut away, he put inside it a little book in which he had written whatever he pleased; and, having sewn up the belly, he handed the hare, together with the nets, to a certain hunter of his household, a man of greatest trustworthiness, instructing him by word of mouth that, when he gave the hare, he should tell Cyrus to open it with his own hands and to do so without witnesses. He sent the man away into Persia. When the messenger had carried this out, Cyrus received the hare, opened it, found the little book that was in it, and read it. See Justin, book 1.

The historians also report that doves served as messengers in difficult circumstances, so that Hirtius the consul sent letters tied to their necks or feet to Decimus Brutus during the siege of Mutina, and Decimus Brutus sent them to Hirtius, after he had arranged food in certain very high places and had taught doves, previously shut up in darkness and worn down by much hunger, to fly back and forth.

Some, in order to avoid suspicion, write on paper with ink, [as if?] they wished to communicate something to a friend about domestic matters; afterwards they write what is secret with urine in the spaces. Others use the juice of lemons or onions; others use pounded sal ammoniac with water poured over it. Things written with letters of this kind are completely hidden before they have been brought near the fire. Some follow the poet’s counsel: “Since he can hide papers bound to his leg, / And jun[unclear].”
