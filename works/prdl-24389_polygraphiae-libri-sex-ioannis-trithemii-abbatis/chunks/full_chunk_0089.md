With the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, savior of faithful souls, who lives in the sacred ternary unity with the Father and the Holy Spirit, threefold in persons and one God in substance, incomprehensible, almighty, and everlasting, from whom, in whom, and through whom every good, beautiful, and true thing proceeds, we have brought this second book of the Polygraphy to its end. To him be praise, honor, power, and glory: he who through the innocence of his death freed our race from the destruction of eternal perdition, and in perpetual mercy opened again the entrance to eternal life for all who believe in him with a pure conscience. We set down, line by line, three hundred and eight paraphrases, deliberately avoiding more lengthy digression. For at the beginning of this night-work it seemed enough to us if we gave to those who wished it a reason for seeking out fuller things, since both time and method were lacking to us for them. Farewell, friendly reader. From [Peapolis?], on the penultimate day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord 1508.

*END OF THE SECOND BOOK of the Polygraphy.*

*The third book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of [Peapolis?], of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

What, from the beginning of human creation, confounded the unity of language? Was it not the division of bodies and the variety of minds? For as Truth declares in the Gospel: out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For the tongue is the instrument for bringing outward those conceptions which the mind has thought within itself in its inner parts. Yet there are several modes of this service of the mind’s secrets to sense, whose properties vary both by use and by art. Use, introduced at will, is common to many; art, however, to few. Finally, even the unregulated use of speaking itself, how much it has varied among the Theutons alone in six hundred years, no one will readily estimate who has not rather often reread Otfried, the monk of Weissenburg, in his poems in the German language. The sea flows and ebbs; human beings, once dying, do not return again to the present life. Customs succeed customs, and the vigor of mortal minds always falls away toward softer things. Who today among the Germans, the ancient imitators of the Greeks, retains knowledge of the old idioms? In chronicles and old histories of the Franks, Hunibald, Doracus, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed into their compositions many things spoken both in ancient verse and in prose of the Theutonic language, which today are understood by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own proper idiom among the East Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhinelanders, Mosellans, Cologne people, Saxons, and Thuringians undergoes notable variation every day?
