*Mystery.* Marcus T. Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, using both talent and art, changed the minutiae of phrasing into signs, and so forth.

*Explanation.* He wrote for his son a book of signs, which the blessed martyr Cyprian afterwards enlarged for the use of Christians, namely in the manner of a dictionary, where first the characters or signs are set down in order, and afterwards the words designated by the same characters, so that any given word is signified by the sign placed before it. Written in these signs or characters we once saw an entire Latin psalter, beautifully copied, in the library of the greater church of Strasbourg; some man, ignorant as a judge of things, had placed on its outside the following title: “A Psalter in the Armenian language.” But he was mistaken, since it should rather have been written thus: “A Latin Psalter written in Tullian characters.” For each character represents one Latin word.

Nor does my assessment deceive me, since I possess the very dictionary of Cicero, corrected by Saint Cyprian, written complete and uncorrupted in an old volume, which I have seen nowhere else. So great is the abundance of signs or characters, with the words subject to them, that they are altogether sufficient for writing in the Latin tongue whatever you wish.

*Mystery.* Faramund, the forty-third from Marcomer, king of the Franks, when in the year of the Lord’s birth 424 he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, and so forth.

*Explanation.* From the time when, in the year 430 before the birth of Christ, the kings of the Franks went forth with their people from the Scythian territories to the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is, they waged continuous war with the Gauls for almost nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject to themselves, as we have written in the first volume of our Annals. Of these, the aforementioned King Faramund, in order to send messengers safely through the kingdom, devised new letters peculiar to himself, which no one could know without his voluntary instruction. Nor did he use only one, nor a few, but many new alphabets.
