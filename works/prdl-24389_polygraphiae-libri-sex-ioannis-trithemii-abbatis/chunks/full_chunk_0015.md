*Preface to the Explanation of the Whole Work of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of [Paeapolis?], that is, of Würzburg, at St James.*

No one who has read these things, I trust, will judge me either proud or superfluous because I myself have provided a commentary on the Polygraphy recently composed by me, especially if he has been able to weigh exactly what cause led us to speak obscurely. For whatever, if made public, can do harm had reasonably to be concealed in mystical language. But the subject which we treat in the Polygraphy is, among good men, very good, useful, and honorable for the advantage of the commonwealth; and for that reason, persuaded by a rational cause, we wrote it down, so that by its service we might render good men safe and secure from the investigation of the wicked when they communicate their secrets to one another. But lest an opportunity be offered to the depraved and perverse, who very frequently misuse even good inventions, to deceive the simpler and less cautious in their judgment of things, we handed down in it mystical or hidden modes of writing, and covered the truth lying hidden in the marrow with certain coverings of figures. Yet we by no means doubt that any industrious man, eager with desire for these matters, will easily grasp everything, if he applies even a moderate persistence in searching them out. For the things I have handed down are not so difficult, nor wrapped in such great mysteries, that they cannot be thoroughly penetrated by a studious man. For everywhere we have so tempered the riddles of our words that they are easily accessible to learned and studious men, and nevertheless remain inaccessible to sluggish men of darkness. But after it pleased us to dedicate and offer the work of the Polygraphy itself to the most serene and most invincible Caesar of the Romans, Maximilian, ever august emperor of the world, I judged it worth the trouble to make it clear and open with brief and few explanations, lest the mind of his sacred majesty, which is daily burdened by many affairs and cares, be drawn back from understanding it by the opacity of the mysteries. For unless my opinion deceives me, the work will be so much the more pleasing and useful to his majesty, the briefer and clearer it has been. Therefore, first of all, with the Lord granting it, we shall briefly explain the meaning of our proposal, which is contained in the prologue wrapped in various riddles; then, through the individual books of the Polygraphy and the modes of operation, the rules, precautions, and distinctions, we shall succinctly set forth, according to order, what necessary things remain, as far as the powers of our intellect allow. All these things, so that they may more quickly become accessible to those who desire to know, we shall show in sequence by examples, and we shall be as brief as the cause and reason permit.

*Exposition on the Prologue.*

Our whole intention in the entire work of the Polygraphy turns upon this purpose: to hand down certain modes of writing secretly, through which kings and princes, noble and ignoble, priests and laymen, learned and unlearned alike, may secretly, safely, and without any suspicion of a hidden secret, by the service of letters, communicate to one another and to any friends, even those far distant from one another, whatever conception of their minds may be necessary and as often as it may be necessary.
