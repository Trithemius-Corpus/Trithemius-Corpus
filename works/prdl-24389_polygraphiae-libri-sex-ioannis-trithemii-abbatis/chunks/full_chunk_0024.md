We have shown a sufficient method of counting by Latin letters in the manner of the Hebrews and Greeks, which by way of digression we have reasonably placed here, although it is found earlier in the sixth book. By the fact that, as we proceeded, we have here concluded our number at thirty hundred thousands, we plainly declare that it can be extended to infinity. If this method of numbers displeases anyone, he will easily be able to devise a better one. In imitation also of the Northmen, from the numerical letters set out above, we have arranged a new alphabet, by which you will be able safely to write and report to an absent friend any secret whatsoever you wish. Indeed this method of writing is as secret as it is new, never seen or heard before us, so that it seems to me altogether impossible that it can be understood by anyone without the service of a teacher. But now let us set down its alphabet.

Behold, you now have here an alphabet from numerical letters in imitation of the Northmen, with which you will be able to write and report most safely to an absent friend whatever you wish. No one lying hidden will be able to penetrate the secret except him who knows the secret of the art. For all who see the writing will think that the letters have been transposed, and they will labor in vain in reckoning them. Let us set down an example: Iaaihidifeib, iceigeicchkib. cal'eihid.iei [unclear]ahk.gidibidi[unclear]ig. eiif.iaaihifidibe icda a.cih.iceieik. The hidden meaning of this writing is as follows: Beware the bearer of the present letters, because he is a very wicked man, a thief, a robber, a liar, worthless. From the demonstration of this example it is easily given to be understood how safe and secure this method of writing is universally, in every language known to the writer, and in every cause or business, however difficult, secret, great, or lengthy. Nor do I think there is a man living in the world who by his own ingenuity, without a teacher, can read this very writing. For all who see it will think nothing other than that the letters have been transposed; and when they cannot obtain an understanding of them, at last worn out by despair they will cease from the investigation, and whatever you have written will remain hidden. For no one, as I suppose, will examine by his own powers this craft of writing secretly as it is, unless he has Polygraphy, or this key to it, at hand, or has been instructed by another. From these numerical letters many other alphabets can also be given according to the order of counting, when the beginning for writing alphabetically is taken not from unity but from any other following number. But in that progression you should know that one must beware lest more than two countable letters be taken to represent one letter. Therefore, when you have come to vovo, that is, ten hundred thousands, stop your advance and do not wander farther, lest the multitude of letters, by spoiling the beauty of writing, confound it. For, after unity has been set aside, ten hundred thousand, with [unclear] letters included, suffice for nearly thirty separate alphabets. Nevertheless the method of the first alphabet, beginning with unity, is so much the fairer for wrapping secrets in writing as it is shorter and simpler. Finally, it renders the writing less contorted.
