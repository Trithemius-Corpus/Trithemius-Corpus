*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James the Apostle at Wurzburg. The Fifth Book of the Polygraphia: the beginning.*

Those things which, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have set forth, in whatever manner, in the four preceding books for the completion of our Polygraphia, reasonably require that we briefly treat, in the fifth, the remaining precepts and methods of this instruction which are left. Therefore in it we shall give a sufficiently concise method by metathesis, that is, by transposition of letters, by which a friend may write to a friend securely, safely, and most secretly whatever he wishes. We read that Caesar Augustus used this mode of writing in secret matters. And although this method of communication is secure in many ways, nevertheless it is always regarded with suspicion by those who look at it: for as soon as a transposition of letters is perceived, anyone curious at once suspects that something lies hidden beneath them. Now the methods of this invention are almost infinite; from them, for the necessary introduction, we have marked out the following for the reader, leaving the rest to be investigated by his own ingenuity. First, therefore, we have placed before him the canonical table of this craft, to which we have added beneath another, reversed one. In these two tables the root and foundation of the aforesaid transposition is wholly set down, upon which the structure of the whole edifice is placed. For when these two tables have been duly known and understood, no transposition of letters will be able to remain hidden from one working carefully. To these we have joined twenty-four alphabets in order, so that we might furnish the reader with an abundance for transposing letters. The use of these, according to one’s wish, consists in each single one, or, if you prefer, even in all of them. If you have secret dealings with many people, assign the individual alphabets to your individual intimates, and commit to memory what you have handed over to each.

Hand over the first alphabet to the first man, the second to the second, the third to the third, and so let it be done in sequence with the rest. But if, because of the multitude of affairs or of one’s household, the alphabets which we have marked out should not suffice for everything, or if some of them seem too open and plain, we shall be able to introduce various new transpositions, of which both the number is very great and the method of the secret may always remain perpetually hidden. Let us set down an example of this matter: let B be a, f be b, l be c, p be d, t be e, z be f, c be g, m be h, and so consequently let it be done through the whole usual alphabet, so that between any assumed letter three idle and transposed letters may intervene. Afterwards four, then five, more or fewer, yet in such a way that the whole alphabet, as though turned around in a circle, comes together, and no letter at all is omitted, but each is placed for another in its own order in the same series. In whatever way you have written it, it must be committed firmly to memory, lest, having fallen into forgetfulness, you yourself can no longer read what you had written. For the transposition of letters can be varied in as many ways as, according to the estimation of the human intellect, which, enclosed in a mortal body by temporal operation, does not properly grasp infinities, an innumerable mortal number will arise. Hence anyone who wishes to have more methods of writing by metathesis, and ones removed from our instruction, if he is even a little ingenious and diligent, will obtain an easy result in his work. For the human intellect can do very great things if it is aided by study and art. But he who despises the smallest things will never attain to greater ones. I know that there will be very many who will deride these studies of ours as empty and childish; to them we give as a reply that we shall be wholly content, provided they please those at whose insistence they have been composed by us.
