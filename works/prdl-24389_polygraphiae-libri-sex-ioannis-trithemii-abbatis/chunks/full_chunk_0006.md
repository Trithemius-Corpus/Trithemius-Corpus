The Mirror of Heavenly Doctrine, of no small service to compunction of mind, one book, which begins thus, “The beginning of human salvation.” He also composed many most devout meditations and prayers on our Lord Jesus Christ, on his most holy mother the Virgin Mary, and on various saints, with beautiful variety, together with very many other things, which all devout posterity will see and admire. These, my dearest Nicolaus, are the volumes of Trithemius our abbot, which through almost thirty years he has worked out with continual study by lamplight, besides those which, not yet completed, are in his hands under the anvil and unknown to me. Farewell, from my house at Duraclusa, on the fifth of the month of October, in the year of our salvation 1515. Johannes Duraclusius.

*Preface of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, to Emperor Maximilian, on Polygraphy.*

We have read that many wise men of the ancients, philosophers, kings, and princes, once devised varied and manifold methods by which they might safely entrust to their messengers, when sending them to more remote places, the secrets of counsel and whatever secret mystery had to be confided, so that they would become fit for carrying them and secure from every assault of the wicked, lest either the simplicity of the cabalists should lay bare the mystery, or the innate curiosity of the [bacutii?] should bring leaven into apostolic matters. Great among these, Augustus, Caesar of the Romans, wishing in difficult affairs to use the service of messengers in obscurity, changed the breathings of the conceived voice from clear signs into obscure ones; and, by a beautiful invention, through the measure of transposition, he made their barbarous form, resistant to wishes, agree with his command in a dance-like arrangement. And this receptive and, as it were, perpetual order, nevertheless subject to the theme and to the reckoning of time, although it keeps the messengers safe on the road from the assault of the [bacucei?], yet, by not keeping the regulated bounds of its own system, will make all who look on anxious with suspicion of the matter.

Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the foremost of mathematicians, took a white bird suited to his own arrangements, bare at head and feet, fixed it around a staff cut into the form of a tetragon, and, after arranging the attendants in due proportion, entrusted the work to a flying instrument, until the image of the white bird appeared changed into a magpie. When this had been duly done, he released with his own hand what had been fixed; and when he had allowed it to fly away, there was suddenly among the familiar faces of the inner chambers a great separation, and soon there arose [unclear].
