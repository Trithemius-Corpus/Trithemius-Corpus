*Apology of Johannes Trithemius Prefixed to the Steganographia*

It is the opinion of many that the ancient philosophers and wise men of art and nature, if they had discovered any secrets, concealed them in various ways and figures, lest they should come to the knowledge of wicked men. That Moses also, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his description of the creation of heaven and earth, covered ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words, all the more learned among the Jews confirm. Saint Jerome too, most learned among our own, says that almost as many mysteries lie hidden in the Apocalypse as there are words. Moreover the wise men of the Greeks, men of no small reputation and glory among their people, who devoted their efforts to composing fables, handed down one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men, in the course of a single narrative, by a prudent invention.

These most zealous lovers of wisdom, even if because of the slowness of my intellect I cannot imitate them perfectly, nevertheless I judge it fine and glorious to admire, and with such diligence as I can to read and venerate. Thus, when I consider that they, by their own study, discovered great things beyond other men, I drive myself, doing a kind of violence to myself, toward some imitation of them, even if among the very last. Nor, as I think, has my opinion altogether deceived me; for through continual zeal in reading I have learned many things which I had not known before, and I have opened [the way] to my thoughts for investigating more secret matters.

For even if I am not of such learning or industry that I dare profess that I have comprehended that method of the ancient wise men for concealing mysteries, nevertheless I have devised certain methods, not altogether contemptible, as it seems to me, of writing and concealing secretly, and wholly without any suspicion, whatever you wish. At the request of the most serene prince, lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, prince-elector of the Roman Empire, the wisest Maecenas of philosophers, than whom no one seemed to me more worthy to whom this great secret should be revealed, I committed them to writing and reduced them into the following volume, not without immense labor, lest this great Secret come to the ears of common emperors or wicked men.

Reader, whoever you will be, I ask you to consider how many evils could be done in the commonwealth by evil and wanton men through this science if it came to the knowledge of many: adulteries, fornications, conspiracies, betrayals, murders, robberies, and infinite evils. For just as a good and honorable man can safely and without any suspicion from anyone make known and express the secret of his will, as often as he wishes, for private or common advantage, to another who knows this art, so every perverse, wanton, and malicious man will be able to intimate his evil desire, however shamefully, to a person agreeable to his pleasure, with every obstacle entirely removed and without any suspicion.

Nor could trust between married persons thereafter remain secure, since a woman, although up to this point ignorant of the Latin tongue, made most learned through holy and modest words of any language or idiom, could understand very fully the evil and shameless will of her lover, even while her husband presents and praises the letters; and in the same way she could most safely send back to him her own desire, as widely as she wished, in sufficiently eloquent speech. Indeed, although this science may seem childish to some, nevertheless if it were made public the whole order of the commonwealth would be disturbed, public trust would perish, letters, written instruments, and finally all the very speech of men would be turned into perpetual suspicion. No one would any longer trust letters, however good, honorable, and holy; no one would put faith in epistles, since, however holy and modest the words might be, nevertheless deceit, fraud, and evil deception would always be thought to lie beneath, and men would become fearful in all things, and no less suspicious toward mutual conversations than toward any letters at all.
