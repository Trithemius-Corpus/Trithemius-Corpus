Thus, at last encouraged by weighing these things, I laid aside all fear and took up great confidence in your innate gentleness. Indeed, since all draw confidence from the fountain of your piety, why should I alone not approach trustfully? You, who are always gracious, gentle, and pious to all, surely cannot be cruel to me alone. Therefore, with that humility which is fitting, and which I owe and am able to show, I approach the throne of your most excellent majesty, prostrate upon the ground: I devotedly offer the aforementioned work of Polygraphy, and humbly commend my littleness to your serenity as a servant to his lord. Therefore now accept, most gracious Caesar, the very small little gift which I bring, in which may you deign to weigh my spirit, most subject to you, not its value. But in the subject system of the Polygraphy there are, in certain places, some mysteries wrapped in riddles, lest the meaning of the more inward art which lies hidden should become manifest to the understanding of all. Just as not all things befit all people, so not all things everywhere and always suit everyone. There are therefore certain mysteries shut up here, which in my judgment can be more useful and more necessary to none than to princely men. For the more people they are concerned with, the greater caution of secrecy must be applied to their counsels. For this book teaches princes to command secrecy for their secrets.

But because your Caesarean majesty, occupied with many affairs, does not have time to search out those things which lie hidden wrapped in mysteries, lest he be deprived of the fruit of my labor, I have appended a little explanatory book of the whole work, which I have entitled the Key of Polygraphy; with it as guide you will more easily understand everything obstructed here by figures. May God preserve you unharmed for our salvation. I wrote on the twenty-sixth day of the month of April, in the year of Christ 1508.

*Table or Index of the Lucubrations of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot first of Sponheim and afterward of St. James at Würzburg.*

Johannes Trithemius the abbot was born in the village of Trittenheim, in the diocese of Trier, on the banks of the river Moselle, on the first day of the month of February, at the eleventh hour, in the thirtieth minute after midday, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1462, in the tenth indiction of the Romans: his father was Johannes de Heidenberg, and his mother was called Elisabeth de Longo Vico. This was in that year, namely, in which the city of Mainz, in the dissension of two pontiffs, was captured, lost its liberty, and found servitude, with many of its citizens driven into exile; in the fourth current year of Pope Pius, the second of that name, and the twenty-second of Emperor Frederick III. But in the year of the Lord 1482, in the monastery of Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, on the first day of February, that is, the day on which he had once been born, he laid aside the secular habit, and there assumed the monastic habit under the rule of the divine father Benedict, according to the observance newly introduced. In the following year, which was the eighty-third above the four-hundredth and the thousandth, after Johannes de Colenhusen the abbot had been transferred to Seligenstadt by canonical postulation, Johannes Trithemius, by the common election of the brethren, was promoted and ordained abbot of the aforementioned monastery of Sponheim on the fourth day before the Kalends of July, and presided for twenty-three years, one month, and nineteen days. When these had passed, not without many labors, provoked by the rashness of certain rivals, he accepted the abbacy of St. James at Würzburg, which was offered to him, and of his own free will resigned Sponheim, full of useless cares.
