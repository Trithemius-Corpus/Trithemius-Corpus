Whatever may happen, my message will always remain hidden, nor could all the men of the whole world, if they were gathered together at once, track it down by natural power. This too I can do without a messenger whenever I wish; I can also make my will known, even when long absent, to one sitting in prison who knows the art, however closely he is guarded, even if he were sitting three miles under the earth. And all these things, most broadly and universally, whenever and as often as I wish, I can do naturally, without any superstition or aid of any spirits whatsoever.

They are wonderful things, I admit; but hear things more wonderful. The third book teaches an art by which I can, in two hours, teach a man who knows that same knowledge and only his mother tongue, and who has never known a word of Latin speech, to write, read, and understand Latin fairly elegantly and fluently, as much as he wishes, so that whoever sees his letters will praise the words and will understand them as composed in Latin. The fourth book contains many astonishing experiments, but purely natural ones. Namely, I can make known my mind to one who knows [the art] [unclear] whenever I wish, even while speaking, preaching, playing the organ, or singing, without hindrance to any other action whatsoever, so that while preaching good and holy things I may make known, not by words, not by signs, to anyone who knows [the art], most broadly, whatever I wish, even with closed eyes; and the fourth book will contain many other secrets which are not to be brought before the public.

All who hear these things marvel at them; among them are many noble and very learned men, who think that these things are either impossible or wholly supernatural. To them, and to you, I answer what I have answered to many: that many things are naturally possible which, to those who do not know the powers of nature, seem impossible, or even supernatural. And behold, I speak before my God, who knows all things, that the wondrous things which I have stated above are much more excellent, deeper, and greater than I can write or you can believe; and yet all are purely natural, without any deception, without superstition, without magical art, without invocation or service of any spirits whatsoever. I have said these things for this reason: that if perhaps at some time a rumor should reach you that I either know or can do wonderful things, you should not, I say, think me a magician, but a philosopher. For what happened to Albertus Magnus, that most profound investigator of natural things, namely that because of the wonders which he performed by the hidden power of nature he was considered a magician by the common people, I am certain could likewise happen to me.

If you ask whence I have these things which no one else knows, hear: I learned these things not from man nor through man, but by revelation from I know not whom. For when in the present year I was once thinking what new thing I could discover that would be hidden from everyone, I began to consider whether I could devise those wonderful things which I have mentioned. When, after long imagining, I utterly despaired of it as an impossible matter, I laid myself down to sleep that night, laughing at my own folly within myself, that I had tried to seek impossible things. That same night someone stood by me, saying, “The things you have thought, O Trithemius, are not vain, although they are impossible for you, and neither you nor another with you will be able to discover them.” And I said to him: “If therefore they are possible, tell me, I beg, how they are done.” And opening his mouth, he taught me each thing in order, showing how what I had vainly pondered for many days could easily be done.

Behold, before God, because I do not lie but speak the truth: I have taught these things to no one yet, although many, promising great things, have often asked, except one prince to whom I am writing, whom I have made more certain of the possibility of the art by evident proof. Nor is it fitting for others to know these things than princes; otherwise many evils would be done through them: betrayals, deceptions, fornications, and whatever else evil men might wish. But those using the art well could do many good things for the commonwealth through it. Moreover I can teach all these things in every language of the whole world which I have never heard. I wanted to tell you these things, Bostius, so that you would not think me idle.

Thus far the words of Trithemius. Lastly, there are other immense uses of these books of Polygraphy, which we shall treat in our books On the Method of Teaching and Learning; for whatever these things may seem to be, they have more in reserve than they promise on the surface. Concerning Steganography, in whose first books hidden matters lie concealed, God willing, we shall deal separately.
