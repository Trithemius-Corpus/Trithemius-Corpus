*Johannes Trithemius, [unclear] abbot, greets the reader.*

Since the letters of the Greeks and the Hebrews are all numerical, I have often wondered that the careful Latin usage of the ancients did not also make its own letters serve in the reckoning of numbers. The Hebrews, by duplicating certain letters, established twenty-eight signs, sufficient for every number. The Greeks, with like foresight, remedied this deficiency of letters by adding four characters. Since we therefore have twenty-four letters, why do we not make ours numerical too, we who can similarly find four duplicated letters? Let us give four as an example, and there will be enough for every number: [unclear] and final [j?]; likewise long s and final or closed s; likewise closed v and open u. When we add these to the twenty-three usual letters, we have twenty-eight characters sufficient for all numbers. And it will be no great labor to grasp this method of numbering for those who already know the letters, if they once commit it to memory. For this invention of ours will be able to be useful to many, since in a few letters it comprises many thousands in number, all of which can be discerned and understood at first sight, more clearly than light, without any calculation. Many who are less experienced in the art of calculation think that this cannot be done so easily by the figures of algorism, especially in large numbers. For as soon as you have looked with your eye upon a number, however large, you will be able both to understand it fully and to express it without circumlocution or division of speech. Since this is so easy, it is fitting that we set down the letters themselves reduced to number. I wrote this on 16 March in the year 1508.

Behold, reader, you have the method by which you can adequately set down and write every writable number in a few letters, whose extension, as you see, proceeds to infinity. But as it was once customary to say in a proverb, the tired ox plants his foot firmly: whoever wishes to be content with the former things should follow the old method of numbering. So that those who do not know it may understand it more easily, it will not be tedious for me to give in what follows the form which Hunibald, historiographer of the Franks, and also Bede, Rabanus, Helperic, monks, and several of the ancients used in chronicles and ecclesiastical calculations, as follows.
