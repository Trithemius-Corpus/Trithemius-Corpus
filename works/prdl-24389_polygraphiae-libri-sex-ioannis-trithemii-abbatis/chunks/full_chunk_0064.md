We rejoice that the end of the first book of our *Polygraphia* is at hand; made readier by it for what remains, we judge that it must be tried what the powers of our intellect are. For newly discovered principles sometimes disturb the mind, since he whom all follow has no one preceding him. The first beginning of any invention is the more difficult; after it has been made firm, it brings greater confidence to its author. Therefore, completing with God’s favor and help the first book of our invention, we have divided the whole into 384 minutiae, placing beneath each, in order as is seen, the proper degrees of the periphrases. And these form a sufficiently beautiful Christian concord: sixteen way-stations, with more than 209,000 phrases duly joined in all respects. Nothing is lacking here for students of these inventions of ours, so that in mystical, secret, and hidden matters, even to be reported very remotely and most safely, they may have the fullest knowledge and faculty.

All things contained in the art proceed in the circle and the point, whose atoms constitute a circular line, and, by the multitude of light, a straight one. Whatever is outside the line serves the common reckoning of day, not night. For night, always enclosed, never passes beyond the limits of the minutiae and paraphrases. Therefore, as often as you cross the straight line of day and do not fulfill the proposed plan of nocturnal syntax, turn the head back to the tail, and when a joining has been made, let there be a binding of each to the other. Arrange the shadow so that it does not confuse the sun by similarity of sound. And do not be ashamed if in the first calculation the day has not comprehended the shadow; rather, consider carefully the minutiae of the lines of day, reckon one together with another, until the necessary proportion follows according to your wish. You will also be able to make a leap from the first to the second, knowing that in the first creation of days there was made evening and morning, one day. But when this evening has been completed, bring the day back into the following one, and, when the sign has been given through the point known simultaneously to the binary and the monad, fix the mystery. But so that you may be able to avoid this difficulty, distinguish the great revolutions of our whole circle by arithmetical degrees; and where day is repeated for night, set the mark of Archimedes upon the flying thing, lest an immature ternary block the return to unity for the binary.

For this reason we have placed minutiae before the individual degrees by an artful invention, so that in these we might relieve the worker from the labor of counting the paraphrases. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in these things which we have handed down as the principles of a new art, believe us: you will find greater things hidden than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these matters, know this one thing, not least: that by the prudent operation of metathesis, even when you wish, you will make the most skilled man in this art ignorant, the seeing blind, and the learned unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, for each person of the eighteen thousand worlds this invention of ours would give individual modes of safely and secretly writing and reporting, most widely, whatever Latin secrets they wished.

*End of the First Book of the Polygraphia.*
