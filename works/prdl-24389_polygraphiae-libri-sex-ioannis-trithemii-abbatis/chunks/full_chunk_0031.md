There are also other devices: for instance, they paint letters on flint with the fat of a goat or ox; when it has been placed in vinegar for several hours and then taken out again, it displays to sight what had been inscribed. Likewise, they write with the white of an egg and dip it into ink; when this is scraped off, the letters are seen. *There were also, as Bede bears witness)* The sense is: the ancients not only wrote or spoke with words, as we do, but also transferred whatever the world contains, and whatever can be grasped by us, to signify things by a certain definite art. Diodorus Siculus, in book four, says: “The letters of the Egyptians closely resemble various living creatures, extremities, and those of human beings and instruments, but especially those of craftsmen; for their words are expressed not by the composition of syllables or by letters, but by the form of images, through their signification handed down to men’s memory by use.” They write, for example, a hawk, a crocodile, a serpent, a human eye, hands, a face, and other things of this kind. The hawk denotes a thing quickly done, since this bird is almost the swiftest of all; this notation is transferred to domestic affairs that are done swiftly. The crocodile signifies evil. The eye is interpreted as the preserver of justice and the guardian of the whole body. Of the remaining parts of the body, the right hand with fingers spread signifies freedom, but the left with them compressed signifies tenacity and avarice. In the same way the form of the other parts of the body and instruments denoted something definite; and those who had observed these things by memory, long use, and meditation immediately recognized what they expressed. Petrus Crinitus also, in book [unclear], chapter 2, writes that this kind of method of writing existed among the Egyptians. Concerning the letters of the Egyptians which are called hieroglyphic, not only is there mention among the ancient authors, but this can still be observed on obelisks, on which those figures and images of different animals, herbs, rivers, and trees, sculpted there, designate the philosophy of that nation, as Strabo and Pliny, most weighty authors, testify. For on the obelisks of Kings Sesostris and Sennosornis, which were brought from Egypt to the city, the interpretation of the nature of things is marked by symbols of this sort and by hieroglyphic signs. For thus the philosophy of the Egyptians is believed to have first been illustrated, as is handed down by the same Pliny and by others. Among other images, they were accustomed to mark scarabs, bees, rivers, oxen, vultures, and other things of that kind. Moreover, from the very image of the bee the symbol of a king was signified, because in performing the duties of life he ought to have no less the sting of justice than the sweet clemency of honey. In the vulture they indicated the genius and majesty of nature, because among these birds only females are found, etc. They also painted many other signs in place of words; on these see Horapollo on hieroglyphic letters; Pliny, book 36, chapter 8, and also chapter 12, where, according to the more correct reading, “Sesostris” is found for “Sesotidis,” and “Nuncoreus Pharaoh” for [unclear]. Moreover, Strabo, book 17; Cornelius Tacitus, book 13; Coelius Rhodiginus, Ancient Readings, book 29, chapter 26; and Volaterranus, book 33, mention letters of this sort, although he writes that they have as yet been discovered by no one.
