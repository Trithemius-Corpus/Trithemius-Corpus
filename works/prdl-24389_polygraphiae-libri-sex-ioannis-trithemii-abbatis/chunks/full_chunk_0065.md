*The Second Book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapolis, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

The method of numbering mortals, by art imitating nature, admits no reckoning of the monad. Hence, although the arrangement of the preceding book of the Polygraphy, through the extension and metathesis of particulars, is, as it were, sufficient for the universals of this art in the infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a certain consideration has moved us to join one to the other. The condition of light is different in each; but night in both preserves inviolate the order of its course. The prudent reader, from these small inventions of ours, and not polished sufficiently to the exact rule, will easily be able, if he is strong in excellent talent, to discover greater and more polished ones. Whatever is visible, set within the whole circuit of the world, readily provides support for the service of this new art of ours. Air and fire, water and earth, springs and rivers, trees and fruits, flowers and grasses, words and names, accident and substance, stones and sand, dung and filth, things and terms, honorable and modest things, wood and metals, hard and liquid things, things existing and not existing, history and fable, beasts and brute animals, men and beasts of burden, each and all things which either speech can express or the soul can think, furnish a very wide field for our Polygraphy, and promise that, so far as it can be established among mortals, the art will be enlarged infinitely. For just as from unity, through the binary, the ternary generates a perfect number in the quaternary, so the consummation of this invention, through the quaternary, establishing its root in the twice-perfect binary, gives us an infinite number under the [unclear] of things and paraphrases of Polygraphy. For even if among us all our things are terminable, nevertheless many things often occur which, because of the slowness of our understanding, seem to us infinite. Who will be able to count the drops of the sea? And who will penetrate the inscrutable heart of man? Just as a finite and circumscribable number can, by progression, be led into infinity, so the arrangement of this art is enlarged to immensity by varied metathesis. Whatever is seen anywhere in the whole world is in every way conformed to our arrangement, nor is anything known to us which is devoid of mystery, if the method is understood from a master. But we, persuaded by reason, have halted under the service of the binary, and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor is occasion given to us to raise what has been begun to weightier matters; if it is offered to us acceptably, we shall not refuse to make better things manifest to those who desire them. For it is written: Where there is no hearing, never pour forth speech. Therefore, adding the second book of the Polygraphy to the first, we keep the same form of method: in the light of day, three hundred and eight paraphrases of the simple, which, if suitable time had been present, we could have given more elegantly, with sufficient broadening, wider in the open field. But studious posterity will easily provide both, adding brightness to the matter and sufficient progress to the form. The first inventor of any art transmits greater things to posterity, because, since the beginnings of an art are hard and unfamiliar, no subtle invention is found completed from its origin. From Peapolis, which is Herbipolis, a city of the Eastern Franks, on the twelfth day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord 1508.
