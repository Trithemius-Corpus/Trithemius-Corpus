*On the signs and wonderful, but excessively laborious, method of writing of M. T. Cicero, and after him of Saint Cyprian, bishop and martyr.*

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, wrote a book of signs of no small size, which Saint Cyprian, bishop of the Carthaginians and martyr, enlarged with many signs and words, adding terms necessary for Christian usage, so that the work itself became useful not only to pagans, but much more also to the faithful.

The codex is rare, and was found by me only once, and bought at a low price. For when, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1496, I was examining many libraries out of love for books, I found the aforesaid codex in a certain monastery of our order, neglected because of its great age, cast aside under dust and despised. I asked the abbot, a doctor of law, how much he valued it at. He replied that he would prefer to it the little works of Saint Anselm, recently printed. I went to the booksellers, since the matter took place in a metropolitan city; I bought the requested little works of Anselm for a sixth part of a florin, handed them to the rejoicing abbot and monks, and rescued the codex, now brought nearly to destruction. For they had resolved, for love of the parchment, [unclear].

But an inscription by someone ignorant of the mystery had been placed outside as follows: “Psalter in the Armenian language.” I called in a doctor, showed the falsity, and advised that it be rewritten: “Psalter written in Ciceronian signs.” Whether he did so or not, I am uncertain, since I did not afterward return to that library.
