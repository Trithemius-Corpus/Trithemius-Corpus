Now you have, in the hidden narration, “beware.” Proceed in sequence also through the other alphabets, and the discourse will come out as follows: “May the merciful Creator, discerning worldly things, impart to those who seek it seraphic delight with all his saints in heaven, amen. The gentleness of the most glorious prince, everywhere reverently to be remembered by the saints, urges us, most abject sleepers, by the best precepts, to forsake present things because of reverence for the director of good things, and with every affection, according to our powers, to await the glory of perpetual rest. Therefore, O vain mockers, receive instruction, so that you may be able, most happily adorned, to enter the most blessed rejoicings of the [unclear] rewarder of the orthodox. Rise therefore from the foulest honors of this pilgrimage, since you are about to enter most holily into the most blessed joys of the kindly Savior of the ages.” In this public narration set down above there is contained in secret this hidden sentence, namely: “Beware of that man, because he is a thief and the worst scoundrel, but be silent, I pray.” The same order must be followed in all other cases whatsoever; one must begin from the head as often as necessary, until you have enclosed the hidden narration of your secret clearly. But the words that are placed outside the order of the alphabet, since they designate no letter and do not serve the hidden narration, have been inserted because of a lack of synonyms, so that the sequence of Latin discourse might be completed. For we could not always have so many words with the same meaning, especially among pronouns, conjunctions, prepositions, and interjections, that we could have made an alphabet in place of twenty-four wandering letters, as is clearly evident after the eighth alphabet, where we placed two words outside the order, lest we leave the discourse defective. For thus we placed them, and thus: “with all.” Of these the writer may choose two, so that the Latin discourse may be completed. Let him do likewise in the others, whenever he finds similar cases. We have adopted a double M because of our native speech, in which we frequently need it in our German language. For one who is ignorant of Latin speech may write his hidden secret in German beneath a Latin discourse without any difficulty.

*Now follow the rules or cautions to be observed in the operation of this book.*

The first rule or caution warns you that the secret of your mind which you wish to announce to an absent friend, one who knows the art and has the book of *Polygraphy*, you should write on a sheet as carefully as you can, in Latin or German, or in any other language you wish that can be comprehended by Roman letters. Then place the sheet so written before you, so that for each single letter of the secret you can fit an appropriate word. For, as we have shown in this practice of writing, a whole word is taken. We gave an example a little earlier in what was set down above.

II. The second caution instructs you, as has been said, not to take more than one word at the same time from any alphabet in a single narration of public Latin discourse; otherwise you would both confuse the open sense and entirely draw away from understanding the secret the friend to whom you had written.

III. Third, one must take the utmost care not to skip through negligence any alphabet from which you do not take one word appropriate to the secret. Likewise provision must be made that, whenever one or more words occur outside the series of the alphabet, you take the whole upper line or the lower one, and not both.

IIII. Fourth, care must be taken that whenever the discourse of the open speech has been brought to a completed sentence, the following sentence should take its beginning from a larger, thickened letter, according to the form of the preceding example, in which, according to the sense of the Latin discourse, we have taken capital letters at the beginning and small ones in the rest, in the common manner. When the secret of your mind is lengthy, so that it exceeds the narration of the open discourse, if much remains, you should begin again from the beginning; if little remains, let the discourse be brought back at will from whatever place one likes. For no one even moderately skilled in this practice will be unable to know where you have begun again. Nor is it necessary to fear the consonan[unclear].
