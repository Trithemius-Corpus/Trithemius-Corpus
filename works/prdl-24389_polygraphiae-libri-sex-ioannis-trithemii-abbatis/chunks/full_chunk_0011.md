[They] are not devoid of the mystery of the art, because they serve luminous propositions, necessary for the consummation of the requisite perfection, not obscure ones. For this reason also they are taken up in the former and neglected in the latter. For they themselves are sure means by which every suspicion of opposition is removed. All things have been set down by reason, so that not even one proposition is introduced idly. By way of example: the farther the sun advances from the equinoctial circle, by so much are the days lengthened for those dwelling in those regions. Therefore, so that unity may advance in the right order, through the mediation of the ternary, into the binary, the paradigm which we hand down must be observed rather diligently. And first indeed let the operator, understanding his intention, after prior consideration, attend to what he is going to grant to the ternary through the binary; let him divide the day together with the night into minutes, and distinguish the minute portions of the winds, not by a foreign but by the proper supposition of the phrase. In any mystical business let him diligently beware lest he overturn the established order of the art, or substitute one paraphrasm for another, or grant to one what is owed to another; otherwise unity does not admit the binary, and the whole operation will be confused and useless. Let the night not wander in excessive prolixity, not advancing unstably beyond the limits established for it by the art; let curiosity not change water into snow, nor let him sleep by day who keeps vigil by night. Let the mind, solicitous in its talent, use art also, lest the hand by its own levity commit an error, which, though slight and small in the monad, is accustomed always to appear great and very large in the binary. One paraphrasm from each order is admitted only into the service of the secret; following its servant’s example, as often as a small particle of the light of day shall demand it, one phrase similarly adhering, not several, is taken up. These things must be entrusted to a watchful mind, and care must be taken lest the wind be stirred.

After these things a rule is prescribed for one approaching the practice of the art: that he imitate, as far as he can by the mystical art, the one person of man, consisting steadfastly of two natures, namely the soul surpassing visible things and the flesh remaining visible. For just as the soul is not seen, although it is established in a visible body, so the secret of the mind, in order that it may remain secret and nevertheless operate at pleasure, requires the guidance of light, while enduring in its workshop of shadows. Therefore as often as the body ministers to the soul, the servant to the master, the will to reason, see that the principal virtue of the power in the light of the paraphrasm is preserved in its natural vigor. For by this instruction the first of the Peripatetics will duly enter upon the second, and the monad will enter the binary. Consequently we likewise admonish the willing man that, as often as a leaf is moved by the wind, he should discern the property of each nature, compare the night itself with the day by degrees and minutes, and conform the shadows to the light. But if Phoebus should prejudice the night, or night the sun, through which a confusion of eclipse would arise, either let unity resume the binary which it dismissed in the light, or let the shadow of night be brought back to the first line of the sun.

Hold fixed in memory whatever is produced anywhere in number, weight, and measure. Therefore, when you have cut into the knot of the difficulty mentioned, number the minute portions of the night, note also the paraphrasms of the daylight, and so temper one from the other that the necessary proportion may follow the action. Beware lest, while the eclipse of the sun lasts, you interrupt the necessary labor. Attend no less that you be mindful of the ancient fathers, Tuisco, who, the grandson of Noah, is the father of the Teutons, and Marcomer, who is read to have been the first to lead the people of the Franks from the borders of Scythia into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine. For the Franks are brothers of the Greeks and, intermingled with the Germans, once made two languages from one. Hence, according to the oracle .g.gg., those who lost one g. take it up. You are warned, zealous investigator of the newest Polygraphy, that after, eager as you are for the water of these inventions, you have obtained the true mode of operating, you should guard all things prudently and not pour them out indiscriminately into the knowledge of all men, especially filthy ones. For this reason do not ask, since you know that there is nothing in the world so good and holy that the wicked do not rather often abuse it for evil.
