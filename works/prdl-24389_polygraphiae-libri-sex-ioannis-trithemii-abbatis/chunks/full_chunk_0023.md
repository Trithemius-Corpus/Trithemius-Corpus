In any word take only the second letter, and you will find this sense hidden: “Beware this man.” For the second letter in Ecorach is C, in hanafar A, in dulay V, in semalas E. Join these four letters and they will make this word: Cave. And so one proceeds through the rest to the end of the book; and if anything remains to be written of the hidden mystery, one must return to the beginning as often as necessary, until the secret has been wholly completed. In this most secret manner of writing, if anyone has been trained by long practice, he has no need of a book, but will easily compose on the spot words suited to his purpose. But he who has had less practice in composing necessarily requires a copy of the book from which he may write.

For the reader, a copy of the volume will not be necessary, since without the book he can easily find the hidden secret by means of the second letters everywhere. Yet in great, difficult, or dangerous matters, this manner of writing is not considered sufficiently safe, because by the ingenuity of a skilled man the hidden mystery might by chance be discovered. This manner of writing can also be altered by an intelligent man, so that the significant letters are placed in the third or fourth position of the word, with the words changed.

*Brief explanation of the fifth book of our Polygraphia.*

In the fifth book of the Polygraphia we have handed down various ways of writing secretly by transposition of the common letters, arranged in such an order that under their covering we shall be able to write and report everything safely to an absent friend. Now, to speak in our own manner, the progression of the transposition of letters is almost infinite, since from one alphabet by metathesis innumerable alphabets arise. First we described a straight table containing twenty-four alphabets; through understanding it, as many alphabets can be composed as there are stars counted in the firmament of heaven. For as many letters as there are in that table, so many times there arise by the art, in order, one million alphabets. After this we set out a reversed table, which will arise into another as often as you change the first letter at the head.

All the synthemata of this book are sufficiently clear and manifest, and therefore they need no explanation, not even a brief one. Yet those things which have been newly invented by us require a definite declaration, lest perhaps they be judged to have been set down without reason. First of all, we made all the letters of the Latins numerical, after the manner of the Hebrews and Greeks, considering it shameful for us if we should be found inferior to them in this respect. For what if we have doubled certain letters so that they might suffice for an infinite number, since the Jews, compelled by the same necessity, did the very same thing long ago? And the Greeks, because of the lack of letters, admitted certain signs into the number, since they were unable to double the letters. But we, because we are pressed by an equal lack of letters, admit two signs: namely this &, which our writers put for et; nor does it matter whether it is written thus, &, or thus, et, provided it represents six in the number. We have added another, 9, which our writers, when abbreviating, write indifferently for con and com; and here in the order of numbers it represents six hundred. By the same necessity we have doubled two letters, namely r, z, and s: of these r means ninety, but curved z means one hundred, long s two hundred, and closed s three hundred. Further, doubled w, which because of the character of the German language we have placed in the order of the alphabet, represents the last letter, and in the series of numbers one thousand.
