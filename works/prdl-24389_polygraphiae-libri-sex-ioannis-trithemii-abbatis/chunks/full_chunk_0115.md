No Greek could understand this method of writing, since, even if all those characters are Greek, nevertheless, because they represent Latin letters according to numerical order, they cannot be read either by a Greek or by a Latin who has not first acquired knowledge of this device. This mode of writing can also be varied in many ways, when by [metathesis?] of the number the letters too are changed. This can also be done with Greek letters [alone?], at the wish and discretion of any industrious writer. Nor will the manner of forming these characters be very difficult, especially for one who has had some practice. The characters set forth above also suit the German language sufficiently well, since with them we can denote all things that occur to us, in whatever speech we are skilled. Whenever iota is placed by itself, so that it may be known to denote the letter l, the tenth of the alphabet, it is marked above thus: ī.

*On the Alphabet and Method of Writing of the Ancient Franks*

It is the opinion of very many that the Germans once had letters from the Greeks, and they firmly affirm that they also used the Greek language, especially in their sacred rites. For they say [unclear] great [unclear] between the Greek language and ours has utterly destroyed [unclear]. That most learned man, once my teacher, Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, the most reverend bishop of the church of Worms, had collected several thousand Greek words which have the same meaning in both languages, Greek and German. And Caesar testifies in his Commentaries that the ancient Druids in Gaul, priests of the sacred rites, used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. But when the Romans were laying waste the world, they at last violently imposed the use of their own letters, together with their dominion, upon many nations. Finally, the remnants of the Trojans, who were afterwards called Franks, in the four hundred and fortieth year of the incarnation, went out from the borders of the Scythians, King Marcomer providing leadership, came into Germany, and received settlements from their Saxon brothers at the mouths of the Rhine. Thereafter, for nine hundred and fifty years they waged an almost continual war with the Romans and the Gauls and, as Hunibald is the authority, finally brought almost all Europe under their empire or kingdom. Yet they did not abolish the letters of the Romans once they had received them, but rather, being themselves mingled among the Germans and Gauls, learned their language, and preserved their letters and the form of their writings. The writer of the histories of this people, Wasthald, wrote in his native tongue books of the deeds of kings, dukes, and princes over almost seven hundred and fifty-eight years, with the characters appended, as Hunibald says, which he mentions in his volume that he published in eighteen books on the origin of the Franks.

Unless a fault of the scribe has deceived me, I have represented Wasthald’s alphabet suitably, as I think; in it some characters differ altogether from the Greek, and some also seem partly to resemble them. Yet I do not know what to judge: whoever their inventor may have been, although he did not keep the form of the Greek letters, he observed the order of the letters. The codex, falling apart from excessive age, could scarcely be read; hence I fear that I have been deceived by some interpolator rather than found the true and certain opinion of Hunibald.

But let us pass on to what remains, since, if Hunibald’s statement is true, it will have been well written by us; but if it is the fiction of someone, it will neither harm us nor wrong the reader. There follows another fictitious alphabet found in the codex of Hunibald, to which this was inscribed:

*The Alphabet of the Franks, Which Doracus Invented*

Whoever wishes to use the characters set forth above should know that they are to be written beneath the lines, in the manner of the Greeks, not above as the Latins do. Likewise it must be observed that, of the double letters, the first are written at the beginning and in the middle of words, but the second always at the end.

*From the Grammar of Otfrid, Monk of Weissenburg*

Leginus [says] that Charles, king of the Franks, surnamed the Great, taking pity on the barbarism of his nation, attempted, with Nanno, Theobald, Albinus, and Berengar as helpers, to regulate the German language. And although, as Turpin testifies, he had begun so arduous a work, he did not complete it, being delayed by frequent incursions of wars, but, overtaken by death, left it unfinished; nor after him did another arise who would call back to mind the care of this matter.
