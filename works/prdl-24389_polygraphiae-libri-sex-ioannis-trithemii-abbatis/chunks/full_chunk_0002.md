## Dedication to Emperor Maximilian

*The letter of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James of Würzburg, on the Polygraphia*

I was hesitating, wavering in mind, most invincible Maximilian Caesar, and doubting somewhat within myself whether or not I should be doing something worthwhile, were I to dedicate the volume of our Polygraphy to your Caesarean majesty. For among the great [you are] the greatest, among the more powerful the most powerful, among all the wise by far the wisest—to whom, therefore, nothing ought to be inscribed except what is great, nothing dedicated except what is elaborated with the highest erudition. But I, having attained little or nothing of literary erudition, not without reason hesitated the longer whether I ought to bring my trifles before so great a majesty: since I am neither suited in talent to elaborate anything, nor adorned with the necessary charm of speech. For I know, and cannot deny, how scanty is my furniture, how rude my talent, and that I have no vein of eloquence in speaking—on account of which, with just consideration, I held back for some time, and dreaded, an inept writer, to appear before your glorious majesty. For since after God you are by right lord of the world,

it is no wonder if I, unlearned and the least of monks, should fear the censure of so great a majesty as yours, and dread to offer those lucubrations of mine which I know to be unworthy of your hands. And while I was revolving these things in my mind, there came a messenger with letters sent from your highness, by which you summoned me to Cologne, the expenses necessary for the journey having already some time before been appointed through another. But when I had read over the letters of your serenity, confidence soon conquered my bashfulness, and the consideration of your innate gentleness put my fear to flight. For I know your nature to be most mild—you who, since you are the most modest of all, are wont both to grow gentle toward the humble and to resist the proud. You know not how to be vainly exalted, nor, in the manner of tyrants, to despise the humble who are devoted to your highness; for just as you have nothing by which to grow on earth, so no envy will penetrate your mind. You are the greatest; many and great things, too, you have accomplished no less bravely than wisely, both in peace and in war, by which you have both won an eternal name and are celebrated with the reverence of the highest honor, the affection and favor of all your subjects. These are the riches of princes: the love and favor of the people, and to do justice to each according to the precept of the charity of God. Since, therefore, all bear your name to the stars with praises, and all proclaim you most mild and most humane, why should I, singularly, suspect you to be more severe [toward me]?
