Finally, how, without letters, you may disclose the secret of your mind to someone absent, even without the knowledge of the very person who performs the office of messenger, remains ascribed to the inventions of steganography. For indeed, since kings and princes encounter many affairs about which, at times, they do not trust that knowledge can be given safely enough by customary letters to their deputies far away, it will be worth the effort for each of them to have certain special methods of writing and sending messages, by which they may most securely make their secrets known to friends, especially in these times, in which, with license to sin prevailing, trustworthy faith is found nowhere in the world. Everywhere the messengers of princes are seized by savage robbers, stripped, and very often killed; nor do the impious plunderers spare even the letters of kings sealed and closed with rings. Hence it would be necessary for your commonwealth, O most glorious Emperor Maximilian, that you put on the skin of Hercules, brandish the club in your hands, and purge for yourself, by God’s help, the world entrusted to you of these savage monsters. There will never be peace in the kingdom unless the [unclear] are removed from its midst. He who disturbs the peace of the kingdom shows himself to be the king’s enemy. These are the robbers who beset the roads of travelers, seize others’ goods; with them the earth today is full, and nowhere is the road safe for those walking.

*A Brief Explanation of Those Things Which Seem More Obscure in the First Book of the Polygraphia*

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphia, nevertheless, lest its composition should perhaps seem profound to the simpler sort, we briefly explain those things that are thought more difficult. We have distributed the words of the Latin language, in order, nine thousand in number, under nearly four hundred alphabets, arranged in such sequence that if from any alphabet one word only is taken after another, from the beginning to the end, they will cohere with one another and produce a fitting Latin discourse. For it will be a very suitable method of writing most secretly whatever we wish, since each word represents the letter set before it at its head. And let it not trouble you, reader, that a multitude of words in the open text contains a brief sense in the hidden one, since in this childish arrangement of ours there lies concealed a higher method of writing whatever you wish, more broadly and much more secretly, which public reason has forbidden us to make open to all. But whoever has been endowed with intelligence and has applied the necessary powers to the investigation of the doctrines hidden in these things will discover subtler matters, and things marvelously unknown before; plainly he will understand more marvelous things. Nevertheless, this open method of writing will suffice for all purposes, since you can repeat it as often as you wish, and the repeated narration will always be varied. For if, as we have said, you take one word from the first alphabet, likewise one from the second, and so consequently one from each, and one from those placed between, outside the ordered line, up to the period or the end of the book, a Latin and sufficiently fitting discourse will arise for you. But care must be taken that at one time, together and once for all, no more than one word be taken from one alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, not skip over any alphabet and confuse the order of the narration. By “day” understand the open narration; by “night,” however, the hidden and secret one, which should not be too long but brief. The examples that follow in the text, concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen, pertain to both narrations, the hidden and the manifest. “Whenever a leaf is moved by the wind,” and the rest: we advise that you count the secret narration in relation to the open one in such a way that both at once end at the foot of each period. But if anything remains over from the secret matter, let it be enlarged with such moderation that both narrations end at one point, lest a confusion of eclipse be produced. The other things that follow in the first Polygraphic book are implicitly referred to the document of explanation and to all the things that we have said.
