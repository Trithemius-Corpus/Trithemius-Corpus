The visible multitude of things is known to be great, but invisible things remain remote from us; if these latter are prudently fitted to the former, the enlightened mind of man would know and behold wonders. But to treat further of these matters is not part of the present purpose; since they are greater and weightier, they require another work and another time.

Matthias, the most powerful king of Hungary and, harder than adamant, the hammer of the Turks, who in our age fought many victorious battles against the enemies of the cross of Christ, discovered many things by wondrous talent and art, adorned owls with circumlocutions, kept his own mysteries secret, concealed by manifold variety, and by manifest diligence destroyed the counsels of his enemies. He preferred wisdom to gold; therefore he always triumphed as a glorious victor. In a wise man there is a sound mind in a well-ordered body; when, borne upward by desire for higher understanding, you have returned through the likeness of the binary from the ternary into unity, thereafter it will now easily rise to the denary. Pythagoras of Samos, that philosopher, warned his disciples not to forget the quaternary, in which the first perfection of numbering is comprised. For thus we number: one, two, three, four. Halt your mind, for you have ten. The denary is the head of perfection. What are eleven, except ten and unity? What is twenty? Twice ten. What is forty? Four times ten. You have a hundred, and you have by no means departed from the quaternary. And what are a hundred, except ten times ten? Deep mysteries lie hidden in these things, and they are manifest to few.

For love of brevity we shall pass over many in silence, who, aiding nature by art, applied prudence to secret matters, and what seemed impossible to many who did not know, by keen study brought into easy use. A cultivated mind can do great things, and he who cleaves to God becomes, in understanding, higher than the world. There are many mysteries of kings, and counsels full of cares, in which trustworthiness and fidelity are required; when rare, this harms the believer, and even when doubt is present, it admits more often. Therefore it would be worthwhile for all princes to be philosophers, endowed with singular wisdom, just, gentle, benevolent, and distinguished for munificence, so that they themselves should neither wish to deceive others nor easily be able to be overthrown by others. Justice alone, the companion of prudence, makes a prince glorious.

At the urging of the aforesaid prince and lord elector Philip, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, I, Johannes Trithemius, now abbot of the monastery of Saint James at Wurzburg, but then of Saint Martin the bishop in Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, began to write eight books of mysteries in steganography. But while I was already continuing my nocturnal labor on the third book of the same work, an event occurred which, if I can, I am going to recount briefly. In the year of the Lord's nativity one thousand four hundred [ninety-]nine, in the second Roman indiction, when Arnold Bostius, a Carmelite of the town of Ghent, asked by letter what new thing I was bringing forth, or with what studies I was occupied, on the day after Palm Sunday I wrote back through merchants, as to a friend, that I had under the hammer a new and wondrous work of steganography, and I disclosed in order what was contained in each of the books. But before my letter had reached Ghent, Bostius, in the days of Easter, departed from life to Christ. The prior of that place received the letter, opened it, read it, and, astonished, as I think, by the novelty of the things promised, shared it with many others who asked to read and copy it.

From this it happened that my letter itself, made known in a short time throughout almost all Germany and Gaul, and often copied by many, also turned very learned men to amazement and admiration, to such a degree that they hesitated what they ought truly to think of me. Among these there were some who steadfastly asserted that the promises I made in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, and had been shamelessly devised by me for the sake of empty glory. Others, however, said: “This abbot Trithemius promises great and wondrous things; if he can do them, he can do so only by the ministry of demons, since they seem by far to exceed the bounds of nature. But if he cannot do them, who will doubt that he is a liar, and deservedly to be refuted by all good and learned men?” But let both be far from me, since I promised natural things.
