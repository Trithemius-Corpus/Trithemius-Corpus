And when, as is usual among friends, especially those devoted to letters, I had shown all my things to my most welcome guest for him to see, among the rest the aforementioned work of Steganography, not yet completed at that time, also presented itself. Bovillus saw it and read it in passing, with his mind occupied elsewhere; he said that he wondered at the invention, praised it, and took no care to understand in what manner it was to be understood. Hence, since he did not ask for the key of understanding, he deserved to hear or perceive nothing of what was contained in that lucubration of ours. After this, having returned to France, he repaid us evil for good and shamefully violated the bond of Christian friendship. For when he was asked by Lord Germain de Ganay, a common friend, later bishop of Orleans, what he had found with me and seen at Spanheim, he wrote back falsehoods as truths and lies in return for benefits, condemning and rashly judging things that he was quite unable to comprehend with his understanding. For since, through envy and falsehood, he believed that the palm of learning would be conferred upon him, he wrote back to the aforementioned most learned man a letter full of falsity, lies, injuries, and many insults, in which, making mention of the Steganography that he had not understood, he falsely, mendaciously, and very injuriously proclaims me, by rash presumption, to be given over to wicked arts, a magician and necromancer. To his most mendacious injuries and blasphemies, God having mercy, the brevity of letters will respond, so that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bovillus is impious, cruel, rash, and a liar in this matter. I affirm steadfastly, I speak truthfully, and I swear confidently upon my soul that I have never had any commerce with demons, or with wicked or pernicious, magical or necromantic arts; but that all and every thing that I have either written or promised that I would write are pure, sound, natural, and in no respect at all contrary to the Christian faith. Hence I do not fear to give my promised things for examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as there shall be need, and I do not in any way intend to resist their determination. The reason why I inserted this history of Bovillus's rashness into this preface of mine was reasonable, so that everyone may understand that it was done by mature counsel that the work of Steganography has hitherto remained buried in darkness, not because I fear the rash judgments of Bovillus, but lest I seem to give occasion to many similar persons to suspect similar things of me. Indeed, as far as I am concerned, I would rather placate my rivals by silence than irritate them by letters or provoke them to madness by writings. For I judged it better to condemn all wonderfully invented things to perpetual silence than, even by the false opinion of men, to incur the mark of magical or necromantic pernicious superstition. Therefore let Steganography lie hidden in darkness, and let it not become common to the lying fellowship of Bovillus, which is accustomed to judge matters unknown to it and to deprave the reputation of a good man by the mere lust of depravity. Nevertheless, at the urging of friends, so that our rivals may understand, together with Bovillus, how rash and lying an injury he has done us, while the Steganography remains within its own bounds, we consent to publish the following work, which we call Polygraphy, divided into six books. Let those who wish read it, and let friends read this volume without resentment; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves be judges, without envy, whether they are natural or superstitious. For I know and am certain that all things are sincere, pure, natural, and far removed from every pursuit of evil superstition. Whenever, however, the discourse is rather obscure, this is done for the reason that a madman should not abuse to destruction the sword of defense which a man sound in mind and intellect carries for his own protection. Yet nothing evil is contained in it on that account, since perverse men abuse even good things for evil. But whoever presumes to condemn this edition of ours is himself conscious and a witness to himself that he has never truly understood it. Mysteries are concealed under enigmas, lest the secrets of [unclear] should in any way be made manifest to owls. If it delights anyone to know these things, let the reader approach with envy put far away. For I am a Christian, I am a priest, and a monk under the rule of the divine father Benedict; I love Christ, and with whatever sincerity of mind I can I always devoutly adore him.
