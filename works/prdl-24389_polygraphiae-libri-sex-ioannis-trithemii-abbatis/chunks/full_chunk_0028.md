Then, on a parchment placed beneath the openings, you will write that sentence, and likewise on another and another; finally fill the spaces of the first parchment, completing and deleting sentences until the sense holds together. You will complete this second sentence on the second parchment in such a way that the words and sentences seem to cohere; on the third also you will fit it so that, without injury to the first letters, the whole sentence, the number and size of the words, agree and retain a certain elegance. When this has been done, place over a parchment of equal size the pattern that you cut out, and at the edges of the openings mark the very finest points, as far as the letters extend which you wish to write. Then take a third parchment, and [write] the letter in continuous words, with a suitable order of spaces and also with suitable size of letters, so that the first sentence and its words are contained within the limits of the points. Therefore no suspicion of deception then remains; when the other man receives it, with the pattern laid over it, he at once understands the thought of your mind. And for this purpose, although it is no light labor, nothing comparable can be found for indicating one’s thought to friends in dangerous times.

*Marcus Tullius.* The account is that Cicero devised individual and special characters for each Latin word, by which, if necessity demanded, he wrote a speech however long both with very few signs and most secretly. And in this way, they say, Cato’s speech, that the conspirators should be punished, was preserved and committed to writing, when Cicero as consul appointed very swift writers who would take down what was said in notes of this kind. That the ancients also, in writing out words quickly and briefly, followed a somewhat similar method, so that frequently a single letter signified a word, is attested by Valerius Probus, Petrus Crinitus, Angelus Decembrius, and likewise by the very ancient monuments most diligently collected by Petrus Appianus and Bartholomaeus Amantius. But this craft of writing will be treated more diligently hereafter.

*Our Bede in England. On computation and the speech of the fingers.* These things I find in Bede’s *De temporum ratione*. When you say one, bending the little finger on the left hand, you will set its joint in the middle of the palm. When you say two, you will place there the second finger from the little finger, bent. When you say three, you will similarly bend the third. When you say four, likewise you will raise the little finger. When you say five, you will similarly raise the second from the little finger. When you say six, you will nevertheless raise the third, with only the finger called the medicus fixed in the middle of the palm. When you say seven, you will place the little finger alone, the rest raised, upon the base of the palm. Next to this, when you say eight, you will set the medicus. When you say nine, you will set the impudicus opposite it. When you say ten, you will set the nail of the index finger in the middle joint of the thumb. When you say twenty, you will put the tip of the thumb between the middle joints of the index finger and the impudicus. When you say thirty, you will join the nails of the index finger and thumb in a gentle embrace. When you say forty, you will draw the inner parts of the thumb over the side or back of the index finger, with only those two raised. When you say fifty, you will bend the thumb at the outer joint, in the manner of the Greek letter called gamma, and incline it toward the palm. When you say sixty, you will carefully gird from the front the thumb, bent as above, with the index finger curved round it. When you say seventy, you will fill the index finger, curved round as above, with the thumb stretched lengthwise, with only its nail raised across the middle joint of the index finger. When you say eighty, you will make the index finger, curved round as above, with the thumb held lengthwise, on the left hand: two hundred on the right, just as [unclear] ten on the left [unclear]. But one hundred on the right, just as [unclear] on the left. Likewise one thousand on the right, just as one on the left. Two thousand on the right, just as two on the left. Three thousand on the right, with only the fingers raised toward the neck. When you say twenty thousand, you will lay the same [hand], spread broadly, over the breast. When you say thirty thousand, with the same [hand] prone but upright, you will put the thumb into the cartilage of the middle of the breast.
