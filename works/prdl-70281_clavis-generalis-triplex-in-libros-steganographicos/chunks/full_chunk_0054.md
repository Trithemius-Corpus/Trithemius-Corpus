For unless the practitioner of the art has carefully observed all these things, he will not be able to make progress, nor to arrive at the effect of his intention. Again, let him attend carefully when each spirit is to be called for which duties or secrets, and in what quarter of the world he dwells, lest he err either in the places or in the [names?] of the several ones whom he wishes to call.

Likewise, let everyone who is to operate in this science be advised to attend most diligently to pronouncing the words of the conjurations properly, for in them lies the great force of our operation; since, when an error is committed in the conjurations, the spirits called, or to be called, not only do not obey, but even wholly resist. Consequently, let him also be forewarned not to call one for another, but each one in his own order, time, and office, just as has been sufficiently stated by us in nearly all the chapters of this book. For if, either through error or through any other negligence, he calls one for another, he will make no progress in his operation, but will bring danger upon himself.

Likewise, in the conjurations the spirit who is called should be expressed by name together with his substitutes; the cause of the calling should first be fixed in the intention; nor should the act of operation be performed in light, profane matters, which one person can otherwise communicate safely to another without danger, whether by letters or by messengers. For this art is to be used only for announcing great and difficult matters which, if made public, would bring loss or danger upon the operator. Likewise, it is necessary for everyone operating in this art to know the natures of the spirits: which are good, which evil, which ready and well-disposed to obedience, which harsh and rebellious. Again, which preside over nocturnal operations, and which over diurnal ones; because unless he has known the natures of the spirits before he begins to operate, he will make progress with the greatest difficulty and will easily be turned to stupor. Likewise, when the spirits called appear visibly before the operator, after the secret word has been sent ahead, let him entrust the secret of his mind to the duke or count in appropriate words, under silence; for there is no need to speak to the spirit in a raised voice, but let him use a low one.

Care must also be taken with the greatest diligence, both by the operator and by the recipient, that they do not perform anything pertaining to Steganography in the presence of other men ignorant of this art, when they can have access to a secret place. But if they cannot be alone, and it happens that they are compelled to operate among others, let them act so secretly, so prudently, and with such industry that none of those sitting around understands anything about the presence of the spirits. For all the spirits assigned to the ministry of this art are of such a nature and condition that they utterly detest and flee the tumult of men, and hate a public gathering. Likewise, the operator must also take care not to direct any spirit anywhere without letters, or at least without the character of his emperor; for when he does not see that very character impressed, he utterly refuses to obey the one calling him and to carry the secret to anyone. We send letters for a twofold reason, although we could announce the secret without letters through the spirit alone: namely, so that we may preserve from suspicion the men carrying the character, and so that, by that very character, we may constrain the spirit bound to obey the friend.

*Preface.*

In the preceding book, relying on the help of the highest God, we arranged in order, not without great labor, the thirty-one supreme spirits, emperors of our art, together with their dukes, counts, and servants necessary for us in operation. Although their ministries are abundantly sufficient for every operator for the announcement of all secrets, however secret, still, lest we seem to omit anything that can conduce to the perfection of this art, we have resolved to join a second book to the first.
