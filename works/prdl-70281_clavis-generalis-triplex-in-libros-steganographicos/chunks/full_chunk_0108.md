*The hidden content of the aforesaid.*

I beg your Grace: keep the art secret until the book is made; I have found still wondrous things for it.

*Mode XVI. It agrees with the preceding mode in its procedure, but, on the contrary, always begins from the vacant words.*

The third mode is opposed to the preceding one as regards the beginning, because that one begins its first narrative from significant words. This one, however, begins from idle words, so that the first word is always empty, but the second significant; and so an ascent is made from unity up to the octave, from which again there is a descent down to unity, and this as often as the proposed narrative is finished. Let us fashion the following example of this kind.

*Example XVI.*

One ascends to eternal life by virtues alone, since those given over to vices have by no means been judged worthy of the kingdom of God, and their pride has been repelled; they are zealous for the iniquity of demons, and they will always be disturbed by zeal [unclear], and the worms of impiety will gnaw them. Their consciences will never rest for eternity, but, blinded, they will be punished with the greatest torments, with everlasting oblivion. In hell there is no redemption, but perpetual weeping and unceasing groaning and a fire never to be extinguished. There are darkness and everlasting night, fear and very grievous misery, cold and chill; there will be no degree, for always to lack perpetual goods, to endure burning or cold or [zeal?], which is blameworthy. For carnal pleasure is very brief; in exchange for its object, wretched men are increased by the greatest miseries, [unclear]; but the salvation of our people remains continually [unclear]. Farewell.

*The hidden content of the aforesaid.*

When this man comes to you, put him in prison until my arrival, etc. And so with the rest as much as one pleases.

*Mode XVII. It imitates the first mode through the individual words, the letters always being in the preceding one.*

The seventeenth mode, after the pattern of the first, begins with the first letters through the individual words, but arranges the letters of the alphabet artfully, so that for a it takes b, for b it takes c, and so consequently up to the end of the alphabet; moreover, for z it takes A. And when you wish to write in such a mode, so that you may the more easily be able to follow the alphabet, consider its order by nature.

A b c d e f g h k l m n o p q r s t u x z.

b c d e f g h i k l m n o p q R S T U X Z A.

For A, b, the immediately following letter, is taken; for B, C; for D, D [unclear]; and so consequently, as is clear in the example.
