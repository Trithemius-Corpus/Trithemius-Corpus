When the charm has been duly completed, and in silence, the summoned spirit will be present. Entrust the secret securely; he will carry out and report back what is to be entrusted. But if you see that those presiding over nocturnal operations, as they are generally accustomed to do, are delaying, compel them manfully by repeating the charm, fearing nothing; for they will be forced to reverence you as their lord.

I have a secret that I entrust to no one to carry, of this sort.

I posit the case. I have a certain friend, noble and learned, whom I know is to be killed secretly by certain assassins hired for pay. I do not dare warn him openly, because it is dangerous for me; in a more private family matter, I fear lest letters come into the hands of others; I do not presume to trust a man to speak it by mouth, because the one who is with me today may perhaps stand against me tomorrow. And so I take refuge in the customary art, by which, whatever I entrust to it, I do not fear will become public, even if it should happen to be tried by an enemy.

I send familiar letters to my friend, in which I fear no danger.

The ancients define Justice and Philosophy as the greatest ornament of nobility, and Impiety and Ignorance as its disgrace. For what is more beautiful than to adorn a noble family, which serves as a soldier, with letters and virtues? We find that very many others, and numerous very noble men serving as soldiers, did this by night and by day, so that they might subdue barbarians by arms, and by uprightness instruct their people and with letters. By their example, [Buma vv?], most learned, [unclear] the letters of humanity and the good arts [unclear], acknowledged, adorned with arms and letters. The wisest princes of old were accustomed not to shut up their wealth in their treasuries, but rather to devote it to literary use, judging the study of wisdom to be the most honorable support for those tending toward happiness, by which the blessedness of eternal retribution is acquired. Indeed, knowledge of the Scriptures makes princes venerable to all, and especially to be feared. Ignorance of the Scriptures, called the burial of a living man, has been accustomed to bring shame and horrible calamity upon noble princes. A man without letters is a two-footed ass, to be buried alive, or rather already buried. It is especially necessary that nobles who govern the commonwealth be wise and notable for knowledge of letters; their ignorance brings in tyranny. Love of letters also commends the renowned deeds of princes and nobles to perpetuity. For we see that ancient philosophers obtained the memory of rule, and attained immortality of name, everlasting among all men more by letters than by arms. For letters made the glorious, excellent, and wise ancient tyrants and princes more so than arms did, and endowed the memory and titles of their praises with immortality. Add that, for Christians, the study of letters provides the highest and most honorable aid for flying to Heaven.
