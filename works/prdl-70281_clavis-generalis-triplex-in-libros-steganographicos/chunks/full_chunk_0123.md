54 *Key of Steganography*

*Chapter II*

*On Other Methods of Writing Secretly, Which Are Contained Above.*

In the preceding chapter of this first book, Most Serene Prince, forty methods of our art of Steganography were arranged in order. Although their abundance can suffice by itself for writing anything whatever secretly, nevertheless, in order that we may show students the way of [unclear] greater things, I think it worthwhile also to explain other little methods of writing by example. Therefore, just as in the preceding chapter, among the other twenty-two methods, we set down the last by transposition of the letters of the alphabet into the following ones in order, so now, according to one alphabet of twenty-two letters, necessary to us alone, let us, in each individual method, anticipate the significant letters into the preceding one. In this anticipation the first method of this chapter will agree with the last of the preceding chapter, the second with the penultimate, and so consequently down to the last of this chapter, which will be the first of the chapter, varying the method of the corresponding transposition.

*The First Method of This Second Chapter Uses Individual Significant Letters, and Each Letter Is to Be Taken in One [word].*

The first method of this second [chapter] uses individual significant words, and the first letter of any word whatsoever is immediately transferred into the preceding one. Thus this preceding first one is to be taken. But you must draw the alphabet in a circle, both in this method and in all the following ones in which letters are transposed, so that here z is taken for A, a for b, b for c, as is clear in the alphabet by the circle.

This method, however, like all the rest, can be varied in many ways: by alternate, double, triple, quadruple, or more [letters], at the writer’s pleasure, yet in such a way that suitable signs [are assigned] to each one; otherwise even he himself can easily forget what he has written, and no one will perceive his intention. Now, however, with the letters placed beneath, mark how much each letter is away, h2, which is the first in the alphabet of all languages; but now let us proceed to explain this method, with rather shorter sentences, as we seem to have used above.

*Example of the First.*

Time passes; let us strive for [zeal?], sincerely choosing light things humbly with our minds, gathering our daily errors. The time for gathering is at hand; let us cleanse the shadows of impulses, let us leave this age, fleeing the torpor of sloth; let us hold fast the zeal of Catholic holiness. Amen. Johannes Trithemius, abbot, wrote this on the sixth day before the Kalends of March, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1500.

*The Second Method Uses Alternate Words, and the Letters of the Significant Words Are Transferred into the Second Preceding One.*

The second method of this chapter, beginning with one vacant word, uses alternate words, the idle and the significant being set forth in the order of the narrative; and the first letter of each significant word is anticipated, transferred immediately into the second preceding one, so that x is taken for A, z for b, a for c, and so consequently, as is clear in the round table placed here, and in the plain alphabet which we have set down for the easier manner of writing. The red letters, however, are our customary alphabet, from which each of them, in this method of writing, being mystically superimposed upon the black [letter] in the round [table], is taken for the proposed narrative.
