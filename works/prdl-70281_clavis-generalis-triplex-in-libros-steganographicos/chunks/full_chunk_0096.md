*Preface of Johannes Trithemius*

It is the opinion of the wise that the ancient philosophers of art and nature, who had discovered secrets, concealed them by various methods and figures, so that they might not come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews also confirm that Moses, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his description of the creation of heaven and earth, disclosed ineffable mysteries of secrets in simple words. Saint Jerome, most learned among our own, says that in the Apocalypse almost as many mysteries lie hidden as there are words. Moreover, the wise men of the Greeks, of no small esteem among their own people, who devoted their effort to writing fables, with most prudent reasoning handed down one thing to the unlearned and another to the wise in the course of a single narration. I cannot but admire these most zealous lovers of wisdom, and I do not fail to read them with whatever industry I can, so that, while I consider that they labored by lamplight more greatly than other men, I may, as best I can, fill the empty belly of my memory with certain crumbs, as it were, of knowledge falling from the table of the rich philosophers.

Nor, as I think, has this opinion altogether deceived me, since by the continual labor of reading I have learned many things that I did not know, and in various ways I have reserved an approach for my thoughts toward investigating greater secrets of things. Although, indeed, I am not of such great learning or industry that I would dare profess that I have grasped that secret method of the ancient sages which they used in concealing the majesty of philosophy, nevertheless I have devised certain methods, not to be despised, as it seems to me, of writing altogether secretly and without any suspicion, and of concealing whatever you wish. At the urging of the most serene prince, lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and Prince-Elector of the Roman Empire, the wisest patron of philosophers, than whom no one seemed to me more worthy, I committed these methods to writing and gathered them into the following volume, not without enormous labor.

But lest this great secret come to the ears of the unlearned or of wicked men, whoever you may be as reader, I warn, ask, and adjure you by the ineffable name of God, JEHOVAH, and by the blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, savior of faithful souls, that you disclose this science of writing to no one unworthy, immodest, carnal, or maliciously old, since through it many things could be done among men: adulteries, fornications, conspiracies, murders, and betrayals, if, which may it not happen, it were made known. For just as a good and honorable man, as often as it is necessary, can safely and without any suspicion from anyone make known and express the secret of his will to another person who knows the art, for private or common advantage, so a perverse man, immodest and blinded by carnal love, can signify to a consenting person of his desire, however shamelessly, foully, and lustfully, his evil desire, by honorable, modest, and holy words, determining the place, time, and manner of meeting, as broadly as he can, without any suspicion. Nor will there henceforth be any security among married people, while a woman can very easily understand the evil will of her lover from the best writings, with her husband looking on and praising the most holy words, without any suspicion of evil; and whatever you wish your mistress to know, however ignorant she may be of Latin words, she can send back in reply, with her husband marveling and, as is possible, carrying the message. Indeed, if this science were to come to the knowledge of the commonwealth, the whole order would be disturbed, public trust would perish, letters, documents, all writings, and the very conversations of men would be turned into the gravest suspicion.
