Now you have, out of the 400 dukes of Demoriel, 12 named, and from the subdukes, counts, and servants, 4,560 in number, who, together with these dukes, according to the order assigned in the 24 hours of day and night, which you must know before all things, perform their parts. Therefore, when you wish to operate in Steganography through one of these dukes, turn yourself to the North, as is customary, and, after first doing what is required for the art, say the conjuration.

*The Song of Conjuration.*

DEmoriel onear dabursoy Cohyne chamerson ymeor pean olayr chelrusys noeles schemlaryn venodru patron myselrochadarbon veuaon maferos ratigiel personay lodiol camedõnasiel fabelmerusin sosiel chamarchoysyn. When the song has been completed, the Spirit, ready for all things, obedient and most prepared, will appear to you visibly; to him you may safely entrust the secret to be carried through.

Let there be for me such a secret, or any other whatsoever, to be communicated.

I have a very close friend, absent far away at this time, to whom I have a most secret matter, which cannot safely be entrusted to anyone at all, not even to letters. Therefore, so that it may remain secret, I always choose to summon some Spirit familiar to me from the art. I entrust the secret: he comes, he goes, he acts faithfully, he carries to my friend what I have entrusted; we are both safe. He himself also brings back to me from my friend the secret reply, which he will tell to no other mortal. But in order that the friend himself may understand that the Spirit has been sent to him, it is necessary that letters of whatever kind, fearing nothing, be devised, in which he may recognize the sign of the spirit that has been sent. For the Spirit does not appear unless compelled.

Let us put first whatever letters one pleases, which need fear the sight of no one.

Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Jakob Wimpfeling of Schlettstadt, theologian, poet, and most eloquent orator, greetings.

I would very much wish, Jakob Wimpfeling, that you would satisfy, even late at last, your promise and my expectation of you. You know, however, what you promised, and you are not ignorant of what I expect. I desire you not to be a monk, but to live with monks. Make haste, that we may live together. Behold, the hour of our passing, greatly to be feared, is at hand. Why then do we delay? Why, unhappy men, do we neglect the brief and wretched state of our [unclear]? Why do we refuse to do penance, in the time of salvation, for our transgressions and excesses? Let us prepare ourselves for death, since it hastens quite terribly, calls all the reprobate, spares no one, reveres no one; it denies audience to those who pray; it despises the tears of those who wail; it scorns the threats of princes, and destroys the names and glory of kings; it brings to nothing the hateful pride of tyrants before all. Therefore, brother, diligently considering the benefits of the Redeemer shown to us, very useful to us, let us repay them by amending our offenses, continually shining with good morals, so that by penance and tears we may be able to placate the strict examination of the judge hastening to the judgment seat, who will show himself terrible to the rejected, pleasant to the elect.
