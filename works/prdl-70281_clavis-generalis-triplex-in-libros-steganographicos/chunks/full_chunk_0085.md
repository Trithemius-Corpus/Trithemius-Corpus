154 *Of the Steganography of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim. To Cornelius Gyrardus of Gouda, canon of St. Augustine near Leiden, greetings.*

Our Lord Jesus Christ, the most gentle savior of our souls, promised us the happiness of eternal consolation, if [unclear] his commandments with a pure [unclear]. Dearest brother, keep diligently the commandments of God and of the holy rule, because unless you preserve inviolate until death all the things that almighty God has commanded you, you will perish by eternal death. Therefore I have handed on to you the form and law of a religious and holy way of life.

Beware, then, diligently, lest you give your heart to be possessed by any mortal creature through the desire of love, because no one comes to those highest joys except the one who despises the world for God. Again, therefore, through this letter of mine I exhort you, as my dearest friend, to observe the warnings that I had given you. For I think that I am doing something good when I write something good in this sacred time, especially for edification concerning the manner of living well, by which you may always become better. Therefore now again I write to you for your good, because I fear your danger no less than my own. But I beg you: always keep watch, advancing daily in the fear of God, and living innocent, harming no one, and altogether persevering in your holy purpose. Do not trust in time, nor fortune, nor age, because they are nothing but smoke. Truly I love you sincerely in Christ Jesus, praying that he himself may arrange that I also be beloved to you; and that I, who am absent from you in body, may always be present in spirit. Therefore, since I continually keep remembrance of you in my prayers, I very earnestly beg you not to forget me. Farewell. 12 Kalends of May, in the year 1500.

*Chapter.*

*Book Two.*

*Chapter XXIV.*

*Whose hour is called Xephan, and its angel, called the supreme emperor, is Sarandiel, having under him many princes, dukes, counts, and servants.*

The twelfth hour of the night, which reaches the dawn, is called Xephan, and its angel is called Sarandiel, who has under his command many spirits, good and evil, princes, dukes, counts, and ministers, who are assigned to various operations: the good to good ones, and the evil to evil ones. These princes, as Hermes the Hebrew, who is called Solomon, says in the fourth book on the offices of spirits, are accustomed often to appear in that form in which the Devil is read to have appeared to Eve, having one beautiful head like that of a maiden, with hair hanging down on either side, but two serpent-bodies, which nevertheless they always veil and hide under certain coverings or leaves, so that they may not be seen. In face, however, they are most beautiful, benevolent, and pleasant, and most ready and cheerful for every obedience to the operator.

R. R. R.
