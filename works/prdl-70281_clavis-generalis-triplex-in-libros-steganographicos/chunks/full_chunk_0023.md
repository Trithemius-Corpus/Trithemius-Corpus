*Of the Steganography*

Write whatever letters you wish, in which you need fear no one’s inspection.

*Health and affection.* My most beloved and most singular friend, the bearer of the present letter, a native of Roermond, very beneficent and [unclear] toward our friends, whom I ask you to receive kindly, will explain his need to you. Since he has always been bound to me by an old acquaintance, I would judge it most sweet, according to my powers, to relieve him and heap benefits upon him. And would that I could apply some consolation to his misfortunes. But since I am exhausted by heavy losses, as is clearer to you than daylight, I cannot satisfy my zeal as I would wish. But you, who abound in necessary temporal things, should show yourself open-handed to a friend, and humane, just as you have been accustomed to show yourself a patron of friends’ necessities. I ask, therefore, that you lend him, under a fixed term, two hundred florins, because he is placed in the greatest need, as he will inform you. I trust that he will restore everything in full when the term appointed for him has come. If you do this, I shall give thanks that will never die, and I shall stand by you in [more serious matters?]. Act so that we may prove in deed your sincere love toward us. I wish you to know that what you do for him is done for me; nor less will you have bound him, most grateful, to you, firmly believe it, and when the term has elapsed he will restore everything most completely. For he is accustomed to keep his promises inviolate and to show himself constant to all men. Moreover, since you rejoice to hear new reports, I would like to write something. I have reared a little dog, rightly called by a Greek name [unclear], flourishing with such learning, training, and memory of all things that to some he seems to have put off the beast and put on the man. He understands Greek and Latin words; he knows how to ask pardon for his faults. Whatever I command, he does, at my nod, with gestures; he discloses the secrets of men. If you saw him imbued with such discipline of manners, you would think him more learned than many men. Ordered to open a window, he opens it; surpassing nature by his intelligence, when forbidden he puts food away. He carries a burden placed upon him; [unclear] to be shown as my zealous defender; he loves himself, plays excellently, and does altogether unusual, incredible wonders. Farewell. Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint Martin in Sponheim, wrote this on March 13, 1500.

*P*

When the friend to whom these or any other letters are sent has received them, he understands that the sign belongs to the prince of the West. Therefore, after the customary preliminaries have first been performed, he turns himself to the West, summons the Spirit, adjuring the invisible messenger that he become visible, and that he tell no one. The song of the Spirit.
