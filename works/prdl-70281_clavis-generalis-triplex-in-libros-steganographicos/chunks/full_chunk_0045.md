*Of the Steganography, 14*

You now have twelve of Emoniel’s dukes, with their subdukes and servants, 1,320, who, according to the order of the hours, suffice for us for every intention of our operation, whether the operation is nocturnal or diurnal. And know that the prince Emoniel, with his dukes, is accustomed to appear with a maidenly head and body, but with a serpent’s tail. Therefore, when you wish to perform something in Steganography through the spirits of Emoniel, do those things which you know are generally to be done according to the art; and afterward read this conjuration, silently, in silence, naming the spirit to be called.

*Song of Conjuration.*

*Emoniel aproifi chamerufyn thulnear peanos meuear, pandroy cralnotiel narboy mauy fabelrontos, arliel chemorfyn nety pranfobyr diuiciel malros ruelty perfon roab chrumeltufyn.*

When this conjuration has been spoken in the manner which the art itself requires, the spirit whom you have called will appear, ready to obey in all things whatever you command him, because he is benevolent and faithful.

A secret, whatever sort you wish.

I have a friend who everywhere trusts all men too much, who cherishes enemies as friends, from whom he often receives both bad repute and harm. He is credulous toward the flatteries of those who fawn upon him, and especially of one man who clings to his side, whom he does not know to be opposed to him in secret, because he sees him to be ingratiating in public. I wish to accuse him, yet in such a way that the advice becomes known to the friend alone; to the rest it will be hidden.

Let the tenor of the letters be formed at pleasure.

Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, to the Reverend Father and Lord Nicolaus, Bishop of [Irbursiensis?], Vicar of the Archbishop of [Pantomoranus?], greeting.

D.P.

When I bring your talent and the uprightness of your life back to mind, Reverend and most excellent Father, I am especially compelled to pronounce you most worthy of all bishops. For indeed, whether one considers the learning in you of the good arts, or the innocence of your life, what else should I call you but most just, most learned in all letters, and to be revered for your morals? You adorn your conduct with works of rectitude, and always show yourself an excellent shepherd of souls; and in letters you continually exercise yourself by study, so that you may benefit yourself and others in both respects. Farewell. Given from Spanheim, 11 Kalends of April, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1501.

After you have received the letters, let the person to whom they are sent, being perfectly imbued in the art of Steganography, do as he knows must be done according to the art; these things having been set forth, let him add the conjuration.

*Song of conjuration.*

*Emoniel lebos chamerothy meor pemorfy dyor medulorfyn fray pean, Crymaf y melrofyne vati chabaryn dayr. Aschre cathurmofabelton erfoyt marduse.*

When the song has been duly completed, the called spirit will be present in the form customary to him, and will faithfully reveal to you each and every secret that had been entrusted to him.

*Chapter XXVI.*

*Whose supreme emperor is called Icosiel, having under him 100 dukes or princes, 300 counts or subdukes; the number of servants is not yet certain to me.*

Icosiel, among the aerial spirits, is one of the more powerful, a great and powerful emperor in the secrets of Steganography, having under him 100 princes of ducal order. But counts, 300; I have not yet found the certain number of servants. He is called Icosiel for this reason, because he gladly spends time with men in houses.
