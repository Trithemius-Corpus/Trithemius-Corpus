Yet always be readier to persuade than to accuse, because the cloistered mind is often led back to the path of rectitude by holy exhortations, whereas it is very often hardened by severe rebuke. Consider the character of your name; consider the condition of the rule you have undertaken; often reflect on what especially befits you. You are called abbot, the father of monks, you who are approved to act in Christ’s place in the monastery. Therefore recognize yourself as the father of your subjects, not their lord; the shepherd of Christ’s sheep, not their patron. And therefore, having been appointed ruler of monks, recognize yourself as a monk, and as bound by a double profession to regular observance. Take care to fulfill the name of superior by your deeds, so that your monks, who call you abbot, may be taught by your works and truly recognize you as father. Be gentle when asked; indeed, if offended, ask first. Consider that you are the shepherd of Christ’s infirm sheep; whatever can contribute to their care, do not fear to undertake for the love of God. Let the health of the souls committed to you occupy your whole self; if you despise working for it, you will be subject to the gravest damnation. Remember what happened to Eli the priest, who did not correct his delinquent sons. He was condemned for their sin, because those whom he ought to have corrected by priestly authority, he left uncorrected through ruinous compassion. So too you, abbot, even if you have committed no sins in yourself, will be subject to damnation for the neglected faults of your subjects, because, being appointed guardian of souls, you do not prevent those very souls from going to perdition; you are justly to be condemned by him whom you mock. How great the learning and knowledge of the Scriptures ought to shine forth in you, the holy father Benedict proclaims in the Rule. “It is necessary,” he says, “for the abbot to be learned in the divine law, so that he may know how to bring forth new things and old in due season.” He must imitate a physician most learned in his art, one who knows not only how to apply the suitable remedy to diseases, but also how sagaciously to investigate the causes, dangers, and outcomes of every illness. Many diseases indeed come upon human bodies daily, which a learned and experienced physician can easily drive away; an unlearned and inexperienced one rather increases them. But the infirmities of souls are both more numerous in number and of greater consequence in art; for by as many evil thoughts as the mind is inwardly and pleasurably touched, by so many hidden diseases, as it were, the soul is wounded. But if a perverse thought has gone outward into word or deed, then the hidden disease rises up into a swelling; unless it is treated prudently and without delay, little by little it grows and gathers strength to the destruction of the whole body. For these diseases of the mind a remedy composed according to art is required in various ways; long experience is judged necessary, since whoever approaches an invalid to cure him while unskilled, so far as lies in him, does not heal but kills. And who is so fit that he can discern the hidden diseases of the minds of cloistered persons? Many, through lack of discrimination among diseases, have killed many sick people. Therefore the greatest learning, doctrine, discretion, and experience are required in a shepherd of souls, so that he may know how to apply remedies suited to individual diseases, lest inexperience destroy knowledge, lest ignorance confuse the remedies. Meanwhile let discretion in the art be present, until experience is learned without danger. In the art of medicine, indeed, many are learned, but few are experienced. And among shepherds of souls we can find very many who are learned and in many things instructed; but we find few who are skilled and experienced in this art. And nothing harms infirm souls so much as the shepherd’s inexperienced knowledge. For one who does not know how to distinguish between one disease and another is often accustomed to drive the sick person into graver dangers. For the diseases of cloistered persons are cured in various ways. At one time the prudent physician of souls uses the authority of the Scriptures; now he strives by reasons to persuade the sick person of the things that pertain to health; at another time he brings forward the examples and paradigms of the ancients; now he subtly lays bare the causes of the disease and the roots fixed in the heart. Indeed, as many kinds of illness as he finds, so many kinds of remedies he sets against them.
