Take away all excuses, O abbot of monks: no one compels you, against your will, to preside over cloistered men, if it is clear to you that you are wholly unfit for governance. Now therefore, since you have once entered upon the care of souls, you must take one of two courses, if you desire to avoid the danger of eternal damnation. Either you should strive to bring the monks committed to you back to the observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict, or, if you are altogether unable to do this, voluntarily resign the abbacy. It is better to flee with Lot from fellowship in sins than to perish in the conflagration with those whom you cannot amend. And I say what I think: it is better for you not to die as abbot than to live as shepherd of those whom you are not able to govern according to the Rule. Let no deceit of flattering fortune deceive you: either you will be saved with the sheep committed to you, or, if you are negligent, you will not be saved without them. And indeed the mighty shall mightily suffer torments, and a most severe judgment shall be made upon those who are in authority. Therefore, if you desire to escape the danger of eternal damnation, feed the sheep committed to you with the word; feed them by example.

*To the shepherds of cloistered men. Third:* feed them with nourishment. It belongs to him to feed his subjects not only with the word of holy exhortation, not only to go before them by the example of good conduct, but also to provide, according to the Rule, for the necessities of the body. This provision is not to be made for monks in money, nor in yearly distributions, but in the daily bestowal of necessary things. Here, shepherd, consider yourself, and carefully ponder how you ought to feed the Lord’s sheep. “Woe,” says the supreme judge, “to the shepherds who feed themselves, but scorn to feed the Lord’s sheep.” What do you think, fathers? Are there not among cloistered shepherds some who feed themselves rather than the Lord’s sheep? I fear that there are not a few but very many among abbots who, with the sheep neglected, despised, and contemned, feed themselves; they seek their own advantages, not the gains of souls; they acknowledge themselves not as fathers of monks, but as lords. Why do you destroy yourself, O shepherd and idol, who, though set as ruler over others, do not live rightly?

Behold, you try to drive your monks by words and blows to regular observance, and you, living without the Rule, think that all things are permitted to you. The good things you say, you do not do; the evil things you forbid, you commit. You wish the brothers to fast according to the Rule, and you excite your appetite with daily banquets. You order others to remain within the precincts of the monastery, and you are always walking about outside. Now you call guests, the companions of your madness, to table, with whom you may break the fast as though lawfully. You say, “I cannot eat alone,” and yet you refuse to remain in the refectory with the brothers. You say that you eat out of reverence for guests, although it is clear that you invited them in order that you might eat. Now you pretend to be sick; now you cry that you cannot eat, though nevertheless in secret you eat forbidden food. You hurry at great expense to the baths; you call male and female companions; and you who importunately deny the least things to your brothers do not fear to spend much on profane uses. You are good and affable to all; only to your brothers do you show yourself hard and inexorable. You love strangers; you despise your own. You who give many orders to make monks poor do either nothing or little according to the Rule. You withhold necessary food from your brothers; you bestow cheap and tasteless things. You satisfy your stomach with the best wine; to the brothers you serve what is worse. You try to shut others in a cell at all times; you order all to devote themselves to readings and prayers; and you can never remain in secret, but, wandering and always running about, you desire to have the consolations of the flesh. And why say much? Set as ruler of cloistered men, you live in a secular fashion; placed over regulars, you conduct yourself irreligiously; appointed abbot of monks, you show either nothing or little worthy of the shepherd’s office. You desire to raise others to heaven, and you do not fear to consign yourself to hell. You desire to be called religious, not to be so; you love the honor and praises of men; you think not at all of future dangers. You rejoice that you are abbot; you do not acknowledge yourself as father. You are glad to be a prelate; you do not show yourself a shepherd. But, in the end, what do you think you will obtain, you who, while desiring to save others, condemn yourself? It remains that you be allotted a place with the impious in hell, you who commit the works of the impious in the cloister.
