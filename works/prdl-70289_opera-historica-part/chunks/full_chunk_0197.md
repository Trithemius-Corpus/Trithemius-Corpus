Indeed, he most zealously cultivated the liberal arts, and, greatly venerating their teachers, honored them with great distinctions. In learning grammar he heard Peter of Pisa, an aged deacon; in the other disciplines he had as teacher Albinus, surnamed Alcuin, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict and formerly a deacon, a disciple from Britain, a man of Saxon stock, most learned in every respect. With him he spent very much time and labor in learning rhetoric and dialectic, but especially astronomy. He learned the art of computation, and with keen attention most carefully investigated the course of the stars. The Christian religion, with which he had been imbued from infancy, he cultivated most devoutly and with the greatest piety, and he made many arrangements for its honor. He attended church zealously and with great devotion in the morning and evening, likewise in the night hours and at the time of the sacrifice, so far as his health allowed him. In his palace at Aachen he assembled a most precious library, filled with a very great abundance of books; when he died, he had it sold and distributed as alms to the poor. Among the other works of his intellect he published many and various additions of the laws and rights of all the nations that lived under his kingdom and empire, in many volumes. Likewise he composed the laws of the Saxons; likewise the laws of the Alemanni; likewise the laws of the Franks; likewise he published many and various songs concerning the deeds of the ancients. He also began a grammar of his native tongue, namely Teutonic or Alemannic, which, occupied with other matters, he could not finish. I have seen fragments of this grammar, and also certain volumes composed according to its rules; mention of these will be made below, when we come to Otfried, monk of the monastery of Weissenburg. Many volumes also were published by various men at his command; these we leave to their own authors. Happy is the commonwealth whose prince was so glorious a philosopher. At length, after many most holy exercises, he died in the basilica of the blessed Mother of God, which he himself had built at very great expense, and was buried at Aachen, in the year of the Lord 814, in the seventh indiction, on the fifth day before the Kalends of February, in the seventy-second year of his age and the forty-seventh of his reign.

Einhard, once chancellor of the aforesaid Emperor Charles the Great, and afterward a monk of the order of the divine father Benedict, and first abbot of the monastery of Seligenstadt in the diocese of Mainz above the Main, a man most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, acute in intellect, eloquent in speech, outstanding in life and conduct, skilled especially in Greek and Latin, and excellent both in meter and in prose: among the other little works of his intellect, he abbreviated the Gallican Psalter, extracting from it all the verses containing words of prayer. He also wrote, both in his own person and in that of the emperor, many elegant and useful letters to various persons on various affairs and cases. Also *The Deeds of Emperor Charles*, one book. Moreover, a notable history of the times concerning the deeds of the Germans and Franks, two books. And certain other things which have by no means come down to us. He flourished under Emperor Charles the Great and his son Louis, in the year of the Lord 820.

Freculph, bishop of Lisieux, once a monk of Fulda, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a disciple of Albinus, to whom Rabanus, abbot of Fulda, exercised his talent in many volumes, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and sufficiently fit for teaching the people; no less distinguished in conduct than in knowledge: he is said to have written certain excellent little works. Of these there are extant seven volumes addressed to Helisachar, containing a history of events from the beginning of the world down to the birth of the Savior, both of the gentiles and of the Jews. In this work he also subtly untangled many difficult questions of the divine Scriptures. He wrote, moreover, elegant and varied letters to various persons; I remember having read some of these to Rabanus. But he also published several other things which are unknown to us. He flourished under the rule of Louis the First, son of Charles the Great, in the year of the Lord 840.

Hamularius Fortunatus, the sixty-third archbishop of Trier from Saint Eucharius, and, as they say, cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently taught in secular letters, skilled in Greek and Latin, wrote little works of reading not to be despised. Of these the following are reported: *On the Divine Offices*, one book. To Emperor Louis on the same matter, seven books. *On the Sacred Mysteries*, one book. Various letters also to various persons.
