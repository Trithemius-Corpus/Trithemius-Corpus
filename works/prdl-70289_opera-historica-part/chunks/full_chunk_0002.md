There, holding fast to his purpose to the end of his life and indefatigable in labor, truly dying in the writing of books, he closed his day.

Among these wonders of immense night-labors, works that by their number and their bulk will bring amazement to posterity, those very extensive *Annals of Germany or France*, divided into three volumes, would easily hold the chief place, if they were extant. These he then left compiled and completed from all the monuments of all ages, by a labor plainly Herculean; but while alive he could not publish them, except for a very brief compendium of the first volume, sent to your predecessor Laurentius de Bibra, from which, as from a claw, one may estimate the lion. This work, certainly, both for the author’s reputation and for the variety of its matter and its own perfection, must be held of any price whatever, and the prayers of learned men have long been seeking it with great desire; so that, if they had it, they would think the loss of the others could easily be borne.

For that man possessed, besides those gifts with which one who is meditating books destined to live ought to be furnished, sharpness of judgment, ease of style, varied reading, accuracy, and endurance of labor; and, for the undertaking of composing history in every form, those further aids which scarcely any others had in such fashion. One, I mean, was the incomparable treasury of various excellent manuscripts in the library of Sponheim, chiefly built up and enlarged by him; that this treasury afterward, partly through the negligence of his successors, partly through the injury of the times amid the disturbances of civil wars, was plundered, carried off, and perished, so that scarcely certain worthless fragments of it now survive preserved at Kreuznach, cannot be lamented enough. The other was his acquaintance with princes and great men throughout Europe, whom in that season God willed to spring up as a good crop, to the great advantage of divine and human letters; they almost vied with one another in courting his familiarity, a companionship bound by true ties of friendship and joined by a shared zeal for virtue and learning. With their help he was able to learn everything concerning serious affairs done at home and abroad, with their causes and circumstances, and thereafter to commit them to papers with no little better trustworthiness [unclear] not only cathedral colleges, but also monasteries [unclear] had appointed for recording annals, were accustomed to do.

This very thought certainly stirs and inflames in the minds of all who are touched by a better care for these letters and by love of their country a marvelous desire to see that outstanding work: since they do not despair that it can be brought forth from some corner of the libraries of your city or jurisdiction; and, to one so judging, it will seem above all a good deed, and worthy of you, so great a prelate and patron of studies, if under your auspices that treasury, rather than book, is restored to the commonwealth. Most reverend prince, if in the public name I should earnestly beseech you to deign to devote some care to this matter, and to allow those Trithemian books, or rather children, demanding with no obscure voice the vindication of their brothers oppressed by an unworthy servitude, to obtain this from you, I have hoped that in accordance with your utmost clemency and humanity you will assuredly take it in good part.

Farewell. At Myrtilletum, on the Kalends of April, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1601.

Most devoted to Your Reverend Highness,

MARQ. FREHERUS.
