Heinrich of Andernach, of the Order of the Brothers of Blessed Mary Ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, of the Cologne convent, a man trained by long study and learned in the holy Scriptures, and subtly imbued with Aristotelian philosophy, excellent in talent, an outstanding declaimer of sermons, who adorned first the Parisian and then the Cologne academy by his learning and presence. He wrote a distinguished work on the Sentences, books IV; Notable Questions of Philosophy, book I; sermons on the season and on the saints through the cycle of the year, books II. The other things which he composed have not come to my knowledge.

Johannes, abbot of the monastery of Saint Bavo in Ghent, of the Order of the divine father Benedict, an outstanding doctor and professor of the divine Scriptures, subtle in talent, renowned in eloquence, venerable no less for religious conduct than for knowledge. He wrote a notable little book on the eating of meat, in which, disputing subtly, he concludes according to the Rule of Saint Benedict and the sanctions of law that the eating of meat is unlawful for a healthy monk, and therefore wholly forbidden. He also wrote explanations on our Rule, book I; also questions of the Sententiary, book I. In addition, he is said to have written various and many treatises, which have by no means come down to us.

Johannes of Werden, of the diocese of Cologne, of the Order of the Friars Minor of Saint Francis, a man studious and learned in the holy divine Scriptures, and sufficiently learned in natural philosophy, renowned in talent, scholastic in speech, the most celebrated of all in his time as a declaimer of popular sermons. He wrote very notable sermons on the season and on the saints, which today are commonly called the sermons “Sleep securely,” because any preacher can make progress in them without labor. Also sermons for Lent, book I. He also published some other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the Cologne convent under the emperor Wenceslaus, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

Johannes of Duren, of the Order of the Friars Minor, of the diocese of Cologne, a not idle preacher of the divine word, renowned in talent, eloquent in speech, studious and learned both in the divine writings and in secular letters. He wrote for the use of simple brothers certain volumes not to be despised, by which he made his name known to those present and to posterity; among these I found the following: On the Seven Sins, book I; on the hidden sins of vice, book I; on the manifest things of vice, book I; also sermons for Lent, book I. The other things which he composed have by no means come into our hands.

Arnold of Austria, of the Order of the Brothers of Blessed Mary Ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, of the Vienna convent, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in talent, an outstanding disputant of questions. He wrote a distinguished volume on the Sentences, books III; also elegant sermons and various questions in Scripture. But he is said to have composed some other things, which have not come into our hands.

Thomas of Haselbach, learned in the holy writings, well taught in secular philosophy, renowned in talent, scholastic in speech, but in many things overly diffuse and prolix, and for that reason subject to the judgment of certain people; for when he was ordinary lector of the Vienna academy, he was subtle in talent, renowned in eloquence, devout in life and conduct. He wrote many volumes on the prophet Isaiah, which I do not know whether all are to be found. Also on the Sentences, books IV; on the Ten Commandments of the Decalogue, book I; on the five senses of man, book I; also many sermons on the season and on the saints, books II; and many other things. He flourished under Rupert the Bavarian, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1410.

Johannes Brammart, of the Order of the Brothers of Blessed Mary Ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, of the Aachen convent, provincial prior of Lower Germany, doctor of Paris, studious and learned in the holy writings, and excellently learned in secular philosophy, subtle in talent, renowned in eloquence, devout in life and conduct. He wrote a distinguished work on the Sentences, books III; also many sermons, various questions, and certain other things which have not come into our hands. Finally, after many good things bestowed upon his province, he died under Rupert the Bavarian, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1307, in the fifteenth indiction. He was buried at Cologne in the middle of the choir of his convent.

Thomas
