*From the Citicensian Chronicle of Paulus Langaeus Cygnaeus, monk of Bosau, under the year of Christ 1515.*

In the same year, when, out of love of letters, with the consent of our venerable abbot Benedict and by leave of dismissal, I was at Wurzburg in the monastery of St James the Apostle, for a time under the instruction and obedience of the reverend and in every respect most learned man, lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the said monastery, while he was then applying himself to setting down the histories of German affairs in three volumes, and while with the same care he wished to continue his book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, already produced long ago at Spanheim down to the year of the Lord 1494, down to the present time, namely to connect, by a certain added notice, the illustrious men who in the meantime had written, together with their lucubrations, to the writers of the churches already previously written up and printed in the aforesaid book: in order that he might be able to complete this praiseworthy undertaking more easily and promptly, he sent me, whom he judged suitable to assist him in this, with letters of recommendation to the foundations of all monasteries, yet especially of our order, and also to gather from every quarter throughout all Germany, for the praise of God, giver of all sciences, and for the honor and renown of the whole German nation, the monuments of clear men, as I have said, and, having collected and copied them, to bring them to him. Now the tenor of the aforesaid letters, and likewise of the commissions, was and is as follows.

Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St James at Wurzburg, of the order of Saint Benedict, to all and singular lords abbots, provosts, priors, and the other rectors, with prayers, ready willingness to obey. We have given in charge to the bearer of these letters, brother Paulus Langius, monk and priest of our aforesaid order, that he diligently inquire after, write down, and report to us, for all monasteries, convents, and churches, of whatever religion they may be, as far as can be done, throughout all Germany, their foundations together with the time and their little works, for the adornment of the Annals or Chronicles of Germany, to which we are applying our labor. Therefore we ask you all and singular, through the Savior of the world, that you kindly admit him to your [unclear], and charitably [unclear], and that in those matters for the sake of investigating which he is sent by us, you not cease to lend him favor and aid according to your strength, being about to receive reward from him who is judge of all [unclear]. [Unclear] on the right hand: Go, blessed of my Father, because when they were strangers [unclear]. Written in our aforesaid monastery at Wurzburg on the first day of the month of April, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1515.

And so I always strove, according to my strength, to carry out the aforesaid embassy or commission, although I was held back and hindered in many places, partly by the difficulty and danger of the roads, partly by fear of robbers and brigands, through mountains and woods, and finally by the unexpected death of Trithemius himself, who had sent me and was my teacher. Nevertheless, while he was still living, several times, when winter was coming on, I returned to Wurzburg to report and consign what had been collected and written, together with various letters and greetings of different wise men. And although many famous, and also very weighty and wholly upright, men praised the aforesaid investigation of antiquities, yet on the other side there were some sycophants and [unclear], and, what is shameful, certain petty religious brethren, biting and Cynical, who quite shamelessly censured that commission and inquiry, not blushing to call me, for so arduous an undertaking, a wanderer and mountebank. But if I am a wanderer, if a circumcellion, who, I ask, was my namesake, the Apostle Paul, of whom it is read in Acts 19: It happened that, when Paul had passed through the upper regions, he came to Ephesus; and who by traveling about from Jerusalem as far as Illyricum sowed the Gospel of Christ, although for a far different and more blessed cause? What, finally, was Jerome, what Rufinus, what Heraclides, what Petronius, Paulus Orosius, Julius Africanus, Severus Sulpicius, Cassianus, Paschasius, and the like, who, on account of pious devotion and the edification of others, crossed seas and most devoutly surveyed the places of the Holy Land? Then, roaming through Egypt, Nitria, Scythia, and Palestine, they most exactly inspected the monasteries of monks, the caves of anchorites, and [unclear], and most religiously and watchfully examined their life and conduct and doctrine; and, generally staying among them for some days and years, and imitating and learning the institutions and morals of the same, and noting down the exhortations of individuals, at last returning to their own country, they elegantly and usefully wrote down what they had seen and heard, for the honor of God and the edification and profit of readers. Had they not gone about the places narrated above, neither monks nor even the world itself would now have the Lives of the Fathers, the Institutions, the Conferences, the Exhortations, the Conversations, and the Itinerary which is ascribed to Jerome, and finally the lives and legends of certain men, and especially of Antony, Hilarion, Pachomius, Onuphrius, Macarius, Romanus, Arsenius, Posthumius, Mary of Egypt, Euphrosyne, Euphrasia, and the like. Happy therefore, and thrice happy, was their travelling about; praiseworthy and holy was their investigation, from which there followed so great a fruit and so great a holiness of morals and of all edification.

Why therefore do envious men make it a charge against me, when it is praised in others; when even heathendom itself extolled it in Ulysses with many proclamations; when the glorious Jerome commended it in the philosophers Plato and Apollonius; when finally the same man extolled it even in women with the highest praises? Well known is that most fervent survey of Saint Paula, by which that most noble one of Roman matrons touched with her own hands, venerated, and kissed countless cells of monks and hermits, and also their beds, garments, hair shirts, and little sweat-cloths. How great a fruit and profit accrued to the world from such journeyings of the holy fathers, neither Cicero himself nor Demosthenes could utter and set forth with their eloquence. For, to pass over others meanwhile, if the holy and most devout fathers of our order, Columbanus, Gallus, Kilian, Boniface, Willibrord, Ludger, and their peers, had remained seated in their monasteries, who would have converted the peoples of the Austrasians, Gauls, Franks, Bavarians, Vindelicians, Thuringians, Saxons, Hessians, and Frisians to the faith of Christ?
