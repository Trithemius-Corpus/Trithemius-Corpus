From that time the Sicambri, laying aside their ancient name, began, out of love for their king, to call themselves Franks. Indeed, as Hunibald bears witness, not only the nobles, but also all the soldiers, the whole people of the entire nation, were so delighted by the very novelty of the name that they induced the king by entreaties to decree by public edict that thereafter they were forever to be called not Sicambri but Franks. This the king established all the more gladly, the more eagerly he loved the immortality of his own name. Therefore they were called Franks, who previously by some were called Sicambri, and by others, by reason of their alliance, Germans. And it is indeed remarkable how they gloried in the novelty of this word, since for many times thereafter, whenever they met one another, they greeted one another thus: “A good day, free Frank.” Nevertheless, not suddenly, but at last after much time, this name came into unity, so that the people itself were called Franks even among other nations; for to the Romans and especially the Gauls the name of the Franks was hateful. Hence among their writers they were more often called Germans than either Sicambri or Franks. Hunibald did not pass over in silence whence, or from whom, the name of the Sicambri had its origin.

*Whence the Franks are called free, “Die fryer Francken”; by what names they were known.*

That the Franks were previously called Sicambri is attested not only by Hunibald, but also by the blessed archbishop Remigius, who, as Gregory, bishop of Tours, records, when he was to baptize Clodoveus, king of the Franks, the first of the kings of the Franks converted to Christ, as we shall say in its proper place, said before the assembly of nobles, while he was putting off his royal ornaments and humbling himself for the sacred washing, with all hearing: “Gently bow your neck, Sicamber; worship what you burned; burn what you worshipped.” Of the history of this matter I have already spoken more fully, after Hunibald, in the first volume of the Annals.

And so in the twenty-fourth year before the birth of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, which was the fourteenth year of King Francus, the Sicambri changed their ancient name to a new one, although both before and afterward they were more often called Germans, or Sicambri, among writers. ...a people hostile to us, or to die fighting.

Gladdened by these words, the king at once sent messengers to the Saxons, the Germans, and also the Thuringians, asking aid by right of alliance against the assault of the Gauls. All these, with great eagerness, sent the requested supports of soldiers: the Saxons, namely, eighty-four thousand fighting men; the German Theutones, sixty thousand; and the Thuringians, forty-six thousand. Joined with these, the Franks with great forces very hastily brought out an army of three hundred thousand men against their adversaries. But King Francus, seeing the desolation of the region which the Gauls had made, moved with great indignation of spirit, said: “See, my bravest fellow-soldiers, you who rightly grieve for the shed blood of your fathers, what these most treacherous Gauls have done. I swear by the head of my little son Antharius, whom these most wicked men killed, that I shall give no rest to my head until I have avenged myself on them in a notable way.” And they all praised his swift resolve.

Therefore, after a bridge had been constructed with boats and many timbers, in two days he led the army across, leaving forty thousand men to guard the crossing; with the remaining multitude he advanced into Gaul, and he made such a slaughter as no one of the Sicambri before him was able to make, or is believed to have conceived in his mind.
