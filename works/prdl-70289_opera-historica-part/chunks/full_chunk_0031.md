*Compendium, Book I.*

... which we call April, King Marcomir himself, with Sunno, Panthenor, and Priam, his brothers, the leaders left beyond the Danubian sea, circling for a long distance through the borders of Scythia and toward the north, and then turning their route toward the region of the Teutons, began his migration westward with the whole people of the kingdom, their wives, children, and all their goods, with great labor, difficult and long-lasting.

There were, apart from women and little children, 175,658 men most ready for war. The names of the princes who were with Marcomir and had descended from his lineage were: Duke Nicanor, uncle of King Marcomir; Sunno, Panthenor, Priam, brothers of Marcomir, the aforesaid king; Duke Helenus, Duke Antenor, Duke Menander, Duke Edrasius, Duke Gethenus, Duke Priamus, Duke Marenus, Duke Ermon, Duke Nicanor, Duke Pyndari, Duke Helam, Duke Marcomir, Duke Colam, Duke Masan, Duke Antenor, Duke Ancreasius, Duke Bengoth, Duke Calomer, Duke Rodanus, Duke Malda, Duke Kylan, Duke Solas, Duke Colo, Duke Bremus, Duke Sunno, Duke Melanus, Duke Nicamor, Duke Thaphon, Duke Salon, Duke Meodarus.

The number of the whole people, as Hunibald bears witness, men, women, and little children of both sexes together, was 489,360, not counting the servants attending them. But if anyone desires to know fully what great difficulties they had on the journey, what wars they waged with the peoples opposing them, and what great dangers they either fell into or escaped, let him read the writer mentioned in the book of histories of the people of the Franks. Just as its beginnings are remarkable, so they seem to me, saving the judgment of those who judge better, to be fabulous in many respects.

At length, after many labors and countless difficulties, King Marcomir came into Germany with his leaders and the whole people. They were kindly received by the Saxons as brothers, and among them received the desired settlements by the Rhine, namely that province in which those now dwelling there are called the West Frisians, and also the Gelderlanders and Hollanders: that is, around that region where the Rhine flows into the sea by three mouths, although at first they received rather narrow settlements, which in a short time they made wider westward by force of arms.

Indeed, in the twentieth year of his reign, King Marcomir, provoked by an injury from the Transrhenane Gauls, gathered an army and, after crossing the river, subjected to his rule that land which, enclosed by islands of the Rhine, is nearest to the sea, expelling its inhabitants; and he entrusted it to his brother Sunno to guard. He brought in by ship people of his own nation to inhabit and cultivate it.

But when the Gauls took ill the incursion of the new foreign people into the borders of the Hollanders, and were provoking it with various attempts, King Marcomir, in the twenty-fourth year of his reign, having gathered a great army both of his own people and of the Saxons, crossed the Rhine by ship, entered western Gaul, devastating everything with sword and fire, and reached as far as the Meuse. Thereafter he fought many battles with the Gauls, triumphing everywhere as victor, and his name was terrible to the conquered Gallic peoples; all who heard of Marcomir feared him. He made a treaty with the Britons dwelling on the island.

From that time, for almost nine hundred years, the kings of the aforesaid people of the Scythians never ceased from harrying the Romans and the Gauls, sometimes defeated, yet more often victors, until at last they compelled by arms into their power not only all Gaul, but also all Alemannia, Swabia, Bavaria, Burgundy, Italy, Sclavonia, Thuringia, Austria, Saxony, the Teutons, Cimbri, Danes, Cisrhenanes, Mosellans, Treveri, Metz people, Austrasians, Liege people, Morini, Tournai people, people of the Meuse, Atrebates, Cologne people, Mainz people, Vangiones, Nemetes, Strasbourg people, Helvetii, Rauraci, Bohemians, and Sorbs, together with the Spaniards, all the Germans, and almost all Europe.

After this King Marcomir died, when the twenty-eighth year of his reign had been completed, namely in the year 412 before the birth of Christ, in the twenty-first year from the entry of the people into the borders of Saxony at the Rhine. He left three sons, dukes, Antenor, Priam, and Nicanor, and twelve daughters.

Down to this year Wasthald the Scythian, or Sicamber in his native speech, carried down, as it were, the historical work of the people, through more or less 758 years, beginning from the fall of Troy. In it he describes the various dwellings of his race, their settlements, and also their leaders, wars, kings, and the different deeds of the Pori in order. After him Hunnibald the Frank, from the written songs of the flamens, continued the history of the people of the Franks for 1,426 years, down to the last year of King Clovis, who, first of the kings, received Christ, baptized by Saint Remigius, in the year of the Lord 498. *Second—*
