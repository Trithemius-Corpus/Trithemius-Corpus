*A Compendium or Abridgment of the First Volume of the Chronicles or Annals of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James the Greater, Apostle, in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, concerning the origin of the nation and kings of the Franks, addressed to the most reverend father and prince in Christ, Lord Laurentius, Bishop of Würzburg and Duke of Eastern Francia, begins.*

With the aid of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have written, not without very great labors, three volumes of our annals, of by no means small size. In them, from the origin of the nation of the Franks down to the twenty-eighth year of Caesar Maximilian Augustus, we have set forth a fuller history, distinguished in the following order of division. For in the first volume we described both the origin and the kings of the aforesaid nation of the Franks, beginning from Marcomitus, who began to reign in the four hundred thirty-ninth year before the birth of Christ. After the death of his father, whom the Goths had killed in war, he reigned twenty-eight years, and was the first to lead the nation from the mouths of the Danube into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine. This people, once sprung from Trojan fugitives, while they were still among their own fellow-citizens, had the Trojan name; afterward, scattered among various nations and wandering, they very often changed their name together with their settlements. For at first, when they were called Trojans, they were Trojans; afterward, among the Scythians, they too were named Scythians; but when they came into the borders of Germany, for a time they were called Sicambri; finally, from Francus their king, they were called Franks. Nevertheless the Sicambri could not at once obtain from the neighboring peoples, especially from the Roman Gauls, to whom their power was always suspect, that they should be called Franks, until at last, expanding their kingdom and name, they powerfully subjected to themselves the Romans, together with Italy and all Gaul. And because they lived with the Germans, they were more often named Germans than Franks by the writers of deeds. Thus the whole period of the first volume of our annals contains 1,189 years, from Marcomirus down to King Pippin.

The second volume of the annals takes its beginning from the year of the Lord's nativity 750, when Pippin, mayor of the palace, was elected king of the Franks, Hilderic having been shorn as a monk. From there down to the eighth year of Richard, king of Germany, who succeeded William amid the dissension of the princes, it contains a period of 515 years, as is clear to one who counts the sequence of the order; in these years the history of the German kingdom is woven together.

The third volume of annalistic notes takes its beginning from the eighth year of King Richard, which was the 1266th year of the Lord's nativity. This we have continued to the twenty-eighth year of King Maximilian, namely the 1514th year of the Lord's nativity, containing 248 years. And thus the whole period of the three volumes makes 1,953 years, whose deeds we have embraced.
