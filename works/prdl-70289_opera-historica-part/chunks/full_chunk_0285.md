I might rest secure under the shadow of the pontifical height. But while I behold in you the flashing splendor of light, I am dazzled, I draw back, and I am wholly shaken by fear of your so great majesty, lest perhaps I, wholly inferior in learning, merit, and dignity, should displease you, an exalted and most learned father, in that very thing by which I presumed to please you. For I know your many occupations, and I am not unaware of your sacred studies; I ought not to have troubled them with my trifles. But your learning gives me courage, which your kindness and humanity render lovable to all, since it shrinks from no one’s letters and judges no one unworthy of its address.

Therefore I approach your highness, although I am very lowly, and to you I humbly submit this present little work; I appoint you its judge and defender. Receive, I pray, most reverend and most learned father, this poor lucubration of ours, examine it, judge it, and defend it; and if you shall judge it worthy, follow it with kindly favor. And you, who in continual reading turn over the writings of the ancients, may you deign also, if ever there is leisure, to inspect their catalogue. But I have not yet communicated this present little work to anyone, until I know it approved by the testimony of your authority. Friends are awaiting it; printers are expecting it. But I judge that the opinion of your fatherhood must be preferred to all.

May your most gracious fatherhood, I pray, pardon me for having presumed to disclose my follies to ears occupied with weighty matters, because I have such confidence in your humanity, gentleness, and kindness that it is in no way permitted to doubt the benevolent reception of our catalogue. And I shall count it among the greatest delights if I learn that you take pleasure in my little lucubrations. Farewell, most reverend father, and hold me, your most devoted servant, commended to you together with the destined volume. From Spanheim, 26 April, in the year of the Lord 1492.
