*Letter of the venerable and eminent father in Christ, Lord Johannes of Trittenheim, Abbot of Sponheim, to the most reverend father in Christ, Lord Johannes de Dalberg, Bishop of Worms.*

To the most reverend father and lord in Christ, Johannes de Dalberg, by divine providence Bishop of Worms: Brother Johannes, called abbot in Sponheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict, the little that he is.

In former times the ancients were accustomed, most reverend father, if they had fashioned any new work, to dedicate it to distinguished men, so that it might receive enduring strength and firmness, and so that the venomous tongue of rivals, beaten back by the light of majesty, would nowhere be able to set the tooth of a wicked bite. Nor did the opinion that proceeded from humility deceive them, since in learned prelates there is always a kindness toward their subjects, to be raised up with praises, by which it rewards the studies of the good and restrains those of the wicked.

Our men too, imitating as far as they can this example of antiquity, are accustomed to submit the works they write, dedicated to prelates of churches, to learned men, so that, approved by their authority, they may safely go forth in public. For those things come forth into the light without fear which are supported by the testimony of a bishop. Indeed no one, unless he is foolish, readily accuses what he knows has been approved by the judgment of his superiors. The innocence of the sheep rests safely when the compassion of the good shepherd watches over it.

Since, therefore, the examples of the ancients are incentives to virtue for us, it is fitting that we imitate the sacred customs of those whose praises we sing, so that, while we profit from them and others from us, the order of the ecclesiastical hierarchy may remain inviolate. And although I am too insignificant to trust that anyone will take pleasure in my little works, yet I have resolved humbly to offer what I have into the Lord’s treasury, in which the widow’s two [coins?] are rightly preferred to riches.

And so, when recently, at the urging of friends, I had gathered together with much labor a book on ecclesiastical writers, as the matter itself bears witness, I resolved, so as not to stray from the tracks of the ancients, to submit this labor of mine to some very learned man, a prelate of ecclesiastical dignity, by whose authority and benevolence it might pass the teeth of the envious without a bite. And when much thought arose in me as to whom I might more acceptably entrust this same little night-work, many presented themselves to me as I hesitated, concerning whose learning and benevolence no small promised hope was strong. Among them you, most reverend father, came to my mind as the more powerful, more eminent, and more learned man, you who surpass all in learning and exceed everyone in erudition.

In you the nobility of lofty lineage shines forth; episcopal authority stands out; uprightness of morals is seen; knowledge of every kind of good art is praised. For where shall we be able to find, apart from you, such a man in whom all these things come together at once? Among the Germans, most reverend father, no one has appeared equal to you, in whom lineage, dignity, and erudition shine so greatly. I do not speak in flattery; may the Almighty be witness, that we do not hesitate to compare [you?] with those of whom we have made mention in the catalogue. The most lucid orations which you are said to have delivered bear witness to this; letters bear witness; many epigrams bear witness. Indeed the very famous renown of your name has become known with praise not only to our Germans, but to Italians and Frenchmen.
