Recovering the booty for their own men who had been despoiled, they returned. But the spoils of the enemy, which were many, they divided among their allied friends.

Meanwhile, when the Gauls who were living beyond the Rhine as far as the British Sea, the Morini, Ambiani, Tungri, and their friends who were in those regions, had heard that Diocles had left the kingdom with a great army of his people, they gathered in a great multitude and resolved utterly to destroy the Sicambri, who were on that side between the Meuse and the Rhine. When the leaders of the Sicambri learned this through scouts, they divided the army into three parts, arranging two in separate battle-lines against the enemy, while they placed the third in ambush. As the Gauls approached with only one line, when they saw that the Sicambri had two lines, they were thrown into confusion; but there was no time to arrange the lines otherwise, since the Sicambri at once directed their first line straight against the Gauls. The second line, breaking in from the side, shattered the order of the Gauls. And when the battle was fiercely fought, and the strength of the Gauls was giving way into flight, the Sicambri came upon them from ambush. Enclosed by the Sicambri, the Gauls were slaughtered like cattle, so that out of 88,000 scarcely 10,000 remained. For many were drowned in the river as they fled.

Exulting in this victory, the Sicambri sacrificed to their gods Jupiter and Venus not only animals, but also children and as many of the Gallic enemy as they had led away captive, offering the males to Jupiter and the females to Venus. From that time the Sicambri, enriched by the spoils of their enemies and by the deeds they had accomplished among the neighboring peoples, became feared throughout the surrounding region. Diocles, through all the days of his reign, had war with the Gauls, and there was scarcely a year in which he did not at least once move his army against them.

He died in the thirty-ninth year of his reign, 297 years before the birth of Christ. He left four sons, whose names were these: Helenus, Priamus, Nicanor, and Basanus, who themselves also left many sons after them.

Helenus, the eldest son of Diocles, reigned after his father for fourteen years, a wanton and idle man, in whose time the Gauls, often making raids, inflicted many losses on the Sicambri. Against them his brothers, with the princes of the kingdom, frequently led the army and fought with varying fortune, sometimes defeated and sometimes also victorious. But the king remained at home, idle and given over to lust.

In the thirteenth year of his reign, the Sicambri, assembled at Neopagus, by the common judgment of all deposed Helenus, an idle and useless king, from the kingdom and compelled him to return to private life, in the year 283 before the birth of Christ our Savior. In his place they elected his younger brother.

Basanus, the younger son of the late Diocles, after his brother Helenus had been deprived of rule, was appointed king by the common will and election of the Sicambri, and reigned for [unclear] years: a man wise in his generation, high-minded, warlike, and not inferior to his father in fortune. The Sicambri made him both king and pontiff of the sacred rites. Because of his wisdom and his wonderfully performed deeds they commonly called him great Basan, in the vernacular die groten Basan. For the Sicambri held him in such reverence and honor that they venerated him no differently than a true god. Under penalty of certain death, no one was permitted to contradict his command, even in the least matter. Wherever he went, he ordered a rope or cord to be carried before him, and a sword as a sign of justice, which he observed so firmly that he spared neither son nor brother, neither princes nor dukes, indeed no one, who had committed even a small offense against the law. By law he marked robbery, theft, adultery, deceit, fraud, and every injury or insult among the Sicambri as subject to the penalty of death, and he punished without any mercy all who offended even a little in these matters against the legal sanctions.

He had a young son named Sedanus. This man, led astray by youthful desire, had committed with the wife of a certain man of humble condition what he knew to be forbidden by law, and [unclear]. His father the king, learning of this matter, ordered him to be arrested and beheaded with a sword. Because of this, the mother of the young man and the king’s wife, who was the daughter of the king of the Orcades, was moved by very bitter sorrow, and, driven by womanly lack of restraint, cried out against the king: “O cruel tyrant, not king, you who practice cruelty under the title of law, how will you spare others, when you have slain your own son?” To her the king said before all the people: “Woman, it is yours to obey your husband, not to reproach the king. I swear to you by my god, Saturnian Jupiter, that if I were not moved by anger, I would kill you this very hour, you who have always been most dear to me.”

After eight days the king, by the judgment of the princes and nobles of the kingdom, repudiated the woman, although she was very beautiful and uniquely beloved by him above all the rest, and sent her back to her father, the king of the Orcades.
