*Compendium, Book I.*

*Nineteenth.*

Herimer, the elder son of King Clogio, succeeding his father, reigned for twelve years, a warlike man and excessively bold. In the third year of his reign, the Gauls, making an incursion, came as far as the Meuse, devastating with hostile rashness everything that belonged to the jurisdiction of the Franks, except fortifications and castles, and after this they quickly withdrew to their own lands with plunder and spoils.

But afterwards, when this incursion of the Gauls became known to King Herimer, he was moved to anger; and, having summoned to himself the dukes beyond the Meuse, through whose negligence the Gauls had broken in, [unclear] according to the laws of the Franks: whoever is established in ducal authority shall diligently protect the province entrusted to him. But if through his negligence enemies enter the region, if they injure our people, or if they despoil the inhabitants of the land of their property, let him pay half of his goods into the king's purse, and let him no longer be duke, but live deprived of honor. If he willingly permitted entry to the enemies, let him be buried alive or be punished capitally; and let his wives, with his children and all his property, pass to the king's service.

Then, having called to his aid allies, Germans, Theutonici, and Thuringians, King Herimer entered against the Gauls with a heavy multitude and subdued them with so great a slaughter that for many years they could not recover their former strength. Finally, however, in the last year of his reign, when he had again led an army across into Gaul, being excessively bold, not to say rash or presumptuous, and fighting rather incautiously, he was killed by them in battle, in the thirty-second year of the Lord.

*Twentieth.*

Marcomir, brother of the aforementioned King Herimer, who had been slain by the Gauls, succeeding his brother in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned for eighteen years: a man tall in stature, harsh in manners, yet favorable to friends, but feared and terrible to enemies. He engaged in many battles with the Roman Gauls, and often severely afflicted the people of Mainz, Trier, and the Agrippinians together.

In the second year of his reign, the only-begotten Son of God, the Lord Jesus Christ, Savior of the world, born man from Mary, ever a most pure virgin, without mixture with a man, according to that same humanity was crucified at Jerusalem, the city of the Jews, under Pontius Pilate, governor of the Romans; he suffered, died for our salvation, and was buried. He descended to the lower world, snatched the righteous from there, rose from the dead on the third day, and, ascending into heaven, sits at the right hand of the Father, to judge the living and the dead; he lives forever.

In the tenth year of King Marcomir, Claudius Caesar of the Romans led an army into Britain, which we now call England by the customary term, and by force of arms made it, together with the islands of the Orkneys, tributary to the Romans. On the journey there and back, he severely afflicted the Franks dwelling between the Meuse and the Rhine, though not without bloodshed among his own men.

At that time Guiderius, son of the late King Kymbelinus, was reigning in Britain. When he had been slain in battle by the army of Claudius, his brother Arviracus at once rose up in his place; putting the royal diadem on himself and killing many of the Romans, he pursued Claudius as far as the ships. Caesar, seeing that he could not overcome Arviracus, offered peace and gave him his daughter as wife. He recovered the tribute denied to the Romans, and with the help of his son-in-law also led his army across to the islands of the Orkneys.

But Marcomer, the aforementioned king of the Franks, died in the eighteenth year of his reign, in the fiftieth year of the Lord's nativity, in the eighth indiction of the Romans.

*Twenty-First.*

Clodomer, son of the aforementioned King Marcomir, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for twelve years, a restless and very warlike man. Hunibald reports of him that he was most hostile to the Roman Gauls; he desired nothing more than to erase the memory of them. There was scarcely a year in which he did not move an expedition into Gaul at least once.

In the first year of his reign, three suns appeared at the same time, which gradually returned into one. There followed throughout all Europe a great and immense famine, and a mortality not only of men but also of draft animals.

Mainz, destroyed by the Romans, is repaired.

In the ninth year, however, King Clodomer, with a great army of his men, crossed the Rhine and the Meuse and expelled the Gauls, recovering everything previously lost. With the help of the Germans he fought with the Romans at Mainz, but he could not prevent them from repairing the city. He died in the twelfth year of his reign, and in the sixty-second year of the Lord's nativity, in the fifth indiction of the Romans.
