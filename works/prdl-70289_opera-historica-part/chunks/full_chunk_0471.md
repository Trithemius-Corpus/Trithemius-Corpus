Johannes Tinctoris, German by nation, from Cologne, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures, nobly learned in secular philosophy, acute in intellect, an outstanding disputant, who for a long time publicly taught theology in the gymnasium of Cologne, acquired immense praise for his learning. He wrote little works not to be despised, of which the following are cited:

Disagreements of Thomas and Scotus, one book. It begins: "He began to agree in..."
Questions on the Sentences, one book. Also various conferences.
And certain other things. He flourished in the aforesaid gymnasium under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1470.

Nicolaus Perotus of Sassoferrato, bishop of Siponto, a man most learned in the divine writings and second to none in his time in secular letters, an outstanding theologian and a most eloquent rhetorician, sharp in intellect and distinguished in life, wrote certain lucid little works, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these there survives a notable work, divided into two volumes, on the poet Marcus Valerius Martial, very beautifully comprising all the elegance, method, and exposition of Latin speech; he entitled it A Volume of Commentaries on the Latin Language, or, as it is commonly called,

Cornucopia, one book. On the Kinds of Metres, one book.
On the Education of Boys, one book. It begins: "The beard, he flatters."
Various orations and letters.

He is said besides to have written many other things, both in divine letters and in humane letters, and to have translated not a few works from Greek into Latin, but they have not come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and the Roman pontiffs Pius and Paul, in the year of the Lord 1470.

Antonius Roselus, Tuscan by nation, an eminent professor of both laws, a most celebrated interpreter, and greatly adorned with the discipline of the other good arts, outstanding in intellect and eloquent in speech, who taught law for many years in the gymnasium of Padua, wrote many distinguished little works, by which he commended the memory of his name to posterity. But I do not remember ever having seen any of them anywhere. His notable work is, however, cited:

On the Monarchy of the World, one book. Various treatises on the laws.
And many other things.

He died under Emperor Frederick III, in vigorous old age, and was buried at Padua, in the year of the Lord 1467, indiction 15.

Johannes Beetz, German by nation, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man zealous and learned in divine writings, not ignorant of secular literature, an excellent theologian, a subtle philosopher, sharp in intellect, distinguished in life, an outstanding disputant of questions, notable in delivering sermons to the people, wrote certain distinguished little works. Of these the following are cited:

On Paul's Epistle to the Romans, one book.
A Preceptory of the Divine Law, one book. It begins: "Sacred Scripture of the divine..."
On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book. Sermons for the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book. Various disputed questions, one book.

He published besides some other things, which have not come to the knowledge of our reading. He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV in his convent at [Tenensi?], in the year of the Lord 1476, indiction 9, on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of August.
