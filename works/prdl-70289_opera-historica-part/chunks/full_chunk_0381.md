Hildegard, abbess of the monastery of Saint Rupert the Confessor, opposite the town of Bingen on the other side of the river Nahe, in the diocese of Mainz, of the order of the holy father Benedict, German by birth, from the county of Sponheim, once a disciple of the blessed virgin Jutta, sister of Megenhard, count of Sponheim, our founder, a virgin of most holy conduct and enlightened from her childhood by divine revelations, whose life, approved by many miracles, spread the fragrance of her holiness into far distant regions, so much so that Eugene III, Anastasius IV, and Hadrian III, Roman pontiffs, visiting her very frequently by letters, commended themselves and the Roman Church to her prayers. And Eugene III, in the council of Trier, with Saint Bernard present, publicly approved her writings and small works when they had been read before all, and again strengthened her by letters to persist in the undertaking she had received. All the things that she committed to writing she learned by divine revelation.

Among these are the following:

She also wrote other things. In all her small works Catholic doctrine shines forth, which either confirms faith or instructs morals; nor did she ever say or write anything that could be called into doubt. And although she was ignorant of Latin speech, nevertheless, as the Spirit of God revealed it, she dictated everything in Latin and fittingly, with notaries taking it down. We have seen many letters of Conrad III and Frederick I, emperors, and of the bishops of Mainz, Trier, Cologne, Bremen, Jerusalem, and of others almost innumerable, who, stirred by the fame of her holiness and revelations from various regions of the world, commended themselves in writing to her prayers and sought solutions. She died under Emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1180, at the age of 82, in the thirteenth indiction, on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of October.

Albert, patriarch of Jerusalem, a learned and most holy man, who gave a rule to the Carmelite brothers while they were still dwelling in the Holy Land, which those same brothers, now established in Europe, profess everywhere after it had been modified and approved by the Apostolic See, at the urging of Pope Innocent III described in a clear and truthful discourse:

The State of the Holy Land, one book. The Rule of the Carmelites.

He flourished under Emperor Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1190.

Innocent III, pope, Campanian by birth, previously called Lotharius, after Pope Celestine III presided over the Church for eighteen years. He was very learned in the divine Scriptures, since from his childhood he had devoted himself to letters in the school at Paris, and he became a man no less upright than learned. He left not a few small works of his genius, of which the following are circulated under the name of Lotharius the Levite, or also of Pope Innocent:

On the Usefulness of the Human Condition, two books. “Why from the womb of the mother...”

On the Seven Penitential Psalms, one book. “There are three things especially in which...”

On the Office of the Mass, one book. On the Cloister of the Soul, one book.
