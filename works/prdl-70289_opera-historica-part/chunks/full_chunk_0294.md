At last, when persecution was raging against the Christians, together with almost all the people of his city he was crowned with martyrdom for the Catholic faith on the fourth day before the Kalends of June. He flourished under Marcus Antoninus, in the year of the Lord 175.

Theodotion, by birth an Ephesian, once a disciple of Tatian while he was still a faithful philosopher, certainly a man most learned in every respect, was the third translator of the divine Scriptures. Besides the translation of the Scriptures, which he made from Hebrew into Latin, he also composed certain other little works, which, however, have not yet come into our hands. He flourished in the times of the emperor Commodus, in the year of the Lord 180.

Rhodon, an Asian by race, once a hearer and disciple of Tatian, a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and also in secular letters, is said to have written many things in Greek speech, copies of which are rarely found among our people. Of these, however, the more important are said to be those which we have set down below:

Against Marcion, three books. To Callistion, one book.
Against the Cataphrygians, one book. On the Hexaemeron, six books.

He flourished under the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 200.

Clement, presbyter of the church of Alexandria, once a hearer and disciple of Pantaenus, a Stoic philosopher and Christian, who after him held the ecclesiastical school at Alexandria for many years, was a devout, learned, and most eloquent man. He wrote many excellent volumes:

Stromata, that is, Miscellanies, eight books. On Disputation, one book.
Against the Gentiles, one book. On the Salvation of the Rich Man, one book.
The Paedagogue, three books. On Detraction, one book.
On Easter, one book. Against the Judaizers, one book.
On Fasting, one book. On Ecclesiastical Canons, one book.

He also published many letters and various treatises, whose titles we do not know. He flourished in the times of the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 200.

Melciades, whom Rhodon recalled in his work which he composed against the Cataphrygians, Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, the mad prophets, was a man most learned in secular letters and trained by constant study in the divine Scriptures. He wrote no few little works for the edification of the faithful. Of these the following are reported:

Against Montanus, one book. Against the Jews, one book.
Against the Gentiles, one book. An Apologetic for Our Faith, one book.

He is also said to have written many other things which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the times of Marcus Antoninus Commodus, in the year of the Lord 170.

Apollonius, a certain learned and most eloquent man, distinguished no less in life than in eloquence, a most valiant destroyer of heretical depravity, composed many things on behalf of our faith. He often refuted the madness of Montanus in words and writings, as is clear to those who read that notable volume which he is said to have composed with the greatest charms of eloquence:

Against Montanus, one book.

But he published several other things which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 180.

Serapion, formerly an anchorite, the eighth bishop of the church of Antioch, a man of keen intellect, very zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, venerable no less for his life than for his knowledge, is said to have forged several little works for the benefit of believers, in which he showed himself a learned man and a most keen defender of the Christian faith. Among their number are held:

To Carinus on Montanus, one book.
On the Gospel under the Name of Peter, one book.
Short but Numerous Letters, one book.

He was ordained bishop of the aforesaid city under Commodus, ruler of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 193, in the first indiction.
