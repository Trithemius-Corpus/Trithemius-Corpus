Johannes of Regiomonte, a German by nation, formerly a pupil of the astronomer Georgius Burbachius, not inferior to Anaximander of Miletus or Archimedes of Syracuse, most learned in Greek and Latin eloquence, the glory of the Germans, was granted a perpetual stipend on account of astronomy and the other mathematical disciplines by Matthias, king of Pannonia, and by the council and citizens of Nuremberg; he obtained great praise for his learning not only among his own Germans, but also among the Italians and the French. For, when he had been summoned to Rome by Sixtus, the supreme pontiff, to correct the Dionysian computation of Easter, while he was preparing many things for posterity, he died of a destructive disease, not without the very great loss of scholars. He wrote certain excellent little works, of which the following are extant.

A very beautiful Calendar, one book.
Ephemerides, that is, [a diary?], one book.
Against Gerard of Cremona, one book. After we put forth the index,
Of letters and orations, one book.
And very many other things.

He died at Rome under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1476, in the ninth indiction.

Gerardus of Elten, a German by nation, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, sharp in intellect and distinguished in life. After he had taught theology gloriously for many years in the school of Cologne, holding the vanities of the world in contempt, in his old age he entered the order of preachers for the love of God in the same city of Agrippina, and soon was appointed inquisitor and exterminator of heretical depravity. Among other things, as they report, he wrote:

Questions on the Sentences, one book.
Disputed Questions, one book.
Sermons and collations, one book.
Various things also in philosophy, one book.
And very many other things. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and the supreme pontiff Sixtus IV, in the year of our Lord Jesus Christ 1475.

Alexander Tartagnus of Imola, a jurist, studious and learned, and well experienced also in the other institutions of secular disciplines, excellent in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, wrote, for the use of those who keep watch in the study of both laws, certain excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of their number the following are reported:

On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, one book.
On the Clementines also, one book.
On the Code also, nine books.
On the Old Digest, twenty-four books.
On the New Digest, twelve books.
On the Infortiatum, fourteen books.
And certain other things. He flourished in the time of Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1470.

Johannes Soreth, a Norman by nation, the twenty-fifth prior general of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, sweet and dear in eloquence, devout in life and conduct, and singularly religious and holy; the honor, glory, splendor, and reformer of the Carmelite order, such as our ages have rarely seen and future ages will see more rarely; wholly given to God, wholly intent on reading and prayer, despiser of the world, lover, rule, and mirror of monastic conduct, imitator of angelic purity, guardian of virtues, most bitter enemy of vices, wrote many excellent little works of his intellect worthy of commendation, of which the following are extant:

On the Sentences, four books.
Various sermons, one book.
On the rule of his order, one book.
A larger commentary on the same, one book.
He arranged the Constitutions, one book.
He wrote also very many other things.

He died at Angers under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus, in the year of the Lord 1471, in the third indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of August.
