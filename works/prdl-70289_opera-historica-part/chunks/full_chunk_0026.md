And for this reason both kings, in the division of the kingdom, determined that each should preserve everywhere the ancient liberty of the other unimpaired.

But the king of Gallic France, in the division of the monarchy, received as his portion the kingdom of Neustria, or Paris, and also the kingdom of Orléans, the kingdom of Aquitaine, the kingdom of [Toulouse?], the kingdom of Armorica, or New Britain, with all the provinces and peoples of inner Gaul, as far as Spain: from the river Rhône as far as the western sea.

After that division of the kingdom of the Franks, I too compressed the history, henceforth leaving aside the Gallic Franks; in the second and third volumes of the Annals I continued the narrative concerning only the German Franks. But since, in so long a course of years, reckoning first from the entrance of the people into Germany, the history became rather lengthy, I judged it worth the effort if from those three volumes of annals I should draw out as many shorter books, which would contain in summary the names, successions, and deeds more worthy of remembrance of the kingdom of the Franks of Germany. For I know that your most reverend Lordship both delights in ancient histories and, by reason of administering your principality, is continually occupied in many affairs. Since, therefore, no prince lives today among mortals to whom, after God, I am by right more bound and more ready, with the most submissive will, to serve and please according to my strength and beyond, than to your most excellent and most reverend Lordship, I judge no one more worthy to whose name I either ought or wish to dedicate these lucubrations of mine concerning the origin of the Franks. Therefore, I pray, may your gracious Lordship kindly receive the work of compendium which I offer, and hold me, your humble servant, commended.

In the year 1515.
