Laurentius Justinianus, Venetian by nation, of the Celestine order, and afterward patriarch of Venice, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, distinguished in character and life, although he could have possessed the greatest riches and honors of this world, entered the aforesaid order for love of Christ and contempt [of the world]. In that order, [unclear] in great religion and abstinence, he published very devout little works for the edification of the simple. Their titles are given below.

The Tree of Life, 1 book; On Monastic Discipline, 1 book; On the Marriage of the Word and the Soul, 1 book; On the Struggle of Christ, 1 book; On Interior Conflict, 1 book; On the Lamentation of the Church, 1 book; On the Sacrament of the Altar, 1 book; On the Solitary Life, 1 book; On Contempt of the World, 1 book; On the Lord’s Discourse at Supper, 1 book; On the Pastoral Office, 1 book; also On Obedience, 1 book; On the Degrees of Perfection, 1 book; Forty Sermons, 1 book.

He flourished in the time of Rupert, King of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 1410. He was buried at Venice in the cathedral church. At his tomb many miracles are divinely performed. [unclear]

Augustinus of Rome, of the order of the Hermit Friars of St. Augustine, prior general, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and also nobly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect and renowned for eloquence, wrote many excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there survive:

On the Sentences, 4 books; On All the Epistles of Paul, 14 books; On the Apocalypse of John, 1 book; On Free Will, 1 book; On Original Sin, 1 book; On Christ and the Church, 1 book; On the Love of Christ toward the Elect, 1 book; On the Perfect Righteousness of the Church, 1 book; On the Power of the Pope, 1 book; On the Books of the Ethics, 8 books.

He is said besides to have written many other things, but they have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the times of Emperor Sigismund and Pope John XXIII, in the year of the Lord 1410.

Baldus of Perugia, once hearer and pupil of Bartolus of Saxoferrato, the most celebrated jurisconsult of all his time, and also nobly learned in secular philosophy, acute in intellect, profound in understanding, cautious and steady in counsel, wrote many distinguished little works in both laws, by which he won eternal memory for his name among posterity. Of their number the following are given:

On the Code, 9 books; On the Old Digest, [24?] books; On the New Digest, 12 books; On the Infortiatum, 1 book; On the Institutes, 1 book; On the Second Book of the Decretals, 1 book; Additions to the Speculum, 1 book; Many Legal Opinions, 1 book; and several other things.

He died in the time of Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1423, in the first indiction. He was buried with honor at Pavia, in the convent of the Friars Minor of St. Francis, in the month of July.

Angelus of Perugia, brother of the aforesaid Baldus and his companion and associate in studies, most learned in civil law, renowned in intellect, and very celebrated in fame and reputation among jurisconsults, also composed many volumes in his own faculty. By establishing monuments of excellent talent through them, he sent the memory of his name to the knowledge of many. Of their number the following very elegant commentaries are reported:

On the Code, 9 books; On the Old Digest, [24?] books; On the New Digest; On the Institutes, [unclear]; On the Infortiatum. Indeed, he is said to have labored over the whole body of civil law, and certain other things. He died in the time [unclear].
