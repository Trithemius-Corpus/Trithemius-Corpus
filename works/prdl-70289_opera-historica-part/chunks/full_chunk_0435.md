Johannes Gluel, a German by nation, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, prior at Cologne, born in the imperial city of Aachen, a man excellently learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature, of excellent and distinguished talent in delivering sermons to the people. Among the other little works of his study he wrote a volume not to be rejected by the followers of this order, on the origin and progress of his order, which he entitled:

The Mirror of the Carmelites, one book. Sermons on the season, one book. Lenten sermons, one book: “Lay up treasures for yourselves.” Sermons on the saints, one book.

He flourished in his convent at Cologne, writing and composing various little works, where at last, failing and meeting his day, he was buried in peace.

Henricus Eutafiuue Oyta, a German by nation, the singular ornament and co-founder from the beginning of the school of Vienna in Austria, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly taught in Aristotelian philosophy, subtle in talent, scholastic in speech, and an excellent preacher of sermons. He wrote many distinguished volumes, of which very few have thus far come into our hands. I have read only:

On the Sentences, four books. Concerning the prologue of the first, one book: “Love justice, you who...” On contracts, one book. “Conjurator virgo de Isra[el?].” And certain sermons. Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished in the school of Vienna, under Emperor Wenceslaus, with Henricus de Hassia, in the year of the Lord 1390.

Henricus of Andernach, a German by nation, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and an excellent interpreter of Aristotelian philosophy, outstanding in talent and, among all men of his time, famous as a preacher of sermons, published little works of no negligible authority, of which the following are reported: a distinguished work:

On the Sentences, four books. Sermons on the season, one book. Sermons on the saints, one book. Questions of philosophy, one book.

And certain other things which have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading. He flourished chiefly in the convent of Cologne, where he is also said to be buried.

Blasius Andernarius, a Frenchman by nation, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the holy Scriptures and nobly practiced in the arena of scholastic discipline, distinguished in talent and an excellent disputant. He wrote several distinguished volumes, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Among their number there stands out a lucid and notable work:

On the Sentences, one book. On the Institutes, one book. On the Code, one book. He is also said to have written some things in moral philosophy, but they have not come to me. And very many other things.

Bartholomaeus de Saliceto, jurist, most celebrated of all men of his time, subtle in talent and distinguished in eloquence, whose sayings are held in great authority among professors of civil law, published certain lucid little works on the laws, by which he consecrated the memory of his name to immortality among posterity, of which the following are reported.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Boniface IX, in the year of the Lord 1390.
