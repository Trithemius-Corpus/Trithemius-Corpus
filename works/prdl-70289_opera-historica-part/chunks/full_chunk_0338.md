AVITUS, bishop of Vienne, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and most skilled in secular letters, a very distinguished theologian, rhetorician, and poet, who in his time was held in esteem no less for holiness of life than for learning in the Scriptures. He wrote not a few small works, both in verse and in prose, but very few have come to my knowledge. Of these, the following are said to have been composed in heroic and elegant meter:

On the Origin of the World, one book.
On Original Sin, one book.
On the Sentence of God, one book.
On the Flood of the World, one book.
On the Crossing of the Red Sea, one book.
On Virginity, to Fuscina his sister, one book.
Letters to various persons, nine books.
Many Homilies, one book.
And many other things. He flourished under the emperors Zeno and Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

GODELBERTUS, priest, learned in the divine Scriptures by continual exercise, rich in the reading of the ancients, and most learned in secular letters; a profound theologian, a renowned orator and notable poet, subtle in talent and ornate in eloquence. He wrote in heroic meter a most polished work, proceeding most elegantly through histories and allegories from the beginning of the world to the birth from the Virgin. The title of this work is:

Allegories of the Scriptures, four books.

But he also wrote many other things. He flourished, as they say, in the times of the emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

EUGIPPIUS, priest and abbot, learned in the holy Scriptures, singularly devoted to the reading of the books of Saint Augustine, and sufficiently instructed in the letters of the secular arts; also distinguished in life and conduct according to monastic institution. Some letters of Saint Fulgentius of Ruspe are said to have been addressed to him. He wrote certain necessary little works. Of these I have found only the following. He excerpted, from the books of the blessed bishop Augustine, a large volume having three hundred chapters, addressed to Proba. It begins thus:

Prologue, Letter 1: “To the lady deservedly venerable.”
The book itself thus, book 1: “That [unclear] wrote [unclear].”
The Life of Saint Severinus the Monk, one book.
His Rule of Monks, one book.

He flourished under the emperor Anastasius, according to Honorius the priest. But according to Sigebert, he flourished then under Tiberius Constantine, in the times of Pope Pelagius II, in the year of the Lord 580.

BENEDICT, first abbot and founder of Cassino, by homeland a man of Nursia, founder and chief of the monks of the West, a man of most holy conduct and illumined by the prophetic spirit; from whose order many men, both most learned and most holy, afterwards became famous. By word and example he converted countless peoples from iniquity to Christ. From his order the Roman see has had eighteen pontiffs; more than two hundred cardinals; sixteen hundred archbishops in various churches; nearly four thousand bishops; fifteen thousand seven hundred notable abbots who became famous for learning and writings or for life. And those canonized and entered in the catalogue of saints, as we are instructed by the monuments of the ancients, number fifteen thousand six hundred, apart from those whose names in all these categories have not come down to us. And concerning these illustrious men of our order we have already written four special books, in which we have recorded the names of some of them, though not of all.

Saint Benedict wrote, in lucid language and with excellent discretion:

The Rule of Monks, one book. “Listen, O son, to the precepts.”

It has seventy-three chapters.

Under this rule twenty orders serve, which we have set forth one by one in the first book On the Illustrious Men of Our Order, although among us some are rare. He flourished under Justin the Elder, in the year of the Lord 530. And he died, as some maintain, in the year of the Lord 542, indiction 5, on the twenty-first day of the month of March, when he was sixty-two years old, on the Saturday of holy Easter. But Marianus Scotus, the excellent computist, writes otherwise, saying thus: “If the holy father Benedict, abbot, as some write, died on 12 Kalends of April, on the Saturday of holy Easter, in the year of the Lord 601, there seems to be an obelus. For unless it was in the ninety-sixth year before this year, his death is not found, because then, if he was born, he was very small.” Therefore the holy father Benedict died, according to this calculation of Marianus, in the last year of the emperor Maurice, namely in the year of the Lord 601, at more or less his ninetieth year of age, indiction 4, 12 Kalends of April.

BOETHIUS Manlius Severinus, Roman ordinary consul, philosopher, orator, and poet
