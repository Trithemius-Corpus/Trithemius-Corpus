*Annals of Trithemius.*

... held office for twelve years. Clodoucus begot Gosvertus, who held office for twenty-six years. In his sixth year Conon, pope of Rome, ordained Saint Kilian, a Scottish monk, as bishop and sent him to the Eastern Franks to preach the faith of Christ. Gosbert begot the younger Gosbert, who held office for nineteen years. The younger Gosbert begot Hetan, who held office for twenty years. When, with him, the male line had died out, in the year of the Lord's nativity 740, in the eighth Roman indiction, the duchy of Eastern Francia remained without a duke for twelve years, under the authority of Pippin, mayor of the palace and afterwards king of the Franks, who entrusted it to his son Charles the Great.

Thus there were in Eastern Francia twenty-one dukes, succeeding one another over 414 years; they make up the sacred sevenfold number tripled in order, and not foreign to mystery. In the year of the Lord's nativity 752, in the fifth Roman indiction, Pippin, raised from mayor of the palace to king of the Franks, as was said above, handed over the often-mentioned duchy of Eastern Francia to Saint Burchard, first bishop of the church of Wurzburg, and to his successors, as perpetual property.

From that time, apart from the bishops of the church of Wurzburg, down to the present day no one by right has been able to claim for himself the duchy of the aforesaid Francia, although by the rashness, not the right, of certain kings and emperors rebellious against the Roman Church and excommunicated, as if in vengeance for themselves, the contrary was sometimes attempted. Among these Conrad, the third of that name, afterwards emperor, since he was Swabian by nation, violently usurped for himself the duchy of Eastern Francia by royal permission; nevertheless, after he had been made Caesar, he restored it to the church with manifold satisfaction.

Let these things, briefly touched upon in a necessary historical digression concerning the origin, genealogy, and succession of the dukes of the people of Eastern Francia, suffice. Let us now return to the order of the kings of the Franks, from which we departed. Clodomir, king of the Franks, after many deeds bravely performed, at last died in the eighteenth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord 337, in the tenth Roman indiction.

*The Thirty-Seventh.*

Richimer, the elder son of King Clodomir, succeeding his father, reigned for thirteen years. Throughout all the days of his reign he was always in contention with the Romans and Gauls; with the aid of his uncle Genebald, that is, the duke of Eastern Francia, residing at the city [unclear], and of the other princes of the Franks, as well as the Thuringians, Saxons, and fierce German allies, he fought against them many times as victor and afflicted them with great slaughter.

In the fourth year of his reign, having gathered the support of his allies with a very great multitude of fighters, he entered Gaul and laid everything waste with fire and sword. Against him Tiberianus, prefect of the Romans, also moved a great army of his men to war; when their forces were joined for battle, they fought for a long time with doubtful fortune, and many fell slain on both sides. At last, however, the Gauls were defeated and compelled to seek safety in flight.

In the sixth year of King Richimer, in the fourth indiction, the Romans, gathering in troops into one body with the Gauls, under Constantine the Great, Caesar, declared war on the Franks. King Richimer, since he was a high-souled, strong, and bold prince, was in no way terrified; as usual he summoned as allies the Teutons, Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians. When these had been united with the Franks, he set against the Romans and Gauls an army of almost two hundred thousand, with whom he fought vigorously. In the first engagement the Romans were victorious; in the second, defeated and overcome, they fled with loss among their men.

In the last year of his reign, Richimer, king of the Franks, when he was [unclear] about to fight again with the Romans, was killed in battle, namely in the thirteenth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord's nativity 350, in the eighth Roman indiction.

*The Thirty-Eighth.*

Theodomir, son of the aforementioned King Richimer, reigned after the death of his father for ten years. Throughout all the days of his reign he lived in contention with the Romans and fought many battles against them with varying outcome. His mother, named Hafila, was the daughter of the king of the Saxons. At that time the kings of the Franks lived in the territories of the Tungri, across the river Meuse, in the fortress which antiquity called Dispargum. From the boundary of the Franks as far as the river Loire the Gauls dwelt under the rule of the Romans; beyond the Loire were the Goths, intermixed with peoples of various nations.
