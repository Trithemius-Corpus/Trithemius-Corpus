*Preface of Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, to the Book on the Illustrious Men of the German Nation, to Jacobus V. [Sletslatinus?].*

Germany, which, because of its fertility in all good things, could rightly be called Germania, if Caesar did not think the contrary, from the beginning of its conversion to the Christian faith has always shone with famous teachers; through their talent and most holy morals it deserved to be illuminated more abundantly than other nations. Of these, many have been inserted into the catalogue of saints; many preceded this custom of canonizing saints in time.

This is proved by the very ancient books composed by them; miracles prove it. Nevertheless, at the request of friends, I have resolved to gather the works of certain talents into this catalogue, intending to provide an opportunity for posterity, so that, where they know that we have stopped, they themselves may by their own zeal complete the praises of the Germans. For I, situated in a corner of Germany, have not had the opportunity to search out the others who are lacking from our catalogue; but those whom I had at hand I offer to our Germans, asking that each one add to ours the writers who come his way, and thus finally learn, in rivalry with us, that they have been and are learned. Nor should it escape anyone that I have placed among the Germans all illustrious writers whom I found to have been Teutons. For the Mosellans and Treverans, although they inhabit a great part of Belgic Gaul, are nevertheless German in customs and language, and for that reason they have not undeservedly been numbered among our Germans. Concerning the other Teutons round about, too, a similar opinion should be held. But let this much about these matters suffice; now, God granting it, let us take up the beginning of writing according to the proposed subject.

*Book of Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, on the Luminaries, or on the Illustrious Men of Germany, who, outstanding in talent, wrote various works.*

Maximus, the seventh bishop of the church of Mainz, a man most holy in life and conduct, nobly learned both in secular letters and in the divine scriptures, was ready in talent, distinguished in speech, and outstanding in action. Since he was an eloquent and celebrated preacher of the divine word, he converted many to the faith of Christ by word and example, not only throughout his own diocese but also through neighboring and more distant regions, sowing the word of faith. He is said to have written many letters to various persons and to have committed many homilies to writing; but antiquity has not allowed any of these to come into our hands. Finally, against the Arians, who in his time did many evils in the Church of God, he composed some excellent works. He flourished at Mainz in the times of the emperors Constantius and Constans, under Pope Liberius, in the year of the Lord CCCLX.

Paulinus, the twenty-eighth archbishop of Trier, by nation indeed an Aquitanian, but in life and conduct for the most part dwelling among the Treverans, disciple of the blessed Maximinus, archbishop of Trier, and his son in baptism, a learned and most holy man, was banished into exile by the Arian heretics for the faith of Christ; in Phrygia he triumphed by a glorious suffering.
