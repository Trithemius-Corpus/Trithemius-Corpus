Antharius, son of King Cassander, reigned after his father for thirty-five years. Hunibald writes of him that he crushed the people of Mainz, Roman colonists, in a wretched disaster, burned and overthrew the city, which afterward is seen transferred and built in that place where now [unclear] and the valley are, on the slope of the mountain by the bank of the Rhine. This pitiable devastation of the Mainzers took place in the sixteenth year of King Antharius, which was the fifty-sixth year before the birth of Christ, as is shown from the sequence of times.

King Antharius entered Gaul with the help of the Germans, burned villages, very often carried off boundless plunder and spoils, caused great devastation throughout Gaul, burned cities, destroyed villages, overthrew castles, broke the strength of the people, and reduced a region long fruitful to solitude.

But when King Antharius had grown old, in the last year of his reign, which was the thirty-seventh before the birth of Christ, the Gauls again burst into the region of the Sicambri and crossed the Meuse. He met them with the army of his own men to fight; there, while he fought too incautiously, he was surrounded by the enemy and [unclear] with twenty thousand Sicambri. This Frankish king was accustomed to fight, not to flee; he was very gentle and beloved by his own people, but always exceedingly to be feared by all enemies.

*Francus, from whom they are called Franks, reigned 28 years.*

In the first year of his reign he contracted a perpetual treaty with the Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians, with the consent of all the dukes and nobles of the Sicambri, confirming the friendship on silver tablets, according to the ancient custom of the people: namely, establishing the terms of the agreements so that the Sicambri, Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians, under distinct kings and princes, should thereafter be regarded as one people, like full brothers, should remain so forever, and that any war which threatened the kingdom of one of these, from whatever people, should concern them all and be repelled with united arms and common expenses. This confederation of kingdoms terrified, more than can be said, the senate and people of Rome; in various ways, by different devices, and with manifold contrivance, they labored, though in vain, to dissolve the treaty contracted by the Germans. From that time the courage and power of the Franks were always suspect to the Romans, who feared, as later happened, that the Germans would gain possession of the monarchy of all Europe. And this was the reason why Octavian Caesar of the Romans afterward, under the birth of Christ, caused the whole world obedient to the Romans to be registered by heads, thinking that by the multitude of the nations the Franks were to be destroyed; at that time the writers of histories still called them peoples of Germany.

But in the third year of Francus’s reign, when the Romans saw that they could by no device dissolve the treaty of the German kings, they again sent the Goths into the borders of the Saxons. Settling for almost ten years in the borders of Germany, they gradually and successively planned to spread themselves into the interior of the aforesaid nation. Meanwhile King Francus often fought with the Gauls, avenged upon them with harsh retaliation the death of his father, and killed many thousands of them during that time. But when the Goths broke more and more each day into the interior of Germany, the Saxons with the Teutons, at last compelled by necessity, resolved to make war on them. Having summoned as allies, according to the agreements, the Sicambri and Thuringians, they led out to battle an army of two hundred and thirty thousand men. But the Scanzians, who are Goths, having themselves summoned allies, set against the approaching Germans an almost innumerable multitude. A most savage battle was joined between the parties in that place which was called Colmasus; with many falling on both sides, they fought from morning until night with doubtful fortune. On the next day the Germans divided the army into three parts and set individual commanders over each; but the Sicambri, fighting very boldly under their king Francus, more frequently shouted the name of their leader Francus as if it were some divine power, crying: “Here Frank! Here Frank!” At last the Goths were defeated, more than eighty thousand of them were slain, and the rest slipped away in flight with [unclear].

*The Seventeenth.*

Francus, the elder son of King Antharius, from whom the Sicambri were thereafter called Franks, as Hunibald is the authority, reigned after his father for twenty-eight years. He was a fierce, bold, warlike man, a most vigorous defender of his people, who avenged the death of his father harshly enough upon the Gauls, waged many wars with foreign nations, always a victorious and glorious triumphator; the people of the Franks not undeservedly boast that he was the first author of their name and honor. This king Francus was accustomed to fight, not to flee; he was very gentle and beloved by his own people, but always exceedingly to be feared by all enemies.
