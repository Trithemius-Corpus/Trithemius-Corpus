John Valeis, otherwise Galensis, English by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, who for a long time gloriously taught theology in the Parisian school, was of such learning and eloquence that he was called the tree of life. He wrote many excellent small works, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. But few of them have come into our hands. For I found a distinguished volume which I judge very necessary and useful for preachers of the word of God, which many commonly call the Commoniloquium, because it contains something concerning every estate; but he himself entitled it:

Summa of Collations, 7 books. “Since the doctors are [unclear].”
On the Sentences, 4 books.
On the Ordering of Life, 1 book. “Since in every business [unclear].”
Handful of Flowers, 1 book. “Abstinence is good in [unclear].”
On the Apocalypse of John, 1 book.
Various Sermons, 1 book.
On Various Subjects, 1 book.

Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

And note that this John began the book called the Handful of Flowers, whose prologue begins, “Poor little Ruth,” but, overtaken by death, did not finish it; afterward Brother Thomas of Hibernia completed the same work. The book itself begins thus according to alphabetical order: “Abstinence is good: to receive food with thanksgiving,” etc. He flourished in the times of Emperor Rudolph, in the year of the Lord 1270.

William Duranti, bishop of Mende, once hearer and disciple of Henry of Susa, whom they call the Speculator, a most skilled doctor in law and very zealous for ecclesiastical observance, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, wrote for Ottobono, cardinal of Saint Hadrian and afterward supreme pontiff Hadrian V, an outstanding work divided into three volumes, which he entitled:

Mirror of Law, 3 books. “To the reverend in Christ.”
Rationale of Divine Offices, 8 books. “Whatever in ecclesiastical [unclear].”
Repertory of Law, 1 book. “By the rust of the first-formed man.”

And certain other things. He flourished under Emperor Rudolph. And he completed his work, the Rationale of Divine Offices, as he himself testifies, in the year of the Lord 1286.

Jacobus de Belvisio, a most distinguished doctor of both laws and a careful interpreter, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, profound in counsel and knowledge, and not ignorant of secular philosophy, is said to have written certain excellent small works in law, by which he transmitted his name to the memory of posterity. Of these I have not yet been able to see any. The following, however, are said to be his:

On the Authentics, 1 book.
On the Use of Fiefs, 1 book.
Additions on the Novels, 1 book.
And various disputations.
And many other things.

He flourished under Emperor Rudolph, in the year of the Lord 1270.

William of Lyon, of the Order of Preachers, as they say bishop of the same church, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, gentle in intellect and subtle, not affected in speech yet well composed, was quite outstanding in delivering sermons to the people. He is said to have written several small works, by which he greatly instructed both the powerful of that time and those who would come afterward. Of these there are said to be:

On the Sentences, 4 books.
Summa of Virtues, 5 books. “Since concerning individual useful things.”
Summa of Vices, 8 books. “About to speak concerning the individual vices.”
Sermons to the People, 1 book.

He is also said to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

“Since, as blessed Augustine testifies.”
