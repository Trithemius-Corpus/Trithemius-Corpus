With great expense and long labor he collected the works of Johannes de Gerson, formerly Chancellor of Paris, of which at last he brought many to us from France, dividing them into three parts in a very fine order and making a quite useful table of all the books; he gave that whole work to the press. Therefore he deservedly ought to be loved in a special way by all learned and devout men, since by his labors and expenses he brought to light so many volumes of that most learned and most devout doctor. He is still living, fifty years old, in the city of Strasbourg, under the Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1495, in the thirteenth indiction.

Johannes Wyric of the town of Neuss, a native of the diocese of Cologne, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, of the convent of Cologne, provincial prior of Lower Germany, doctor of divine Scripture, a learned interpreter, a very acute expounder of Aristotelian philosophy, ready in intellect, orderly in speech, devout in life and conduct, a sharp disputant, an excellent preacher of the divine word. He wrote certain commendable little works, by which he made himself useful to those now living and memorable to those to come. Among them there is an outstanding volume on the Sentences, four books; also many sermons to the people, and various questions disputed in the schools; but certain other things are to be brought to light in their own time. He is still living in mature age, residing at Cologne, under the Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord 1495, in the thirteenth indiction.

Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, Bishop of Worms, a most celebrated jurisconsult, most learned both in divine Scripture and in secular letters, skilled in the three principal languages, Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, a very distinguished rhetor and poet, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, notable for experience of many matters, who magnificently adorns the nobility of his family by his character and learning. He wrote and writes, both in verse and in prose, certain works of excellent talent, and he has not yet allowed them to come to light, because, being too much occupied with the affairs of the commonwealth, he cannot complete them to the degree that is his purpose. Yet some of them have become known to us: a Sapphic poem on the death of Rudolf Agricola, his teacher in Greek; [unclear]; On the Origin and Theory of Coinage, one book; very many elegant orations; also many poems and epigrams; and several very elegant letters to various persons; and certain other things. He is still living, [unclear] forty, [unclear] he will write various things in Latin, under the Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1495, in the thirteenth indiction.

Johannes de Arundine, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin [unclear], [unclear] of Camerarius, Bishop of Mechlin, Bishop of [unclear], a man studious and learned in divine Scripture, and also excellently instructed in secular philosophy, which they call Aristotelian; subtle in intellect, clear in eloquence, notable as a declaimer of popular sermons. He wrote many excellent volumes, of which the following are reported: a notable lecture on the Book of Wisdom, one book; on the Epistle of Paul to the Romans, one book; sermons on the psalm “Blessed are the undefiled,” one book; also sermons for the season and on the saints, two books. He is said to have written many other things too, which have not come to my knowledge. He is still living in the city of Lower Utrecht, being vicar of the bishop of that place in pontifical matters, under the Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1495, in the fourteenth indiction.

Johannes Capnion, otherwise Reuchlin, a native of the town of Pforzheim in Swabia, my teacher in Greek, an outstanding doctor of imperial law, and counsellor of the illustrious duke Eberhard of Württemberg, at once most eloquent and most upright in law and faith, a most diligent investigator of all the most ancient philosophy and literature, a most skilled interpreter of the three principal languages, Hebrew together with Chaldaic, likewise Greek and Latin. He is also especially distinguished in French and in the more polished form of our vernacular, most learned in every respect both in divine writings and in secular letters, since he has for many years penetrated with the greatest effort the secret mysteries of the Greeks and Hebrews. Truly he is worthy to devote himself to letters alone, not also to be burdened in the noisy popular court. For not only Italy, but not even our age, could set against us anyone equal to so great a man. He wrote many excellent volumes, by which he commended the memory of himself to posterity.
