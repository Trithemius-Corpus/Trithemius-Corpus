*Johannes Trithemius*

... made himself useful to those present and memorable to posterity. Of these there is extant a notable Summa on contracts, insofar as it pertains to the forum of conscience and to theological decision, divided into more than one hundred questions, seven books. There is also a not inelegant oration on the reform of monks. The other things that he wrote he always strove so much to keep hidden that not even the bond of friendship could ever induce him to reveal them to me. He sent letters to me and to several others. He is still living, directing the theological school in the college of Tuebingen, a town of Swabia belonging to Duke Eberhard of Wuerttemberg, and he is writing various works, under King Maximilian, in the year of the Lord 1495.

Conrad Celtis Proticius, an East Frank, born at Schweinfurt, a man most learned in every respect, a famous philosopher, an eloquent orator, a distinguished astronomer and cosmographer, and a poet laureate, not ignorant also of the divine Scriptures, skilled in Greek and Latin, excellent in intellect and fluent in speech. He wrote many splendid volumes in meter and prose, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of these he once read the following to me: To King Maximilian, on both Rhetorics of Cicero, an epitome, that is, a compendium or breviary, one book; a poem on the praises of eloquence, one book; On the Art of Versifying, one book; On Composing Letters, one book; On the Site of the City of Noricus Mons, which work he entitled Nuremberg, one book; his Sarmatian Journey, one book; Poems or Epigrams, four books; Loves, which according to the four sides of Germany are inscribed [Istulae], [four?] books; a Panegyric to the Duke of Bavaria, one book; a Sapphic poem on the life of Saint Sebald, patron of the people of Nuremberg, one book; Germania, one book; many and various orations, one book. He has issued very many most elegant letters, both to me and to others, in meter and prose, and in cosmography, of which he is most learned, he has written various things which hereafter, in their own time, will come to light. He is still living in the college of Ingolstadt in Bavaria, lecturing ordinarily on poetry and oratory, being twenty-five years of age, under Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1495, in the fourteenth indiction.

Johannes of Veteri Aqua, otherwise of Gouda, a Hollander by homeland, a Brabanter by upbringing, of the order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, of the convent of Mechelen, at this time subprior of Kassel in Hesse, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures and skilled by long practice, of excellent talent in delivering sermons to the people, and a fervent lover of his order and a devout cultivator of it. For the instruction and edification of many brothers he wrote a commendable work on the beginning and progress of his order, which he entitled The Three-Part Fascicle of Times of the Order of Carmelites, four books. In the first of these he proves that Elijah and the prophets were founders of the Carmelite order; in the second he enumerates the saints and canonized persons from the same order; in the third he places in chronological order the successions of the priors general of his whole order. He also wrote On the Most Pure Conception Ever of the Blessed Virgin Mary, one book; also countless sermons to the people, two books; letters both to me and to others, almost infinite in number. He wrote besides many letters against a certain Wigand, of the Order of Preachers, lecturer of the Frankfurt convent, who rashly attacked the most pure conception of the Mother of God. For he, attacking with mad loquacity the seventh chapter of our treatise on Saint Anne, wrote against me a long and impudent letter, in which he signed himself “the one weighing,” and fraudulently suppressed his own proper name. To his rashness answer has been made quite sufficiently, both by this Johannes the Carmelite and by other learned men. For I, writing back only one letter, did not wish to contend any longer in words with a Barthologus. The other things which this Johannes composed have not come into our hands. He is still living in the convent at Kassel, holding the office of subprior, and is writing various works. He is more than sixty years of age, under Emperor Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1495, in the fourteenth indiction.

Rutger Sicamber of Venray, by homeland a Gelderlander, of the order of the Canons Regular of Saint Augustine, of the monastery of the holy Apostle Peter of Dumetum, commonly called Heyen, not far from Worms, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and outstandingly learned in secular letters, sharp in intellect, eloquent in speech, devout in life and conduct, excellent in meter and prose. He wrote, both in verse and in prose, many commendable little works, the reading of which is no less useful than pleasant. Of these I have read the following: A Dialogue on the Education of Boys, one book; A Dialogue on the Inquiry into the Blessed Life, two books; Hymns on Saint Mary in various kinds of meter, two books, which contain almost twelve thousand verses; A Dialogue on the Various Use of Letters, three books; A Dialogue on [unclear]...
