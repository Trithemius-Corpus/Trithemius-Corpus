*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

He flourished under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1280.

[Goffredus?] of Trani, subdeacon of the Roman church and, as they report, chaplain of the supreme pontiff, a man nobly learned in canon law and secular philosophy, outstanding in talent and distinguished in eloquence, who among the doctors of this faculty was not considered the least in his time. He wrote little works of no contemptible reading, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these there is extant a notable and quite widely circulated summa:

On the Titles of the Decretals, 5 books. The Diversity of Glosses, in 1 book.
Various Questions.

Other works which he is said to have composed have by no means come to our knowledge.

Guido de Baysio, archdeacon of Bologna, a man very skilled in both laws, sharp in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, whose authority among the professors of this science has remained great even to the present day. He wrote many notable volumes, by which he rendered the memory of his name, so to speak, immortal. Of these only a few have so far come to my knowledge. For I have found only the following: a large and very notable volume which he entitled Rosarium.

On the Decree, 3 books. “To the reverend in Christ.”
On the Decretals, 5 books.

He flourished under Emperor Adolf, in the year of the Lord 1290.

Thaddaeus, Florentine by nation, a very skilled doctor of the medical art, who taught that science excellently at Bologna for certain years, being hired at a public salary; and, gathering an almost incredible treasure, he acquired a great reputation for his experience. For when he was summoned by some prince, he wished to receive no less than fifty gold pieces as his daily wage. Once summoned by Pope Honorius IV, he refused to come before those stated gold pieces had been fixed for each day; and the pope, cured by him of a very grave illness, bestowed ten thousand gold pieces on him. He wrote:

On the Aphorisms of Hippocrates, 1 book. On the Prognostics of the same, 1 book.
On the Work on Acute Diseases of the same, 1 book. On Galen’s Tegni, 1 book.
On the Isagoge of Johannitius, 1 book. On Preserving Health, 1 book.
Various Consilia, 1 book. And certain other works.

He flourished under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1280. And at last he died at Bologna, an octogenarian.

John of Parma, born from the territory of Bologna, minister general of the Order of Friars Minor, a man learned in the divine scriptures, devout in life and conduct, and a notable lover of holy poverty, who was elected general in his absence from the Parisian school, where he had taught the sciences. He is said to have written many and various treatises, by which he [unclear] his name to our memory; only these have been found:

On the Sentences, 4 books. On the Conduct of Religious, 2 books.

He flourished when the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1260.

John Christophori, of the Order of Friars Preachers, trained in the scriptures by long and continuous study, and nobly learned in secular philosophy, of ready intellect and scholastic in speech, is said to have published many little works on the holy scriptures; the publication of these made him, useful then to those present, known to those to come. For over a long time he expounded the divine letters by public lectures, and explained them with subtle understanding. From his commentaries the following volumes are reported, namely:

On the Gospel of Matthew, 1 book. On Mark, 1 book.
On Luke, 1 book. On John, 1 book.
On all the Epistles of Paul, 14 books. And many others.

He flourished when the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1260.

Hannibaldus, Roman by nation, professor of sacred theology and an excellent interpreter of the scriptures, subtle and forceful in intellect, and sufficiently fit for disputing and resolving the difficult questions of the same, is said to have written certain excellent little works for the instruction of his disciples, by which he made his name known. Among them there are extant:

On the Sentences, 1 book. Quodlibets, 1 book.

He flourished under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1270.

Henry of Ghent, archdeacon of Tournai, German by nation, a man among all the doctors of his time most learned in the divine scriptures, and very subtle in Aristotelian philosophy, was of such reputation and authority in the Parisian school that he was called the Solemn Doctor throughout the whole Christian world.
