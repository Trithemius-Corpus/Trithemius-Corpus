This conflict had remained hidden from the Franks and the Saxons until the contest came to an end; they had pitched their camps not in the same place, but in different stations. But afterwards, when the Franks, together with the Saxons, had learned the cause, they labored with many negotiations for peace and concord between those at variance. Yet since each party complained that it had been gravely injured by wrong, they could not restore full concord between them. They did, however, establish truces, or armistices, of three years between them, in which they ordered the parties to abstain from war until anger should cool with time and the disturbance be quieted.

From that time the Suebi always held the Thuringians suspect of treachery, as Hunibald says, and they were troubled by a hidden and manifest hatred against them, although many do not know the cause of this mutual hostility.

At that time there were great movements of peoples in Britain and in all the adjacent islands neighboring the kingdom of the Franks; in consideration of these, King Clodomir was distressed, fearing the treachery of the Romans, lest, while the army of fighters was absent, they should contrive some new incursion into the kingdom.

Therefore it in no way seemed prudent to the counsel of the Franks, especially in these dangerous times, to divide the people and, neglecting their own affairs, to undertake the defense of others, since perhaps with God’s favor we might suffice for one of the two; for even to protect this our kingdom from the incursion of enemies in these days is not in our power. Moreover, the very great distance of places greatly hinders us from being able to bring you aid so often against the Suebi, since they hold the source and beginning of the river Rhine, while we hold its farthest mouths and its western end. Nevertheless, lest we seem to have been altogether lacking to you in the friendship of the Franks, it has pleased our counsel that we direct our messengers and envoys to the Suebi, through whom we may again obtain for you three-year truces and armistices. Meanwhile let the Thuringians take counsel, so that they may either obtain a lasting peace with the Suebi, or by some other means interpose the necessary wall of defense, that is, either from their own people or from friendly foreign peoples.

When these things had been set forth by the king of the Franks, the envoys answered: Excellent, O king and you Frankish princes, is your counsel, if indeed you will be able to obtain three-year truces from the Suebi. But as for your counsel that we place between ourselves and the Suebi a wall of defense, either from ourselves or from any foreign people, this is the judgment of our king and of all the nobles of the Thuringian people. Hear our consultation concerning this matter, we beg, and at the same time, as faithful friends, provide aid. We have a land broad in extent toward the south, fertile in abundance of fruits, whose furthest boundaries separate the Suebi and the Thuringians. If you wish to place inhabitants from among your people in it, with a duke, this will certainly be a perpetual honor and advantage to you and to the kingdom of the Franks, but to the Thuringians a wall of defense, and to the Suebi a cause of fear. No difficulty concerning the grant will be able to intervene, by which it should not, through the voluntary offering of the princes of the Thuringian people, come into your power.

After consultation had been held on these matters, the Franks gladly agreed, as men who had nothing more at heart than to extend the Frankish name and kingdom at the same time. Therefore, first sending letters and envoys to the Suebi, they obtained peace for the Thuringians for three years, keeping secret their plan concerning the new possession to be undertaken. Then they sent their own men with the aforesaid envoys of their friends into Thuringia, so that they might confirm, according to the judgment of the king and nobles, the proposed business concerning the grant of the promised region. When all these things had been duly completed, the events which we shall write followed.
