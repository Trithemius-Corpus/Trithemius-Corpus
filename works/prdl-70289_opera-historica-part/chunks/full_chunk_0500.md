*Additions of Certain Men* ...we may consider it glorious to displease the malicious, yet we judge it far more glorious to have pleased good and learned men; by them, if there had been error anywhere, we are gladly corrected, and, once corrected, are more fruitfully amended. Let love of the Scriptures excuse us, for whose sake we have taken this heavy burden upon an intrepid neck. *End of the book On Ecclesiastical Writers of Lord Johannes de Trittenheim, Abbot of Sponheim. There follow additions of certain illustrious men.*

*An addition to the foregoing, of illustrious men, partly excerpted here and there, partly recently inserted.*

Marianus, Roman by nation, [Genazzanensis?] by native place, of the order of the hermits of St Augustine, a most skilled theologian and prior general of the whole order, shone forth in this our age, like the morning star and a certain most brilliant sun, by his most exquisite declamations and by a certain new manner of preaching; and, offered to the world as if another vessel of election, he displayed such force of talent and strength in teaching that I would dare to say that no one could ever have been found in this century who [unclear] might be compared with him. Indeed he was of such quality and so great that nothing in any more polished discipline could be vainly desired in him. For in his declamations to the people he was so eloquent and sententious in choice words, and also so suited to the matter by his melodious voice, countenance, and other movements of the body, that you could add nothing to the full measure of praise. How great this man’s learning was, and how great the force and majesty of his eloquence, anyone can clearly gather in part from the sermons and orations most learnedly published by him. For he wrote various sermons; one book of orations; one book of letters. He died, moreover, with great loss to the Christian commonwealth, in the year of the Lord 1500.

Felinus Sandeus, of the most noble family of the Sandei, a native of Ferrara, a most accomplished doctor of both laws and not ignorant of the rest of good letters. First at Ferrara, then at Pisa, in the public university of those cities, he professed canon law to the great admiration of all. Summoned to Rome by Innocent VIII, he was placed in charge of the college of the Rota, where difficult and very important business is handled. Although he was occupied with a thousand affairs, because he was of almost divine and most consummate prudence in both laws, he published many little works and commentaries in it. And these indeed so clearly, so learnedly, so exactly, that he untied all the knots of this discipline, restored obscure matters to the light, and, with right judgment applied to doubtful matters, made them easy and open; in this he brought no small assistance to our jurists. The works which he sent forth are appended: first, on the books of the Decretals of Gregory: On Constitutions; On Rescripts; On the Office of the Ordinary; On Majority and Obedience; On Witnesses; On Compelled Witnesses; On the Credibility of Instruments; On Presumptions; On Oath-taking; On Exceptions; On Prescriptions; On Sentence and Res Judicata; On Appeals; On Accusations and Inquisitions. Additions to the Decretum, to the work begun by the abbot. One treatise On the Right of Patronage. A sermon On Plenary Indulgence, whose theme is: Today you will be with me in paradise, etc. In history, one little book On the Neapolitan Kings. There are also other commentaries of his. He flourished at Rome under Innocent VIII and Alexander VI.

Matthaeus Bossus, a native of Verona, of the order of the regular canons of blessed Augustine, a man excellently learned in every kind of erudition, but still more learned in elegance and gravity of speech, was held in great esteem among all, both from the office of preaching and teaching, in which he was long engaged, and from the various works composed by him, by which he so adorned and polished ecclesiastical dogma with rhetorical embellishments that you would find nothing more refined or more elegant. He wrote the following little works: a Dialogue on Enduring Adversities, divided into two books; On Bearing Office and Cultivating Justice, addressed to Johannes Ludovicus Bossus, jurist and golden knight, one book; Letters to various persons, one book. He also published certain other works, in all of which, since varied learning shines forth, and everywhere [unclear].
