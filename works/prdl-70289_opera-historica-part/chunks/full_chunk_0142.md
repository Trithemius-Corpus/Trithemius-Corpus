*On the Origin of the Franks.*

Genebaldus, the second duke, first-born son of Dagobert, the first duke, held for nineteen years the principate assigned to him by his father. Moreover his two brothers, Marcomerus and Suimo, were dukes in the places marked out for them by their begetter: one, that is, on that side of the Rhine which faces the Saxons, and the other on the other side, where the Sea divides Gaul, Holland. Genebaldus, however, held Tongres and a great part of Gaul, together with Brabant.

This division of the principate did not please the nobles of the Franks, by which they did not doubt that they would become weaker day by day; yet it was quite pleasing to the Romans. At that time Marcomerus, son of Chlodius, fourth in the order of dukes from the time when the Franks had received seats from the Thuringians in that place, ruled over the eastern Franks. His dominion extended from the borders of Bavaria and Bohemia in the east as far as the Rhine, having under him the [Hogii?], the [Meneganii?], and the [Caraganii?], as far as the baths of Alsace. The Romans were never able to subdue this duke, although they tried more than once.

In the second year of Duke Genebaldus, which was the 387th year of the Lord’s Nativity, Maximus, of the stock of the emperors, who had drawn his origin from Britain, whom Octavius, then king, had called from Rome into England and had made his son-in-law and successor to the kingdom, was made emperor through a mutiny of the soldiers, against Gratian. For after he had reigned in Britain for five years in good peace, lifted up in pride by the Roman soldiers whom he had brought with him, he commanded that he be made emperor, and soon sailed with all his soldiery into Gaul against Gratian, in order to subject it to his rule.

First, therefore, he invaded the kingdom of the Armoricans, which is now called New or Lesser Britain, and with the Franks who inhabited it he joined battle under the aforesaid Duke Genebaldus. Iubalchus had been appointed by Genebaldus as commander of the Franks’ war; meeting Maximus with the whole army of Franks that he had, he fought with him, and victory fell to Maximus. In that battle fifteen thousand of the Franks fell, and the duke Iubalchus himself perished. The rest fled; with the little children, [unclear], the old men also, and all the young men, as many as he found throughout all Armorica, he cruelly killed. But he gave that kingdom of New Britain to Iunanus, who had been the nephew of Octavius, king of the English, leaving with him a great soldiery, after, that is, he had made all Gaul subject to himself.

For after Maximus had subjugated all Gaul to himself, Gratian, fleeing from Paris, was captured and killed at Lyon. Maximus set the seat of empire in the city of Trier, where he made his son Victor, still a boy, his associate in the empire; later, when he was about to set out for Italy, he entrusted him to Nannenus and Quintinus.

Meanwhile Conamus of the Armoricans, king of the Britons, wishing to give wives to his soldiers who had none, and to bring over with their children those who had left their own in England, so that they would have no cause for intermingling with the Gauls, sent messengers into England to Dionotus, king of Cornwall, who had succeeded his brother Coradocus in the kingdom. He asked of him that his daughter, named Ursula, a beautiful virgin, be given and sent to him as wife. Likewise, secondly, he asked that he similarly send to him, placed on ships, the wives with the children of the soldiers and nobles who were then in Armorica. Thirdly he asked that, for the soldiers who did not yet have wives, he send virgins.

King Dionotus, wishing to satisfy the prayers of Conamus, gathered from the whole island eleven thousand daughters of nobles, and sixty thousand from the stock of the lower order, that is, in sum, eighty-one thousand; among these were likewise counted the wives of nobles and soldiers with their children. He also ordered ships to be brought from different shores, in which he placed everything necessary for those sailing. And although the voyage pleased many in that company of women, it displeased many others, who loved their parents and fatherland more intensely with greater affection. But the king’s command prevailed.

When the ships had been prepared, the women, together with the virgins, boarded around the river Thames. While they directed the fleet toward Armorica, a great storm arose on the sea, and the ships were separated from one another. The greater part of that whole assembled multitude perished in the clouds on the sea; when the ships collided, it went down into the deep.

But the remaining women and virgins, who were able to escape so great a danger, were driven to barbarous nations and were either slaughtered by an unknown people or consigned to perpetual servitude. A great part of them fell into the army of Gnanius, king of the Huns, and Melga, king of the Picts, who, hired for pay and defending Gratian’s party against Maximus, were laying waste the maritime regions from Spain as far as England with a pitiable destruction by land and sea. Refusing their lust, all were killed along the shore, in the ships, and destroyed to the last one. For they were all Christians.
