Up to this point Hunibaldus continued the history of the Franks, for seventeen hundred years from the destruction of the Trojans. What follows concerning the deeds of the Franks has been taken from Hoduardus, Richer, Hermanfredus, and other authors.

Lotharius, son of Clodoveus, through whom the direct line is continued, reigned after his father at Soissons for fifty-one years. His brothers each reigned in their own places. For Theoderic, son of Clodoveus by a concubine, reigned among the people of Metz for thirty-two years; after him his son Theodebert reigned, that is, at Metz, which contains part of Upper Champagne, and also through Alsace and the Alemanni bordering the Rhine, for fourteen years. His son Theodebald succeeded him and reigned for nine years; when he died without children in the year of the Lord 560, Lotharius received the kingdom of Austrasia.

Clodomir, the third son of Clodoveus, reigned at Orleans. He was killed in war by Gundovald, king of Burgundy, leaving three sons, Theobald, Gunther, and Clodoald, whom, after their father’s death, their grandmother Crothildis kept with her to be raised.

Not long afterward Hildebert and Lotharius killed two of the boys with their own hands; the third, Cloald, slipped away in flight with the help of the nobles and escaped. He became a cleric and lived well.

Hildebert, already mentioned, the fourth son of Clodoveus, reigned for forty-five years. He died in the year of the Lord 559, and was buried at Paris in the church of St. Vincent; Lotharius his brother received his kingdom together with all his treasures. At the same time Theodobald, his nephew, also died, who had held the kingdom of Austrasia of his grandfather Theoderic; this passed to Lotharius. And thus Lotharius alone obtained the whole kingdom of the Franks.

In the tenth year of Lotharius, king of the Franks, Ermenfrid, king of the Thuringians, at the urging of his wife Amalberga, deceitfully killed Bertharius and Baldeticus, his brothers, as if they were aiming at his kingdom. They were, like him, of the noble blood of the kings of the Franks. When this became known to King Theoderic, he was greatly moved, and resolved in his mind to avenge the death of his kinsmen. For Theoderic was staying among the Austrasians in the city of Metz, where he had placed the seat of the kingdom; he ruled over the Austrasians, that is, the people of Metz, Liege, Cologne, Trier, the Westrasii, the Alsatians, and the Alemanni on this side of the Rhine.

Fortunatus assigns another cause of the war, which we shall state briefly. Amelberga, wife of Ermenfrid, king of Thuringia, was the daughter of Clodoveus and Crothildis. She took it very badly that her husband was placed under tribute to King Theoderic, whom she considered unworthy of the kingdom, because he was the son of a concubine of his father Clodoveus, not of the queen. Hence she complained that the kingdom of Austrasia was owed by right rather to her, who was the legitimate daughter of the king and queen, than to Theoderic, who had been born illegitimately. With these and many other words the woman continually harassed Ermenfrid, and finally drove him by her excessive importunity so that, sending letters and messengers to Theoderic, he demanded from him the kingdom of Austrasia, as owed to himself together with his wife, with insulting and abusive reproaches. Moved by this, King Theoderic, when Ermenfrid threatened that he would come into Austrasia with an army of fighters, resolved to forestall him. Therefore, calling to his aid his son Theodebert and his brother Lotharius, kings, he entered Thuringia with a great army of Franks, captured King Ermenfrid with his wife and sons, killed him, and subjected the whole region of the Thuringians to himself. From that time the Thuringian kings ceased down to the present.

When the kings were dividing the booty among themselves, Radegundis, a very beautiful virgin who had been the daughter of King Bertharius, killed by his brother Ermenfrid, fell by lot to King Lotharius. He took her with him into Gaul as a captive and received her as his wife. She shone with great sanctity, and after the death of King Lotharius became a nun.

Lotharius, king of the Franks, undertook many battles. He fought twice with the Saxons: the first time he was victor, the second time he was defeated, with many Franks killed. He himself slipped away in flight and returned with a few men to Soissons. The cause of the war was the matter of tribute.

He had seven sons, one of whom, named Granus, from whom the baths at Aachen are said to be named, [unclear], rebelled against his father. After he was finally captured, Lotharius ordered him to be burned with his wife and two children, with many Franks having been killed.
