Johannes de Imola, by birth a Bolognese, a most celebrated doctor of both laws, excellent in talent and renowned in eloquence, whose learning and eloquence in his own time magnificently illuminated all Italy. He is said to have published many distinguished volumes in both laws, whose authority is raised with the highest praise among the professors of this faculty. Among these the following in particular are reported:

On the three books of the Decretals, 3 books.
On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, 1 book. On the Clementines, 1 book.
On the Digestum Infortiatum, 14 books. Before we come to.
On the Digestum Novum, and certain others.

He died at Bologna in the times of the Emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1436, indiction 14.

Antonius of Parma, prior general of the Camaldolese order, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular literature, excellent in talent, composed in speech, sufficiently suitable in delivering sermons to the people, wrote for the direction of preachers of the word of God some distinguished little works, by which he made his name known. Of these the chief are reported:

Sermons on the season, 1 book. When he had approached.
Sermons on the saints, 1 book. Sermons for Lent, 1 book.

And certain others. He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1420.

Leonardus Aretinus, philosopher, orator, and distinguished historian, and a man most learned in every respect, skilled in the Greek and Latin languages, first secretary of the Roman pontiffs Innocent VII, Gregory XII, Alexander V, and John XXIII down to the Council of Constance, and then, after chancellors had been summoned by the Florentine senate, was appointed general chancellor; in this office he remained until the end of his life with great fidelity and authority. He wrote, among other things:

A distinguished work of dialogues, 1 book. A history of his own time, 1 book.
On the good deeds of the Florentines, 12 books. On the praises of Florence, 1 book.
A history of the Goths, 4 books. A history of the First Punic War, 2 books.
The life of Aristotle, 1 book. On the life of Cicero, 1 book.
A volume of letters, 1 book.

He also composed some other things most elegantly, which have not come to my knowledge. Moreover he translated many things from Greek into Latin, namely the treatise of the blessed priest Basil on the instruction of pupils; he also translated from Plutarch the lives of Tiberius and Gracchus among the Greeks, and of Paulus Aemilius, the later Cato, and Sertorius among the Romans. He likewise translated Xenophon’s little book on the tyrant, Plato’s Gorgias and Phaedo, and certain letters, and Aristotle’s Economics and the books of the Ethics and Politics. And other things which have not come to me. He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1426. He died, however, under the Emperor Frederick III and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1443, indiction 6, on the seventh day before the Ides of March, at almost the seventy-fourth year of his age.

Poggio the Florentine, a philosopher and eloquent rhetorician, perfectly instructed in the Greek and Latin languages, for many years a most eloquent writer of apostolic letters. He is said to have composed some little works of excellent talent, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Laurentius Valla was a very keen rival of this Poggio, and wrote various things against him. He himself published:

On the situation of the region of India, 1 book. Against Laurentius Valla, 2 books.
Letters to various persons, 1 book. Various orations, 1 book.

And very many other things. He made Dionysius Siculus and many other Greek authors Latin. Moreover, at the Council of Constance he delivered very many funeral orations. The work of obscenities which he entitled Facetiae we have judged rightly to be rejected from this catalogue of illustrious men, because the reading of it offends the devout, harms the unwary, and infects the carnal. He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1420.

Laurentius Valla, a Roman patrician, by far the foremost of the grammarians of this age, a most outstanding philosopher, rhetorician, and theologian, subtle in talent, sharp and vehement, preferring Latin eloquence to Greek, composed many things for its adornment. Most skilled in the Greek and Latin languages, he published little works of reading not to be despised, by which he transmitted his name with praise to the knowledge of posterity.
