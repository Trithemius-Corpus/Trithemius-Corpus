He had come, however, when summoned into Britain, with an army of Roman soldiers, and the greater part of the soldiers remained with him, a smaller number returning to Italy. Then, after the death of Valentinian and Valens, the Caesars, in the fifth year of his British reign, lifted up in mind into pride and impatient of peace, he had himself made Caesar of the Romans by those soldiers who had remained with him, against Gratian, whom the Romans had set over them. Therefore, as soon as he was hailed as Caesar by the soldiers, he entrusted the kingdom of Britain to be governed by a deputy, and, summoning into one body all the nobility and the military youth of the whole island of Britain, which is now called England, he sailed with them into Gaul, having this intention in mind: that, once the Gauls had been conquered, he would set out for Rome and subject the empire to himself.

Accordingly, in the ninth year of Marcomir, king of the Franks, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 487, in the fifteenth Roman indiction, Maximus, king of Britain, entered Gaul by ship with a great multitude of his men and laid waste everything round about the region in hostile fashion. And first indeed he landed with his ships on the shore of the land of Armorica, which from that time thereafter has been called New Britain, or Lesser Britain, down to the present day. The Franks then inhabited it, under the governance of Duke Iubalchus, whom the kings of the Franks had appointed deputy of the kingdom in those places. Duke Iubalchus, not a little disturbed by the entry of so great a multitude of peoples, drew together the forces of his men as best he could, and, advancing to meet those who were coming, joined battle with them. In that battle he himself was killed with fifteen thousand Franks, while the rest sought safety in flight.

Then, in order to strike terror of his name into all throughout the kingdom, he used great cruelty: for he slaughtered without mercy women, virgins, old men, young men, and little children, as many as he found. The kings, dukes, nobles, soldiers, and all the people of the Franks were still blinded and polluted by the empty superstition of idols. Maximus, however, although a tyrant, was nevertheless, with all his soldiery and his people, Christian. After this victory over Iubalchus had been won from the Franks, Maximus bestowed that Armorican principality on Duke Conan, who was the nephew by his brother of Octavius, the aforesaid king of Britain; and, leaving with him a good part of the army of the Britons for protection, he himself departed from there with the remaining multitude of troops and, ranging through all Gaul, made those who resisted his rule obedient by war and arms, no less cruelly than powerfully.

Meanwhile Gratian Caesar at Rome, learning that Maximus had usurped for himself the name and diadem of the Roman empire and, having gone out from Britain with a great army, had begun to lay waste Gaul, himself gathered troops and moved against him into Gaul, entering the city of Paris with his army. Afterwards, engaging with Maximus, he was overcome through the treachery of Merobaudes, master of the soldiers; slipping away in flight, he came to Lyons, where later he was killed.

Conan, however, whom Maximus had established as king in the Armorican region, frequently met [unclear], fought manfully with [unclear], and, after many of them had been killed, turned the rest to flight. Having at last obtained peace and quiet possession of the Armorican kingdom, he began to take counsel with his nobles as to how they might as quickly as possible have their wives and children, whom they had left in Britain, brought to them with all their household equipment, lest the offspring of their race should fail.

The number of soldiers who had come from Britain with Conan into Armorica was thirty thousand fighting men; of commoners and farmers, however, as Geoffrey is the authority, there were one hundred thousand, all of whom had sailed by command from Britain into Armorica. Of these many did not have wives; but the others who did have them had left them in Britain with their little children when they were compelled to follow Maximus. Conan, however, and all his people, since they were Christians, by no reasoning consented to take as wives the daughters of pagans who worshipped mute things. Even if they had been Christians, nevertheless it did not please them to have any matrimonial commerce of mingling with the Gauls.

There was in those days a certain petty king in Britain, which is now called England, named Dionotus, who, after the death of his brother Caradocus, held the principality of Cornwall; to him King Maximus, when about to set out for Gaul, had entrusted the kingdom, as to a most prudent man. He had an only daughter named Ursula, in the judgment of all the most beautiful among all the maidens of Britain, and very devoted to Christ.
