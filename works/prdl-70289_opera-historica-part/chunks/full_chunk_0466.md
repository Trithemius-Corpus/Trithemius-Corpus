Bessarion, Greek by nation, cardinal of the holy Roman Church, commonly called the Cardinal of Nicaea, and patriarch of Constantinople, a man in every respect most learned, a philosopher and orator second to none in both languages. He wrote certain outstanding volumes of his genius, by which he transmitted his name to posterity with great glory. Among these the following are reported: Against Mark, bishop of Ephesus, who held wrong opinions, in the Greek manner, about the Holy Spirit in the Council of Florence. He wrote a distinguished and excellent work which he entitled:

Confutation, book I. In that: Thus I wish him to remain, book I.
In praise of Plato’s philosophy, book II. Praises of his homeland, book III.
To the Emperor of the Greeks, of consolation, book I. Several orations and letters.
And certain other works. He also founded at Venice a distinguished library, most richly furnished with many books both Greek and Latin, over which Marcus Antonius Sabellicus, a most learned man, of whom we shall speak later, was placed with no small stipend. At length, full of days, he died while on embassy to the Gauls, under Emperor Frederick and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of our Lord Jesus Christ 1473, the sixth indiction.

Johannes Hagen, otherwise de Indagine, German by nation, of the Carthusian order, of the house of the Mount of the Holy Savior near Erfurt, prior at Eisenach and at Stettin, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and excellently instructed in canon law, distinguished in intellect, ready and provident in counsel. In plain speech he wrote many excellent volumes to princes, bishops, and other prelates of churches, having been questioned on various and diverse matters; of these I recall that few have come into our hands. For, as I have learned from the writings of a certain devout father of the same order, he published more than three hundred treatises; of all these I have seen and read only those which I have set down below.
