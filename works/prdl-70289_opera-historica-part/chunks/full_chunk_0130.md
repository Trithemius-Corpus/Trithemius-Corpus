After this he himself crossed the Meuse, fought many wars with the Gauls, and withdrew and fled, sometimes victorious and sometimes also defeated. At last, however, when the Romans were bringing aid to the Gauls in the war, he was killed in the twelfth year of his reign, and in the thirty-second year of the Lord’s nativity, while Christ himself was preaching salvation to the human race in Judea.

*Marcomerus reigned eighteen years.*

Marcomerus, brother of Herimer, succeeding to the kingdom of the Franks, reigned eighteen years. In his second year Jesus, the Son of God, suffered and died in Judea according to the flesh, and, rising from death on the third day, lives forever. In his fourteenth year Claudius Caesar of the Romans made Britain and the Orkney islands tributary to the Romans. By crossing between the Meuse and the Rhine he grievously afflicted the Franks, and with the aid of the Gauls forced them to retreat as far as the islands of the Rhine. King Marcomerus died in the fiftieth year of the Lord, and the eighteenth of his reign.

*Clodomarus reigned twelve years.*

Clodomerus, son of Marcomerus, succeeding his father in the kingdom, reigned twelve years. Crossing the Rhine with the army of his own people and of the Saxons, he compelled the Gauls to retreat beyond the Meuse, and recovered everything that had been lost. With the aid of the Germans he waged war against the Romans not far from Mainz and laid waste the fields of the Treveri. He died in the twelfth year of his reign, and in the sixty-second year of Christ.

*Anthenor reigned six years.*

Anthenor, son of Clodomarus, succeeding his father, reigned six years. Throughout all the time he reigned he waged war with the Gauls, and for this cause he died miserably. For in the last year of his reign he led an army over the river Meuse after a bridge had been built, and, laden with much plunder, returned without waiting for the Gauls. When part of the army of the Franks had crossed the river and the king with others was remaining last on the bank, suddenly the bridge, overloaded by the multitude of horsemen, broke and fell, and many were killed. The king, stunned and dismayed in spirit, was taking counsel with those who had remained with him about recovering the bridge, when it was announced to him that the Gauls were present with a great army and ready to fight. But since they were few, it did not seem safe to him to await the presence of the Gauls; and yet he could not flee otherwise than through the river. Therefore, throwing away his arms and whatever could weigh him down, keeping his belt over his tunic and his sword, he mounted his horse and tried to cross the river. The other foot-soldiers did likewise, having nowhere to flee, and entrusted themselves to the waters. Some of these reached the bank by swimming; the rest were submerged by the force of the waves, among them King Anthenor. With him perished nine most noble dukes of the Franks, all born of royal stock, namely: Duke Priamus, Duke Clodius, Duke Richimer, Duke Anthenor, Duke Marcomet, Duke Walther, Duke Clogio, Duke Herimer, and Duke [Luthvicie?], with sixty-three other nobles. And so Anthenor perished in the sixth year of his reign, the sixty-eighth year of the Lord’s nativity.

Ratherius, after the death of his father, reigned twenty-one years. In his second year Nero Caesar of the Romans began the first persecution against the Christians, in the fourteenth year of his rule, and committed other dreadful crimes. King Ratherius, however, was in conflict with the Gauls and Romans on all the days of his reign, and fought several battles with them. He died in the twenty-first year of his reign, in the eighty-ninth year of the Lord’s nativity.

Richimer, succeeding his father, reigned thirty-three years. He waged war with the Goths and came out victorious. For in the eleventh year of his reign the Goths were again laying waste the territories of the Saxons and Germans by a hostile incursion. Therefore Windekind, king of the Saxons, Richimerus, king of the Franks, and Hermanfrid, king of the Thuringians, engaging the Goths with a very great army of their peoples, killed twenty thousand of them and put the rest to flight. There is a town beside the river Oder named Franckenfurt, which the Franks are said to have founded at that time, after obtaining victory over the Goths. There King Richimerus appointed his second-born son duke, and left with him a great part of the army. The king [unclear] the wives, children, and all their household goods back into [Cisthenan?] Francia to them.

The name of the duke, the king’s son, was Suimo. His son Clodomer succeeded him in the dukedom, and the successor and son of that man was Suimo the Second, who in the year of the Lord 146 founded a city at the outlet of the river Spre-[Sui?], not far from the Germanic Sea, and from his own name called it Suimonia. They remained in that land for a long time, retaining the name of Franks, until at last they were called Marcomeri from a certain duke Marcomerus, whose son Brand built the city of Brandenburg.
