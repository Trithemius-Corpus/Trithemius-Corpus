Alexander of Hales, English by nation, of the Order of Minors, teacher of Cardinal Eustachius, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and second to no one in his time in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence: when he was doctor of sacred letters in the Paris school, in order that he might become a true disciple of heavenly philosophy, he entered the order of the poor of Christ, which he wonderfully adorned by his learning and holiness. At the command of Pope Innocent IV he wrote a distinguished work, in four volumes of parts, usefully comprising many things, which he entitled:

A Summa of Theology, 4 books. “Since, as Boethius says...”
A Declaration of the Rule of the Minors, 1 book. “To the reverend in Christ...”

The rest of his works I have not seen. It is said, however, that he postilled both the Old and the New Testament in great part, and that he subtly discussed many things concerning the concord of divine and human law, and composed various other works, which have not come into our hands. He flourished under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

John of La Rochelle, of the Order of Friars Minor, master of sacred theology at Paris, learned in the divine Scriptures, and splendidly taught in Aristotelian philosophy, who together with Alexander of Hales subscribed himself in the declaration of the Rule of the Minors to the minister general. He published certain excellent little works, by which he transmitted the fame of his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these the following are reported:

On the Sentences, 1 book.
A Summa of Virtues and Vices, 1 book.
Also On the Soul, 1 book. “Alpha and Omega are one in...”

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Henry, archbishop of Embrun and cardinal of the Holy Roman Church, commonly called Hostiensis, a man most skilled in both laws, who, because of the excellence of his intellect and learning, was held in esteem by Pope Alexander IV. He wrote certain little works, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have seen only the distinguished work known to all:

On the Title of the Decretals, 5 books.

The rest that he composed have not yet come to my knowledge at all. He flourished while the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1150.

Galerius, bishop of Poitiers, of the Order of Friars Minor, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly taught in the disciplines of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, was of excellent industry in sermons delivered to the people. He wrote certain little works not to be despised, of which the following are reported. For at the command of Pope Alexander IV he arranged in style very useful sermons through the cycle of the year:

Sermons, 1 book. “It is asked how virtue...”
Quodlibets, 1 book.
On the Sentences, 4 books.

He flourished while the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Galerius, bishop of Maguelone, born from the town of Lille in Flanders, a man exercised by long study in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, outstanding in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, who in his time, on account of his learning and erudition, was held in great repute. At the request of the provost and clerics of the church of Lille he wrote:

On the Psalter, 1 book.
And certain letters.

He also published some other works, which have not come to our knowledge at all.

Alexander, called de Villa Dei, of the homeland of Dol, of the Order, as they say, of Friars Minor, certainly a man practiced in every respect, a grammarian, philosopher, astronomer, and notable calculator. He wrote not a few little works both in meter and in prose, by which he transmitted his name to the memory of posterity. Of these I have so far found only the following: a volume written in leonine meter on grammar, which today is in constant use in boys’ schools; I do not know whether it is so useful, because of the defect of weaker age and the sloth of teachers. He entitled this same work:

The Doctrinale of Boys, 1 book. “To write for little clerics...”
On Ecclesiastical Computation, 1 book. “Although now at the end...”
On the Sphere, 1 book.
On the Art of Numbering, 1 book.

He is also said to have written many other things: sermons, letters, and various treatises, which have not yet come into our hands at all. He flourished in great religion and learning under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.
