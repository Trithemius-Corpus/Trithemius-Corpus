*Of Johannes Trithemius*

*Letter of Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Master Jacob Wimpfeling of Sélestat.*

Since there are some, my very dear Jacob Wimpfeling, who, blindly treading upon the histories of the times, despise our Germany as though it were barren and devoid of the liberal arts; who contend that in it outstanding men of intellect and writers on ecclesiastical discipline have flourished only few and rare. For they have become such shameless praisers of their own nation that they believe they can become more famous by disparaging others. They extol the slight deeds of their own people and the small talents in their own men with painted colors of eloquence, and, blinded by envy, utterly conceal the great virtues of the Germans, their brave deeds, their most brilliant talents, their faith, fervor, and devotion. If they find among ancient authors anything of virtue, anything of learning, anything of praise concerning our people, lest it come to the knowledge of many, they either scrape it away at once, or, publishing their own things, omit ours. Thus, indeed, Pliny on the wars of the Germans is said by report to have perished; thus many and various histories, written in antiquity concerning the deeds of our people, are said to have been lost.

But you, most learned man, stirred by this rashness of rivals, have appeared as a true lover and defender of our fatherland, since you do not cease to write various things in praise of the Germans, and to urge others by words and examples to do the same. You have won over to yourself Lupold of Bebenburg on the fervor, zeal, and constancy of the ancient German princes in the Christian religion; and you have brought many volumes of ancient authors on these very matters into your library. Sebastian Murrho of Colmar also, a man most learned from every quarter and not moderately skilled in the three principal languages, you have brought by your exhortations to the point that he has published a very fine epitome on the praises of the Germans, in which he set in order, in lucid speech, the brave deeds of our princes on behalf of the Catholic faith, the commonwealth, and the holy Roman Church; and he reviewed in brief compendium the most brilliant talents, works, and inventions of our nation.

But not even content with these things, you always raise your mind to greater proclamations of our praises; and the more things worthy of our praises you find, the greater things you desire. Hence you also exhort me by frequent letters and entreaties that I ought to write something on the praises of the Germans, and to arrange a catalogue of the illustrious men of this nation who, outstanding in learning and intellect, wrote various works. I confess I put it off for a long time, partly detained by concern for domestic affairs, partly occupied by the sweat of higher study. But now at last I have been overcome by your excessive persistence, and I confess myself defeated.

Receive therefore, my Jacob, the catalogue which you have so greatly requested; correct it with the file of your chastening and learning, and then at last allow it to come to the examination of others. Nor should you think that I have gathered together all who made our Germany illustrious by their talents and little works, but only that I have kept the memory of those whose writings, amid my preoccupations, I could find ready to hand. I have reviewed a few out of many, so that by these the envious detractor may perceive how many are still lacking to us whom this catalogue by no means contains. For neither was leisure given me to seek out the rest, nor expense to gather them through others.

Finally, at the end, after I have woven together the sequence of those who have departed life, I have added certain men still living in the flesh, lest, if they should now be passed over by us, they would by no means reach the knowledge of posterity. For indeed those who are now very well known to us will quickly fall into oblivion if they have not been commended by letters and writings. I have no less concern to bring to the knowledge of posterity learned men who have written something for the benefit of readers than to bring the little works of the ancients into the library of my monastery.

Yet I know that it does not sufficiently please certain people that I wished to commend in writing the talents of the living, who think that this was done not for the benefit of posterity but for the vain praise of the human condition. I think these men must be answered briefly, since I hasten to other matters. And if I ought not to praise a man before death in those things which are meritorious, I am not forbidden to commit to writing talent, which is natural and common to good and bad alike; I observe that Jerome, Prosper, Gennadius, Isidore, Honorius, Sigebert, and many others did this. Indeed, what the most holy fathers laudably did for the benefit of posterity, no wise man should forbid me, who desire to do the same with the same intention. Finally, there are many most learned men in these times whose memory it is pious, and of no less benefit, to commend to posterity. Unless antiquity had done this with equal piety, not all the most famous ancient writers would long since have come down to our memory
