He also wrote many other works which have not yet come down to us. He died at Bologna, not without great loss to the world of letters, in the year of the Lord 1510.

Ponticus Virunio, by birth, as they say, of Treviso, a most learned philosopher, a student of Latin and Greek literature, by industry, toil, and liveliness of talent, without any help from his parents, advanced so far in learning that, because of his enormous erudition, he was held in great esteem with Ludovico Sforza throughout all Insubria.

He wrote the following works: Commentaries on Claudian.
Commentaries on the Sphere of Johannes Sacrobosco.
Three books On Obscure Passages in Authors and Corrupt Names.
Two books On the Misery of Letters, in verse.
One book On the Praises of Beatrice, queen of Milan, in verse.
Two books of elegies and epigrams.
A commentary on the Chremata of Chrysoloras.
A commentary on the Achilleid of Statius.
Commentaries on Ovid's Metamorphoses.
Commentaries on the works of Virgil.
Sixteen books On the Art of Divination.
He translated Homer's Odyssey into Latin.
Four books of hymns of the same Homer.
Six tragedies of Euripides. In medicine he translated Theophilus.
On pulses and urines. Likewise Aeneus on pulses and urines.
One book of Inventions.
One book On Titlings.
He translated Apollonius' Argonautica into Latin in four books. He also made and published many other Latin works. He flourished under Ludovico Sforza.

Nicolaus Leonicenus, the most elegant of all orators of his age, most accomplished in philosophy, not ignorant of natural things, treated many matters in medicine elegantly and copiously.

Likewise there is a little work in the same discipline, in which he taught that health and life can be restored and procured for all the sick.

Very many letters of the same man are extant, which display his very great learning and elegance. He flourished at this same time in Italy under Ludovico Sforza.

Ambrosius Calepinus of Bergamo, of the order of Saint Augustine, flourished in this age, a most zealous investigator of divine and human letters. Hence, by his own industry and with great labor, he made common to us a very large work, collected from almost innumerable authors, concerning knowledge of vocabulary and the interpretation of Greek and Latin prepositions, with attestation from the authors. In it not only those words which contribute very much to knowledge of literature, poets, and learned men, but also the words which pertain to physicians and philosophers, and also to ecclesiastical doctors, are explained learnedly enough and elegantly, so that in clearer light than in certain other works, where barbarous men had already made barbarous things, they may become Latin. But those things which among the doctors were being called into doubt now lie open, the cloud of uncertainty having been dispelled.
