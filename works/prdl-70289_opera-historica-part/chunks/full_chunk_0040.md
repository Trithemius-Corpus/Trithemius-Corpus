Thereafter the kingdom of the Sicambri rested from the turmoil of wars for a few years; but afterwards, when peace was broken, the people more often took up arms on a great scale. The other deeds and wars of King Clodomirus, which he had with foreign nations, following Hunibald we have written down in the first volume of our annals.

King Clodomirus died in the eighteenth year of his reign, 230 years before the birth of the Lord, leaving three sons by Queen Ermendrude, who had been the daughter of the king of the Teutons: Nicanor, Helianus, and Helenus.

*Ninth.*

Nicanor, the elder son of King Clodomirus, succeeding his father, reigned for thirty-four years. He was a stern man, just in his generation, warlike, and most impatient of any injuries from enemies, especially from the Gauls; for this reason, with varying fortune, he very often fought against them very fiercely.

For from that time when this people, sprung from the stock of the Trojans, first came from the borders of the Scythians into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine, it never ceased from the harassment of the Gauls, until at last, not without much bloodshed among its own people, it powerfully brought all Gaul under its yoke. No one was ever able fully to overcome the Franks or Sicambri; no one imposed the bridle of subjection upon these most fierce men, but they did not lay aside the desire of ruling, which they had once drunk in, before they had brought all Gaul, and Italy as well, into their power, after the Roman empire had been annihilated and many kings destroyed.

For, as was said above, it is clear from the calculation of the times that, from the beginning of the Franks’ entry, as they are now called, into the lands of Germany on this side [of the Rhine], down to Clovis the great king, who, first as victor over the Alemanni, believed in Christ, 930 years elapsed. During these years the Sicambri fought with the Gauls, never ceasing to make war upon them, although more often defeated in part, until finally they overcame them all.

It should be known, however, that they were not always called either Franks or Sicambri, but frequently changed their names along with their settlements. For indeed, as Hunibald bears witness, when after the destruction of Troy they wandered among various nations for many years and did not long have fixed settlements in one place, among the Scythians they were called Scythians; among the Armenians, they were called Armenians. When they dwelt at the mouths of the Danube, they were called neither Franks nor Sicambri; but because they were neighbors to the Scythians, they received the name of the Scythians’ languages. Afterwards, driven out through the Goths by the envy of the Romans, as Amerodacus is the authority, they descended into the territories of Germany, and, settled in their seats, as has often been said, at the mouths of the Rhine, they learned the speech of the German Saxons. At first they were called Neomagi, afterwards also Germans; then, named Sicambri from Cambra, they changed even that name with time, stirred by love of King Francus, from whom the name of the Franks, as will be said later in its proper order, took its beginning. Hence it has happened that the writers of other peoples, whenever in the descriptions of their histories they introduce mention of the deeds of this people, often call them Germans rather than Sicambri, or Franks. This has usually brought no small harm of error to those who do not know the true origin of the Franks, while they believe that the Franks were either natives or began from a conspiracy of a few men.

King Nicanor, joined with the Saxons and Thuringians, in the fourteenth year of his reign advanced to war with a great multitude against the Goths, who were again breaking into the lands of the Teutons and Saxons. In the first encounter they were superior to the Goths and crushed the Germans with great slaughter. But soon, when the army had been restored, they were defeated by the Goths; many thousands of them perished, both by the edge of the sword and submerged in the waters; the rest sought safety in flight.
