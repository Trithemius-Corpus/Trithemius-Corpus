Hence the priests who were educating the sons of the nobles in the temples named the king Dickeobasan, that is, the just Basan. From this arose a proverb among the Sicambri: whenever one saw or heard another doing or saying evil, “Kennent gy die groten Basan nitt.” That is: “Do you not know great Basan?” As if he were saying, “Do you not know how just King Basanus is, who, if he has perceived you doing or saying such things, will without doubt cut off your head?”

In the fifth year of his reign, the king of the Orcades, having as helper the king of the Britons, came with many ships and armed men against the Sicambri and declared war on them. But King Basanus met them near the seashore, around the island where afterward a city was built by the Franks, whose name is Mesoburgus. But when the Orcadians and Britons saw from the ships so great an army of the Sicambri, they did not dare to disembark from the ships, but went back without accomplishing anything. The cause was the repudiation of Basanus’s wife, whom he had sent back, as I have said, to her father, the king of the Orcades.

When the Gauls again broke in toward the Rhine, where the colonies of the Sicambri were, Basanus came against them in the great strength of his men; there the Gauls were again routed and driven beyond the river Meuse. Having obtained the victory, the aforementioned King Basan built many castles and very strong fortifications on that side of the river Meuse toward the Rhine, where it turns to the east, in which he placed very strong military garrisons against the incursions of the Gauls. In the upper region between Agrippina and the Meuse he built a fortified castle bearing his own name, which he called Basan. In it many kings of the Sicambri and Franks afterward, for many years, held the palace and seat of the kingdom.

In the twenty-sixth year of his reign, Basanus, after many wars and very fierce battles which he waged with various peoples and nations, appointed priests in the temple of Jove, men most learned in Greek and German, in prophecy, in dreams, in the judgment of the stars, in ethics, in physics, and in metaphysics, who, with the gods conveying it, could give sound and right counsel to all who questioned them in any necessity whatsoever. When the king, who was the chief pontiff, was absent, the highest power of decrees rested with the priests, except for the person of the king, on whom they did not pass judgment.

At the temple of Pallas and of Venus the Tritonian goddess he appointed only virgins as priestesses. At certain times all men everywhere were permitted to enter to them, provided they were not joined to wives, and to do in the temple of Venus what her very name signifies. Whatever priestess conceived by such intercourse offered the offspring to Pallas, not to be killed, but to serve forever. Whoever from among these Tritonian priestesses obtained such a one as a wife was, after the birth of a child, praised as holy and very close to the gods. After the first birth each one was compelled to leave the temple, because the priestess who was now a mother had offered her chastity to Venus, and she could marry whomever she wished. Hunibald says more about these matters, which I pass over no less for the sake of modesty than of brevity.

Finally King Basanus led an army from the north toward the south, against the people called the Tegarañ, and it lies between Mainz and Saxony as far as the river Main. King Basan possessed all this land from the northern sea as far as the Main, between the Saxons and the Rhine, that is, from the mouths of the Rhine as far as Mainz; and, having built very strong cities and many garrisons, he fortified it so that it could not easily be overcome. The Saxons and Thuringians, enemies of the Romans, rejoiced together.

After this victory, the great King Basanus, on his birthday at Neopagus among the marshes of the Rhine and the sea, summoned his brothers and sons with many nobles of the kingdom to the solemnity. For seven days he performed sacred rites to the ancestral gods; he showed Jove, visible to all, clouded above the temple. On the eighth day, with the consent of all the nobles, he designated his firstborn son Clodomirus as king over the Sicambri. Afterward, in a marvelous song in the language of his people, he foretold future things to the nation, commended his sons, enjoined justice, and suddenly appeared nowhere. I do not know what divinity I should infer. As Amerodacus says, he was most eager for it; he cultivated justice, loved his subjects, was most dutiful to the good, most cruel to the wicked. To this day it is not known where he went. But the Sicambri, together with the Franks, having enrolled him among the gods, celebrated him for many years with sacred songs. He vanished in the public council.

In his first year, as soon as the Gauls heard that Basan had vanished, they did not doubt that he was dead; and therefore, sending envoys to Clodomer his son, they demanded back from him all the land between the Meuse and the Rhine which their ancestors, contrary to justice, had made violently subject to the Sicambri.
