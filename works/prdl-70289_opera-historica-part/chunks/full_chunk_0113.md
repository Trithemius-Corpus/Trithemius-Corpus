For some time ago, at the urging of the abbots Blasius and Johannes, we wrote two volumes, not small in size, of chronicles or annals of the famous monastery of Hirsau of our order, which is situated in the diocese of Worms, or Speyer; in the first of these, by many arguments, we sufficiently proved that the aforesaid monastery of Saint Peter in Erfurt was first founded not by Dagobert, king of the Franks, but by Saint William, the twelfth abbot of Hirsau. And because we have now come to know that this assertion of ours is all the more true, from the very foolishly fabricated letters which we saw in the aforesaid Erfurt monastery, supported by no seal of anyone, and which we read under the name of Dagobert and copied, lest anyone accuse us of falsehood, we thought it worth the effort to join a copy of those letters, with other arguments, to our assertion.

*In the name of the holy and undivided Trinity.* I, Dagobert, king of the Franks, make known both to clergy and to people, both to Christians and to pagans, that our city named Merovegesburg, which had come to us by hereditary right from our own grandfather Meroneus, namely the founder of the same city in Thuringia: we, by royal power, have given to Saint Peter and to his monks serving God there, to possess after our death, with all its servitudes; from him the mountain itself, after the foundation of the monastery which I built in honor of the aforesaid apostle, took its name, having previously been called by its pagan name [unclear], but by its new name Petrimons. How this was done by the ordinance of God we shall relate.

There was in the aforesaid city, next to the church of Saint [Ulastus?] the martyr, a man of God named Adeodatus, enclosed at my request by Rigibert, bishop of Mainz. This recluse, because of danger to his soul, not wishing [unclear] by the excommunication of clerics, and [unclear] to savor the word of true faith, asked that I summon for him from some cloister of mine a monk from whom he might hear the divine office and the word of true faith, and receive the communion of the sacred mystery. Assenting to his petition because of our mutual familiarity, I sent to the monastery of monks which I had built in like manner in Selsenburg, and, summoning from there a venerable senior monk named Truckindus, I directed him as teacher and rector to the aforesaid recluse Adeodatus. When he had come there, gradually, by the teaching and persuasion of the recluse, the number of believers began to be multiplied, both through the conversion of pagans to baptism and through that of clerics and laymen to the monastic habit. Seeing this, and understanding divine favor in this matter, I handed over my often-mentioned city, with all things belonging to it, to Saint Peter and his monks, so that monastic religion might be maintained there and an abbey established with my aid.

Moreover, I gave to Saint Peter and his monks, for various uses, the forest which, because of the great number of stags, is called Hirsbrulis, from the southern side of the city toward the east, as far as the borders of the region of Orlant, which region I handed over as a benefice to Pippin, count palatine. I nevertheless gave to the above-mentioned monastery villages made by the Slavs in the same forest: [S.] Tuneckdorf, Tubrachtet and Tuttellet, Hochdorf, and several others; and the water of the Gera, [unclear], to the monks on the southern side of the city as far as the boundary of the manor of my ministerial Gisbotus. I also gave from my court, for the uses of the brothers, fifty measures of wheat, one hundred of barley, and as many of oats, and all my other possessions in Thuringia. To maintain this place beloved to me, I gave my ministerials, with all their offspring and possessions, whose names are these: Gisbotus, Gilbertus, Eckhardus, Volffoldus, Hysen, Egenhen, Volffgeirius, Omarus, Sigeberius, Theodoricus, Aidelborus, Adelgerus, Gelferatus, Hingfridus, Huntzelin, Guntherinus, and all the rest of my male and female servants in Thuringia.

So that this handing-over to my lord Jesus Christ may remain firm forever, we have caused it to be confirmed by our letters, and we have asked it to be confirmed by the [unclear] of Rigibert, bishop of Mainz; we have fastened it with our ring, and with our seal affixed we have completed the charter. At Mainz, on the Kalends of March, in the presence of Bishop Rugebert. The sign of the most [unclear] lord Dagobert, king of the Franks. Witnesses of all these matters are these: Pippin, mayor of the palace, and his son Charles; Rikebert, bishop of Mainz; Gunther, a pagan count; Count Eckebi; the whole church in Thuringia around the Rhine in Mainz. These things were enacted in the seven hundred sixtieth year of the Lord’s incarnation, in the ninth indiction.

Far be it from me, Trithemius, to fight against the truth of this asserted foundation, so ancient, if it stands; but I do not quite see how truth can subsist in it. Therefore I shall here also briefly note the reasons which occur to me for doubting, after those, that is, which I wrote on behalf of Hirsau.

The first consideration, which prevents me from granting that Dagobert was the founder of the often-mentioned Erfurt monastery, is that the calculation of the years does not agree; for if this very foundation is ascribed to Dagobert the Great, it conflicts with the written instrument no less than with the truth of the computed time. For he died, as we said above, in the six hundred forty-fifth year of the Lord’s nativity, in the third Roman indiction, not less than sixty years before the donation on this page. But if it is assigned to that last Dagobert, who is Clovis, the disagreement of the years follows nonetheless, because he began to reign in the year of the Lord 715 and died in the year 719. How, then, could he have handed over letters of a foundation of this kind in the year of the Lord 706?
