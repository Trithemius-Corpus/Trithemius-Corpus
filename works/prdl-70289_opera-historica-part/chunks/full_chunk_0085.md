*Abridgment, Book I.* The kingdom of the Franks, greater than all the kingdoms of all Europe, at last grew immeasurably through most bloody wars and through a very great shedding of the blood of kings, dukes, nobles, soldiers, and common people over about nine hundred years, and took increase above all the kingdoms of all Europe, so that it ruled with power over many kingdoms and peoples; and from several kingdoms there came to be one, which, gathered by the sword, should be called in honor of the victor, and should be, by the name of the Franks.

The kingdom of the Franks today is, and remains, divided in two. The Franks, with [unclear] among whom they [unclear], are counted [unclear]. From this it is manifestly clear that, just as the Franks who remained with the Germans use both the language and the customs of the Germans, so also those who, entering the Gauls with their kings, remained with the Gauls changed both their customs and their manner of speaking along with their settlements; and those whom nature had made Germans from the beginning of their birth, a change of places in the midst of the Gauls likewise made Gauls in speech and customs. Indeed, when they were in Troy, they also practiced Trojan customs and tongues among the Scythian ones; in Germany, German ones; and in Gaul they learned and kept Gallic ones. So too Aeneas, coming from the Trojans into Italy with his people, gradually forgot the speech of his fatherland and began to use the customs and language found at Rome.

After this Meroveus, king of the Franks, seized by illness, died in the paganism of idols, as did all his forefathers, in the twelfth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord's nativity 458, in the eleventh Roman indiction.

*The Forty-Sixth.*

Childeric reigned 23 years. Childeric the king, out of fear of the Franks, fled into Thuringia. For after only one year had passed in his reign, the nobles of the kingdom, conspiring against him, wanted to kill him, because, living too insolently and wantonly, he had defiled by his shameful conduct several daughters and wives of theirs and of the nobles. When he learned this, he fled with a few men into Thuringia and remained with Bisinus, king of the Thuringians, his kinsman, for three years. He withdrew, deposed by the Franks, in the year of the Lord 459, in the twelfth indiction. After his departure from the kingdom, in that same year the princes of the Franks at once set over themselves as king a certain Aegidius, a Roman prefect among the people of Soissons, who, while Childeric was in exile, occupied the kingdom of the Franks for three years. He, continually raging against the princes and magnates of their kingdom, slaughtered many, having no reasonable cause apart from empty suspicion. Hence the rest, fearing that similar things might at some time threaten them, unanimously arranged a conspiracy against the tyrant, judging that it would be more tolerable to suffer that man to preside over the kingdom who abused the daughters and wives of his subjects, than to endure any longer the one who thirsted for nothing more than the blood of princes.

Therefore in the year of the Lord's nativity 462, in the fifteenth Roman indiction, Childeric, by the industry and loyalty of his friend Wimbald, who was one of the more powerful princes of the kingdom, was recalled from Thuringia into the kingdom of the Franks, and with the favor of all the nobles and of the people was restored to his father's kingdom, and Aegidius, hated by all, was driven from it. And thereafter Childeric reigned most moderately for another twenty-two years. And when Basina, wife of Bisinus, king of the Thuringians, had heard that Childeric had been restored by the Franks to the kingdom, she left her husband and followed him into Gaul, stirred by excessive love for him, which she had contracted from the long familiarity of living together among the Thuringians. Childeric, receiving her as she came with a cheerful face, took as his wife the woman known to him, the wife of Bisinus the king, by whom he had been kindly received as a guest for three years, badly repaying this kindness.

But as the first night of the marriage approached, Basina advised Childeric to postpone intercourse for a little while, and to watch the doors of the bedchamber, and to report to her, who would prophesy faithfully in all things, what portents he should see in the courtyard for the third time. The king, trusting the woman, obeyed as she commanded; and after watching the buildings three times, he returned to her, saying that first he had seen in the palace courtyard rhinoceroses, that is, unicorns, and also lions and leopards. Secondly, wolves and raging bears, attacking one another. Thirdly I saw, he said, many dogs, but small ones, which were tearing one another with their teeth, and biting fiercely; but what they signify, you must say.
