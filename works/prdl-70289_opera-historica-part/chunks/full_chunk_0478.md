He happily departed from this life under Frederick III, Roman Emperor, in the year of the Lord 1487.

Jodocus de Calve, a German by nation, a Swabian by homeland, a man long practiced in the divine Scriptures through lengthy study, and not ignorant of scholastic philosophy, ready and forceful in intellect, was held in esteem for many years in the Heidelberg school by lecturing and preaching; there also, according to custom, he for some time held the office of preacher in the church of the Holy Spirit. He wrote, as they say, certain treatises not to be rejected, but they have not come to the notice of my reading. There are extant from him on various subjects:

Various sermons, two books.
Vocabulary for preachers, two books.

And various treatises. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1480. And under Pope Innocent VIII he died three years ago.

Nicolaus of Kreuznach, a German by nation, from the diocese of Mainz and the county of Sponheim, a man most learned in the divine writings and not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, distinguished in life, an acute disputant and an excellent declaimer of sermons, publicly taught theology for many years in the school of Vienna, and there obtained great praise and reward for his learning both among the scholastics and from Emperor Frederick III. He wrote certain distinguished little works, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these the following are reported:

Questions on the Sentences, two books.
Various sermons, two books.
Many collations and orations, two books.
On the conception of Saint Mary, two books.

And very many other things, which, since he concealed them up to his death, have not yet been able to come to the notice of my reading, though nevertheless they are to come day by day. He died at Vienna in Austria under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1491, indiction 9.

Petrus Scotus, a German by nation, from Strasbourg by homeland, canon of Saint Peter there, a man practiced in the divine writings, most learned in both laws, and very learned in the other humane arts, a philosopher, rhetorician, and most celebrated poet, outstanding in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct. He wrote, both in verse and in prose, certain chaste and most elegant little works by which he commended himself to posterity. On Saints John the Baptist, John the Evangelist, and John Chrysostom he wrote a beautiful and delightful work:

In elegiac verse, two books. John Gerson [unclear].
Most elegant letters, two books.

Moreover he composed poems, both various and not opposed to the compunction of the reader, but very aptly and suitably fitted to it, in various kinds of meters. He died at Strasbourg and was buried in the church of Saint Peter, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1491, indiction 9, in the thirty-first year of his age. Being made perfect in a short time, he fulfilled many times, and he was taken away lest wickedness should change his understanding.

Nicasius de Voerda, of Mechelen, a German by nation, a Brabanter by homeland, deprived of his eyes from the third year of his age, and for that reason naturally altogether ignorant even of all letters, presented, as it were, a great miracle in our time, a second Didymus of Alexandria, when he became most learned in every doctrine and science, both divine and human. For in the school of Cologne, where he also died, he publicly taught law; the books of both laws, which he had never seen, he learned by hearing, retained in his mind, and recited openly. In the university of Louvain he first received the degree of master in arts; afterwards he directed schools for some time in Mechelen; then, having returned to Louvain, in [unclear] theology he was appointed licentiate and, by a singular apostolic dispensation, was ordained priest. He preached publicly, heard confessions, ministered at the altar, and recited the Gospel from [unclear] before everyone, using his mind in place of a book; only he did not celebrate the divine offices at the altar by himself. Finally, in the Academy of Cologne, by the consent of all the doctors, he was made doctor of canon law, and also a cautious interpreter of the imperial laws.
