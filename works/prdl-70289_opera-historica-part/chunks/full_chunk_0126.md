*Johannes Trithemius*

*King Clodomer reigned 20 years.* Clodomer, son of Anthenor, reigned after his father for twenty years. In his first year the Romans overthrew the Numantines through Scipio and brought them under their power. In his sixth year the Gauls, after the truce which they had made with his father, King Anthenor, ten years before had ended, gathered a great army and crossed the Meuse. Clodomer met them, and a very severe battle was joined, and many fell on both sides. But the Gauls who remained withdrew, and as long as King Clodomer lived they crossed the Meuse no more. King Clodomer died in the 121st year before the birth of the Lord, and in the twentieth year after receiving the kingdom of the Sicambri.

*King Merodactus reigned twenty-eight years.* Merodach, the first-born son of Clodomer, reigned after his father for twenty-eight years, a vigorous, very bold, and exceedingly warlike prince, who waged many wars against the Romans, the Gauls, the Mainz people, the Trier people, and the Cologne people. For, with Saxons and Germans as auxiliaries, he led an army of 220,000 fighters against the Romans into Italy; and, devastating the region, he reached as far as Ravenna with his forces, and, enriched with much spoil, returned victorious to his own lands. Yet on his return he grievously afflicted many colonies of the Romans and inflicted great losses upon them. Hence, after many victories as well as battles, the Romans at last, joining the Gauls to themselves, advanced to war against him in the borders of the Sicambri, between the Meuse and the Rhine. And lest the Saxons, according to their custom, should come to the aid of the Sicambri, the Romans first drew away the Slavs and Goths, who were to make incursions into the borders of eastern Saxony. King Merodach therefore had as his helper the northern king of the Cimbri with one hundred thousand fighters and islanders. Of his own men, however, he had two hundred thousand.

And so in the twenty-second year of his reign Marius, prince of the Romans, sent the Roman legions together with the Gauls, under the leadership of Theodomenus, beyond the Meuse against the Sicambri, and at the same time they fought fiercely; and many thousands on both sides fell dead, and the Sicambri fled. For on their side twenty thousand were killed and more were captured.

After this Merodach, renewing his army, again both invaded and overcame the Gauls. He died in the ninety-third year before the birth of Christ, in the twenty-eighth year of his reign.

Cassander, succeeding his father in the kingdom, reigned for twenty-one years. In his time the Gauls, with auxiliary Roman soldiery, crossed the river Meuse and often harried the borders of the Sicambri. Borbista, king of the Goths, fought bravely against them, and came with a very great number of fighters together with the king of the Saxons; a fierce battle was joined. Borbista, king of the Goths, fled, having lost many of his own. Afterwards [Pugnatus?] again devastated the region, plundered the Saxons as far as the river Vistula, and fled back. He often fought against the Gauls, often victorious, and no less at times scorned by fortune. He died in the seventy-second year before the birth of Christ, in the twenty-first year of his reign.

Antharius, the first-born son of King Cassander, reigned thirty-five years. Of him Hunibald writes that he crushed the Mainz people, Roman colonists, with a pitiable slaughter. With an innumerable army he entered Gaul, burned villages, and carried back endless booty and spoils. But when he had grown old, in the last year of his reign, the Gauls again burst into Batavia; he met them with an army and was killed with twenty thousand of his men. The Gauls made a great slaughter against the Sicambri. He was killed in the thirty-eighth year before the birth of Christ, in the thirty-fifth year of his reign.

Francus, the elder son of King Antharius, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-six years. From him the Sicambri thereafter obtained the name of Franks, which they retain down to the present day. For he was a fierce man, warlike, and the most keen defender of his people; he waged many wars with foreign nations, always victorious, and a glorious triumphator. In his third year the Goths again, together with the Scandinavian peoples, laid waste the borders of Saxony and settled there for ten years. But when, successively, they were always bursting into Saxony more and more, the Saxons, compelled by fear of them, called Francus, king of the Sicambri, together with the Thuringians, to their aid; and with a multitude of 230,000 fighters they advanced to war against the Goths. The Sicambri, however, fighting more boldly than the rest and frequently invoking the name of their king in battle as if it were some divine power, cried out, shouting: “Here, Frank! Here, Frank!” From that time that people changed its name, and little by little not only among themselves but also by other nations they were called Franks rather than Sicambri, until at last that name prevailed everywhere, so that they were called Franks and no longer, or only rarely, Sicambri, as Salagastald testifies. For the people itself took such delight in the novelty of the name that they asked the king and princes to sanction by perpetual edict that they should no longer be called Sicambri, but Franks.
