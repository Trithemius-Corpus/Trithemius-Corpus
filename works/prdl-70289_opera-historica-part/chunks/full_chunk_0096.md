*Compendium, Book I.*

*The Huns, ravaging Thuringia, are cut down by the Franks.*

In the third year of King Hilperic, the Huns went forth with a great army and began to ravage the territories of the Franks in Thuringia in hostile fashion. Against their insolence Sigebert, king of the Franks of Austrasia, brother of King Hilperic, under whose kingdom the Thuringians were contained, brought out his forces, joined battle, laid low many thousands of the Huns, and turned the rest to flight. Meanwhile, while Sigebert was occupied in Thuringia with the Huns, his brother King Hilperic besieged the city and received it in surrender.

In the same year spears were seen in the air, and battle-lines of soldiers; at the same time the din of conflicts was heard, portending the irruption of the Lombards into Italy, about which Pope Gregory the Great speaks in a homily.

For in the following year, namely in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 568, in the first indiction of the Romans, Alboin, king of the Lombards, entrusted Pannonia, in which he had previously settled with his people, to the Huns, friends of the Lombards; and, taking as auxiliaries Saxons, Marcomanni, and other neighboring peoples, with all his people, he occupied part of Italy, which from the name of the people is called Lombardy down to the present.

Therefore, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 583, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans, the Saxons who were with the Lombards in Italy, no longer enduring the pride and unjust domination of the Winuli, for so the Lombards were formerly called, returned with their children and wives, under the guidance of Sigebert, king of the Franks, through Austrasian Gaul to Cologne Agrippina; there, crossing the Rhine, they returned to their former seats of Saxony, which they had abandoned.

In the same year Sigebert, king of the Franks, who dwelt at Metz in Austrasia, brother of Hilperic, surveying the land of Saxony empty of inhabitants, who had gone with the Lombards, as we have said, into Italy, lest it should be reduced entirely to desolation, handed it over to the Suevi to inhabit. And so, while the Saxons dwelt with the Lombards in Italy, the Suevi lived in Saxony for fourteen years. When these had passed, since the Saxons saw themselves in the lowest place among the Lombards, in the condition of slaves, they considered returning to Saxony.

But the Suevi, to whom King Sigebert had given the land to inhabit, at first denied the Saxons entry; secondly, they agreed to restore a third part to them. But when the Saxons demanded back not a part but the whole, the Suevi offered half the region. The Saxons remained constant in their resolve, demanding back not a part, not the half of the land, but the whole entire. At last the matter was committed to the sword, and both armies fought most fiercely for possession of the seats. At length the Suevi were defeated; more than twenty thousand of them perished in that battle by the sword of the Saxons. And so the Saxons recovered by war the land which, at the persuasion of the Winuli, they had abandoned rather imprudently, in the fourteenth year after their departure, some of the Suevi being slain and others expelled.

*Whence the name.* From that time the aforesaid Saxons, returning from Lombardy into Germany, are called Westphalians, that is, western Gauls, down to the present day, although they are in fact true Saxons and Germans. They built a new city in the middle of their region, which they named Milan, in remembrance of another Milan in Lombardy; in it now is the episcopal see of the church of Münster. Hence some writers have also called these Saxons or West-Gauls Lombards, because they carried their customs and rites into Saxony.

In the fifth year of King Hilperic, the king of Austrasia, his brother, mindful of the injuries which he had received from him when in the previous year he was absent from the kingdom fighting with the Huns in Thuringia, gathered a great army and advanced against him for battle. He occupied the city of Soissons, the seat of the kingdom of his brother Hilperic. He took Theodobert, his son, captive and condemned him to exile; he defeated Hilperic himself in battle and put him to flight; he recovered for himself again everything which he had previously taken away, and usurped a great part of his kingdom.

In the same year King Sigebert took as wife Bruna or Brunhild, daughter of Athanagild, king of the Visigoths, as was said above, who was reigning in Spain.

In the seventh year of Hilperic, king of the Franks, the Huns again devastated Thuringia, doing great things by magical arts. Against them Sigebert, king of the Franks, advanced to battle; by their incantations he had almost been deceived and defeated, but, the name of Christ having prudently been invoked, he stood firm bravely, fought manfully, and finally conquered. With him the Huns made a treaty and returned to their own land.
