*Annals of Trithemius. The Twenty-Second.*

Antenor, son of King Clodomer, reigned after his father for six years. During the whole time in which he reigned he waged war with the Gauls and the Romans; and at length, on account of this, he miserably perished with many nobles of the kingdom, when, as we shall say, the bridge broke and he was drowned in the river Meuse.

In the last year of his reign, then, after a bridge had been built of wood over boats in the ancient fashion, he led his army across the river into Gaul. Having laid waste the region, he hastened his return into Francia with much booty and many spoils. And when the greater part of the army had already crossed the river, while the king himself with the nobles and the last of the rest was still waiting on the bank, suddenly the bridge, weighed down by the number of horsemen, collapsed; and the boats, released from one another when the ropes broke, gave all a miserable spectacle, with very many falling into the river. The king, stunned and surely troubled in mind, began to take counsel with those who were present about repairing the bridge, when behold, at once the Gauls were announced to be coming behind them in a great multitude of fighters, ready for battle. On hearing this, all who were with the king were thrown into consternation; time and place denied them flight, while the smaller army forbade battle. Therefore, since no way of escape lay open except that of swimming through the river, they threw into the water their arms and whatever could weigh them down, and tried for safety by swimming. The king also, having thrown off his cuirass and being girded over his tunic only with belt and sword, committed himself with his horse to the waves. Compelled by necessity, all the rest committed themselves to like danger. They all began to swim at once, some on horses, others with hands and feet sustaining the force of the waters. Many of them reached the bank by swimming; many also who had no skill in swimming, choked by the waters, perished amid the waves. Among them King Antenor too was drowned, with nine princes, whose names are these: Duke Priam, Duke Clodius, Duke Richimer, Duke Antenor, Duke Marcomer, Duke Walther, Duke Clogio, Duke Herimer, and Duke Rutwicus, with sixty-three other nobles of the Franks. Thus King Antenor of the Franks perished in the sixth year of his reign, in the sixty-eighth year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the eleventh indiction of the Romans.

*The Twenty-Third.*

Ratherus, or, as he is written by others, Rhatherius, the eldest son of the aforementioned King Antenor, after the death of his father reigned over the Franks for twenty-one years. He was a great-souled, bold, and brave man, who always most vigorously defended the people of the Franks by his sword from every incursion of the Gauls, fearing no one, dreading no one, but himself to be feared by all. In his second year, Nero, the wicked emperor of the Romans, stirred up the first persecution against the Christians, killed Peter and Paul, princes of the apostles, at Rome, and throughout the whole empire cruelly destroyed many thousands of believers.

Ratherus, moreover, king of the Franks, crushed the Gauls repeatedly; he avenged his father’s death with arms, fire, and sword; he restored the borders taken from his people; he built castles, cities, and fortifications; he renewed the treaty with the kings of the Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians, which King Francus had once struck a hundred years before, as we said above; and he enlarged the boundaries of the kingdom of the Franks.

After these things he finally died in the twenty-first year of his reign, in the eighty-ninth year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the second indiction of the Romans, and was buried in the city of Ratherdama, which he himself had newly built from the foundations and dedicated to his own name.
