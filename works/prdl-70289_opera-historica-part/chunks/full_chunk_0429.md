Gerard the Great of Deventer, German by nation, of the diocese of Utrecht, excellently learned both in divine Scripture and in canon law, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, was the first to establish at Deventer the house of the brothers whom they call “of the common life.” He wrote several little works of reading not to be despised, by which, exercising his intellect, he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. For he was renowned throughout all Germany not only for learning, but also for religious life and praiseworthy conduct. Among his little works these are reported:

On Contracts and Usury, one book. On the Instruction of Novices, one book.
On the Leasing of Pastoral Care, one book. It is asked whether anyone can?
On Internal Conduct, one book. On the Governance of Nuns, one book.
On the Disadvantages of Marriage, one book. Letters to Various Persons, one book.
Various Sermons, one book. And many other things.

He died under the emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Urban VI, in the year of the Lord 1384, in the seventh indiction, in the forty-fourth year of his age.

William of Calford, English by nation, of the order of the brothers of the most blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, very learned in the divine writings, an excellent cultivator of secular philosophy, of powerful and excellent talent in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote for preachers of the divine word little works not to be despised, by which he made his name known to many. Of these there are extant, notably:

A Certain Lecture, one book. Sermons on the Season, one book.
Sermons on the Saints, one book.

He flourished in the Cambridge school in England, where by writing, teaching, and disputing he acquired great glory for his learning.

Francis Martini, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, Catalan by nation, a man learned in the divine writings, not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, an excellent expounder. He published a splendid volume, prepared with much diligence, on the holy and most pure conception of the glorious virgin Mary, mother of God, which he entitled:

Compendium.

And certain other things. He flourished in the convent of Barcelona, where at the beginning of the aforesaid golden work he states that he compiled it in the times of the emperor Wenceslaus and Pope Boniface IX, in the year of the Lord [1390?].

James of Lausanne, of the order of the Friars Preachers of St. Dominic, bishop, as they report, of a certain city, a man most devoted to the divine writings, skilled by long practice, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, and quite excellent in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote little works of utility not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these are reported:

On Genesis, one book. In the beginning God created, one book.
On Leviticus, one book. I shall come to you [unclear], one book.
On Deuteronomy, one book. On the Book of Numbers, one book.
Sermons on the Season, one book. As in the day honorably.
Sermons on the Saints, one book. I am fixed to Christ.
Lenten Sermons, one book.

Besides these he published many other lectures on the sacred books, conferences, determinations, and various treatises, which have not come into our hands.
