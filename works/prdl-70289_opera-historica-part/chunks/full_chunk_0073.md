Maximus, moving from Trier with a very great army, appointed his infant son Victor king of the Gauls, and deputed Quintinus and Nanneus, princes of the city of Trier, as his guardians. Then he himself set out with the army into Italy and was killed by Theodosius, in the thirteenth year of Marcomir, king of the Franks. Not long afterward his son Victor also perished by the sword, to win the favor and goodwill of Theodosius.

After the death of Maximus, Marcomir king of the Franks assembled forces and sent a great army of his men into Gaul under the leaders Sunno, Genebald, Priam, and Antenor, so that they might not only recover what had been lost but also make all Gaul subject to the Franks. In a short time, recovering many things which Maximus had taken away, they also added no small part of Gaul to the kingdom of the Franks. Yet the kingdom of the Armoricans, which the Britons inhabited, as we have said, under Conan, they could not obtain on that occasion; nevertheless they afterward recovered it.

Meanwhile, while these leaders were intent on the Gallic war, the Romans, under Valentinian Caesar, who ruled with Theodosius, sent a great army into Germany, hoping to destroy the Franks utterly. Marcomir, king of the Franks, advanced against them to battle with Germans and Saxons; when the engagement was fought, he fell, slain by the enemy with many others, while others slipped away in flight, in the fifteenth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 393, the sixth Roman indiction. Valentinian, wishing to advance farther and desiring to subjugate to his rule the whole kingdom of the Franks that was in the Cisrhenane regions, was compelled to withdraw into Italy because of an incursion of the Lombards. But, craftily concealing the necessity of going back, he sent messengers to those Franks who had remained in the kingdom, through whom he commanded that they either choose war anew or pay tribute to the Romans. But the Franks, now deprived of their king, and with the strength of their soldiery [unclear] with the leaders in Gaul, since they did not know the emperor’s necessity for withdrawal and thought nothing other than war was threatening, saw that they were unequal in strength and promised tribute to the Romans. Yet they could neither promise it without the consent and will of the princes, then, as we have said, far absent, nor pay it. When Valentinian the emperor had carried out these matters with the Franks as he wished, he set out against the Lombards.

Meanwhile all the things that the Romans, under Valentinian, had done with the Franks were reported to the leaders of the Franks, Dagobert, Sunno, Genebald, Priam, Antenor, and the other elders who were directing the wars of the people in the region of the Gauls: how they had condemned the king to death and bound to tribute a people which had always been free and had been subject to no one since its Trojan origin. They were angry, they were moved, they were greatly disturbed, and many deliberated in councils as to how they might prudently avenge this injury.

After much and long consultation, therefore, they finally came to one opinion: that they ought rather to preserve their ancestral fatherland in liberty than to press on with Gallic affairs. Therefore, leaving Duke Priam in the Gallic territories with a sufficient [unclear], [unclear] was ruler of the kingdom for almost five years, as Hunibald indicates, a magnanimous and warlike man. In his time Marcomir, son of Clodius, held the duchy among the Eastern Franks for sixteen years. Dagobert took up the government of the kingdom under the name of duke in the year of the Lord’s nativity 393, the sixth indiction.

*Dagobert*, brother of Marcomir king of the Franks, who was killed by Valentinian, son of the aforesaid king Clogio, after the death of his brother was, by the consent of the Franks, interim king or governor of the kingdom for almost five years, as Hunibald indicates; he too was magnanimous and warlike. In his time Marcomir, son of Clodius, held the duchy among the Eastern Franks for sixteen years. Dagobert took up the government of the kingdom under the name of duke in the year of the Lord’s nativity 393, the sixth indiction.

In the second year of Duke Dagobert, Valentinian Caesar, after sending messengers to the people of the Franks in Germany, demanded the tribute imposed in the preceding year. To him the leaders and people of the kingdom sent back word through the same messengers as follows: The everlasting liberty of the Franks is accustomed to impose tribute on other nations, not to pay it. But you, who fought with an unarmed few when the strength of the Franks was absent, conquered by war, not by courage. If you trust that you have the right to exact tribute from the Franks, come whenever you wish; by the sword we shall show [unclear] that we are, and have always been, utterly free. We make this known to you, so that you do not send anything unarmed to us on this matter, since we contend to give up life rather than lose liberty.
