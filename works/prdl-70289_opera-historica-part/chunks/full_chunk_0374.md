Emondus, a monk of Canterbury in England, of the Order of St. Benedict, formerly a hearer and disciple of Archbishop Anselm, a man learned in the divine Scriptures through long practice, and not ignorant of secular letters, wrote little works not unworthy of reading, by which he benefited the edification of the faithful and made his name known to posterity. Of these there is extant one volume on the discord which existed between William, king of the English, and the aforesaid St. Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury.

On ecclesiastical liberty, one book. The life of the same Anselm, one book.
And certain other things. He flourished in the time of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Gratian, a monk of Bologna or of Classe, of the Order of St. Benedict, by country, as some think, an Etruscan, a man most learned in the divine writings, burning with marvelous zeal, ready in talent, distinguished in eloquence, and notable also in life and conduct. After Ivo, bishop of Chartres, he wrote a distinguished volume of Decrees from the statutes, sanctions, and writings of the ancients, which professors of canon law use in the schools down to the present, approved by Pope Eugene III. Its title is:

The Concord of Discordant Canons, four books: “The human race by two.”

He composed certain other things. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Hugh, a monk of Corbie, of the Order of St. Benedict, called “of Fouilloy,” a man rich and learned in the holy Scriptures through the reading of the ancients, distinguished in talent, sweet in eloquence, and no less venerable in conduct than in knowledge, made his name known to posterity by his writings. For he wrote a notable volume, for all religious persons fearing God not so much useful as delightful, divided into four little books, entitled as follows:

On the material cloister, one book: “On the ordering of the cloister.”
On the spiritual cloister, one book.
On the cloister of the soul, one book: “The cloister of the soul contem[unclear].”
On the cloister of paradise, one book: “In the heavenly Jerusalem cloi[unclear].”
And certain other things. He flourished in the time of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Richard, a monk of St. Victor at Paris, of the order of regular canons of St. Augustine, a man most learned in the divine writings and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in talent, scholastic in eloquence, and excellent in morals and conduct. He wrote many, almost innumerable, little works, by which he greatly benefited the usefulness of believers and consecrated his name to immortality. Among these the following are reported:

On the Holy Trinity, one book: “I have established my [unclear] from faith,” etc.
On contemplation, one book: “Arise, O Lord, into thy rest.”
On the twelve patriarchs, one book: “Benjamin the youth.”
On the mystical ark, one book: “That mystical ark of Moses.”
On the Song of Songs, one book: “In the little bed,” etc., “the soul which.”
On the extermination of evil, one book: “What is it to thee, O sea, that.”
On the advancement of good, one book: “Every head is sick.”
Against the Judaizers, one book: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive.”
On the tabernacle of the covenant, one book. On the forty-two stopping-places.
On the four degrees of charity, one book. On the priesthood of Melchizedek.
On the twelve apparitors, one book. On the study of wisdom.
On the progress of monks, one book: “Since to thy question,” etc.
On the threefold holocaust, one book: “Bring to the Lord, O sons of God.”
On the authority of Peter, one book: “The things which in the Gospel.”
On judicial power, one book. On diverse affections.
