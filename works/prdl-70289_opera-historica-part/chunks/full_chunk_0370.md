Sigebert, a monk of Gembloux, of the Order of St. Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man from his youth most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and in secular letters second to none in his time in the territory of Liege, practiced in verse and prose. He wrote many volumes in both styles, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. He is reproached, however, for this: that, adhering to Henry, persecutor and rebel against the Roman Church, he wrote letters and little works against Hildebrand, Pope Gregory VII, which did not befit his purpose. What he wrote, indeed, he openly declared at the end of On Illustrious Men. When he had been placed in the monastery of St. Vincent at Metz to instruct boys, he wrote in heroic meter:

Calixtus II, pope, a Burgundian by nation, brother of the duke of Burgundy, formerly archbishop of Vienne, sat in the pontificate after Gelasius for six years. He was a man learned in the divine writings and not ignorant of secular letters, a special lover of St. James the Apostle; in his honor he raised the church of Compostela to an archbishopric. He wrote, in well-composed speech and with the greatest diligence:

On the Miracles of St. James, one book. “Calixtus, bishop, servant.”
Letters to various persons, one book.

And certain other things. He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Stephen, also called Herrand, the fourteenth bishop of the church of Halberstadt, formerly abbot of the monastery of Ilsenburg, of the Benedictine Order, a German by nation, was a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech. He wrote little works not of contemptible reading, but few have come into our hands. In the person of Count Louis he wrote a very elegant and useful letter to Waltram, bishop of the church of Naumburg, defending Henry, rebel against the Roman Church.

A Defense of the Pope, one book. “As a good man from the good.”
And several letters to various persons, one book.

And certain other things. He died under Henry V, in 1107, in the fifteenth indiction. And he was honorably buried in Reinsburg.

Bernard, the first abbot of Clairvaux, of the Cistercian Order, a Burgundian by nation, a noble and holy man, who was renowned for many miracles both in life and after death, learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect and adorned in speech, since he neglected nothing in his writings that could serve to adorn discourse.
