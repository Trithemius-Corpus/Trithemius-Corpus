Erhard, a monk of the order of St. Benedict, a man zealous in the divine Scriptures and learned by continual exercise, subtle in intellect and ready in speech, was of excellent industry in delivering homilies to the people. By expounding the divine Scriptures he made his name known, and by frequent study of them became an outstanding teacher. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, of which there are reported:

Commentaries on Genesis, 1 book. On Exodus, 1 book.

On Leviticus, 1 book. On Numbers, 1 book.

On Deuteronomy, 1 book. Also of homilies, 1 book.

He also published some other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Bernold, priest of the church of Constance in Swabia, a German by nation, a devout man, zealous and learned in the holy Scriptures, and sufficiently instructed in the discipline of secular letters, distinguished in intellect and [unclear] in eloquence, left some little works of his study, which nevertheless have not at all come to the knowledge of our reading. He is said to have composed, for the usefulness of the ecclesiastical commonwealth, a volume which he entitled:

The Roman Order, 2 books.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Marbod, bishop of Rennes, very zealous in the divine Scriptures, learned by reading the ancients, and nobly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote not a few volumes in both styles, which could no less please readers than profit them. Among these there excels a notable work which he composed in elegant meter with a threefold sense:

On the Song of Songs, 1 book. The Passion of the Theban Martyrs, in verse, 1 book.

And certain others. He flourished in the times of Henry III, in the year of the Lord [1050?].

Marianus, a monk of Fulda, of the order of St. Benedict, a Scot by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, a historian and distinguished computist, left his homeland and came into Germany as a pilgrim for the love of Christ. First, at Cologne, he was made a monk at St. Martin's, and, from desire for solitude, was enclosed; he remained there for two years. Afterwards, however, transferred to Fulda, he remained enclosed there for 12 years, until at length, on account of his learning and sanctity, by order of the bishop of Mainz and the abbot of Fulda, he moved to Mainz and, staying again enclosed at St. Martin's for 17 years, was held by all in great honor. He wrote from the beginning of the world down to his own times:

A notable Chronicle, 2 books. We are instructed by the divine.

On the Harmony of the Evangelists, 1 book. It was in the days of Herod.

Also On Computus, 1 book.

And certain others. He died under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1086, indiction 9, in the 58th year of his age, and was buried at Mainz at St. Martin's, in the greater church, not without a reputation for sanctity.

Weneric, bishop of Vercelli, formerly a cleric of the church of Trier, a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in the discipline of secular letters, made his name known to posterity by writing, although his little works have not come to the knowledge of our reading. In that discord which existed between Hildebrand, Pope Gregory VII, and Henry, third emperor or fourth king, he wrote, in the person of Theoderic, bishop of Verdun, while he was still a cleric at Trier, to that same pope, beseeching him as a father and, with friendly affection, as though grieving, suggesting to him all the things which talkative rumor had spread abroad that he had done or said against the right of laws and the justice of equity. He entitled it:

On the Discord of the Kingdom and the Priesthood, 1 book. I have found nothing concerning the rest.

He flourished under King Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080.
