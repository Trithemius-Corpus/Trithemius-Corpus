Moving on from there through the Carbonarian Forest, he directed his hurried march toward Tournai, a city which had been fortified with a garrison of Romans. Therefore, when the Roman soldiers heard of the arrival of King Clodius, they advanced against him to battle; they were all slain by him, and not one remained. Thus Clodius for a second time subjected a great part of Gaul to the Franks, which the Romans were never thereafter able to recover, down to the present day.

Now after Clodius, king of the Franks, had magnificently extended the boundaries of his kingdom and had subjected to the rule of the Franks all the land of the Gauls which lies between the Rhine on the east and the sea on the west, and likewise from the sea on the north as far as the Seine toward the south, that is, the Batavians, Menapians, Tungrians, Taravanians, Morini, Ambiani, people of Cambrai, Atrebates, Bellovaci, and Crusi, who are now the Hollanders, Gelderlanders, Brabanters, Zeelanders, Picards, people of Louvain, people of Traiectum, Namurians, people of Beauvais, people of Noyon, and whatever adjoins these, he died in the twentieth year of his reign, and in the year 451 of the Lord’s Nativity, in the fourth Roman indiction.

Meroveus, son of Clodius, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned for ten years. In the third year of his reign, in the month of June, assisted by the support of Helenus, duke of the eastern Franks, he besieged, took, plundered, and subjected to his rule the city of Trier, still at that time subject to the Romans. He killed a Mosellan leader named Sabinus, and powerfully obtained the whole region on this side of the Moselle from Mediomatricum, which is now Metz, as far as the Rhine. And lest the people of the land be able to rise in rebellion, he transferred twelve thousand Mosellans, with their children, wives, and possessions, into eastern Francia under Duke Helenus; in their place he set twelve thousand eastern Franks along the banks of the Moselle, in cities as well as in castles and villages. And from that time the use of the wine which is called that of the Franks first began to exist among the Mosellans. For the Franks, cultivators of wine, transferred to the Moselle, were the first to bring with them from eastern Francia that kind of vine.

In the eighth year of King Meroveus the Huns were devastating Gaul; against their insolence King Meroveus himself led a great army of Franks. Battle having been joined on the Catalaunian fields, the Franks slew one hundred eighty thousand of the Huns.

In his time the Franks added almost all Gaul to their kingdom, the strength of the Romans now diminished and failing, especially after the death of Aetius, whom Valentinian had killed out of envy. Therefore the Franks, mingled with the Gauls, took wives for themselves from their daughters, and begot sons and daughters, who all learned their language, and likewise their customs; with them they have henceforth lived together down to the present day. But those who remained in eastern Francia, and also the Mosellans this side of the Rhine, the people of Wurzburg, Mainz, the Hangones, Neometae, Argentorates, Helvetii, Alsatians, Westerasians, Treveri, Agrippinenses, Holgii, Batavians, Menapians, Hollanders, and Brabanters, differing only in dialects, all use the German language.

King Meroveus, however, after the kingdom of the Franks had been greatly enlarged, died a pagan, in the tenth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord 461, in the fourteenth indiction.

Hilderic, son of Meroveus, succeeding his father, reigned for six years. Then, driven from the kingdom, he fled and lived in exile in Thuringia for eight years, while a certain Roman, Aegidius, meanwhile occupied the kingdom of the Franks. After eight years had passed, Aegidius was cast out of the kingdom by the Franks; Hilderic was recalled, and thereafter reigned for another nine years. And all the years in which he reigned amounted to fifteen. For in the sixth year of his reign, the princes and nobles of the Franks conspired against him because, living insolently and too luxuriously, he had violated many wives and daughters of the nobles, and they wished to kill him. When this became known to him, he secretly fled with a few men into Thuringia to King Basinus, and there he remained for eight years. He fled in the year of the Lord 467, in the fifth Roman indiction.

When Hilderic had been put to flight and was living in exile with Basinus, king of Thuringia, who was joined to him by blood, the Franks set over themselves as king a certain Roman named Aegidius, who ruled over the Soissons; he reigned for eight years. The Franks at length, no longer able to endure him as he tyrannized excessively and cruelly killed all the nobles, cast him out, judging that the shame of daughters and wives should rather be endured or disregarded than that they should be so cruelly slain by a tyrant.

Therefore, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 475, in the thirteenth Roman indiction, Hilderic, through the loyalty and industry of Wimbald, returned from Thuringia into Gaul; Aegidius having been put to flight, he was restored to the kingdom of the Franks by the consent of all, and thereafter reigned for nine years, and changed the former habits of his youth for the better.
