*The Abbot of Sponheim*, 374. Very learned, and excellently instructed in secular philosophy, keen in intellect, distinguished in life, and of a most celebrated reputation in declaiming sermons to the people, since after attaining the mastership of teaching in the gymnasium of Erfurt he held the office of preacher at Mainz for a long time. Among other things he wrote a certain notable work: Various Sermons, one book; Diverse Questions, one book; and certain other things. He died at Strasbourg, buried in the monastery of the Penitents, under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1481, indiction 14.

Franciscus Philelfus, born from the ancient town of Tolentino, golden knight and poet laureate, a most celebrated philosopher and orator, once in his youth at Venice a hearer of Chrysoloras, the Byzantine philosopher, from whom he learned Greek and Latin eloquence most perfectly; a man so studious, and so indefatigable a lover and cultivator of the good arts, that, like another Pliny, amid continual occupations of wars he was always either reading something to himself or dictating to others. For he wrote in both languages, in prose as well as verse, many distinguished volumes, by which he left to posterity the memory of his most celebrated talent. Of these there are extant: Eutyphron, On Piety, one book; On Moral Discipline, two books; Life of Pope Nicholas V, one book, “Since I desire something,” one book; “Of how many laws [unclear],” nine books; Satires in the same meter, ten books; Lyrics in every kind of meter, six books; “If I judged it possible to be done,” sixteen books; “How I regarded nothing among myself,” four books; in Greek verses, by odes, two books; also On Exile, three books. Besides, he translated many things into Latin, of which the following are reported: Aristotle’s Rhetoric to King Alexander, one book; from Plato, [unclear] books; Hippocrates, On Winds and the Passions of the Body, two books; from Plutarch, the lives of Theseus and Galba and Otho. Apophthegms [unclear]. He was his hearer and son-in-law; and when he had become most learned from him, first among the Venetians, afterward among the Florentines, the Sienese, the Bolognese, and finally the Milanese, under four dukes and with an annual pension, he taught Greek and Latin letters to very many with distinction. He died at Bologna, ninety years old and poor, indeed as a philosopher and Christian, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus, in the year of the Lord 1481, indiction 14, in the year after the death of his son Marius. Of the Art of Rhetoric, one book; “I had already decided long since,” one book; Letters to Various Persons, three books; elegant Orations; songs and rhythms. But he also composed many other things in meter and prose, which have not reached me. He died at Mantua under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1480, indiction 13.

Theodericus de Herxen, a German by nation, visitor of the order of the brothers, clerics living in common, a devout man, and studious and learned in the divine scriptures, ready in intellect, clear in speech, distinguished in life and conduct, wrote for the edification of simple brothers many devout volumes, with which, usefully exercising his talent, he left the memory of himself to posterity. Of these the following are reported: On the Whole Psalter, one book; On the Lord’s Prayer, one book; On the Passion of the Lord, one book; On the Desire of Dying, one book; and many other things. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1476.
