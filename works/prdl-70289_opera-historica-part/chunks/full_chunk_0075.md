*Compendium of Book I*

When Valentinian received this news, he wondered at the constancy of the Franks, and said to those standing around: “Frank, Frank, you are a hard people, to be named rather from ferocity than from liberty.” And the matter rested in the current year, because at that time the Roman commonwealth was suffering many and varied hostile incursions from different nations.

But in the following year Valentinian, misled by the counsel of his own men, again sent Sisinnius, a count of the court, to the Franks, so that by good persuasions he might induce them to the payment of tribute and to the very firm friendship of the Romans. But the Franks, not forgetful of what they had previously sent back to Valentinian, and desiring to avenge the death of Marcomir, their most beloved king, killed with the sword the count sent to them, together with the whole company that he had brought, in contempt of Valentinian and of all the Romans.

After this the Franks, having united a very great army of their men into one force, under the leaders Sunno and Genebald, whom the Gauls called Gundebaud, crossed the Rhine with violence and, breaking into the territories of the Romans, devastated everything, and even struck fear into the people of Cologne. For they were striving to subject to their own rule all that lay between the rivers Rhine, Meuse, and Tabula. But after the incursion of the Franks became known among the Treveri, Nannenus and Quintinus, leaders of the Treveri, who, like the people of Cologne, followed the Roman side, gathered an army and resolved to make attacks upon them. For meanwhile the Franks, after laying waste the region of the Gauls and killing many of them, sent the spoils on to their ancestral seats with part of the army, while they themselves proceeded farther.

When Nannenus and Quintinus of the Treveri had come with the aforesaid army to that same place, the Franks, as we have said, already laden with plunder in good number, had crossed the Rhine, intending to return to their companions, to lay waste with all their strength whatever remained untouched among the Gauls. Great confusion followed the Franks because of the ill-considered and insufficiently cautious division of the army. For the first seats of the Franks, as we have often said, were around the borders and mouths of the Rhine, where now are Holland, Zealand, the peoples of Utrecht, Gelderland, and the Frisians. Part of these later received the name Westasen, that is, western Gauls, by comparison with the others, who in the region of Münster were called Ostualen, that is, eastern Gauls. And the Franks had to the east the Saxons, Teutons, Germans, and Marcomanni; to the south, the Gauls of Cologne; to the west, the Tungri and Morini, who inhabited the seacoast; but to the north, the Scots, the Orkneys, the Danes, and likewise the sea.

Therefore, when Nannenus and Quintinus had arrived with the army of the Treveri at the places where the remainder of the Franks had stayed, awaiting the return of their companions, they drew up a battle line and pursued them as they fled as far as the Carbonarian Forest, forcing them to retreat, after slaughtering not a few of them. From this arose the hatred of the Franks toward the Treveri, because of which they later crossed the Rhine and began to winter in the region of the Treveri and to devastate everything.

After these things had been done, it seemed useful to Quintinus that they should lead the whole army of the Treveri across into Francia and, by a sudden incursion, seize the Franks while they were unprepared. But Quintinus’s counsel did not please Nannenus, who did not doubt that the Franks would be bolder in resisting on their own soil. Therefore Nannenus withdrew from Quintinus with his men and returned to Mainz.

But Quintinus, persisting in his purpose, crossed the Rhine near the fortress of Musium and led out the army. He was planning to destroy the Franks at the first attack, with a spirit prouder than his strength for victory. But the Franks, not sluggish, met the Gauls with a manly encounter; and when battle had been joined, many thousands of the Treveri perished, with few saved by flight. In that encounter fell Heracleus, tribune of the Roman legion; almost all who were in command of the soldiers perished with him by the missiles and sword of the Franks. For, with a few exceptions, to whom the black night gave safety and the woods afforded the opportunity of hiding, the whole army of Quintinus perished by sword, bow, and marsh.

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 393, in the seventh indiction, the emperor Valentinian perished at Vienne, a city of Gaul, by trickery and by the severity of Arbogast, master of the soldiers, by a noose. A certain Roman, Eugenius, by the machination of the same Arbogast, seized the kingdom of the Gauls and reigned for three years; afterward, when war was joined with the emperor Theodosius, he was killed by him.

When the Franks heard of the death of Caesar Valentinian, they resolved to cross the Rhine and invade Gaul. Arbogast, understanding their plan, anticipated it; at Cologne Agrippina, in the harsh winter, when the Rhine was frozen, he led an army across and began to devastate, by hostile incursion, the Franks nearest to the Rhine.
