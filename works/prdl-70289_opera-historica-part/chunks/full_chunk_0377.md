*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

...a close friend, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant also of secular letters, ready in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, conspicuous no less in conduct than in learning, made his name known to posterity by writing. For when William, abbot of Saint-Thierry, had died and left unfinished the work on the life of Saint Bernard, while that saint himself was already holding the heavenly realms, he wrote and composed:

*The Deeds of Illustrious Men*, one book.
*The Life of Saint Bernard*.
And certain letters.

He flourished under Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Richard, a monk of Cluny, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and in secular letters the most learned of all men of his time, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, who by writing and composing various treatises transmitted his name with great glory to the knowledge of posterity. Yet I do not recall that any of them has so far come into our hands. His notable and distinguished work is reported, however:

*Histories of the Times*, one book.
*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He flourished in the times of Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Ganfred of Auxerre, a monk of the Cistercian order, formerly a hearer and disciple of Peter Abelard; but lest he should also become an imitator of his error, he betook himself to the teaching of Saint Bernard of Clairvaux. Under his discipleship, distinguished in learning and religion, he composed little works not contemptible for reading, by which he made his name known also to posterity.

*Against Peter Abelard*, one book.
*On the Apocalypse of John*, one book.
*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He flourished in the times of Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Peter, cleric of the church of Reims, called de Riga, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and in secular letters most learned among all the doctors of his time; a grammarian and an outstanding philosopher, excelling in verse and prose. He wrote not a few volumes in both styles, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these I have seen only the notable metrical work on the Bible, which he entitled *Aurora*, as follows:

*On Genesis*, one book. It begins: “The beginning of the world, what sort...”
“The twelfth of the fathers...”
One book. “But the voice of the Lord Moses [unclear]”
“God addresses Moses.”
One book. “These are the words of God.”
“After Moses, Joshua.”
One book. “The Hebrew sins, he says.”
“Solomon reigned.”
“To Ruth [unclear] quickly...”
“Dwelling on Mount Ephraim...”
“Origen alone with [unclear].”
One book. “After [unclear] and [unclear] for himself...”
“You who read the history...”
“Certain persons [ascribe] the book of Job to Moses.”
“To Eustochium and Paula...”
“As far as under the Ethiopians...”
“Great Alexander.”
“Herod flourished [unclear].”
“In the nineteenth year of Tiberius.”
One book. “These things concerning the new law...”
*On Grammar*.

Joachim, abbot of the monastery of Fiore, of the Order of Saint Benedict, born in Calabria, a man devoted to and practiced in the divine Scriptures, who, since he was regarded as a prophet in his own time, even attempted to foretell future things. But he seems to me, if I may speak without prejudice to his own opinion or that of his followers, to have spoken more from conjectures of the Scriptures than to have prophesied. For, to be silent for the moment about the rest, he dreamed that Emperor Frederick would be a future enemy of the Church, whom all [unclear]. He wrote, however, in plain speech, a number of little works, in almost all of which, as though foreknowing the future, he wove in mystical prognostications.
