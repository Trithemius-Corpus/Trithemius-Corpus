Julian, a Campanian bishop, a man of sharp and vehement intellect, learned in the Holy Scriptures, schooled in Greek and Latin speech. Before he revealed in himself the impiety of Pelagius, he was distinguished among the doctors of the Church. Afterwards, however, wishing to defend his heresy, he wrote little works against the holy doctors, in conformity with his own opinion. Of these there survive:

Against Saint Augustine, 4 books.
Against the same, another 7 books.
A dialogue of disputation between the parties, 1 book.

And many other things, all of which, because of the author’s errors, have rightly been cast out from the ecclesiastical library. In a time of the hardship of famine he distributed all his possessions in alms to the poor; he associated with his heresy many noble religious men, deceived under the appearance of piety. He flourished under the emperors Arcadius and Honorius, in the year of the Lord [420?].

Innocent, the first pope of that name, a learned and most holy man, Alban by nation, the forty-second after blessed Peter, presided over the universal Church for fifteen years, and gloriously ordained many things for its benefit. To him, while he was still a priest, blessed Jerome composed the history of the woman struck seven times, as the prologue of the same work shows. Since he was most learned in the divine Scriptures, he wrote some excellent little works for the edification of the faithful. Of these I have found only the following:

A decree against the Pelagians, 1 book.
Against the Bonosiacs, 1 book. It begins: “I have long greatly wondered.”
Letters to various persons, 1 book. It begins: “In inquiring about these things.”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He died in the year of the Lord 417, indiction 15, on the fourth day before the Kalends of August.

Eusebius of Cremona, monk, disciple of blessed Jerome the priest, a studious and learned man, venerable in life and conduct, continually devoted, after the example of his most reverend master, to holy prayers and divine readings, [unclear] a great reputation for holiness [unclear]. Having lived on familiar terms for many years with Saint Augustine, he shone forth, distinguished in life and doctrine among ecclesiastical prelates. Among other things, he wrote for the edification of the faithful:

On the passing of Saint Jerome, 1 book. It begins: “To the most reverend Da-.”

He flourished in the time of Saint Jerome, in the year of the Lord 430.

Siricius, pope, Roman by nation, sat after Damasus in the pontificate for 15 years; a learned and holy man, a very keen defender of the catholic faith, all the Manichaeans whom he found in the city [unclear].

He died under the emperors Theodosius and Valentinian, in the year of the Lord 398, indiction 6, on the ninth day before the Kalends of March.

Fastidius, bishop of the Britons, a man learned in the Holy Scriptures, an outstanding preacher of the word of God, distinguished also in life and conduct, notable in speech and intellect, wrote some devout little works. Among these, a certain volume addressed to Fatalis is said to have been written with sound doctrine.

On Christian doctrine, 1 book.
On preserving widowhood, 1 book.

He flourished under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year 420.
