*Abbot of Sponheim*

Widukind, a monk of the monastery of Corvey in Saxony, of the Order of Saint Benedict, German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and very well instructed in secular letters, quick in talent, clear in eloquence, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote many distinguished little works in an alternating style. Of these I have so far found only the following. From the beginning of the Saxons down to the death of Otto, the first emperor, he wrote to Mechthild, his daughter:

A History of the Saxons, 3 books. *Flore virginali cum ma* [unclear].
The Deeds of Otto I, 1 book.
The Passion of Thecla the Virgin, in verse, 2 books.
The Life of Paul the First Hermit, 1 book.

He flourished under Otto, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 950.

Liutprand, deacon of the church of Ticinum, Italian by nation, born from the city of Pavia, formerly scribe and secretary of Berengar, king of Italy, a man very learned in secular letters, not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, sharp and forceful in talent, skilled in Greek and Latin speech, no less excellent in verse than in prose. He is said to have written several distinguished little works, by which he made his name known. Driven out of Italy by King Berengar, he came into Germany and stayed at Frankfurt. He wrote a notable historical work on his own time, containing the tyranny of that Berengar; this, as if in recompense for the injuries received from him, he entitled:

The Antapodosis, that is, Retribution, 7 books. *Regnantibus, imo senien* [unclear].

Of the others I have seen nothing. He wrote the aforesaid volume to [Recemund?], bishop of the church of Elvira in Spain; in it he sometimes uses verse, sometimes Greek speech, interspersing many things about the deeds of the Germans.

He flourished under Otto I, emperor, in the year of the Lord 950.

Abbo, abbot of the monastery of Fleury, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures by constant reading and study, exceptionally learned in secular disciplines, and also distinguished in life and conduct, was proved worthy even to the crown of martyrdom: while he was preaching the word of God in Vasconia, he was made a witness of the Christian faith by the shedding of his blood. He wrote:

On the Reckoning of Victorius, 1 book.
Additions to the same, 1 book.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Otto I, in the year of the Lord 970.

Robert, king of France, noble, brave, just, learned, and religious, rightly receives a place among ecclesiastical writers, since he composed chants and sweet responsories in regular melody for the honor of the catholic Church. In humane letters, especially in music, he was most learned; and he was of such religion that he sang the canonical hours in church with the clerics, and outside, at home, read them with his own people. He was of such compassion and mercy toward the poor that, sometimes stripping himself of his own garments, he many times covered their nakedness. A book never left his hands, so much so that, sitting on the tribunal amid the noise of judicial cases, he would have the Psalter open in his lap. Hence he is read to have shone with miracles after death. His is that well-known sequence which is sung on the days of Pentecost:

On the Holy Spirit, namely, “Come, Holy Spirit.”
A responsory on Saint Peter, namely, “Cornelius the Centurion.”
On the Nativity of the Lord, namely, “Judaea Jerusalem no” [unclear].
And that Alleluia, namely, “Deliver me from my enemies.”
And many other things which I do not know.

The responsory “Cornelius the Centurion,” when the supreme pontiff was celebrating Mass at Rome, he offered with great devotion to Saint Peter on the altar at the time of the offertory. The ministers of the altar, hurrying up, thought that the rich king had offered gold of great weight; but, finding a written and notated sheet, they marveled, praising the talent and devotion of the prince. Therefore the pope decreed, at the request of the clerics and in memory of the most holy king, that this responsory should thereafter be sung in honor of Saint Peter.

He flourished under Otto II, emperor, in the year of the Lord 980.

Ammonius, a monk of the monastery of Fleury, of the Order of Saint Benedict, French by nation, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in the disciplines of secular letters. He is said to have composed several little works both in verse and in prose, but they have by no means come into our hands. He afterwards wrote one volume to the monk Renald:

On the Miracles of Saint Benedict, 1 book.

He flourished under Emperor Otto III, in the year of the Lord 990.

Heriger, abbot of the monastery of Lobbes, of the Order of Saint Benedict, most learned in the divine Scriptures, nobly instructed in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose. Berno the abbot
