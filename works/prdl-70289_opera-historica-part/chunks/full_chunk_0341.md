*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

Justus, bishop of the church of Urgell, brother of the aforesaid prelate Justinian, was himself also a man practiced and learned in the holy Scriptures, of excellent talent in expounding the divine and canonical books. He wrote a sufficiently elegant and commendable volume in scholastic style: On the Song of Songs, 1 book. And many other things. He flourished with his brother under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

Aprigius, bishop of the Pacensian church, learned in the divine Scriptures and most highly instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, expounded the sacred Scriptures so gloriously and profoundly that, in comparison with him, the ancient writers seemed not to have expounded them. Among his little works there survives a distinguished volume, subtle in meaning and brilliant in expression: On the Apocalypse of John, 1 book. On the Song of Songs, 1 book. He also published many other works which have not come into our hands. He flourished in the times of Theodotus, king of the Goths, under the great emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 530.

Cassiodorus, monk and abbot of a monastery near the city of Ravenna, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and easily the most learned of all in his time in secular letters, shone forth most brilliantly no less in holiness than in knowledge. He was first chancellor of Theoderic, king of Italy, and a senator of Ravenna, and consul of the city of Rome; afterward, despising the empty honors of the world, he became a monk for the love of God. From then on, applying the study of humane letters to the exposition of the holy Scriptures, he composed many very elegant treatises. Of his little works the following are reported. In the person both of himself and of King Theoderic he wrote: Letters to Various Persons, 28 books. On the Nature of the Soul, 1 book, “Since now, having undertaken...” On the whole Psalter, 1 book, “Blessed is the man,” etc.; “Exceedingly...” On the Song of Songs, 2 books, “Since the studies of secular men...” Instruction in Divine Readings, 9 books, “Solomon, inspired...” He also published many other works which have not come into our hands. Complexiones on the Apocalypse, “Our purpose is concerning...” Instruction in Secular Letters, 9 books, “Very usefully in the beginning...” He compiled the Tripartite History, 12 books. He also composed many other works which have not come to the knowledge of our reading. He flourished from the times of the elder Justin almost to the end of the reign of the younger Justinian, having more than 95 years of age, in the year of the Lord 575.

Arator, subdeacon of the most holy Roman Church, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and nobly learned in the letters of secular disciplines, strong in verse and prose, offered it in the presence of bishops and men of learning, and, at the pope’s command, recited a large part of it publicly before everyone. As the hour was passing, Pope Vigilius handed the codex to the venerable Surgentius, primicerius, to be placed in the archive, so that he might complete the reading of it at a free time. His works: Acts of the Apostles, 2 books, “As judge of his own crime...” To Abbot Florianus, 1 letter, “He who by merits the flower...” To Pope Vigilius, 1 letter, “With watery walls...” And certain other things which I have not seen.

This Arator, a most illustrious poet and distinguished theologian, composed in elegant meter a codex on the Acts of the Apostles and offered it to Pope Vigilius before the confession of St. Peter on the seventh Ides of April, in the presence of many bishops and men of learning. Since only half of the book was read in one day, [unclear] he recited it before everyone. As the hour was passing, Pope Vigilius handed the codex to the venerable Surgentius, primicerius, to be placed in the archive, so that he, when free, might complete the reading of it in due time. When he had ordered this to be done in the church of St. Peter in Chains, a great crowd of religious persons and noble laymen assembled, and, with Arator himself reciting in distinct words, both books were heard four times, since only half of the book was read in one day because of the constant repetitions which the listeners demanded with manifold favor.
