JOHANNES Berthachinus of Fermo, a very celebrated jurist, and exceptionally diligent in that faculty, outstanding in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote certain excellent little works on law, by which he transmitted the memory of his name to posterity. Among these there is an outstanding work, divided into three large volumes, which he compiled with much labor, most diligently and in fine order, and entitled:

Repertory of both laws, three books. “Princes are accustomed [unclear].”
Various questions.

He also published many other things besides, which have not come to the knowledge of our reading.

PIUS, the second pope, Tuscan by nation, of Siena by homeland, previously called Aeneas Sylvius, sat in the supreme pontificate after Calixtus III for six years. A man most learned in every respect, a philosopher, orator, and distinguished poet, he left many monuments of his talent to posterity. He once, poor, served a certain prelate at the Council of Basel. Then, taken on as a scribe of the chancery of Emperor Frederick III, and created poet by him after receiving the laurel, he was first bishop of Trieste, afterward of Siena, then cardinal, and finally was elected pope by the vote of all. Concerning his writings, the following are reported, composed with great powers of eloquence:

On the miseries of courtiers, one book. “That those are fools who [serve] kings.”
On the education of children, one book. “To all who [advance] toward virtue.”

One book. “The city [unclear] whence to you.”

One book. “About to write something to you.”
On faith to the Turk, one book. “Of the prophet Ezekiel.”

He is said to have written many epigrams and certain other very elegant little works, which I have not seen, by which he made his name known to posterity. He died under Emperor Frederick III, at Ancona, as is said, in the year of the Lord 1464, indiction 11, on the nineteenth day before the Kalends of September, likewise in the sixty-fourth year of his age. He was buried at Rome in the church of Saint Peter, beside the altar of Saint Andrew.

LANFRANCUS of Oriano, a very celebrated jurist and not ignorant of secular literature, outstanding in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote in his profession certain volumes not to be despised, by which he transmitted the memory of his talent to posterity; and in study he proved himself in many ways and splendidly. Of their number the following exist:

Repetitions. “Since against these decrees.”
Commentary on the Clementine Saepe, On the Meaning of Words, one book.
On the chapter Dispendiosam, On Judgments, one book. “Since against these decrees.”
“This Clementine contains in itself.”
“It is not necessary to divide.”
