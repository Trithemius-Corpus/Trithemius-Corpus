JOHANNES Danckonis of Saxony, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher and astronomer, the most erudite of all his time, distinguished in talent and eloquence, taught the science of the stars for a long time to the French at Paris, among whom he was held in the highest honor. He wrote certain excellent little works, of which I have found only the following: Canons of the First Mover, one book; Tables of the same, one book; On the Plane Astrolabe, two books; also On [incensions?], [unclear] book[s]; On Eclipses, one book; On the Truth and Usefulness of the Science of Astronomy, one book. The other things that he composed have by no means come to the notice of our reading. He flourished in the times of Emperor Louis IV and John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1330.

PETER of Lutra, a monk of the Premonstratensian order of the monastery of Lutra, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular literature, a most distinguished philosopher and jurist, subtle in talent, scholastic in eloquence. He wrote many excellent volumes, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of these the following are extant: On the Sentences, three books; On the Gospel of John, one book; To Baldwin the Archbishop, On the Prerogatives of the Church of Trier, one book; The Sevenfold [Candelabra?], On the Eminence of the Supreme Pontiff, three books; Against Brother Michael of the Order of Minors, formerly general, and those adhering to him, who were attacking the Supreme Pontiff, one book; On the Form of Marriages, one book; To Cardinal Colonna, On the Mystical Columns, one book; On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book; Letters to Various Persons, one book; also various Sermons on the Season and on the Saints through the circle of the year, two books. He also published many other things, which have not yet come to the notice of our reading. He flourished in the times of Emperor Louis the Bavarian and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1330.

Lupold of Bebenburg, bishop of Bamberg, formerly a hearer and disciple of Johannes Andreae of Bologna, a jurist most celebrated by the praise of all, excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, cautious in talent and ready in speech, wrote certain little works of great learning by which he consecrated his name to immortality: A distinguished volume On the Zeal and Fervor of the German Princes and Nobles for the Catholic Faith, the Honor of the Roman See, and the Prelates, one book. Likewise On the Rights of the Roman Empire, one book. He also elegantly composed many letters to various persons. But he is said to have written some other things, which have not come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Louis IV and Pope Benedict XII, in the year of the Lord 1341.

CONRAD of Monte Puellarum, canon of the church of Regensburg in Bavaria, a man learned in the divine writings, most learned in secular philosophy, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech. In the gymnasium of Erfurt he first learned the rudiments of the liberal arts, and then, betaking himself to the French, he devoted himself to sacred letters. Raised to the doctorate at Paris, he instructed many in saving knowledge for eight years with glory. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote: On the Sentences, four books; also To Lupold, the above-mentioned bishop of Bamberg, a very distinguished Oeconomica, three books; to the Duke of [Austria?], a Monastica, one book; also a Politica, one book. He determined various disputations and questions at Paris. But he published many other things, which have not yet come to the notice of our reading. He flourished under Emperor Louis the Bavarian, the fourth, in the year of the Lord 1341.

HEINRICH of Urimaria, of the order of the Hermit Friars of Saint Augustine, a man from boyhood most devoted to the holy writings and much learned, and in the philosophy of the Peripatetics second to none in his time among the Parisians, since, having ascended the doctoral chair, he publicly taught theology and philosophy for many years in the same gymnasium. He wrote not a few volumes for the edification of readers, of which the following are reported: On the Sentences, four books; quodlibetal questions disputed at Paris, one book; On the Ten Precepts, one book; An Epitome of the Conferences of the Saints of John Cassian, twenty-four books; On the Perfection of the Human Word, one book; On Instincts, one book, which is ascribed by some to Heinrich of Hesse; On Exemption, one book; Exposition of the Decretal on the Celebration of Masses, one book; Sermons on the Season and on the Saints, two books. He is also said to have written commentaries on many books of the Bible and of Aristotelian philosophy, and to have composed various other treatises, which have by no means come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Louis IV, in the year of the Lord 1341.
