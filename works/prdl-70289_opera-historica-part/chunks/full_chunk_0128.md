Yet the name Sicambrian was brought into oblivion not suddenly, but over much time and by degrees, as is found among the writers of those times, who sometimes called them Sicambri, sometimes Franks.

Remigius also, bishop of Rheims, when he was about to baptize Clovis, king of the Franks, who was the first of the Frankish kings to become Christian, after the Alemanni had been overcome, in the presence of a multitude of nobles, said to him as he laid aside the royal ornaments and humbled himself at the font, with all of us hearing, in another intelligible voice: “Meekly bow your neck, Sicamber; adore what you burned; burn what you adored,” that is, adore Christ, whose churches you burned as a pagan; burn the idols which you then adored.

This King Francus, after the Goths had been driven from the borders of the Saxons, on his return with his army met a messenger saying that the Gauls had crossed the Meuse, had laid waste the region, and had withdrawn with a great booty of spoils. Immediately King Francus, having accepted eighty thousand Saxons as auxiliaries, joined with his own an army of [unclear] thousand fighters, crossed the Rhine, and came as far as the Meuse. Seeing the desolation of the region which the Gauls had made, he was moved in spirit and swore, saying: “See, my fellow-soldiers, what these treaty-breaking Gauls have done. I swear by the head of my father Antharius that I will give no rest to my head until I have avenged myself on them.” And all praised his purpose.

Thus, when the Meuse had been crossed, he stationed forty thousand to guard the bridge; with the rest of the army he advanced into Gaul and made such slaughter as none of the Sicambri before him either could or dared to imagine. For, advancing as far as the river Tabul toward the sea, from the north he consumed the Tarubani, the Tongri, the Legii, and whatever lies between or beside them, with fire and sword so cruelly that he spared neither infants nor women. He routed the Gauls who engaged him with such fierceness that he seemed to be some divine power, not a man. Hence all the nations round about, greatly terrified, called them Franks, that is, fierce men. More than two hundred thousand Gauls fell by the sword of the Franks, with men, women, boys, and infants.

Moved by this, the Senate and People of Rome sent legions of soldiers against the Germans, with Marcus Lollius as commander; for they could not safely attack the Franks unless they had first overcome the Saxons. At first the Romans were victorious and killed eighteen thousand Germans. But when King Francus heard of the slaughter the Romans had made, he sent his son Chlogio as commander, with a great army of fighters, against the Romans; after many of them had been killed, they compelled the rest to flee, in the twenty-fourth year of King Francus.

Francus, king of the Franks, after many deeds bravely done, died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, but in the ninth year before the birth of Christ. His wars, customs, institutions, and laws were written by Clodomer, the supreme pontiff, in verse, and by Hunibald in prose.

Chlogio, the elder son of King Francus, succeeded his father and reigned thirty years: a most cunning man, an augur, seer, and wondrous magician. His mother Lothildis, daughter of the king of the Thuringians, was high priestess of the temple of Pallas; by her arts she drove mad all the enemies whom she wished against her son and the Franks. Clodomer, counsellor of the Franks, writes great things about these matters in Hunibald, things which exceed belief.

In the tenth year of his reign, King Chlogio, at the insistence of his mother, appointed his second-born son, named Phrisus, prince and duke of that region which afterward from his name has been called Phrisia down to the present day, against the incursions of the Cimbri and the Orchades. After ten years, with King Chlogio and all the Franks permitting, Phrisus was raised to kingship, yet on this condition, that he should pay tribute to the Franks each year: three hundred sixty oxen, twenty talents of pure butter, and three thousand royal cheeses. And thus Phrisia became a most powerful kingdom.

In the tenth year of the aforesaid Chlogio, king of the Franks, our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, the Son of God, was born in Bethlehem, a city of the tribe of Judah among the Jews in the province of Palestine. Before this, in the fourth year of Chlogio, the Romans laid waste Germany in hostile fashion, sometimes defeated and sometimes also victorious. Concerning the other deeds of Chlogio, his wars, prophecies, oracles, truces, and actions done with the Romans, see Hunibald. He died in the year of the Lord 20, but in the thirtieth year of his reign, after many deeds magnificently performed.

Herimer, the second-born son of King Chlogio, reigned after his father for twelve years, a man exceedingly warlike and bold.
