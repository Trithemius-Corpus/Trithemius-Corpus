As the Gauls approached and brought forward one battle-line, when they saw the Sicambri coming against them with two, they were thrown into confusion; but there was no time to order their forces otherwise, since their adversaries were hastening to close battle.

For as soon as the Gauls came within sight of the Sicambri, the Sicambri, impatient of delay, directed one battle-line against the enemy wedge. When the battle had already begun, wheeling the second line around at Neomagus against the enemy from the flank, they threw their order into confusion. And when the fighting was fierce on both sides, and the army of the Gauls was already inclining toward flight, suddenly the Sicambri burst forth from ambush and, making an attack, held the Gauls enclosed among themselves, so that no place for flight lay open to those ready to flee. Thus the wretched Gauls, enclosed in the midst of the Sicambri, were butchered to death like cattle, and they suffered so great a slaughter that, out of eighty-eight thousand of them who had assembled for the war, scarcely ten thousand escaped, saved by the benefit of night, in flight. Of the Sicambri, Duke Marcomir, Duke Helenus, and Duke Antenor fell, with about four thousand men. Nevertheless the victory, with the spoils of the enemy, fell to the Sicambri.

Diocles the king, throughout all the days of his reign, stood bravely in contest with the Gauls, and scarcely in the meantime was there a year in which he did not trouble them by some incursion or battle. At last he died in the thirty-ninth year of his reign, 298 before the birth of the Lord. By the queen he had four sons, Henus, Priamus, Nicanor, and Basanus; by other wives he had several sons.

*The Sixth.*

Helenus, the eldest son of the aforementioned king Diocles, succeeding his father, reigned fourteen years: a sluggish, wanton man, and memorable for no activity of virtue. In his time the Gauls, making incursions several times, stirred up no few disturbances for the Sicambri. Against them his brothers, with the nobles of the kingdom, often led out the army, and, fighting with varying fortune, were sometimes defeated, sometimes victorious. But the king sat at home, idle among the gatherings of concubines, dissolving in lust, useful neither to the kingdom nor pleasing to the people, stupid for peace and unfit for war.

Therefore, before the fourteenth year of his reign had yet been completed, the nobles of the kingdom, gathering at Neopagus, together with the whole soldiery of the army, by the common decree of all deposed him from the kingdom as useless and harmful to the people, in the year 284 before the birth of the Lord.

*The Seventh.*

Basanus, the younger son of the above-mentioned king Diocles, after the deposition of his brother Helenus from the kingdom, was raised to the kingdom of the Sicambri by the common election of the dukes, nobles, and soldiers, and reigned thirty-six years: a man in his generation most prudent, brave, bold, and exceedingly warlike.

When Theocalus died, the Sicambri appointed him chief priest of Jove; because of the greatness of his prudence and the magnificent deeds accomplished by him, they called him Basanus the Great. The Sicambri held him in such reverence that they paid him honors not only royal but almost divine. No one was ever allowed to contradict his commands with impunity, whether in a small matter or in a great one, under the undoubted penalty of bitter death. For he was most eager for the estimation of divinity; on that account he both did and said great things. Hence, wherever he went, he ordered a rope and a sword to be carried before him, as a manifest sign of justice; and he was accustomed to preserve it so tenaciously that he would spare neither his own son nor any of the princes who, knowingly, had offended even a little against the law of equity which he had established.

He had a son named Sedanus. Accused of adultery, he was soon judged by his father to death, according to the law which he had established for the Sicambri. When the nobles pleaded for the youth, the father answered: “Do not, O noble men, fight against justice and the laws which I decreed with you as mature witnesses; for it is easier to bind by hand the wind blowing in the air than to turn the mind of King Basanus away from the execution of equity.” When this had been heard, the princes fell silent; but the father himself said to his son: “I do not kill you, son, but the law of the fatherland, which you knowingly transgressed”; and, seizing him, he cut off his head with his own hand.

At that time the Sicambri began to fear their king Basanus to such a degree that no one among them, great or small, presumed to say or do anything evil against the law. Hence the priests of the gods, by a Greek word [unclear], that is, called the king Just Basanus. But the common people called him Theobasanus, which in the German tongue is Basangoth [unclear].
