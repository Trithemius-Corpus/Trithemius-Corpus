Now let us pursue the sequence of the Counts Palatine of the Rhine and Dukes of Bavaria, descended indeed from Rudolf, but divided into another line, after King Rupert and his son Stephen, with John and Otto of Mosbach.

Christopher, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of John, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, after the death of Erik, king of Dacia, Norway, and Sweden, took as wife the daughter of John, Margrave of Brandenburg. When he had had her for three years, [unclear] without children. He was buried at Helmensberg.

Frederick, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Stephen of Zweibruecken, [unclear] son, [took as wife] Margaret, daughter of the Duke of Guelders, who bore him John, heir of the paternal possession; Stephen, dean of the church of Cologne; Frederick, canon and cantor of the church of Mainz; Rupert, bishop of Regensburg; and William, canon of Trier, who died as a young man in Trier. This Frederick, a just and pious prince, died in the year of the Lord 1480, and was buried in the choir of the monastery of the regulars at Ravengiersburg.

Louis, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Stephen of Zweibruecken, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, took as wife Margaret, daughter of the Duke of Guelders, who bore him Caspar, Alexander, and John. The wars that took place between him and Frederick, Count Palatine of the Rhine, prince and guardian of our Philip, and how great were the losses he suffered from them, are known to all round about.

Rupert, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Stephen, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, was bishop in the church of Strasbourg.

Stephen, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Stephen, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, was bishop in the church of Cologne.

John, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Stephen, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, a learned man and most renowned for integrity of morals, was first bishop of Muenster and afterward archbishop of Magdeburg. Many heralds of praise concerning him present themselves to me, which at present are not to be entrusted to the service of the pen. In the year of the Lord 1475 he died, not without suspicion of poison.

Otto, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of the elder Otto of Mosbach, [unclear] of Rupert the king, and brother of the above-mentioned younger Otto, is bishop of Strasbourg. Frederick was succeeded by Philip, whom Rudolf Agricola, Jerome Aleander, and Jakob Wimpheling, men of the greatest weight, testify to have surpassed so many mortals in piety, magnanimity, clemency, and in every measure of virtue. He was most handsome both in the strength of his limbs, in the endowments of marriage, and in the habit of his body; and he would have been most fortunate, had the German wars raged less against him. Eight sons survived him, so great a hope of descendants. Dying, he handed over the Palatinate to the elder Louis; Bavaria to the sons of the deceased Rupert, over whom he placed Frederick, a most accomplished duke. Three others act as prelates; the remaining two are very great provosts. Among the daughters, Elizabeth, the eldest by birth, as Alexander says, was given in marriage to Philip, Margrave of Baden; Amelia to the Duke of Pomerania; Helena to the man of Mecklenburg: three heroines to as many heroes and to the greatest princes in Germany. Catherine, however, most worthy of such a name, imitating the example of the saint who shared her name, despised the enticements of perishable things and sought Christ as her bridegroom. So great was the lineage of the Palatines! So many bishoprics, that one would be wearied merely by recounting the names; so many kings of Bohemia, Denmark, Hungary, and of the Romans came forth from that stock, that if one must rely on the opinion of the ancestors, there is no family in Germany to which the nobility owes more.

This is Irenicus, book 3 of the *Exegesis of Germany*, chapter 50.
