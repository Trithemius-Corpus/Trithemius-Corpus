Many letters to various persons, one book. And certain similar things.

He died under the emperor Constantine, son of Constantine, in the year of the Lord 677, in the fifth indiction, on the fifth Nones of July.

Adamannus, priest and abbot of the monastery of Iona, which St. Columbanus once built on an island of Scotland, a man very devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant also of secular letters, careful in intellect, sweet in speech, outstanding in life and conduct, not without a reputation for holiness, was the father of many monks. Among other things he wrote, in sufficiently well-composed style, a volume:

On the places of the Holy Land, one book. And certain letters.

He flourished under Justinian the Younger Augustus, in the year of the Lord 690.

Benedict, abbot in Britain, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and an outstanding preserver and expounder of regular discipline, wrote a distinguished little work for monks, in which, comparing all the rules of the holy fathers with the rule of our most blessed father Benedict, he shows that the rules of all the others harmonize with it, and that it disagrees with none at all. For this reason he entitled his book:

The Concord of the Rules, one book. He wrote other things.

He flourished under the emperor Constantine, in the year of the Lord 670.

Adelmus, bishop of the West Saxons, formerly abbot of the monastery of Maildub in Scotland, of the Order of St. Benedict, a devout man of exceptional holiness, devoted to and learned in the holy Scriptures, and in secular letters the most learned of all in England in his time, excellent in verse and prose, a most fervent preacher of the divine word. He wrote certain instructive volumes in a twofold style. Of these I have found only the following. While he was still abbot, at the command of the synod of his people, he wrote against the error of the Britons a distinguished and useful work:

On the Celebration of Easter, one book. “Almighty Father of the world...”

On the Virginity of the Saints, in verse, one book. “Most reverend man of Christ...”

On the same subject, in prose, one book. Several letters to various persons, one book.

He is also read to have written many other things, which have not come to the notice of our reading.

He flourished under Justinian the Younger, in the year of the Lord 680.

Ceolfrid, after Benedict the second abbot of the monastery of the holy apostles Peter and Paul in England, of the Order of St. Benedict, teacher and nurturer of Bede the priest, a devout and learned man, and a vigorous governor of regular discipline. He wrote to the king of the Picts, who had requested it, a fairly lengthy letter in the manner of a little book:

On the Observance of Easter, one book.

He flourished in England in the times of Justinian the Younger, emperor, in the year of the Lord 690. At length, when he was returning after visiting the thresholds of the Apostles, he died in the city of Langres, in the seventy-fourth year of his age.

Egidius, a monk of the Order of St. Benedict, Greek by nation, learned in the divine Scriptures, most skilled in secular letters, a physician, philosopher, and distinguished poet, venerable no less in his way of life than in knowledge. He wrote certain little works of no negligible usefulness, of which I have found only the following: a distinguished work composed in meter:

On Pulses, one book. “The powers of genius with modest...”

On Veins also, one book. “From here a vein is led when it becomes...”
