He was a man most learned in every respect, and was regarded as a second Plato, so that it was commonly said: either Plato imitates Philo, or Philo imitates Plato. Josephus, a Jew by nation, a Jerusalemite, a priest by religion, son of the priest Matthias, a philosopher, rhetorician, and distinguished writer of the times, whom I would not place in our catalogue unless the Church read his writings and blessed Jerome had done this before: after the destruction of Jerusalem, he was captured by the emperors Vespasian and Titus and brought to Rome; because of the excellence of his talent he was magnificently honored by the senate and people of Rome. Although he remained a Jew, nevertheless he often praised the race of Christians, and in the eighteenth book of the *Antiquities* he wrote a notable testimony concerning our Lord Jesus Christ. His volumes were held in such authority among the Romans while he was still living that they were entrusted to the public library; after his death, moreover, he was presented by the senate and people with a bronze statue. Of his works I have seen only the following.

*On the Jewish War*, seven books. “Since the war which.”
*Antiquities of the World*, twenty-four books. “To write a history.”
*Against Apion*, one book. “Sufficiently, as I think.”
*On the Power of Speaking*, one book. “To the holy elder John.”

He flourished at Rome under the emperors Vespasian and Titus, and remained among men until the times of the emperor Trajan, in the year of the Lord 100.

Ignatius, third bishop of the church of Antioch after blessed Peter, a man of exceptional holiness and a singular, most fervent lover of our Lord Jesus Christ, was accustomed to say, as Dionysius testifies: “My love has been crucified.” Since this most blessed prelate was a learned and zealous preacher of the divine word, he wrote several elegant letters full of fervor, in which he showed that he was a lover of holy martyrdom. Of these, the following are considered the more important.

To the Church of the Ephesians, one letter.
To the Magnesians, one letter.
To the Trojans, one letter.
To the Romans, one letter.
To Saint John, one letter.
To the blessed Virgin, one letter.
To the Philadelphians, one letter.
To Polycarp, one letter.

He suffered martyrdom at Rome under the emperor Trajan, in the year of the Lord 111.

Papias, hearer and disciple of Saint John, bishop of Hierosolyma in Asia, a most steadfast propagator and defender of the Christian faith, a disciple and devoted imitator of the holy apostles, wrote works of no contemptible authority. Of these there survives an outstanding work, divided into five volumes, which he entitled thus: *Explanation of the Sayings of the Lord*, five books. The other things that he wrote we have not seen.
