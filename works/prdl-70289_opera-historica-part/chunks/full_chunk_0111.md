In the thirteenth year of Hildebert, king of the Franks, Saint Hubert the bishop, warned by divine revelation, brought back the body of Saint Lambert the martyr from Upper Traiectum to Liège with great glory of miracles, in the twelfth year after he had undergone martyrdom for the truth. The same Hubert also transferred the episcopal see from Traiectum to Liège.

In the sixteenth year of Hildebert, king of the Franks, Grunwald, mayor of the palace of the Franks, now weighed down by greater old age, had appointed [unclear] as substitute; while praying at Liège before the altar of Saint Lambert, he was killed by Ranegarius, a retainer of Radbod, duke of the Frisians.

After his miserable death, Theobald, son of [Progo?], was appointed prince of the court by his grandfather Pippin, mayor of the palace of the Franks. In this principate he could scarcely rule for two years, but was cast down by the Franks.

In the following year, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 713, Pippin, son of Ansegis, mayor of the palace of the kingdom of the Franks, died. He designated as heir of his principate his son named Charles, surnamed Tutides or Martel, born from the concubine Alpaida.

But Plectrude, the lawful wife left by Pippin, seized that Charles, her stepson, and in Cologne Agrippina assigned him to very close custody; with her grandson Theobald she invaded the principate.

Meanwhile Hildebert, king of the Franks, died in the eighteenth year of his reign, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 715, the thirteenth indiction.

*The Fifty-Eighth.*

Clodoveus, son of King Hildebert, succeeded his father in the kingdom of the Franks and reigned four years, or, as others will have it, five. Many writers of histories affirm that this man was not named Clodoveus, but Dagobert; whether this difference occurred through the fault of copyists or from some other occasion, we do not know. The greater part, however, call him Dagobert, but the fewer, agreeing with Sigebert, the monk of Gembloux and a most diligent writer, have named him Clodoveus. Whether he had two names I do not know.

The Franks, laboring under hatred of Theobald, conspired together; after forces had been gathered from here and there, they waged against him a quite dangerous war, in which, after many had been killed on both sides, Theobald was finally defeated, put to flight, and cast down from the principate. After him the Franks appointed Regenfred mayor of the palace.

Meanwhile Charles, son of Pippin, freed by divine will from the custody of his stepmother, soon strove to wrest the ancestral principate from the hand of Regenfred; and first, having attacked Radbod, duke of Frisia, who was helping the enemy, he suffered loss of his army.

In the third year of Clodoveus or Dagobert of the Franks, Charles, in the district of Cambrai at Vinciacum on Palm Sunday, which was the third day before the Kalends of April, engaged Hilperic and Regenfred and turned them both to flight; he pursued them as far as Paris. Now Hilperic was by rank a priest, named Daniel, whom the Franks had made king for themselves in Neustria.

After this Dagobert, or Clodoveus, king of the Franks, died in the fourth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 719, the second indiction.
