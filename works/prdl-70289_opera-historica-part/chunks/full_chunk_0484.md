JACOBUS of Bergamo, of the order of the hermit brothers of Saint Augustine, a man studious and learned in the divine scriptures, nobly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, outstanding in intellect, eloquent in speech, distinguished in life and conduct, a very celebrated historiographer. He wrote certain very lucid little works, by which he made posterity indebted to the memorials of himself. Among them there is a work of histories from the beginning of the world down to the year of the Lord 1486, a large and notable volume, which, gathering it with much and long labor from various sources, he entitled:

The Supplement of Chronicles, 15 books. “For [unclear] Cicero of yours.”

Of the others I have seen nothing. He is still living among his own Bergamasques in esteem, nearly sixty years old, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI. In the year of the Lord 1494.

BAPTISTA de Sancto Blasio of Padua, a man most skilled in both laws, distinguished by the learning of the philosophers, subtle in intellect, adorned in eloquence, who in the Paduan academy publicly and excellently taught law, where he educated a great number of disciples. In that faculty he wrote certain distinguished little works, by which he showed the force of his intellect to present and future men. Among these I have read:

On Dowry Privileges, 1 book. “Since concerning dowries many things.” 20
Also On Correlatives, 1 book. “In this work [unclear].”
Questions on [unclear], 1 book. “In the name of Christ. [unclear]. Since.”
On the First Rubric of the Decretals, 1 book. “In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.”

He wrote other things. He completed these same works in the Paduan academy, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV. In the year of the Lord 1480, in the thirteenth indiction. And I believe that he is still alive.

MARCUS Antonius Sabellicus, a man most learned on every side, a philosopher, an orator, the most celebrated of all the poets of our time. Adorning Venice with his learning and books, excellent in intellect, eloquent in speech, skilled in both languages; to him Hermolaus Barbarus, Venetian archbishop and patriarch of Aquileia, once composed a book on the rules for writing history. He wrote, both in meter and in prose, certain distinguished volumes, by which he secured everlasting remembrance for his name among posterity. Of these I have seen the following: three large volumes of histories from the beginning of the world; a most celebrated work addressed to the Venetian senate, containing from the beginning of the city:

The History of the Deeds of the Venetians, 33 books. “I, about to write from the city.”
On the Charm of Aquileia, His Fatherland, 1 book. “And if from all writers.”
On the Fortification of Fontiacum, a poem, 1 book. “It is poured forth to Illyrian [unclear].”
On the Slaughter at Fontiacum, a poem, 1 book. “Whither headlong fortune.”
On the Shipwreck of Peter’s Boat, 1 book. “Now, the ropes having been let out, [unclear].”
On the Inventors of Things and Arts, 1 book. “The [unclear] fluctuates.” 40
On the Origin of Udine, a poem, 1 book. “Among pleasing suns of study.”
In Praise of Saint Mary, 14 elegies. “These things, spread out on the ground.”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He is renowned today among the Venetians in the highest esteem, composing many and various works, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI. In the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.
