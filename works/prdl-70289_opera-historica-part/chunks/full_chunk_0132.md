*On the Origin of the Franks*

In the fifth year of King Richimer, Domitian Caesar of the Romans, the younger brother of Titus, after Nero stirred up the second persecution against the Christians; he also, first of all the Caesars, in his sixth year, ordered himself to be called Lord and God.

King Richimer, in the eleventh year of his reign, waged war with the Roman Gauls not far from the city of Basana, which is now called Aachen. At the same time the legions of Roman soldiers at Cologne, after a war with the Franks had been engaged not very successfully, on receiving news of the death of Caesar Cocceius Nerva, made Ulpius Trajanus Crinitus, a Spaniard by nation, emperor. He stirred up the third persecution against the Christians. Later, having been instructed, he ordered that Christians should not be sought out, but that only those who presented themselves voluntarily ought to be killed. Afterwards, however, changed again, he raised a fourth persecution against them. Richimer, king of the Franks, died in the year of the Lord 113, in the twenty-fourth year of his reign.

Odemar, the first-born son of Richimer, king of the Franks, reigned after his father for thirteen years, a prince very beloved by the people of the Franks. He abstained from wars as far as could be done. He made truces, that is, truces or assurances, with the Gauls and Romans, and showed himself so much a friend of both that all lamented his death. Therefore, having peace with everyone, he devoted all his zeal to the dignity and adornment of the nation, renewing laws and establishing new cities and fortifications. Among many cities he founded one in his own name, in the land that was called Tuendia, which lies between the Saxons and the Franks, bordering near the little river Vecht; he ordered it to be called Odemarshesin, in the fourth year of his reign, and in the 117th year of the Lord’s nativity. King Odemar died in the fourteenth year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord 128.

Marcomer, son of Odemar, reigned after his father for twenty-one years. He had as wife the daughter of the king of Britain, named Athildis, by whom he begot seven sons, whose names are these: Clodomer, who succeeded his father in the kingdom; Marcomer, who was duke among the Nitriones and founded a city of his own name, called Marcoburg; Clogio, who perished in Britain; Franc, who was duke among the Hogian peoples from Coblenz as far as the Main, toward the east between the people of Mainz and the Saxons, who, restoring the old city Helenopolis beside the bank of the Main, ordered it to be called Frankfurt; Merodac, who died when struck by a boar in the hunt; Nicanor, who was duke of the island of Holland; and Odemar, whom his father appointed pontiff of sacred rites. At that time Doracus, counsellor and philosopher of the Franks, flourished, a man eminently learned in all the sciences of the world, who presided over songs and annals. The Franks had peace all the days in which Marcomer reigned, and under him they greatly increased in wealth and power, augmented in number and dignity. Suimo reigned eighteen years and fought many battles with the Gauls and Romans. For in his time Severus Caesar of the Romans brought Britain, which is now England, back again into subjection to the Romans, which did not please Suimo and his princes. Therefore, when Severus had died in Britain, Suimo laid waste Gaul in the penultimate year of his reign and returned victorious. He died in the 148th year of his reign, and in the 223rd year of the Lord’s nativity.

Suimo reigned 28 years.
