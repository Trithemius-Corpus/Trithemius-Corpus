John de Columna, a Roman by birth, of the Order of Friars Preachers, archbishop of Messina, a man whose mind was practiced in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, a most acute investigator of the histories of past time, wrote, with noble talent, works not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these there is extant the volume which he entitled:

The Sea of Histories, ten books. Letters to various persons, one book.

He also published some other works, which have not at all come to my knowledge.

Martin, of the Order of Friars Preachers, penitentiary of the lord pope in the city of Rome, and afterwards, as they say, bishop of a certain city, was a man studious and learned in the holy Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, of excellent talent in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote certain distinguished little works, by which he transmitted the memory of his name to posterity. Of these I have seen only:

A Table of the Decretum, one book: “Among all things whatever, if ...”

A Concise Chronicle, two books: “Since to know the times is of the highest ...”

Sermons for the season, three books: “Knowing that it is the hour ...”

Sermons on the saints, four books: “With mouth confessed it becomes unto salvation ...”

On Various Miracles, five books: “A certain householder ...”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished under Louis, the fourth emperor, in the year of the Lord 1320.

There are those who write that this Martin was of the Order of our divine father Benedict; in my judgment they have been deceived by the similarity of the name of Marianus Scotus, of our Order, whom I mentioned above, and who himself also wrote a chronicle. There are likewise some who think he should be added to the fellowship of the Cistercian Order; these either ignorance of the facts or spite has obscured.

Gerardus Odonis, of the Order of Friars Minor, eighteenth minister general, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly trained in scholastic philosophy, subtle in intellect, clear in speech, and sufficiently fit for disputation, is said to have worked at certain writings on the holy Scriptures and other little works, of which few have become known to me:

On the Sentences, two books. On Logic also.

On the Books of the Ethics, ten books.

Various Questions, three books. On Moral Philosophy.

He also composed the office of the stigmata of Saint Francis, founder of the Order of Friars Minor. Finally, he is said to have published commentaries on many books of the Bible, which have still by no means come into our hands. He flourished under Louis the Bavarian, emperor 4, and Pope John 22, in the year of the Lord 1330.

Nicholas de Lyra, English by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor, a man most devoted to the study of the holy Scriptures and, through long practice, [unclear]. Perfectly instructed in the Hebrew language, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, and no less venerable in conduct than in knowledge, he wrote many distinguished little works on the sacred volumes, through which his name came to the knowledge of posterity. How useful and how necessary they are to all who desire to learn sacred Scripture, no one knows better than he who has exercised himself in reading them. For there are some who think he should be despised because of the simplicity of his eloquence; they seem to me vain and unskilled, and unworthy of sacred knowledge and saving philosophy, since they both disparage ecclesiastical simplicity and embrace words, not things. Of the works of this devout and learned man the following are reported:

On the Sentences, one book: “In the beginning,” etc. [unclear].

On Genesis according to the letter, two books: “According as Isidore says ...”

On Leviticus, three books: “And he called Moses,” etc.
