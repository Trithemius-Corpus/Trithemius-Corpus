Johannes Picus, an Italian by nation, count of Mirandola and lord of Concordia, a man most devoted to every kind of good literature, and, just as he is of illustrious blood, so too most noble in an almost divine talent, surrounded by a great household crowd of all kinds of arts, with whom he practices the arena of Latin studies and letters, and likewise Hebrew and Greek, worthy and most deserving, whose journey the Muses follow, is the most learned philosopher of our age and a very keen disputant. In a most elegant style he wrote some things which the age of our contemporaries will approve and then indeed will hold in the greatest admiration; by these he has not unworthily made the memory of his name and glory everlasting. Of these there have already appeared:

Heptaplus on the Beginning of Genesis.

Also: Nine Hundred Philosophical Conclusions.

Apology. Also On the Psalms, 1 book.

And many other things which have not yet presented themselves to our sight. He is still living today among the company of his gifted young men among the Italians, and he does not cease either to write loftier works or to do good for posterity, under Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, and Maximilian, king of the Romans. In the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

Johannes Capnion, otherwise Reuchlin, a German by nation, from the town of Pforzheim in Swabia, an outstanding doctor of imperial laws, and counselor of the illustrious count Eberhard of Württemberg, both most eloquent and of the greatest integrity in law and faith, a most diligent investigator of all the most ancient philosophy and literature. Of the three principal languages, namely Hebrew and likewise Chaldaic, and equally Greek and Latin, he is a very skilled interpreter. He is also especially distinguished in French and in our more polished vernacular tongue. And he is most learned both in the divine scriptures and in secular letters. Indeed, in every kind of learning he is foremost in this age, as one who for many years has very earnestly penetrated the secret mysteries of the Greeks. Worthy indeed to devote himself only to letters, and not also to the very noisy popular court. For not only Italy, but not even our age, could set against us any equal to this great man. He wrote some works by which he commended the memory of himself to those present and to posterity. To the bishop of Worms, chamberlain Dalberg, he wrote a dialogue in which he explains all the secret words of Greek, Hebrew, and Christian philosophy, wondrous words, which he entitled:

Capnion, or On the Wonder-Working Word, 3 books.

On the Preparatory Exercises of the Orator, 1 book.

It is from the ancients.

On the Diversity of the Four Idioms of the Greek Language, 1 book.

A Funeral Oration on the Death of Rudolf Agricola.

Of Epigrams and Elegies, 1 book.

Besides, for our common utility he translated many little works from Greek into Latin: Xenophon’s Apology for Socrates, Nestorius the heretic, the Confession of Cyprian, Proclus on the blessed Virgin, some of Lucian’s Dialogues of the Dead, a dialogue of the same author on the council [unclear] in verse, Epiphanius on the life and death of the prophets, Maximus Tyrius the Platonist [unclear]. That renowned Capnion makes the name more illustrious by his talent. In the year in which we write these things, 1494.
