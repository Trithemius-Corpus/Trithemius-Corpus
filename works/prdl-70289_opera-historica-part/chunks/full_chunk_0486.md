*The Abbot of Spanheim.* Distinguished in [unclear], he wrote many excellent volumes, by which he made his name known. Of these the following are reported. A notable lecture.

On the Book of Wisdom, one book. On Paul’s Epistle to the Romans, one book.

On “Blessed are the undefiled,” sermons, one book. Sermons on the season, one book.

Sermons on the saints, one book. And certain other works.

He flourished at Traiectum, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1490. And I believe that he is still alive.

Georgius Valla of Piacenza, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher, rhetorician, and very celebrated poet, excellent in talent and eloquent in speech, who, now adorning all Italy with his learning, teaches both languages most brilliantly at Venice. He wrote many excellent volumes, by whose elegance and usefulness he has made all learned men truly indebted to him.

On orthography, two books. On the quantity of syllables, one book.

On the figures of orators and poets, one book. “Long since foreseen by me.”

On Cicero’s On the Universe, one book. Plato’s book which [is called?] Ti[maeus?].

On the same author’s On Fate, one book. “On Cicero’s On Fate.”

On the Topics of the same author, one book. “The observance and especially [unclear].”

On the Rhetoric to Herennium, one book. Letters to various persons, one book.

On Juvenal, one book. On the method of disputing, two books.

On Horace’s Poetics, three books. And many other works.

On things to be sought and avoided, one book. The same author’s introduction to medicine. Also four books of the Problems of [Alexander?]. And many other works.

He translated into Latin from Greek a little work of Aristotle On Virtue; likewise two books of his Ethics; also the work of Nemesius, a Christian philosopher, On the Nature of Man; also the little book of Athenagoras, a very acute philosopher, On the Resurrection; also Brisson’s book of dialectic; in addition a treatise of Galen the physician on the sects of physicians; the same author’s introduction to medicine; also four books of the Problems of [Alexander?]. And many other works.

Johannes Camerarius von Dalberg, bishop of Worms, German by nation, a very celebrated jurist, most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters; once a pupil of Rudolf Agricola, a most learned man; skilled in both languages, a most distinguished rhetorician and poet, subtle in talent, eloquent in speech, notable for wide experience of affairs, magnificently illustrating the nobility of his family by his character and learning. He has written and writes, both in verse and prose, certain little works of excellent talent, which he has not yet allowed to come to light, because, being occupied with affairs of state, he could not complete them quite as his purpose had been. Of these, however, some have become known to us. A Sapphic poem.

On the death of Rudolf Agricola, one book. Also On Money, one book.

A speech before Pope Innocent VIII. Various poems and speeches; and several very elegant letters. He is distinguished today, in manly age, in the chair of the church of Worms, a bishop worthy of God; he is composing various works, under Maximilian, most merciful king of the Romans, and the supreme pontiff Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things about him, 1494.

Battista Guarini of Verona, son of Guarino, a most learned man, of whom we have made mention in his own place; a man certainly not inferior to his father, but most skilled in every branch of literature and in the discipline of good language, very eloquent in both tongues. He wrote and translated many excellent little works, by which he commends the force and keenness of his talent, almost divine, both to those now living and to posterity. Of these the following are reported. A very beautiful work of dialogues.

On the Epicurean sect, one book. “And if, most renowned Mapheus,” one book.

On the order of teaching. On Cicero’s letters, one book.

On administering a kingdom. On Lucan’s Pharsalia, one book.

On the poet Catullus. Letters to various persons, one book.

Also several speeches, and poems of various kinds, one book.

He translated the following into Latin: from Xenophon, the Praise of Agesilaus, which he dedicated to Ermolao Barbaro the elder, bishop of Verona; also certain speeches of Demosthenes and Dio the historian; but also some speeches of Gregory Nazianzen to Christ and Mary.
