Benedict Capra, of Perugia, a man most celebrated for his learning in both laws, well practiced both in the divine Scriptures and in the precepts of the philosophers, subtle in intellect and an outstanding disputant, distinguished also by experience of many ancient and new matters, publicly taught law for a long time in the Perugian university and acquired a great reputation for his learning. Among other things he wrote:

On the Decretals, five books. Also several Legal Opinions, one book. And certain other things. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1480.

John Andreas, bishop of Aleria, secretary of the apostolic library, formerly a companion of Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular letters, a jurist and a most eloquent orator, outstanding in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct, skilled in both Greek and Latin speech, wrote certain little works of no slight authority, by which he transmitted the memory of his name with glory to posterity. Among their number the following are given:

On the Fourth Book of the Decretals, one book. On the Use of Fiefs, one book. On Appeals, one book. Letters to various persons, one book. And very many other things. He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1493, in the eleventh indiction.

John Campeius of Bologna, a most celebrated jurist and not moderately learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect and clear in speech, publicly taught law for a long time in the university of Bologna; there, by instructing many very distinguished pupils, he gained great fruit and praise for his knowledge. In that faculty he wrote many excellent commentaries and treatises, as is reported, by which he commended his name to posterity. But none of them has come into our hands. There is reported to be by him an outstanding Treatise on Dowry, one book; On the Conception of Saint Mary, “With no slight grief.” And very fine Legal Opinions, with many other works.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1484. And I do not know whether he is still surviving.

Dominic Bolanus, a Venetian by nation, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and excellently learned in secular letters, a philosopher and rhetorician not to be despised, keen and forceful in intellect, composed in speech, published some distinguished little works of his talent, in which he showed himself a devout and faithful worshipper of the Christian name and commended his memory to posterity. Of these I have seen only the notable little work against the detractors of Mary’s purity, which he dedicated to Niccolò Marcello, the seventy-first doge of the Venetians.

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished at Venice under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord [1480?]. I hear that he is still regarded as surviving.

Francis Niger, a Venetian priest, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures, a learned professor of the arts and of philosophy, a poet and no trifling orator, excellent in intellect and fluent in speech, wrote various little works of his talent, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these there is extant a work not to be despised, but to be extolled with great praises, addressed to Leonardo Botta, a Milanese knight:

Entitled Grammar, one book. “From Annaeus Seneca [unclear].” Letters by him are reported, and certain other things.

He flourished at Venice under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1480. I do not know whether he is still surviving.
