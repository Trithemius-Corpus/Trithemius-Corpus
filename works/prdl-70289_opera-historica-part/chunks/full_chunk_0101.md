Yet Pippin, mayor of the palace, or count palatine, who had been duke of Brabant and at the same time count of Namur, nephew of Saint Arnulf and father of Saint Gertrude, the virgin nun of Nivelles in Brabant, resisted his shameful deeds to no small degree; nor did he in every respect allow the king to sink so freely, at his own pleasure, through so many vices.

In the second year of King Dagobert, which was the six hundred thirty-third year of the Lord's nativity, his wife Nanthild bore him a son, who was named Sigebert. This infant, forty days old, when he was baptized by Saint Amand the bishop, while all were silent, himself answered Amen in a clear voice. His uncle [Charibert?], who reigned at Toulouse, lifted this Sigebert from the holy font of baptism. But after King [Charibert?] died some years later, and not long afterward also his son, named Chilperic, the monarchy of the whole kingdom of the Franks returned to Dagobert, many parts always tending into one, and again, by the plurality of sons, confounding unity.

After King [Charibert?] died, the Gascons, [unclear] the Franks, scorned to be subject; King Dagobert attacked them in war and compelled them to submission.

At the same time Nidachil, who held the dukedom of that same Brittany, terrified by the threat of imminent war, subjected himself and all the people of Armorican Brittany to Dagobert, king of the Franks, and to his successors forever. King Dagobert also attacked in war the Slavs, the Sorbs, who are now called Bohemians, conquered and overcame them, and made them subject and tributary to the Franks.

If this Dagobert, the great king of the Franks, founded that once distinguished, exempt, and royal monastery of our order in the town of Wissembourg in the diocese of Speyer, concerning which I have heard many persons expressing doubt, it seems to me that, if this deed accords with history, it was done in the third year after his father's death, not the twenty-third, since he reigned only fourteen years, although certain uncertain authors seem to have thought otherwise. With this reckoning of times agrees the number of the years of the Lord's nativity, six hundred thirty-four, which was the third year of the aforementioned Dagobert, king of the Franks, but the sixth of his reign over Austrasia, not the twenty-fourth, as we read in Dagobert's own rescripts. Whether these are true or false, how shall we be able to know unless we see their archetype? Desiring to untie the knot of this doubt, let us set down a copy of the letters.

Dagobert, by the favor of divine clemency king of the Franks, to all the chief men of his kingdom, indeed to all who profess the Christian faith, both future and present. A vow by which anyone has bound himself to God or to the saints cannot be made void without the stain of guilt: the greater and more worthy he is to whom the vow is made, by so much the heavier judgment will strike the presumption of the one who vows and does not pay. Therefore we too ought, and we wish, to pay what we have vowed to the Lord, to the terrible God, to him who takes away the spirits of princes, terrible among all the kings of the earth. How he terrified us by the arm of his power, how, by striking, he again visited us with [unclear], we have thought should be made known to all as an example of correction, especially so that every faithful person, hearing of our danger, may abstain from devastation of the churches of God and may show fitting honor to things dedicated to God. First, therefore, we shall briefly set forth the dreadful examination of God held over us, and afterward the devout payment of our vow. After my father Chlothar, of most blessed memory, died, I undertook the care of my father's kingdom; but, led by frivolity of life and distracted by desire for rule, I neglected the duty of the royal office, showing no diligence, or [unclear], toward what had been entrusted to me, making little of or judging lightly the zeal of gentleness or institution. Among my other excesses, I had begun to be a destroyer and dissipator of the churches of God, of which I ought to have been the defender and exalter, fearing neither the eyes of the divine majesty nor the outcry of men. At last the kindness of God, breaking the pride of an obstinate mind, visited my iniquities with the rod of correction, and, when I had been chastised, did not deny me pardon. For, snatched up on high and brought more immediately before the divine majesty, seated among the choirs of saints in the aspect of one judging, I was gravely and steadfastly accused by the most holy patrons of the churches for the ecclesiastical goods taken away by robbery and consumed by me, especially by the most holy Mary, mother of God, and by the most blessed Peter and Paul, princes of the apostles.
