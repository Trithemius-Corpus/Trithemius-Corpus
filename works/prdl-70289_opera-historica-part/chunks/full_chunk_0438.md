JACOBUS of [Cessolis?], of the Order of Friars Preachers, a man learned in sacred writings, not ignorant of secular letters, excellent in intellect, polished and well arranged in speech, second to none in his time in delivering sermons to the people, produced certain distinguished volumes, by which he left to posterity the praiseworthy memory of his name. Among these there is extant a notable work, entitled On the Manners of Men, the Duties of Nobles and Commoners, setting forth as its subject:
On the game of chess, one book. “Among all kinds of signs.”
Various sermons, one book.
But other things which he is said to have written have by no means come to our knowledge.

THOMAS of Haselbach, a German by nation, learned in the holy Scriptures, not ignorant of secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect, scholastic in speech, but too diffuse and prolix, and for that reason liable to the criticism of some. For when he was ordinary reader in sacred theology in the university of Vienna, lecturing on the prophet Isaiah for twenty-one years, he did not reach the end of the first chapter. He wrote several little works on the divine Scriptures; among these the following many volumes are reported.
On the prophet Isaiah, one book. On the Sentences,
On the Ten Commandments, one book.
On the five senses of man, one book. “After, by God’s gift, thus.”
Sermons on the season, one book. Sermons on the saints.
And many other things. He flourished in the times of Rupert, king of the Romans, and Pope Gregory XII, in the year of the Lord 1410.

THOMASINUS, by homeland a Ferrarese, of the Order of Friars Preachers of Saint Dominic, a man studious and learned in the holy Scriptures, an outstanding declaimer in correcting the sins of peoples. He is reported to have fashioned certain excellent little works for the instruction of simple priests, by which he transmitted the knowledge of his name with praise to posterity. Of their number especially the following are reported.
Sermons on the season, one book. Sermons on the saints, one book.
Sermons for Lent, one book.
But the rest which he composed have not come to our knowledge up to this point.

THOMAS à Kempis, a German by nation, of the Order of Canons Regular of Saint Augustine, of the mount of the blessed martyr and virgin Agnes, near Zwolle in the diocese of Utrecht, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, devout in life and conduct, a special lover of the blessed Mary ever virgin. He wrote, for the instruction of simple brothers, little works not to be despised. Among these the following are reported.
On contempt of the world, one book. “He who follows me, not.”
Sermons to novices, one book. “Behold how good and how pleasant.”
Sermons to the brothers, one book. “He who has not renounced.”
Dialogue of novices, one book. “I ask you, dearest Father.”
On the solitary life, one book. “Dearest, when you were in.”
On the three tabernacles, one book. “It is written in the Prophet.”
On true compunction, one book. “Weep with me, all friends.”
Little garden of roses, one book. “With the holy you will be holier.”
Valley of lilies, one book. “I am the flower of the field, the lily.”
Alphabet of a monk, one book. “Your ways, O Lord, [unclear].”
Consolation of the poor, one book. “Be comforted, be comforted.”
Epitaph of a monk, one book. “The way of the just is straight.”
What a monk ought to be like, one book. “Listen, religious monk.”
Manual for little ones, one book. “Let the little ones come.”
Instructional book for youths, one book. “My son, from your youth.”
Letters to various persons, one book. “The Lord rules me; nothing.”
And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Rupert the Bavarian, king of the Romans, and Pope Gregory XII, in the year of the Lord 1410.
