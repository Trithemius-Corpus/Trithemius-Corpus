Guido, the fifth prior of the Carthusians, a man skilled through long study in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, gentle in disposition, clear in speech, and venerable no less in his way of life than in his learning, is reported to have composed several little works, by which his name has been bestowed upon immortality. Of these there survives an excellent work written in lucid style:

*On the life of Hugh, bishop of Grenoble*, one book. *On the institutions of the Carthusians*, one book.

And certain other things. With diligent study he corrected, according to the rule of truth, the letters of the most blessed father Jerome, corrupted by the excessive fault of scribes, and brought them together into one. He died under the younger emperor Lothar, in the year of the Lord 1137, in the fifteenth indiction, on the sixth day before the Kalends of August.

Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris after being a scholastic, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and nobly learned, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, transmitted his name to posterity by writing with such great glory that, down to the present day, the school of theologians everywhere, exercised in his little works, names and holds him as Master with singular veneration. For with sagacious study he wrote, from the sayings of the holy fathers, a commendable work:

*Sentences*, one book. “Desiring something concerning.”
*On all the Epistles of Paul*, fourteen books. “Since all the prophecies of the Spirit.”
*Various sermons*, one book. “The beginning of things require [unclear].”

He also composed not a few other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Eckard, first abbot of the monastery of St. Lawrence, which is called [Vraugia], in the diocese of Würzburg in the Hercynian forest, of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and, in secular letters, inferior in learning to no one among the Germans in his time, excelling in verse and prose, wrote little works not to be scorned for reading, by which he made clear the liveliness of his talent. Of these I have seen only an excellent work, composed in a double style in imitation of Boethius, very useful and necessary for the instruction and consolation of monks and of all the faithful who fear God, which he entitled:

*The Lantern of Monks*, one book. “Concerning lives and virtues.”
*Elegant sermons*, one book. He was buried in the middle of the church of the aforesaid monastery.
