Rupert, French by nation, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and notably instructed in scholastic philosophy, distinguished in talent, clear in speech. He published various little works on the Holy Scriptures, in which, magnificently exercising his talent, he also made the memory of his name known to posterity. He was an excellent preacher of the divine word, and wrote many things useful to declaimers of sermons. Of these the following are reported:

On the Sentences, 4 books. On the Epistles of Paul, 19 books.
Sermons on the Season, 1 book. Sermons on the Saints, [unclear].

He also composed many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Clement VI, pope, from Limoges by country, previously called Petrus Rogetius, abbot of Fécamp of the order of St Benedict. He sat in the chair of Peter after Benedict XII for ten years and six months; a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, an eminent doctor of canon law, venerable no less in conduct than in knowledge, and of excellent talent in declaiming sermons to the people. He published certain distinguished little works, in which he showed the liveliness of his talent. Of these the following are reported:

Various sermons, 1 book.
At the canonization of Ivo, 1 book. Exult and praise [unclear].
At the approval of King Charles, 1 book. Solomon shall sit upon.
Many conferences, 1 book.
Letters to various persons, 1 book.

And certain other things. He died at Avignon and was buried under Emperor Charles IV, in the year of the Lord 1332, in the fifth indiction.

Gregory of Rimini, of the order of the hermits of Saint Augustine, prior general, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and much instructed in scholastic philosophy, distinguished in talent and speech. In the Parisian school he earned from all men great glory for his learning, since he shone forth as most celebrated in life and doctrine. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, of which these are reported as the more important:

On the Sentences, 4 books. On the Epistles of Paul the Apostle, [14?] books.

And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Charles IV, emperor, and Pope Clement VI, in the year of the Lord 1350.

Philip of Bergamo, prior of the monastery of Saint Mary of [Avantia?] in Padua, of the order of our holy father Benedict, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, outstanding in talent and distinguished in speech. He wrote several little works of holy doctrine, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I found only a large volume:

On the Ethics of Cato, 1 book. To the glory and praise.

He is also said to have written sermons, 1 book.

The rest which he composed have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading.

Johannes de Beka, German by nation, cleric of the church of Utrecht, a man very studious and learned in the sacred Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular philosophy, published certain little works of his talent not to be despised. Of these I saw only one work, written in a simply clear style, addressed to Lord John, bishop of the church of Utrecht, and William, duke of Holland, concerning the deeds of the bishops of Utrecht and the counts of Holland. Carrying it down from the time of Saint Willibrord, the first bishop, to the year of the Lord 1346, he wished it to be titled:

Chronicle of the Church of Utrecht, 1 book. Therefore from the year [unclear].

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished in the times of Louis IV and likewise Charles IV, emperors, in the year of the Lord [1350?].

Francis Petrarch, Tuscan by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and by far the most learned of all in secular letters in his own time, a most celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, and poet, who after a long silence called the dead letters of humanity, so to speak, back from the underworld to those above; he shone forth as distinguished no less in holy conduct than in knowledge. For love of Christ and of saving philosophy he undertook the solitary life, in which, persevering until death in meditation, prayer, and writing, his fame was borne throughout the whole world.
