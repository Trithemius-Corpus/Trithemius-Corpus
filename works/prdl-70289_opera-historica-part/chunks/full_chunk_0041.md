At length he died in the thirty-fourth year of his reign, in the one hundred ninety-sixth year before the birth of Christ, leaving five sons: Marcomirus, who succeeded to the kingdom; Antenor, who ruled the maritime region; Priamus, who died at his uncle’s in Britain; Helenus, who held the Cisthenan duchy, between Saxony and the Rhine, over the Tegarani; Clodius, duke of the Mosani.

*Tenth.*

Marcomirus, son of King Nicanor, the elder by birth, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-eight years, a prince singular in modesty and prudence in his generation: pious, mild, and beneficent toward his subjects, but severe toward enemies at all times. Instructed from youth by the priests of the gods sufficiently in all the wisdom of this world, he became a most learned astrologer, augur, seer, interpreter of dreams and events. As often as he could, in leisure, he ordered the brave deeds of his ancestors to be sung before him, both in the palace and in the temples, so that by their example his spirit might be stirred to the defense of his people and always to the increase of the kingdom. This was the custom among our ancestors, the Franks and Germans: that the deeds or sayings of heroes worthy of remembrance should be entrusted by the priests of the temples to songs in the fatherland’s tongue, in the learning, remembering, and singing of which the talents of the young were exercised. This custom lasted for many years, until at last it failed.

*Eleventh.*

Clodius, first-born of Marcomirus king of the Sicambri, reigned after his father for eleven years. In his time the sword of the Romans, directed into Gaul, brought no small harm upon the Sicambri. For in these times the Romans increased the strength of the Gauls, who, making an incursion beyond the Meuse, very grievously and often afflicted the foreign people. The Sicambri, fierce in spirit, meeting them, repelled force with force.

After many battles joined with the Gauls, King Clodius, in the last year of his reign, having gathered a huge army both of his own men and of Saxons, again fought with the same not far from the fortress of Basanburg. In this battle, while he was reconnoitering too incautiously, he was slain in the line by the adversary. But the Sicambri, encouraged by the exhortation of their leaders, notwithstanding the death of the king, continued the war, and fighting bravely at length defeated the Gauls; after killing many of them, they put the others to flight. King Clodius died, slain in the war of the Gauls, as has been said, in the eleventh year of his reign, and in the one hundred fifty-seventh year before the birth of Christ.

*Twelfth.*

Antenor, son of King Clodius, the elder by birth, reigned after his father for sixteen years. He made truces, which our people call *Treugae* from the pledge of faith interposed, with the Gauls for ten years. This one thing was also [pleasing?] to the Romans: because, being more humane than the rest, he forbade human beings to be sacrificed to the gods. This displeased many nobles of the Sicambri, who thought that the fear of them among foreign nations was diminished. But because this pious measure had been decreed with the counsel of the priests, the constitution itself remained inviolable. For priestly authority among them was always greater and more excellent than that of all leaders and princes. King Antenor died in the sixteenth year of his reign, and in the one hundred forty-first year before the birth of the Lord.
