Johannes Stephanus flourished in our time, Italian by nation, bishop of Vercelli, cardinal of Bologna, a most exacting cultivator of literary study; the higher fortune raised him by the most eminent dignities, the more nature adorned him with nobler gifts of mind. Indeed, he always set leisure for letters and the refined natures of things above even all the most illustrious summits of dignities. And at last, by his own unwearied labor and industry, he brought it about that Aristotle’s *Great Ethics*, never known beforehand in earlier ages, became common property for us. Jacobus Faber, the most eminent philosopher of our age and a most vigorous defender of philosophy, arranged these for his printing, which will bring no small benefits to posterity.

Guilhelmus de Mera flourished in this time, a Neustrasian by nation, born from the village of [Constantinus?], of parents of noble lineage and of most holy life.

He is now canon and treasurer of the cathedral church of Coutances, and a distinguished orator and poet. When he was still a boy, the distinguished theologian Doctor Johannes Boucardus, formerly bishop of Avranches and royal confessor, undertook to have him educated in the disciplines because of the excellent gifts of mind that he saw in the boy. Nor did he merely see to it that the boy was instructed by others; when he had leisure, he also taught him himself, taking no small delight in so fertile a cultivation. And he hoped so much from his excellent natural ability that he did not hesitate to endow him at that age with a benefice, and certainly would have given greater things if he had survived. Finally Guilhelmus himself set out for the renowned study of Paris and in a short interval of time reached such a point that no one was regarded as more celebrated than he in the art of oratory or poetry. When the once archbishop of Reims and chancellor of France, [Robert Briçonnet?], heard the reputation of so great a man, he summoned him to himself among his secretaries. When this Robert died, Charles VIII gave this Guilhelmus to his chancellor, Guido de Rochefort. For Charles loved Guilhelmus because of the not inelegant rhymes which he knew how to compose most elegantly in the French tongue, and also because of his skill in writing in every kind of letters, in which he saw no equal. Afterwards the most reverend cardinal of Saint-Malo, Guilhelmus Briçonnet, obtained him from Guido de Rochefort to be his confidential adviser, and he remained with him for a long time. But at last, weary of the misery of courtiers and ill bearing the waste of time, he withdrew to the study of Caen, where he was endowed with the dignity of rector and afterwards with the doctoral laurel in both laws. And in that study he professed the laws for several years. At last he was taken up as canon and treasurer of the cathedral church of Coutances, where he resides, serving himself alone with good letters, with which he is going to enrich posterity.

These are the proofs of his learning that already survive:

*Conflictus in Chimeram*, addressed to Johannes de Ganay, great chancellor of France, a very excellent and holy work, in which he elegantly attacked those three principal vices by which the whole world is infected and poisoned, namely pride, lust, and avarice.

A little work on the fact of the holy Eucharist, addressed to Guilhelmus, bishop of Coutances.

A paraphrase on Musaeus, concerning Hero and Leander, addressed to Nicolas de Cerisay, dean of Saint-Germain of Paris.

One book *On Loves*, addressed to the same man. One book *On Divine Things*.

In addition he wrote many things in [metrical?] discourse which have not yet come to light.

One book *On Praises*, addressed to Johannes Briçonnet, president of the royal accounts.

Matthaeus of Evreux, a Norman, of the Order of Friars Preachers, a most profound investigator of the Scriptures, a man full of [strict?] religion and of letters, produced an excellent postil on the Pentateuch with Isaiah and very many other books of the Old and New Testament. It was placed in the common library of the convent of Evreux of the aforesaid order, of which he had been an alumnus, [unclear].
