Secondly he asked that, when the husbands went out by Maximus’s command, they should put on ships the wives and children of the soldiers and common people left in Britain, together with their household goods, and send them to him into the Armorican kingdom. Thirdly he commanded that, according to the number which he had expressed in his letter, virgins should be sent: noble women, for the soldiers of noble lineage; and likewise common women for the commoners, whom each man who then had no wife might take as wives for themselves.

Dionotus, seeing Conanus’s messenger and wishing to satisfy his desire, gathered from the various provinces of Britain eleven thousand daughters of nobles, and from the rest, of lower rank, sixty thousand virgins and women, all of whom he ordered to assemble on a fixed day within the walls of London. He also ordered ships to be brought from all the shores of Britain, sufficient for the transport of so great a multitude. Although this had pleased many in so great an assembly, nevertheless it displeased many also, who loved their parents and fatherland with greater affection. Nor were there perhaps lacking some who, preferring chastity to marriage, would have rather lost their life in any land than be married.

At last, when the things necessary for the transport had been prepared, the women entered the [appointed?] ships and, by the river Thames, passed into the seas. Finally, when they turned their sails toward the Armoricans, that is, from north to south, suddenly a great storm arose at sea, and, the fleet being divided, the ships were separated from one another. Great danger pressed upon all, and the greater part of the ships was scattered into the depth of the sea and was submerged with the women. Those who escaped so great a danger were driven to barbarous nations and islands of the sea, and by an unknown people were either slaughtered or consigned to perpetual slavery. For they had fallen upon the army of the impious princes Wanius and Melga, who, hired for pay by Caesar Gratian, were devastating Britain first, and then the maritime nations and the surrounding islands, out of hatred for Maximus; by them they cruelly perished for the love of chastity.

Wanius, moreover, was king of the Huns, or, if you prefer, Gnauius in the manner of the Gauls; Melga was king of the Picts. The emperor Gratian had hired them for pay and sent them into the borders of Germany and Gaul together, so that they might afflict those who followed the party of Maximus. Around [unclear] regions they encountered [the women] cast there by the wind; and since they were both beautiful and Christian, they solicited them to intercourse with themselves. But when the women, faithful in Christ, refused to consent to the shamefulness of the wicked men, they were miserably slain by the impious.

This lamentable history of the holy seventy-one thousand virgins and women, besides infants and little children, Hunibald does not relate; but the aforesaid author Geoffrey of Monmouth records it at the end of the fourth book, on the origin of the people and the deeds of the kings of the island of Britain, which was first called the Island of Giants; then from Brutus the first king it was called Britain; afterward it was named Albion; and finally, having obtained the name of England, it retains it to this day.

But Marcomirus, the above-mentioned king of the Franks, because he was a prudent man, so governed the reins of his principality that he followed neither the party of Maximus nor that of Gratian, because the former had taken Armorica away from the Franks, after many of them had been cruelly and without reason killed; and they cruelly slew an innumerable multitude of the people. When this had been reported to King Maximus, who at that time was staying at Trier, he sent a certain Gratian, a citizen, with two legions into Britain, to drive the enemies from there. With the Huns and Picts, however, were Scots and Norwegians, all of whom Gratian, after an engagement had been made, put to flight into Ireland.

In these times Maximus, king of the Britons and usurper of the Roman empire, after all Gaul had already been conquered and Caesar Gratian had been killed at Lyon, established the seat of his kingdom in the city of Trier; there he also called a synod of bishops, at which Saint Martin, bishop of Tours, appeared in person and shone forth with many signs and miracles.
