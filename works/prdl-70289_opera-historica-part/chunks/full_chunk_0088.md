*Annals of Trithemius.*

... the Sicambri, not among the Scythians, but among the Germans. Thus Clovis, first of all the kings of the Franks, received the faith of Christ, and was baptized by Saint Remigius in the thirty-sixth year of his age, the fifteenth of his reign, and the 499th of the Lord's nativity, in the seventh Roman indiction.

On the same day, after the king, three thousand of the courtly chief men and nobles of the Franks were baptized. And in the days that followed, the bishops and priests of the Lord baptized all the rest of the people with great joy, giving thanks for the mercies of almighty God, who by his grace, that he might give peace to the Church, subjected a most fierce people to the yoke of faith.

Thereafter great things were done for the honor of Christ, not only by Clovis but much more by his successors, the kings of the Franks: churches were built, several monasteries of those serving God were founded throughout Germany, Italy, and Gaul, and the ministry of divine worship was in many ways increased at that time. King Clovis, however, although he was a most dutiful man toward his subjects, is nevertheless read to have been excessively cruel toward his own blood-relations, since he had many very noble men sprung from the ancient stock of the kings of the Franks, kings, dukes, and princes, cruelly and treacherously killed, so that he alone might have the kingdom.

But love of historical truth impels us to commend a few examples out of many to posterity. At that time there was ruling at Cologne Agrippina an old man, born of the seed of the kings of the Franks, named Sigebert, whom Clovis's father Childeric, after Cologne had been acquired, had placed there as vicar of the kingdom. He had a son named Ludovicus, to whom King Clovis wrote thus in cunning deceit: "Your father is now old and useless for the kingdom. If he were to die, his kingdom would come to you, with our friendship, and not to another."

The young man, led astray by desire for the kingdom, resolved to kill his father. And when King Sigebert on a certain day, suspecting nothing of the sort, went out from Cologne, crossed the Rhine, and was hunting through the forest of Buronia with a few men, the son had his father killed by assassins sent against him while he was sleeping in his tent at clear midday, as if he were going to possess his kingdom according to Clovis's promise.

When the murder, indeed the parricide, had been most wickedly carried out, Ludovicus wrote to King Clovis: "My father is dead; I hold his treasures in my possession, together with the kingdom. Send men, therefore, and whatever of my father's treasures pleases you I shall send to you with good will." King Clovis wrote back to him: "I give thanks for your good will. I ask that you show everything to our men when they come, since you are henceforth to possess your father's kingdom alone, with our friendship."

When the envoys of King Clovis came, Ludovicus showed them his father's treasures. While they were looking at the various different things, the young man said, "In this chest my father was accustomed to put back his gold coins." "Put your hand," said one of those standing by, "down to the bottom, so that you may test whether it is all gold." When he had done this, and was bent far down, that one who had given the order raised his hand and swung an axe into his brain; he was dead at once in that same place.

When these things had been wickedly done, King Clovis came to Cologne, as if unaware of the crimes, and, after summoning all the people of the city, said: "Hear, best citizens, the unspeakable evil that recently happened. Ludovicus, the son of my kinsman Sigebert, misled his father with false words, saying that I was plotting against his life. And when he was fleeing through the forest of Buronia, Ludovicus sent brigands against him and killed his own father. He too, while brooding over the treasures, was struck down by someone I do not know and perished. But in these matters I am in no way complicit. For I cannot shed the blood of my kinsmen, since that is a crime. But since these things have happened so, I give you counsel, if it seems acceptable: turn to me, so that you may stand under my protection." But they applauded with hands and voices, and, after he had been raised on a shield according to the custom of the people, they set him as king over themselves. And thus he received into his own power the kingdom of Sigebert with all his treasures.

For God was daily casting down his enemies beneath his hands [unclear]; afterward he had both their heads cut off.

He also killed King Riuacharius, who was ruling the Franks at Cambrai and living very wantonly and luxuriously, together with his brother Richarius, after they had been treacherously captured while fleeing from battle, striking them with an axe by his own hand.

He also had Lingomirus, their brother, who was ruling in the region of Le Mans, killed with similar cruelty.
