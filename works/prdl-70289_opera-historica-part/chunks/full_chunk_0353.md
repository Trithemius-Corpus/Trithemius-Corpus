*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 800. And he wrote many doctrinal works.

Amalarius Fortunatus, archbishop of Trier, German by nation, a monk of Luxeuil, and cardinal of the Roman Church, a learned and most eloquent man, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence, was sent by Charles the Great, emperor, to Constantinople to the emperor Michael, and by his remarkable diligence and eloquence confirmed peace and friendship between them, as is contained in the deeds of the Treveri. He wrote, among other things:

On ecclesiastical offices, 1 book. “Satis et nimium quidem.”
To Louis on the same matter, 4 books. On the mysteries of the Mass, 1 book.
Letters to various persons, 1 book.

He died under the emperor Louis, in the year of the Lord 813, in the sixth indiction, in about the sixteenth year of his episcopate.

Angelomus, a monk of Luxeuil, of the order of St. Benedict, a man endowed with a noble talent for expounding the divine Scriptures, so that, in comparison with him, the ancients seem not to have expounded what he himself expounded, and not ignorant of secular writings either, at the command of Drogo, bishop of Metz, who was the son of Charles the Great and brother of the emperor Louis, wrote a most excellent work, of such a kind as I do not remember having read elsewhere in the holy Scriptures:

On the Books of Kings, 4 books. “Igitur cum a quampluribus.”
To Lothar on the Song of Songs, 1 book. “Priusquam augustale decus.”
On the divine offices, 1 book. And certain other things.

He flourished under Louis and his son Lothar, in the year of the Lord 830.

Rabanus Maurus, sixth archbishop of Mainz, formerly abbot of the monastery of Fulda of the order of St. Benedict, German by nation, from the city of Fulda in Buchonia, once a hearer of Albinus the Englishman, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, astronomer, and most subtle poet, to whom, that I may speak without envy, neither Italy bore anyone similar nor Germany an equal. For while still a young man he put on the monk’s habit in the monastery of Fulda, and advanced wonderfully both in morals and in knowledge; whence he was consecrated the fourth abbot of the same monastery, and for twenty-four years presided by word and example over the sheep committed to him, and composed almost innumerable little works of Scripture. At length, offended by the wickedness of his monks, who said that he was too intent upon writings and neglected temporal matters, the devil doing this among them, they conceived displeasure against him, scandalized by him in that by which they ought most of all to have been edified. Therefore, giving place to anger, and not consenting to remain longer with ungrateful sheep, he abandoned the monastery and its inhabitants and moved to the emperor Louis, with whom he also remained for many days. But the monks, led by penitence, sent a delegation to him and begged that he return to the monastery, but they did not obtain it. He therefore remained with the emperor until the death of Otgar, archbishop of Mainz; appointed in his place, he was in charge of the aforesaid church of Mainz for nine years. Having also held a synod, he established many things for the [unclear] of the faithful. He wrote while still established in the monastery of Fulda, both in verse and in prose:

On the book of Ruth.
On Chronicles.
On Ezra and Nehemiah.
On Judith.
On Esther.
On Job.
On the Proverbs of Solomon.
On the Song of Songs.
On the book of Wisdom.
On Ecclesiasticus.
