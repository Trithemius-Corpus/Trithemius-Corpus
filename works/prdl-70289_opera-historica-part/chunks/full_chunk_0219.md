*Catalogue of Illustrious Men.*

*Caesarius, monk in Heisterbach.*
Caesarius, a monk in Heisterbach, of the Cistercian order in the diocese of Cologne, a devout man and an outstanding lover of regular discipline, and trained by long study in the divine scriptures. For the edification of novices, whose instructor he was by office, he wrote in simple and plain speech several little works, the reading of which is not to be despised by devout people. Among them I have found the following: a large volume of dialogues concerning miracles and various visions that occurred in his time in Germany, in [twelve?] books. Also homilies on the Sunday Gospels, two books; likewise on the Gospels concerning the saints, one book; many varied sermons pertaining to the instruction of novices, one book. The other works which he is said to have composed have by no means come to my knowledge through reading. He flourished under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

*Peter de Vineis, archchancellor of Emperor Frederick II.*
Peter de Vineis, archchancellor of Emperor Frederick II, a man very learned and eloquent in secular letters. He wrote, both in his own person and in that of the emperor, six books of letters to various persons; also one book on imperial power, and certain other things. He always defended Frederick’s party against the Roman Church and the supreme pontiff, the vicar of Christ; often in a public council, in the presence of the pope and the prelates, he tried by pleading with such eloquence to excuse his emperor’s rebellion that he turned everyone to admiration. But at last he was repaid by Frederick with a fitting reward, when he expelled him from his court and tore out the light of his eyes. This is the reward of those who defer to princes against obedience to the Roman see of the vicar of Christ; afterwards, unless they repent, they go down wretched to the lower regions. He flourished under the same Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.

*Jordan of the Order of Preachers.*
Jordan, of the Order of Friars Preachers, after Saint Dominic master general, a man sufficiently trained and learned in the divine scriptures, an outstanding preacher of the word of God, venerable no less for holiness than for knowledge, who by word and example converted many to the way of righteousness. Among the other monuments of his talent he wrote many useful exhortatory letters to the brothers, in which the whole integrity of the spiritual life is seen. He is also said to have composed very useful sermons. He is reported also to have written a book on the institution of his order, and certain other things unknown to us. He flourished at the same time as Saint Dominic, under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

*Simon, monk of the monastery of Affligem.*
Simon, a monk of the monastery of Affligem, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man zealous and learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, excellent in talent, distinguished in speech. He so turned into practice the counsel of our holy lawgiver Benedict, by which we are enjoined especially to love the divine scriptures in the monastery, that nothing in this life was sweeter to him than to devote himself to holy study, and he always turned over the little works of the holy fathers. Among other things he wrote: sermons on the Song of Songs, [unclear] books; also the admirable vision of a certain lay brother, one book; also several sermons and letters. He also abbreviated the Moralia of the blessed Pope Gregory in ten books, extracting all the better things that suited his purpose. The other works which he wrote have not come to my knowledge. From the time when the orders of the mendicant friars began, the study of letters perished in our order, and down to the present day it scarcely revives. For from the times of Emperor Frederick II down to the last year of Frederick III, in which we wrote these things, for nearly two hundred and seventy years, scarcely anyone appeared in our order who, after the example of the ancients, [unclear] knowledge and holiness of life [unclear], who because of knowledge and usefulness composed anything worthy for the edification of posterity, as is clear in what follows. But these things do not pertain to the matter.

*John, bishop of Strasbourg and Bosnia.*
John, called from his fatherland the German, from the town of [Freiburg?], of the Order of Friars Preachers, of the convent of Strasbourg and afterwards bishop of Bosnia in Hungary, a man much trained and learned in the divine scriptures, and singularly learned in secular philosophy and canon law; later, abandoning that office, he returned to his own order. After Raymond he was appointed master general of the whole order. He wrote certain excellent volumes, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among them there is extant a notable summa on cases, which they call the Summa of Confessors, in five books. On the decrees of Gratian also, the first among the named doctors, he produced an apparatus. The other works which he composed have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1250; he was buried in his own convent at Strasbourg, once famous for many miracles.
