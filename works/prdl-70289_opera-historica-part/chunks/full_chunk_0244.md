Heinrich Kalteisen, by homeland a man of Koblenz, of the order of the Friars Preachers of the convent of his native city, archbishop of the churches of Nidaros and Caesarea in Norway, was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in eloquence, a most eloquent declaimer of sermons. He wrote certain distinguished volumes of his talent, of which the following are extant: On the Magnificat, 1 book; various questions, almost without number, disputed in the schools and elsewhere, 1 book; sermons on the season and on the saints, 2 books; sermons through Lent, 1 book. He is also said to have written on the Sentences of Master Peter Lombard a great and notable volume, which I have not yet seen, and other things. He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1465, in the twelfth indiction, on the sixth day before the Nones of October, buried at Koblenz.

Heinrich of Gorkum, a learned doctor of sacred theology, an outstanding interpreter of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, an acute disputant, presiding from the master’s chair in the University of Cologne, by teaching, writing, and explaining earned great praise for his learning. He wrote works not to be despised in reading: Against the Hussite Heretics, 1 book; Summary of the Sayings of Saint Thomas, 1 book; sermons and various collations. He is also said to have written various questions, determinations, lectures, books, verses, and treatises, but they have not come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Pius II, in the year of the Lord 1460.

Johannes Kanneman, of the order of the Friars Minor, was a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect, distinguished in eloquence, outstanding in delivering sermons to the people. He is said to have thought otherwise than he should have concerning ecclesiastical power, whence he provoked the talents of many against himself. When he was sought by the minister of Saxony for imprisonment, he escaped by flight and betook himself to the Observance; thereafter he amended his error together with his life. Long before, Johannes of Hagen, a most learned Carthusian monk, had reproved him and recalled him to the paths of justice. Among many writings indeed unknown to us, he also wrote the following: A Defense of His Propositions, 1 book; a very notable treatise On the Passion of the Lord; sermons on the season and on the saints, 2 books; also various letters and questions. I have found nothing about the rest. He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick III and Pope Pius II, in the year of the Lord 1460.

Jacobus of Jüterbog, a monk of the Carthusian order of the house of the Savior at Erfurt, was a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, a very eminent interpreter of canon law, ready in intellect, clear in speech, devout in life and conduct. He is said previously to have been abbot of a certain monastery of the Cistercian order; for the hope of amendment of life he gave up the dignity of pastoral care together with the order and obtained a place on the aforesaid Mount of Saint Savior, where, made famous by writing and disputing, he gained the name of Doctor to such a degree that his words and writings were held almost as the oracle of Apollo.
