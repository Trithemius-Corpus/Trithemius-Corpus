*The Abbot of Sponheim*

John of Mount Carmel, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, prior of the convent of Rouen, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and sufficiently trained in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence. He wrote some little works of no contemptible authority, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there survives an excellent work:

On the Sentences, 4 books.
On the office of the Mass, 1 book. “Sing to the Lord a song.”
Various Questions. He also published some other works besides, which have not come into our hands.

Henry of Dolendorp, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the divine Scriptures through long practice, and perfectly instructed in the disciplines of worldly philosophy, which they call Aristotelian; acute in intellect, and very well suited to debating and resolving scholastic questions. For the instruction of readers he wrote certain excellent volumes. Of these I have found only:

On the Sentences, 4 books. Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished in the time of the Emperor Charles IV and Pope Urban V, in the year of the Lord 1370.

Gallus, abbot of the monastery of Aula Regia in Bohemia, of the order, as they say, of the Cistercians, a German by nation, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures through long practice, and rich in his reading of the ancients, an outstanding contemplator of divine things, subtle in intellect, clear and ordered in eloquence, also distinguished in the conduct of regular life, published for the edification of his brothers certain excellent volumes, by which he directed his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there survives a large and notable dialogue between father and son, which he entitled:

Malogranatum, 3 books. “Son, approaching to the service.”
Sermons to the brothers, 1 book.

This volume of the Malogranatum is today much used among religious and truly devout persons, because it is suitable for beginners, the progressing, and the perfect.

Richard, archbishop of Armagh and primate of Ireland, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy and canon law, distinguished in intellect, scholastic in discourse, of excellent industry in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote, from his own talent, several little works, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. When he once preached to the people on the subject of mendicancy, he set forth nine conclusions; offended by these, the brothers of the mendicant orders appealed before the people to the Apostolic See. Therefore, when both the archbishop and the brothers appeared at Avignon before Pope Innocent VI and the cardinals, in the year of the Lord 1357, on the eighth day of the month of November, Archbishop Richard publicly set forth, read, and pronounced:

Of his Propositions, 1 book. “Do not judge according to.”
He also wrote Sermons, 1 book. Questions on the Sentences, 1 book.
And certain other works.

He flourished in the time of the Emperor Charles IV and Pope Innocent VI, in the year of the Lord 1370.

Matthew of Cracow, a Pole by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, ready in intellect, scholastic in eloquence; having been raised to a master’s chair in the university of Prague, he earned great praise from all for his learning. He wrote many excellent little works, of which, however, few have come into our hands. I have seen his work, not to be rejected, addressed to Henry, bishop of the church of Warmia, on predestination, and on the fact that God made all things well; the speakers of this dialogue are father and son, and he entitled it:

Rationale of Divine Works, 7 books. “I should wish it to be declared by what.”
Also On Contracts, 1 book. “On the transfer of things [unclear].”
On the celebration of Mass, 1 book, to the bishop of Warmia.
Letters to various persons, 1 book. Sermons and Collations.

He flourished first in the university of Prague; then, when he was driven out with the other Germans by the Hussites, he taught letters for a time in the university of Paris.
