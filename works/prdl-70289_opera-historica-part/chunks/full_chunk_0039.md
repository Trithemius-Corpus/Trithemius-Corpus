*Annals of Trithemius*

In the thirty-sixth year of his reign, Basanus, king of the Sicambri, at the solemnity of his birthday at Nijmegen, summoned his sons, brothers, dukes, nobles, and distinguished members of his household to a very great banquet. With them, feasting lavishly for seven days, he performed sacrifices to the gods of his country, and showed Jupiter visible in the air above the temple [unclear]. On the eighth day, with the consent of all the nobles, he designated his son Clodomirus king over the Sicambri; in his native song he foretold many future things to the people, commended his sons and the kingdom to the princes, enjoined justice, and, entering the chapel of the temple, appeared nowhere again.

I do not know what I should assign to these little tales, indignation or laughter. Whether he was removed by deceit or by art remains uncertain; but this one thing I judge to have been the cause: the desire of a man, undoubtedly withdrawn either by his own ingenuity or by the voluntary will of a demon, to attain the reputation of divinity. For, as Amerodacus, philosopher of the Sicambri, writes, King Basan was most eager for divinity; he cultivated justice, loved his people, overthrew the enemy, was most dutiful to the good, and always cruel and severe to the unjust.

He vanished, however, or, to speak more correctly, withdrew himself, as Amerodacus is the authority, from the public assembly of the nobles, as we have said, in the thirty-sixth year of his reign; but before the birth of the Lord, in the year 248. The Sicambri, judging that he had been translated into the company of the gods, worshipped him under the name Basangot.

*The Eighth*

Clodomirus, the elder son of Basanus, succeeding in the kingdom, as his father had ordained with the consent of the princes, reigned eighteen years. In his first year, as soon as the Gauls learned that Basanus had vanished, whom they did not doubt to be dead, they sent messengers to Clodomirus and demanded back from him all the land between the Meuse and the Rhine, and around the [unclear] in circuit, which the Sicambri, from their first entry into the Cisthenan borders, had violently usurped for themselves contrary to justice. If he disdained to return it, they threatened that they would be ready to recover it by the sword, whether he sent back a negative answer through the appointed messengers or made no answer at all.

King Clodomirus, however, prudently and deliberately detained the messengers of the Gauls with him for a long time, as though he were going to take counsel with his princes concerning the urgent matter; and, taking the greatest care that the secret of his counsel and mind should not come to their knowledge, he ordered his servants to feed them abundantly and arrange that they should have cheerful days.

Meanwhile King Clodomirus sent messengers to the kings of the Saxons and Thuringians, urgently asking that they very secretly and quickly send forces to aid the Sicambri, their friends, against the Gauls, their common adversaries. The Saxons came with the Thuringians to assist their friends, as they were asked, bringing forward an army of one hundred thousand fighters. When matters had been arranged most secretly at his command, King Clodomirus allowed the messengers of the Gauls, furnished with gifts, to depart; and, lest anything of the foregoing could come to their knowledge on the road, he assigned escorts for a safe journey to those setting out, who would accompany them and observe them more carefully, with letters of this sort:

“Clodomirus, great king, to the Gallican satraps. We marvel at your rashness, that you demand back from us the land which our forefathers long ago, by right of war, most justly vindicated by the sword from yours, as from wrongful [unclear], their manifest enemies. Consider that our strength is far superior to yours. Cease from this rashness of unjust folly. Remember the defeats which you long ago deservedly received from our begetter, translated among the gods, and from his parents. Rest in your caves, hiding with foxes. It is ours to command you; yours, however, humbly to obey our precepts. But if you presume to come against us, we have decreed to meet you powerfully and to defend by the sword what our ancestors handed down to us.”

When the Gauls received these letters from the messengers, they were stirred to anger and resolved to avenge themselves upon the Sicambri as quickly as possible. Having skillfully built a bridge over the Meuse, they led across a huge army of fighters. King Clodomirus met them most powerfully with two hundred sixty-four thousand men; and, having divided the army into parts, he stationed one part in ambush; another he sent from the region at a distance so that, after killing the guards of the Gauls, it might seize the bridge first; the third part, which was greater in number of soldiers, he led against the adversaries.

The Sicambri and the Gauls engage in a place not far from the river Meuse, which is called Delon; for some time they are driven back in an uncertain battle. At last the Sicambri, pretending flight, began to withdraw little by little, until at length they drew the Gauls farther from the river Meuse to the place of ambush. There the Sicambri then stood immovable, and those who had previously feigned flight fought back most bravely like giants. Meanwhile the soldiers, circling around, came to [unclear].
