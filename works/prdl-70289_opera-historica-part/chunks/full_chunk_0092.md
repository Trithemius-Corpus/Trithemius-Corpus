But let Hunibald praise Clovis as much as he wishes, as though he acted well in these matters; for my part, in this respect I cannot praise a parricide, and one who killed so many innocent men.

For on a certain day, in the presence of many of his courtiers, he is said to have spoken about these relatives and kinsmen of his whom he himself had destroyed: “Woe is me, because I remain alone like a stranger among foreigners, and today I have no one of my relatives or kinsmen still living who, if adversity should come upon me in any matter, could help me as friend is wont to help friend.” He said this deceitfully, thinking that someone among those standing by would reply by way of consolation: “Look, you still have this man or that man of your people living,” so that any who survived among his kinsmen might also themselves be killed as soon as possible.

In the seventeenth year of his reign, King Clovis, at the continual urging of Queen Clotildis, who desired the cruel death of her parents to be avenged upon her uncle, declared war on Gundebald, king of the Burgundians. After he had been defeated, Clovis subjected Burgundy to the tribute and subjection of the Franks.

But Clovis, king of the Franks, after his kingdom had been extended through all Gaul and a great part of Germany, and after many deeds had been bravely accomplished both in war and in peace, at last died in the thirtieth year of his reign, but in the fifty-first year of his age, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 514, in the seventh indiction of the Romans, and was buried at Paris in the church of Saint Peter.

He left four sons: Lotharius, Clodomirus, and Hildebertus. These divided their father’s kingdom in equal portion. But because thereafter more kings were born from these, and because at one time the kingdom of the Franks was subject to one monarch, while at another time, divided into parts, it was ruled by many, we have thought it worthwhile to continue in order the names of those kings, marked beforehand chapter by chapter in red, who carried the line of the royal stock in a direct line down to King Hildericus, the last. For Lotharius was not the firstborn among the sons of Clovis; rather Theodoricus was older by birth. Clodomirus was born second, and Lotharius himself, from whom the genealogy of the kings is continued, was born third. After King Clovis had died, Hunibald also finished his own history concerning the origin of the Franks, distinguished into eighteen books, comprising a period of not less than 950 years, during which there were forty-seven kings from Marcomirus, son of Antenor, who first led the people from the borders of Scythia to the mouths of the river Rhine in Germany, down to the death of King Clovis. The things that follow, however, in this second volume of our annals, we have taken from authors whose names are these:

Einhardus, abbot of Saint Amand; Flodoardus, priest of the church of Reims; Richerus, monk of Reims; Gregorius, archbishop of Tours; Lampertus, abbot in Hasungen; Regino, abbot of Prüm; Eghardus, first abbot in [Vraugia]; Lampertus, monk of Hersfeld; Freculfus, from monk made bishop of Lisieux; Strabus, monk of Fulda; Windekindus, monk in Corbie of Saxony; Rupertus, monk of Saint Alban near Mainz; Ioannes, monk of the same monastery; Adelbertus, monk of the monastery of Saint Vincent of Metz; Meginfridus, monk of Fulda; Golstherus, monk of Saint Matthias, in the deeds of the Treveri; Marianus Scotus, monk of Fulda; Sigebertus, monk of Gembloux; Richardus, monk of Cluny; also Robert Gaguin, formerly minister general of the Order of the Holy Trinity; Ado, bishop of Vienne; Galfridus Brito; Saxo Dacus; Ptolomaeus Lucensis; a Saxon by homeland, in the Rudimentum novitiorum; Wernherus, Carthusian monk of Cologne; Hartmannus Schedel from Mount Noricus; Iacobus Bergomensis, hermit of Saint Augustine; Marcus Antonius Sabellicus. From the aforementioned and various other writers of histories I wrote the two volumes of our annals that follow, not without very great labor, because there is much diversity among historiographers, not only in years, but also in deeds and in the names of persons: what is narrated as having been done by me in one year is narrated as having been done in this one, and a deed done, which by this one is told thus, by another otherwise. Since, therefore, it is not within my powers to be able to reconcile so great a discordance of histories, I follow the opinion of those whom I find to have differed less from one another, something that could be observed not uniformly in every individual case, but in some.
