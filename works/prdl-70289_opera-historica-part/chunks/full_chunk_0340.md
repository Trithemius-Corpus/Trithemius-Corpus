Faustus, a monk and disciple of our most holy father Benedict, Italian by nation, a man venerable in learning and character, when he was seven years old was offered by his parents to the divine father Benedict. By him, at length, after the completion of his instruction in the regular profession, he was sent with blessed Maurus into Gaul to build a monastery; there, after living for many years in great holiness, when Saint Maurus had died, he returned to Monte Cassino. At the brothers’ request, he wrote, in a very clear style, to Boniface II, pontiff of the city of Rome:

*The Life of Saint Maurus the Abbot*, 1 book.

He flourished under the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord 600.

Justinian the Great, emperor, nephew by his sister of the elder Justin, reigned after him for 38 years. A man of great and excellent talent, he rightly acquired a place among ecclesiastical writers; he both published a very famous volume against the heretics and, through the hands of John the patrician and the consuls Theophilus and Dorotheus, corrected with marvellous brevity the Roman laws, which lay scattered in more than two thousand books. For he reduced all the constitutions of the princes, which were contained in many volumes, to twelve books and called them the Code. Again, reducing the laws of the individual magistrates and judges, which, as we have said, extended to almost two thousand books, to the number of fifty books in three volumes, he named them the Code of the Digest or Pandects. He also composed four books of Institutes, in which the kernel of the laws is contained and explained. The new laws too, which he himself had established, he reduced into one volume. Therefore the order of all the books of the emperor Justinian that have come down to us is the following, namely:

*The Code of Justinian*, 12 books. “These things which are necessary.”

*The Digest or Pandects*, 50 books.

*Institutes*, 4 books. “The Imperial Majesty.”

*Epitome of the Laws*, 1 book.

*On the Incarnation of the Lord, against Eutyches*, 3 books.

*Against the African Bishops*, 1 book. “Who would doubt that he also wrote other things, especially many elegant letters, which have not come into our hands?” He at last, in his old age, became deranged and foolish and died, in the year of the Lord 565, in the thirteenth indiction. Justin the Younger succeeded him.

Dionysius, a Roman abbot, surnamed, as is reported, Exiguus, a man certainly great and most renowned in talent and learning, trained in the holy scriptures and nobly learned in the discipline of secular letters, was distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence. Of these I have found only the following:

*The Great Paschal Cycle*, 1 book, of 532 years.

*On the Calculation of the Paschal Feast*, 1 book. “The calculation of the paschal feast.”

Concerning the rest I have found nothing. He translated from Greek into Latin the life of Saint Pachomius the abbot, also the book of Gregory of Emesa *On the Creation of Man*, and the writings of Proterius, bishop of Alexandria, to Pope Leo, and certain other things. He began his cycle in the year of the Lord 532, which was the last year of the great paschal cycle; once it has been completed, it always returns to the same point.

He therefore flourished under the great emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.
