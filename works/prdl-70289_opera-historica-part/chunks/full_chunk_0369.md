Wilhelm, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, in the diocese of Speyer, of the Order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, to whom blessed Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury, wrote several letters, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, inferior to no one among the Germans in his time, a philosopher, astronomer, and distinguished musician, most holy in life and conduct, since he is read to have shone with many miracles, reformed regular discipline, which had already collapsed, in many monasteries of Germany. Therefore, for the conversation of monastic observance, from the statutes of the ancients, he wrote:

Constitutions of monks, two books. “Since I, brother Wilhelm.”
On music and tones, one book. “After, by God’s gift, [unclear].”
One book. “Whoever [is] most truthful.”
On the clock, one book. “If anyone in this necessary [matter].”
On the correction of the Psalter, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.
To St. Anselm of Canterbury, three letters.
And certain other works.

He also published several other works, which through the negligence of the ancients have fallen into oblivion. He died under Emperor Henry III, but as king of the Romans the fourth, in the year of the Lord 1091, indiction 14, on the fourth Nones of July. He was buried in the middle of the church of the Apostles, with a great reputation for holiness.

Giselbert, provost of the monastery of Weltin in England, of the Order of St. Benedict, hearer and disciple of Anselm of Canterbury, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and skilled in the practice of secular [learning], and nobly taught in the disciplines of secular letters, a clear theologian and poet, ready in talent and cautious in speech, not ignorant of philosophy, sufficiently endowed in talent, made his name known to posterity by writing, while he composed certain necessary works, in which he exercised his talent magnificently. Of these are reported the following; [he wrote] splendid volumes, of which few have come to my knowledge. There exists his volume, written in elegant meter; the following are reported:

On the Passion of St. Cyriacus, one book.
Life of Saint Bavo, one book.
On the prophet Isaiah.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Henry IV, with Anselm, in the year of the Lord [1090?].

Gunther, monk of Elno, of the Order of St. Benedict, skilled in the practice of the divine writings, and nobly taught in the disciplines of secular letters, a theologian and clear poet, sufficiently instructed by talent, excelling in verse and prose. Not ignorant of philosophy, ready in talent and cautious in speech, he wrote his name; he made [it] known to posterity by writing, while he magnificently exercised his talent in certain works. Of these splendid volumes, of which few have come to my knowledge, there exists his volume written in elegant meter; the following are reported:

Life of Saint Trudo, one book. “To the one and eternal first [one].”
Life of Saint Remaclus.
And certain other works.

And many other works. He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord [1090?].

Theoderic, abbot of the diocese of Canterbury, of the Order of the divine father Benedict, a German by nation, a man studious and learned in the sacred writings, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, excelling in counsel and [unclear]. Nobly taught in philosophy, in proof of his sincere faith, he wrote in both styles. He wrote, under the name of Moses, what he had been as a Jew, and of Peter, what he had begun to be as a Christian, a distinguished dialogue, necessary concerning Christ, concerning the Church, and concerning the future life, which he entitled:

Against the Jews, one book.
On free will, one book.
On knowledge and philosophy. “You are said to wonder.”
One book.
Offense concerning priests, one book.
Seal of St. Mary.
Summary of histories, one book. “The army in the camps of the eternal [one].”
