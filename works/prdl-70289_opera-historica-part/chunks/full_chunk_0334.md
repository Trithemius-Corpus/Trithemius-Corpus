*Abbot of Sponheim*

[unclear], an outstanding preacher of homilies to the people, skilled in the Greek and Syriac languages, flourished first at Edessa; afterwards, coming to Constantinople, he was held there in great esteem and ended his last day. He wrote, in both Greek and Syriac, almost innumerable small works. Of these very few have come to my knowledge. For I have found only:

Against the Nestorians, one book. Against the Eutychians, one book.
Against the Timotheans, one book.

He flourished under the emperor Zeno, in the year of the Lord 480.

Gennadius, bishop of the city of Constantinople, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote in Greek a number of small works on the sacred volumes, but they have by no means come into our hands. For he was an outstanding preacher of homilies, and distinguished no less for his life than for his knowledge of the Scriptures. Among other things, however, a work of his commentaries is reported:

On Daniel the prophet, one book.

Many homilies to the people, one book. He died under Leo, the first emperor who reigned, in the year of the Lord 460.

Claudius, bishop of Vienne, to whom Salvian the priest composed one book, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and most highly trained in secular letters, famous no less for holiness than for knowledge, shone forth in his own times. Because of frequent earthquakes, he first instituted the litanies, which the Roman Church thereafter undertook to continue. He also wrote letters. He wrote:

On the State of the Soul, three books.

And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Zeno, in the year of the Lord 480. And lest the name Claudius offend anyone who is unaware, let him know that the same man is called Mamertus.

Sidonius, bishop of the Auverni, learned in the divine Scriptures and, in the judgement of all, the most learned man of his time in the disciplines of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote many distinguished little works both in meter and in unbound discourse. Of these I have been able to find only those which are set down below, namely:

Panegyric to the emperor Anthemius, one book. Auspicio et numero.
Panegyric to Julius Valerius, one book. Concepe praeteritos.
Panegyric to his father-in-law, one book. Phoebe peragrato tam.
To Felix, one book. Dic dic quod petoma.
Epithalamium for Ruricius and Hiberix, one book. Inter cyaneas epieram.
Epithalamium for Polemius, one book. Forte procellosi reme.
Eucharisticon to Faustus, one book. Phebum et ter ternas.
Also of epigrams, one book. Quatuor ante dies.
To Pontius Leontius Burgus, one book. Bistonii regis stabulum.
In Praise of the City of Narbonne, one book. Salve Narbo potens.
Letters to various persons, nine books. Diu precipis domine ma.

He also wrote many other compositions, brief indeed, but sufficiently learned; for the sake of brevity I have passed over their titles. He flourished in the times of the emperor Zeno, in the year of the Lord 480.

Simplicius, pope, by nation a Tiburtine, after Saint Hilary presided over the Roman and universal Church for [unclear] years. He was a learned and holy man, who appeared not only simple according to his name, but also upright, turning away from evil and fearing God. He restored many churches that had fallen down from extreme age, issued decrees necessary for the Christian commonwealth, and wrote some things for the edification of the faithful. Of these I have read only:

To John, bishop of Ravenna, one letter. Si quis esse intuitus Eccle.
To Florentius and Equitius, bishops, one letter. Relatio nominis vestrae.
To Severus, bishop, one letter.

But he also wrote many other very useful letters, which I have not yet seen.

He died under the emperor Zeno, in the year of the Lord 483, in the sixth indiction, on the sixth Nones of March, and was numbered among the saints.

Servusdei, bishop of a certain church whose city-name I have not been able to find, a man learned in the divine Scriptures by constant exercise and rich in the reading of the ancients, subtle in intellect, clear in speech, is said to have fashioned a number of little works. Of these there exists a volume furnished with sacred Scriptures, against those who say that Christ, while living in this life, did not see the Father with his bodily eyes. The heading of this work is:

That the Son Saw the Father, one book.

He also wrote certain other things.
