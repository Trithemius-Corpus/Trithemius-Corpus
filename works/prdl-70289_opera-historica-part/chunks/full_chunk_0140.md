*On the Origin of the Franks.*

[unclear] our king, with the whole people of the Thuringians, [is ready?] to give to your people an excellent land, which is by the banks of the Main and among the Alemanni, so that it may be a wall for us and the destruction of the Alemanni. At the petition of the Thuringians, the Franks gave their consent and accepted what was offered.

Therefore in the year of the Lord’s nativity 316, which was the seventh year of Clodomir, king of the Franks, on the nineteenth day of April, there went out from Cismarine Francia, where now are Utrecht, Holland, Guelders, Zeeland, part of Frisia and Westphalia, and also Brabant with many adjacent lands, thirty thousand armed men and two thousand six hundred eighty-six farmers and craftsmen of various trades, with their wives, children, and possessions, chosen and approved by the king to go up into the land which the Thuringians had promised to give to the Franks; and they received it with great joy.

The king appointed as duke over the people sent out and the whole army his brother Genebald, who in earlier years had fought with the Alemanni. He was a man of great prudence, bold and warlike, who not only held well the borders given to him and his people, but in a short time greatly enlarged them. And when the Franks had left their ancestral hearths, Duke Genebald advanced with twenty thousand fighters to the place assigned to him by the Thuringians; the remaining ten thousand, for the protection of the people following behind, he left on the road after him with his son Marcomer.

When the Alemanni learned that the Franks had received new settlements from the Thuringians beside the banks of the Main, in their midst, they were greatly afraid, and, sending envoys to Duke Genebald, they begged for peace, and [unclear] every cause of quarrel [with?] Thuringia and Saxony. At their entry all the Thuringians, by command of their king Herol, withdrew into Thuringia and received the dwellings assigned to them, leaving, by order of the king, provisions sufficient for the Franks for a whole year. But the Franks, newly arrived, settled in the land now their own; first in that [unclear] they send. The Thuringians did likewise. And so the great discord between them was extinguished by Genebald’s prudence.

In the same year, in the month of September, Marcomer, the son of Duke Genebald, came with the remaining people of the Franks into southern Thuringia, and they received the promised settlements around the river Main, having Bohemia on the east, Alemannia on the south, the Rhine and Mainz on the west, and Thuringia and Saxony on the north. At their entry all the Thuringians, by command of their king Herol, withdrew into Thuringia and received the dwellings assigned to them, leaving, by just command, provisions sufficient for the Franks for a whole year. The Franks, impatient [unclear] and very ready for wars. Yet he could not restrain them because they were more than usually occupied in many provinces.

Duke Genebald, however, [unclear] with Clodomir, king of the Franks, who reigned in western Francia around the sea, the Meuse, and the Rhine, entered into an alliance with the Alemanni and Thuringians, so that he might be stronger to attack the Romans and Gauls, who threatened the borders of Francia on the other side of the Rhine at Mainz and Strasbourg. Against them he often fought after crossing the Rhine, and killed many of them.

Duke Genebald begot Dagobert, who ruled the people for twenty-one years. Clodius, however, begot Marcomer, and he begot Clodius, who ruled for twenty-three years. Clodius begot Faramund, who in the second year of his dukedom was elected king of the Franks, as is written below in its proper order. The aforesaid four leaders of the eastern Franks, presiding over the people for ninety-four years, extended the borders of the possessions they had received as far as the river Rhine, and did great damage to the Gauls and Romans. But now what remains concerning the succession of the kings of the Franks must be continued. King Clodomir died in the eighteenth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord 328.

Richimer, son of Clodomir, reigned after his father for fourteen years, and throughout all the days of his reign was in conflict with the Romans and Gauls, and joined many battles with them. He had also in his father’s lifetime fought with Caesar Constantius, the father of Constantine, namely in the year of the Lord 218. For Constantius, about to enter Britain by command of the Romans, made a hostile attack on the people of the Franks dwelling along the borders of the Rhine by the sea and the Meuse, and compelled them to retreat, many of them being slain in battle.
