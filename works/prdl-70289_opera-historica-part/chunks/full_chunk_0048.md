*Compendium, Book I.*

After the Goths had again been driven out of Saxony, the Franks held a conference with the Germans and the Teutons, debating among themselves how they might thereafter bar the Goths and the Scanzian peoples from returning into the territories of the Saxons or Germans. At last it pleased all the kings and nobles that from the people of the Franks, Saxons, Thuringians, Germans, and Teutons, who around the sea were nearer neighbors to the Dacians and Goths, they should gather one nation, with children, wives, and beasts of burden, to inhabit the land beyond furthest Saxony, nearest to the Scanzians, and to prevent the incursions of enemies.

When this agreement had been concluded, Richimer, king of the Franks, sent eighteen thousand men, with children and wives, into that region; over them he appointed as leader and prince Sunno, his second-born son. To him, according to the agreement, many then came together from the Germans, Saxons, and Thuringians; from diverse peoples they became one, and within a short time they grew into a great nation, and thereafter did not permit the Scanzians to devastate Saxony. Yet they were rather often harassed by the Romans.

This duke Sunno established the seat of his nation with power in the borders of Saxony, having to the east the Sarmatians; to the south the Slavs of Bohemia; to the west Saxony and the Teutons; and to the north the Cimbri, the sea, the Dacians, the Scanians, the Goths, and island-dwelling men inhabiting marshy places.

Sunno increased not a little the possession he had received. After him Sunnon received [unclear] his principate, who in the year of the Lord’s nativity one hundred forty-six, indiction [unclear], received the nation, who in the year of the Lord’s nativity one hundred forty-six, on the banks of the river Oder, built a new city, which, out of love for his forefathers, he called Frankfurt. After this he also founded another city at the mouth of the aforesaid river, not far from the sea, which from his own name he called Sunda, or, as Hunibald testifies, Domicianus.

The name of the Franks remained for some time in these lands, until at last, when the dukes had multiplied, the region was divided into several parts, and each division received its name from individual princes or events. From Marcomer and his son Brando, who founded the city [unclear] Brandenburg, some were named [unclear]; from Brando his son, who founded the city [unclear] Brandenburg, others were named Braburgi; and others took other names from others.

Previously, in the fifth year of King Richimer, Domitian Caesar had worn down the Sarmatians and Dacians who dwelt on the borders of Germany toward the east, and afflicted them greatly. And, adding sin to crime, after the most impious Nero he stirred up the second persecution against the Christians; he was the first of all the Caesars to command that he be called God and Lord by all.

In the eleventh year of his reign Richimer, king of the Franks, had a war with the Romans and Gauls, not far from the city of Basana, aided by the common votes of the Germans, in which many fell on both sides. Meanwhile, when the Roman legions had returned to Cologne Agrippina, word was brought that Nerva Caesar was dead; in his place the soldiers raised Trajan, a man of Spanish nation, to be Caesar. He ruled nineteen years, six months, fifteen days.

But Richimer, king of the Franks, after many brave deeds, finally died in the twenty-third year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 113, the eleventh Roman indiction. Ruthvicus the Frank wrote in verse of his life, character, deeds, and worship of the gods.

ODEMARS, the elder son by birth of the aforesaid Richimer, king of the Franks, reigned after his father for fourteen years, a prince of the Franks very beloved by the people, who refrained from wars as far as could be done without damage to the commonwealth. For he made truces, which the Franks, because of the pledge interposed, call, as it were, “confidences,” with the Gauls and the Romans, to whom thereafter he showed himself so much a friend that he compelled all to lament his death.

Thus as long as he lived among men he kept the promised peace, and devoted all his zeal to the honor and adornment of his nation; he [built/adorned?] very many castles, cities, and fortifications throughout the kingdom. For the Franks, Saxons, and Germans at that time were still subject to the vanity of idols and far removed from knowledge of the Christian faith.
