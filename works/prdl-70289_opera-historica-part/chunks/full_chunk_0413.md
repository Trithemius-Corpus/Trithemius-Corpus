Acting and writing against John the Twenty-second, who condemned the heresy of the Fraticelli, together with Peter of Picenum, whom Emperor Louis had set up as an idol and had caused to be called Nicholas the Fifth, and declaring him to be a heretic, he was excommunicated by him. Fleeing to Emperor Louis, likewise excommunicated, he said to him: “O Emperor, defend me with the sword, and I shall defend you with the word.” And so he remained excommunicated with the excommunicated man until the end of his life, and at length at death he was absolved. He was a very fierce opponent of Scotus his master, and attacked almost all his sayings. He wrote:

Against John XXII, on the poverty of Christ and the apostles.
An Apology, one book.
On the Sentences, four books.
Also Quodlibets, one book.
On the Supporters of Heretics, seven books. In all curious matters.
On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished in the time of Emperor Louis IV and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1320. And he was buried in a convent of his order in the Norican province, which is now called Bavaria, of which Emperor Louis had formerly been duke.

Alpharus, of the order of Friars Minor, a Spaniard by nation, a hearer of John Scotus, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, held in esteem by John XXI, acted in writing and in words against William Ockham the Englishman. He published certain distinguished volumes of his intellect, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these there is a great and notable work describing the defects of ecclesiastical persons, which he entitled:

On the Lamentation of the Church, one book.
On the Sentences, In the name of our Lord Jesus, four books.
An Apology, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished at the same time as Ockham, under Emperor Louis IV and Pope John XXII, in the year of the Lord 1320.

Matthaeus Sylvaticus, a Mantuan by homeland, noble by birth, a physician by art and profession, in which faculty in his time he was held before many others both learned and experienced, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence. He wrote in that science volumes not to be despised, by which he brought his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have seen only the notable work addressed to Rupert, king of Sicily, and physician, on all simple medicines and their properties, which he entitled:

Pandecta, one book. Aaron in Greek, in Arabic.
I have seen nothing of the rest.

He flourished under Emperor Louis IV, in the year of the Lord 1320.

Johannes Canonicus, of the order, as they say, of Friars Minor, formerly a hearer and disciple of John Scotus in the school of Paris, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and subtly learned in secular philosophy, and also not ignorant of both pontifical and imperial law. He is said to have composed certain distinguished small works, by which, exercising his intellect, he also made his name known to posterity. But I have seen none of them. They are said to be:

On the Sentences, four books.
Disputed Questions, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Emperor Louis IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.
