Christophorus Porsena, a Roman, prior of Saint Albina in the City, of the order of the hermit brothers of Saint William, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, highly skilled in the Greek and Latin tongues, since from early youth he drank in those letters in Greece itself and under Greek teachers, so that he seemed to have been born from Greece; subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote, among other things:

Letters to various persons, 1 book. And many other things.

He translated into Latin Origen against the philosopher Celsus, eight books, for Pope Sixtus IV; also the moral sermons of Saint John Chrysostom, twenty-five in number, which he dedicated to Marco Barbo, cardinal of St Mark. But he also converted certain little works of Theophylact, archbishop of Bulgaria, together with other very fine works, and gave them to be read by Latins. He died at Rome, carried off by plague, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1486, in the fourth indiction.

Hermolaus Barbarus, Venetian by nation, patrician by birth, archbishop and patriarch of Aquileia, and finally cardinal-bishop of the sacred Roman Church, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular letters, a very celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, poet, astronomer, and geometer, highly skilled in Greek and Latin speech, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, devout and distinguished in life and conduct, and most worthy of every ecclesiastical honor, applying zeal and labor from youth to good letters and holy morals, became so great and of such quality that he left no second to himself. Beginning to write from the eighteenth year of his age, he composed many most elegant little works, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of their number the following are reported:

And many other things. The Plinian Corrections is a large work, which he began as legate at Rome and completed as patriarch; he dedicated it to Pope Alexander VI, and in it he healed almost five thousand wounds of copyists. When once, in the year of the Lord ’86, he was passing through Cologne on his way to Mainz, at the request of Theoderic Flas of Neuss, a physician, he composed a little book on how much astronomy agrees with medicine. To Marcus Antonius Sabellicus, the historian, he published a book on the precepts of writing history. He interpreted Aristotle’s Rhetoric and the whole Dialectic, and also Dioscorides; he composed elegies, letters, and metrical orations, up to twelve thousand verses, as we have said. He translated into Latin the responsive treatise of the patriarch of Constantinople on the Catholic faith, to the questions of Mehmed, the great Turk. Also Plutarch on Isis and Osiris, and the same author’s Why the Oracles Ceased. To Arnold Bostius, Carmelite of Ghent, once a little work of Dionysius the Areopagite. He translated many other things as well. His work On the Married State contains about six hundred verses, in which he most elegantly disputes whether it is expedient for a wise man to take a wife, concluding that it is not. He died in the year of the Lord 1493, in the thirty-ninth year of his age, in the eleventh indiction.
