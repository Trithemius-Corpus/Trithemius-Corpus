*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

Ambrosius, a hearer of Origen, at first a Marcionite and afterward corrected by Origen himself, was a distinguished confessor of the Christian faith; he also deserved to be promoted to the office of deacon. He was certainly a very learned man and a devoted lover of books; by supplying funds, shorthand-writers, and scribes, he enabled our Adamantius to compose almost infinite volumes. He was so great a promoter of Origen’s nightly labors that by day and night he urged him, by words and writings, to write something new, so much so that in a certain familiar letter he is named by Origen as “the taskmaster of the work.” He used to say that he had never gone to bed or taken food in Origen’s presence unless first one of the brethren, who were always at hand, had sounded forth the sacred writings. He wrote to Origen, giving thanks for his conversion.

Letters, 1 book. And certain other works. He flourished with Origen, in the year of the Lord 230.

Tryphon, a hearer and disciple of Origen, to whom some of Origen’s letters are extant, was a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech. He wrote in Greek certain excellent little works, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these the following are extant:

On the Red Cow in Deuteronomy, 1 book. On the Quality of Sacrifices, 1 book.
On the Dove and the Turtledove, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Alexander, in the year of the Lord 240.

Minucius Felix, an advocate, Roman by birth, a man most learned in secular letters and devoted to the divine readings, excellent in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote in Latin certain excellent little works, by which he commended the memory of himself to posterity. Of these there is extant a dialogue between a Christian and a heretic, which is entitled:

Octavius, 1 book. On Fate, against the Mathematicians, 1 book.

He flourished at Rome under the emperor Alexander, in the year of the Lord 35.

Beryllus, bishop of Bostra in Arabia, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently instructed in secular matters, after he had ruled the church gloriously for some time, at last fell into the heresy of the Paulianists, who denied that Christ existed before Mary; but, corrected by Origen, he wrote many things. Of these there is extant between them:

A Dialogue Held, 1 book. Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.

And certain other works. He flourished under the emperor Alexander, in the year of the Lord 245.

Hippolytus, bishop of a certain city whose name Jerome did not state, was certainly a man both devoted to and learned in the divine writings. He wrote many and almost incredible volumes, so that Ambrosius, of whom we have already made mention above, used to exhort Origen to imitate him and strongly persuaded him to expound the divine Scriptures, providing him, as we have said, with seven shorthand-writers, and as many very swift girls for writing, and an equal number of copyists, with their costs and expenses. One could see a beautiful and delightful contest, in which each greatly desired to surpass the other in the publication of books.

Concerning the works of Hippolytus, the following are reported:

On the Hexaemeron, 1 book. On the Apocalypse, 1 book.
On Genesis, 1 book. On the Proverbs of Solomon, 1 book.
On Exodus, 1 book. On Ecclesiastes, 1 book.
On Numbers, 1 book. On Saul and the Pythoness, 1 book.
On the Song of Songs, 1 book. On Antichrist, 1 book.
On the Prophet Zechariah, 1 book. Against Marcion, 1 book.
On Daniel, 1 book. Against All Heresies, 2 books.

He also published many and various treatises on the Psalms, and almost innumerable homilies and sermons, and many other works still unknown to us. He flourished under the emperor Alexander, in the year of the Lord 250.

Alexander, bishop of Cappadocia, when he was going to Jerusalem out of desire for the holy places, was ordained bishop there by divine revelation after Saint Narcissus. He shone forth not only as famous for sanctity and learning, and at last, under Decius, [unclear] for the faith of Christ, he consummated many [unclear] and martyrdom. He wrote many elegant letters to various persons serving the most impious tyrant. Of these the following are especially reported:

On the Peace of the Church, to the Aaronites, 1 letter. To the Antiochenes, 1 letter.
For Origen against Demetrius, 1 letter. To Origen, several letters.

He suffered at Caesarea under Decius, in the year of the Lord 253, indiction 1.
