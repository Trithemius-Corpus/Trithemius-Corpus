of the [unclear] diocese, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and a philosopher, rhetorician, poet, and distinguished theologian most learned in all the letters of secular studies, skilled in Greek and Latin, subtle in talent, profound in knowledge, sweet, ornate, and eloquent in speech, most excellent in expounding the divine Scriptures. Among the other works of his talent he wrote: On Leviticus, a very celebrated and highly useful work, 20 books; On the Epistles of the Apostle Paul, 14 books; also a History of the Kings and Princes of the Franks, 1 book; also a Chronicle of His Own Times, 1 book; Letters to Various Persons, 1 book; Various Sermons, 1 book; and many poems of diverse kinds. He is read to have made very elegant commentaries on many books of the Bible, and also to have written several other treatises, which have by no means come down to us.

Aribo, the nineteenth archbishop of the church of Mainz, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, renowned in talent and pleasing in speech, wrote, among other monuments of his talent, a very beautiful work on the fifteen Gradual Psalms, 1 book, and various letters to different persons. Having celebrated a synod in the monastery of Seligenstadt with his bishops and abbots, in the year of the Lord 1032, indiction 6, he established many good and useful measures, which are contained in authentic writings. Burckhard, bishop of Worms, a most learned man, of whom mention will be made immediately, was personally present at this synod. To this Archbishop Aribo, because he was a lover of letters, many famous men, exercising their talents, wrote various little works. He died under Emperor Conrad, in the year of the Lord 1031, indiction 14, in the twelfth year of his episcopate.

Burckhard, formerly a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, bishop of Worms, or of the Vangiones, once the hearer and disciple of Albert, abbot of Gembloux, a most learned man (whom Gratian mentions in the Decrees, distinction 73, “In the name of the Lord”), a man certainly most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, subtle in talent and renowned in speech. He wrote for the edification of the church several distinguished volumes, by which he commended his name both to the present and to posterity. Of these I have seen that large volume and notable work of decrees, divided into twenty books, which the diligence of jurists used before the compilation of the monk Gratian. He also wrote many elegant letters to various persons. The rest that he composed have not come down to me. He flourished in the times of Henry I, in the year of the Lord 1020.

Berno, abbot of the monastery of Reichenau, of the order of Saint Benedict, in the diocese of Constance, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and excellently instructed in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, and musician of very celebrated reputation, no less distinguished in life than in learning. He wrote several little works of reading not to be despised, concerning which the following are reported: to Pilgrim, archbishop of Cologne, an excellent volume On Music, of which the original is with us, 1 book; also On Musical Instruments, 1 book; to the aforesaid Aribo, archbishop of Mainz, On the Advent of the Lord, 1 book; On the Office of the Mass, 1 book; On the Fast of the Four Times, 1 book; On the Fast of Saturday, 1 book; On the Measure of the Monochord, 1 book; Letters to Various Persons, 1 book; also Sermons to the Brothers. And many other things. He flourished in the times of Saint Henry, emperor first, but king second, in the year of the Lord 1020.

Adelbold, bishop of Utrecht, formerly a monk of the order of the divine father Benedict (under whose name Heriger, abbot of Lobbes, composed a dialogue On the Advent of the Lord), a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently instructed in secular letters, subtle in talent, eloquent in speech, outstanding in life and conduct, and on account of those virtues held in great esteem in the court of Saint Henry the Emperor. He wrote some little works of reading by no means to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these there is extant the Life and Deeds of that same Saint Emperor Henry, 1 book; On the Praises of the Holy Cross, 1 book; also On the Praises of Saint Mary, 1 book. He also published various sermons in praise of the saints. And many other things, both in verse and prose, which have not come to my knowledge. He died under Conrad II, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1027, indiction 10, in the eighteenth year of his episcopate, on the fifth day before the Kalends of December.

Bruno, bishop of Würzburg. [Würzburg] devoted to and learned, and not ignorant of secular letters, [unclear] in talent and deceased, shone with miracles. He wrote and compiled from the sayings of the ancient fathers a work not to be despised on the whole Psalter, and with wondrous knowledge corrected the text, previously corrupt in many places. If he wrote anything further, it has not come down to me. He died under Emperor Henry II, in the year of the Lord 1045, indiction 13, on the sixth day before the Kalends of June, and was numbered among the saints.
