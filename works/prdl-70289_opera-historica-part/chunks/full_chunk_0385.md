FRANCIS, founder and head of the Order of Minors, an Italian by nation, sprung from the city of Assisi in Italy, a man of most holy conduct and a most ardent follower of evangelical poverty, who by word and example converted many from iniquity and directed them into the way of Catholic justice, was so great a lover of Christ that in contempt of the world and of transitory consolation he scarcely had an equal. In his order many very learned men have already flourished, who for the usefulness of the faithful have composed almost innumerable little works. Francis himself, however, wrote a brief work:

The Rule of the Minors, book I, "The rule and life of the Minors."

A brief Testament, book I, "The Lord gave to me, brother."

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

CAESARIUS, a monk in Heisterbach, of the Cistercian order, a German by nation, of the diocese of Cologne Agrippina, a devout man, outstanding in regular discipline, and by long practice devoted to the divine scriptures, composed for the instruction of the novices, whose instructor he was, some little works in a simple and open style, the reading of which is not to be despised by more simple devout brothers. Of these I have found only the following.

A lengthy work on miracles and diverse visions made in Germany in his own time, which he entitled in imitation of the holy Pope Gregory.

Dialogues on various matters, 12 books, "Gather up the fragments, lest."

On the Sunday Gospels, book I, "The cluster of Cyprus, beloved."

On the Gospels for saints, book I, "Our Lord and Savior Jesus."

Sermons to novices, book I.

Besides these he published several other works, which have not come to my knowledge.

WILLIAM, monk of the monastery of the divine martyr Dionysius of Paris, of the Order of St. Benedict, a Frenchman by nation, a man skilled in the divine scriptures by constant study and excellently learned in the disciplines of secular letters, clear in talent and eloquence, and a distinguished knower of times, not ignorant, notable in morals and life, was of excellent talent in declaiming sermons to the people; gloriously preaching the cross against the Albigensian heretics, by apostolic authority he signed many of the faithful against them. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which, writing and teaching various things, he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these there is extant:

A volume of Histories, 3 books. And many letters to various persons, book I.

He is said, moreover, to have published commentaries on the divine scriptures, which I have not yet seen.

JACOBUS de Vitriaco, bishop of Acre, afterward cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, bishop of Tusculum, a man learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, notable in morals and life, was of excellent talent in declaiming sermons to the people; gloriously preaching the cross against the Albigensian heretics, by apostolic authority he signed many of the faithful against them. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity.

The Eastern History, book I. The Life of Mary of Oignies, 3 books.

Against the Saracens, book I. On the holy women of Liege, book I.

Letters to various persons, book I. Besides these he composed some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

BONAGUIDA of Arezzo, a man in both laws the most learned of all in his time, of great authority among the ancient professors of this faculty, subtle in talent, clear in eloquence. He wrote for the usefulness of students of law certain splendid volumes, by which he made himself useful to those present and known to those to come. Among these there is extant the noble summa which he entitled:

The Introductory Work for Advocates, 2 books, "Since the office of advocacy."

The Pearl of Law, book I, "Since after the invention."

Explanations of various cases. But he is said to have composed certain other works, which have not come to my knowledge.

PETER de Vineis, a German by nation, scribe and chancellor of Emperor Frederick II, a learned and eloquent man, but especially blameworthy in this, that adhering to that same Frederick and relying on his favor he foolishly barked against the Roman Church; by him at last he was worthily rewarded, when he had offended him in certain matters, and lost the light together with his eyes. He wrote certain little works of his talent, of which I have found only these:

Letters in the person of the Emperor, 6 books, "The pontiffs gathered."

On imperial power, book I.

He flourished in the times of the same Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.
