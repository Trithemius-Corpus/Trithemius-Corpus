*The Second of the Annals of Trithemius.*

Antenor, the elder son of King Marcomir, raised to the kingship in the same year, reigned after his father for thirty years. He was held in the highest honor among his people; he secured for himself the very firm friendship of the Saxons, with whose help, as victor, he engaged in many battles with the Romans and the Gauls. His brothers Priamus and Nicanor assisted him, together with the rest of the dukes, with steadfast loyalty; after crossing the river, they often inflicted many troubles upon the Gauls. Sunno too, the king's uncle, with his sons, always adhered to Antenor himself with the utmost fidelity in all things, and instructed him in government as his own son, while nevertheless venerating him as lord.

King Antenor had a wife named Cambra, who was the daughter of Belinus, king of Britain. She was a woman of surpassing beauty above all the daughters of the whole kingdom, and of such great prudence that the king was always accustomed to obey her counsel in all matters, no otherwise than that of some deity, before the princes of the Franks. The manners of the people, still excessively rude with Scythian savagery, she rendered more cultivated by laws; she taught the nobles to build cities and castles. There she introduced adornment of the forehead, gave women likewise the practice of sowing flax, committing hemp to the earth, and weaving and preparing it for clothing and various uses. She composed laws, pronounced judgment, and presented herself as a prophetess and priestess of Diana, an Aliruna.

And since, though a woman in sex, she bore in mind the character of a king and priest, it came among the Saxons from laughter into a proverb that, whenever they heard someone exulting or speaking with more than customary prudence, as if wishing to imitate the prudence of Cambra, they would say reproachfully: "Behold Cambra." This in their language is said thus: "Si Camber," as though they were saying, "Do you understand the prudence of Cambra?" Thus in time the proverb became established, because the last two words, that is, the adverb and the name, "Si Camber," made one word, which as a whole makes "Sicambrus."

After the queen had died years later, the Sicambrians, by a decree of the kings and nobles, counted her among the goddesses in her memory. They had formerly been called by their Saxon friends Novimagi, that is, Neumagi, as it were "new kinsmen"; thereafter they were called Sicambrians for almost four hundred years, until at last, from a certain king of theirs named Franco, as we shall say, they were called Franks, down to the present day, although with an admixture of peoples.

Meanwhile, however, because they were with their Trojans, they were also called Trojans; with Armenians, Armenians; with Scythians, Scythians; with Germans, Germans; with Gauls, Gauls. Frequently acquiring one name after another from others, they often changed their customs, language, idiom of speech, and name along with their settlements.

In the twelfth year of Antenor, his uncle Sunno died, leaving two sons, Marcomir and Hector. The first of these afterwards, while riding on horseback over the ice, was drowned in the Rhine; the younger governed his father's duchy in Holland under the king's authority. He left four other sons, begotten by four other concubines, who are not counted among the dukes, since the law of the Sicambrians forbade it; this law prohibited, while children by the first wife survived, the sons of subsequent women from being or being called princes. Queen Cambra enacted that law so that the kingdom would not be thrown into confusion.

Priamus, brother of King Antenor, held the duchy among the Gruni, namely in that region bounded by the sea which is now called Frisia. In it the duke, Priamus's son, founded a new town in the year 307 before the birth of Christ, which from his own name is called Groningen down to the present day. For the duke's name was Grunus, from whom the peoples also, although Franks by origin, were for a time called Gruni.

Antenor, after many deeds bravely performed, died in the thirtieth year of his reign, and in the 382nd year before the Lord's nativity. The Sicambrians, escorting him with great pomp according to pagan custom, counted him among the gods by order of the queen.

*The Third.*

Priamus, the only son of King Antenor by Queen Cambra, reigned after his father over the Sicambrians, who were later called Franks, for twenty-six years. In his time the Sicambrian people gradually began to forget their ancestral speech and to use the language of the Saxons, on whose borders they dwelt. Nevertheless, from the use of the old speech many words remained among them, which seem rather to echo a Greek origin than the property of the Teutonic or German language; anyone who has full knowledge of both languages will easily discern this. Nor was the change of the ancient speech among the people sudden: what began with time at length, through usage, passed into nature.

In the sixth year of King Priamus, Duke Marcomir died. He was the grandson of the first Sunno, of whom it was said above that, with the other dukes, he led the people into Germany.
