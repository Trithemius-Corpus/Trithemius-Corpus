But when Marcomir, king of the Franks, had been slain by Valentinian Caesar, as was said earlier, in the year of the Lord 393, the Doringi and the Saxons, together with the Germans and the Teutons, withdrew from alliance with the Franks, matters having been managed under the dukes without sufficient foresight.

On the occasion of this dissension King Chlodio powerfully entered Doringia, defeated the king and the people, and compelled them to pay annual tribute to the Franks and thereafter to obey his commands, so that those who had refused to remain free allies under the right of the ancient treaty were forced to be their servants, subjects, and tributaries. The same thing later happened to the Germans and to the Saxons, who, after breaking the treaty of alliance, thereafter found the Franks no longer, as before, friends, but kings and lords. Then King Chlodio, crossing both the Rhine and the Meuse, set an expedition in motion into Gaul; and devastating the whole region of the Tungri and powerfully taking possession of it, he subjected it to the kingdom of the Franks. And for the confirmation of the new possession, once lost but then recovered, he settled there for some time; then, advancing as far as the Tabula, he recovered everything.

After this, in his eleventh year, in the fifth indiction of the Romans, Chlodio, king of the Franks, considering that the power of the Romans had declined not a little, sent scouts into farther Gaul, so that they might diligently reconnoiter the [unclear] of the land, the strength of the people, the [unclear] of the cities, and the guard of the fortifications, and take care to report back to him as quickly as possible. At that time the Burgundians lived in the province of Lyon, and the Goths in Aquitaine; together with the Spaniards and Gauls they were occupying the land.

When the scouts returned to Chlodio, they reported that the regions were excellent, the land thoroughly fruitful, and the site also very pleasant; that the people were unarmed, fearful, and weak, and that the Roman guard in the cities was small. Hearing this, King Chlodio, whose mind was set on nothing more than extending the name, glory, and kingdom of the Franks, gathered a great army of his men and, moving beyond the river called the Tabula toward the sea into Gaul, besieged and took the city of Cambrai, killing all the Roman soldiers whom he found in it.

Moving from there through the Carbonarian forest, he hastened swiftly and directed his course to the city of Tournai, which was fortified by a Roman garrison. The Roman soldiers who were in the garrison, when they heard of the coming of the Franks, advanced outside the city against them for battle; in that battle they were all killed by the Franks to the last man, so that not even one remained from all of them to carry news of the others’ destruction to the Romans. They subjected the city of Tournai, with the whole region, to themselves.

Then, advancing farther, the Franks received the Morini, the Taravani, and whatever remained of the Gauls as far as the sea into their complete power, and they possess all of it down to the present day, which the Romans thereafter neither recovered, nor indeed will they be able to recover it forever. For that which once flourished has withered.

But Chlodio, distinguished king of the Franks, after he had magnificently enlarged the kingdoms and had powerfully subjected to the kingdom of the Franks the whole land of the Gauls which lay between the Rhine on the east, the Morinian Sea on the west, the British Sea on the north, and the Seine toward the south, that is, the Batavi, the Menapii, the Tungri, the Taravani, the Morini, the Ambiani, the people of Cambrai, the people of Tournai, the Atrebates, the Bellovaci, and whatever lies between these regions, and beyond the Seine as far as the Loire, died in the twentieth year of his reign, and in the year 446 of the Lord’s Nativity, in the fourteenth indiction.

*Forty-fifth.*

Merovech, the eldest son of the aforesaid King Chlodio, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for twelve years: a prince inferior to none of the preceding kings in power, courage, and greatness of deeds, who crushed the Roman Empire and, following in glorious imitation the footsteps of his father, enlarged the kingdom of the Franks not moderately in the few years in which he reigned. For all the territories of the Gauls which his father had joined to the kingdom of the Franks he not only kept under his subjection throughout all the days of his reign, but also, with the Romans wherever found either killed or put to flight, he wonderfully extended far and wide the boundaries of the acquired possession.
