When King Leovigild wished to lead him over to the Arian impiety and could not, he condemned him to a rather harsh exile for ten years. Thereafter, recalled by the most Christian King Reccared, he founded a monastery, where by word and example he showed his brothers the path of regular discipline. He wrote:

An Exhortation to Monks, book 1. Additions to the Chronicle, book 1.

He also composed many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished under Reccared, most Christian king of the Visigoths in Spain, in the times of the emperors Maurice and Phocas, in the year of the Lord 600.

Maximus, bishop of the city of Caesaraugusta, learned in the divine Scriptures, not moderately instructed in secular letters, ready in intellect, polished in eloquence, excellent in verse and prose, published sufficiently distinguished volumes of both kinds, which as yet have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading. But a notable volume of his is reported, a pleasing work:

On the Deeds of the Goths in Spain, book 1.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord 610.

Jonas, a monk, disciple of St. Columbanus the abbot and companion of his pilgrimage, a Scot by nation, a studious man, learned, distinguished in knowledge, venerable no less for religion than for doctrine, at the request of many wrote in composed discourse for their edification:

The Life of Saint Columbanus, book 1. The Life of Saint Attala the Monk, book 1.

The Life of Saint Eustasius the Abbot, book 1.

He flourished under the emperor Heraclius, in the year of the Lord 630.

Leontius, bishop of the Neapolitan city on the island of Cyprus, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, and also most distinguished in life and conduct, a prelate worthy of God. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote a notable volume on the acts and conduct of that most admirable Alexandrian patriarch, who because of his incredible piety and compassion toward the poor was surnamed:

John the Almsgiver, book 1. I have found nothing about the rest.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord 640.

Isidore the Younger, bishop of Seville after Leander, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, second to none in his time in secular letters, subtle in intellect, clear in understanding, composed in eloquence, excellent in verse and prose, shone out no less eminent in sanctity than in doctrine. He is called the Younger to distinguish him from the elder Isidore, bishop of Cordoba, of whom we made mention above, or also from another who preceded him as bishop of Seville. This Isidore the Younger wrote many distinguished little works. Of these I have found only the following. To Bishop Braulio:
