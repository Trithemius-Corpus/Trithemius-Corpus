Lothar, therefore, the third-born son of King Clovis, from whom the line of the kings of the Franks descending in direct succession is continued down to Childeric, the last of the stock of Pharamond, reigned after his father in the Frankish city of Soissons for fifty years. When his brothers and all their sons had died, he at last brought the kingdom, divided into four parts, back into one monarchy; each of his brothers had reigned for a time in his own place.

For Theodoric, the firstborn of King Clovis, whom he had begotten not by Queen Clotilde but, before his marriage to her, by a certain concubine, received Austrasia in the division of the kingdom of the Franks, in which is the city of Metz, together with other cities belonging to it. At that time it was called the kingdom of Austrasia, or of the Austrasians, in which he reigned twenty-three years. After him his son Theodebert reigned over the Austrasians fourteen years. When he too had died, his son Theodebald reigned over the people of Metz for nine years. When he died without children in the year of the Lord’s Nativity [unclear], in the eighth Roman indiction, his paternal uncle, the aforesaid Lothar, received the kingdom of the Austrasians, or of the people of Metz.

Clodomir, however, the third son of Clovis, but the firstborn by Queen Clotilde, reigned at Orléans in Gaul for seven years. In the year of the Lord’s Nativity [unclear], after he had slain Sigismund, king of the Burgundians, with his wife and sons, and had again engaged his brother Godomar, he was killed by him. He left three young sons, Theodebald, Gunther, and Clodoald; after their father’s death, their most pious grandmother Clotilde, living at Tours, kept them with her and reared them as a mother. But a few years later Lothar and Childebert, the kings and brothers of the said Clodomir, summoned to themselves their nephews Theodebald and Gunther and, devising wicked things out of ambition to rule, killed them with their own hands. The third, however, named Clodoald, snatched away with the help of courtiers, escaped by flight; exchanging worldly nobility for divine service, he became a cleric.

Childebert, the fourth son of Clovis king of the Franks, received the kingdom of Neustria in the division of the monarchy and reigned at Paris for forty-five years. When he at last died, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity [unclear], in the seventh Roman indiction, his brother Lothar received the kingdom of the Parisians into his own power. Thus, when his three brothers and their sons and grandsons had died or been cruelly killed, Lothar alone obtained the monarchy of the whole kingdom of the Franks, and thereafter, after all his brothers, reigned still another five years.

In the second year of King Lothar, Sigismund, king of Burgundy, who had built the monastery of the martyrs of Agaune, killed his son Sigeric, following the evil counsel of the boy’s stepmother. Moved by great repentance, he devoted himself wholly to the holy martyrs of Agaune; frequent miracles after his death testify that he was worthy of pardon, and in his honor health is given to those suffering from fever.

In the fourth year of King Lothar, Theodoric, king of Austrasia, having been asked [with the promise of] half of his kingdom, treacherously killed Bertharius and Balderic, his brothers, on the road, while they were returning toward Thuringia from the city of Metz, which they had approached for the sake of greeting him, suspecting no evil. But when the crime had been committed, and Theodoric demanded from Ermenfrid the half of the kingdom of Thuringia promised to him, Ermenfrid neither gave what he had promised nor sent a fitting reply to the petitioner, deceived by the counsel of his wife, who was the sister of Theodoric and Lothar. This matter, afterward, as we said in the first volume of the Annals, brought on the final destruction of all Thuringia and extinguished the royal name and title in it. In the same year the holy virgin Brigid, dying in Scotia, ascended to Christ.

In the seventh year of Lothar, king of the Franks, Boethius, the Roman consul, worn down by a long imprisonment, was killed by Theodoric, king of the Goths, who had occupied Italy after Odoacer had been slain. He wrote many useful works for the faith of Christ. In these times also there flourished in Italy our lawgiving father, Saint Benedict, the first founder and abbot of the monastery of Cassino. He became the chief of all western monks, to whom he handed down a rule of life preeminent in every discretion, and he showed forth to all, by his most holy works, the lucid teaching of his words as worthy of imitation. Under his Rule many thereafter served the almighty Lord in a spirit of humility, distinguished by the names of orders or congregations. Among these, first and principal of all is our own order, which, having received an honorable name from its founder, is called by the name of that Saint Benedict himself.
