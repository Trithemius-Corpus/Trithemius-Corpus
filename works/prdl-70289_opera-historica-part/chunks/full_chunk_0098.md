*Compendium, Book I.*

In the following year King Agilulf entered to lay waste the borders of the Romans, but through the action of Theodolinda, queen of the Lombards, who was the wife of Agilulf himself, as we have said, not long afterward he made a treaty of friendship with them. Yet they entered, plundered, and destroyed the monastery of our holy father Benedict, abbot, on Monte Cassino, while at the same time the monks fled to Rome.

In the tenth year of Lothar, king of the Franks, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans, Gunthram, king of the Franks, the second-born son of Lothar the Great and nephew of King Clovis, died at Orleans in the thirty-third year of his reign, not without a reputation for holiness; his kingdom passed to his cousin Childebert by the embassy of one making a testament.

In the following year Richard, king of the Visigoths, entered into a treaty with the kings of the Franks, and joined to himself in marriage the sister of the aforesaid King Childebert. In the same year, on the twenty-first day of the month of November, Saint Columbanus died, abbot, priest, and teacher of the Scots and Picts, who left to his successors many memorials of holiness, except that, in rustic simplicity, he neither learned nor taught that Easter ought to be celebrated on the Lord’s day, and never on any other day.

In the fourteenth year of Lothar, king of the Franks, Childebert, king of the Franks, died at Orleans; he was the son of King Sigebert, who was the son of Lothar, who was the son of Clovis, the first Christian king. He left two sons, Theobert and Theodoric, who divided their father’s kingdom between themselves; their grandmother Brunichild sowed manifold dissensions.

Therefore in the six hundredth year of the Lord’s nativity, in the eighth indiction of the Romans, which was the eighteenth year of Lothar’s reign, Theobert and Theodoric, the aforesaid brothers, with their grandmother Brunichild stirring up their fury, she being a supporter of Theodoric and laboring under incredible hatred of Theobert, waged a most savage war against one another, in which more than thirty thousand fighting men fell. The angel of the Lord also was seen by many who were present, holding and brandishing in his hand a drawn sword in the air above the people.

At last Theobert, having been defeated, when he could do otherwise, sought safety at least by flight. His brother Theodoric, pursuing him as far as Cologne, caused his soldiers, likewise fleeing, to wander without cease along the whole road. But Theobert entered Cologne and immediately ordered the gates of the city to be closed. Theodoric his brother, pursuing him, as has been said, surrounded Cologne all around. The citizens, hastening to the city walls, asked from Theodoric those things that belonged to peace. He answered them: “You shall never have peace from me unless you first cut off my brother’s head and throw it out to me from the wall. But if you refuse to do this, I shall not depart from here until, when the city has been violently taken, I destroy all of you with your lord, your little children, and your wives.”

When they heard this, the citizens were exceedingly terrified, and, after holding counsel among themselves, at last, lest it happen that all should perish, they cut off the head of King Theobert their lord and threw it out over the wall to Theodoric, his impious brother, as he had ordered, while his own men were then present, shamefully purchasing peace in the blood of their own king. Thus made victor in his brother’s blood, the bloodthirsty king Theodoric entered Cologne in peace, and received that city and its citizens, together with the whole kingdom of his brother’s Austrasians, in surrender. He himself also at last perished by the poison of his grandmother Brunichild, as we shall say in its place, deprived of heirs.

But King Theobert, pursued by a miserable fate, left three sons and one, as is read, exceedingly beautiful daughter: namely Sigebert, Gunthram, and Lothar. I have nowhere found the daughter’s name. But King Theodoric, having entered Cologne, sent as captives to the city of Metz the aforesaid two younger sons of his brother, Gunthram and Lothar, and their sister. Their cruel grandmother Brunichild met them, and, inflamed with excessive fury, in hatred of their slain father rushed upon them and killed with her own hand the grandsons who were still little children; she herself also died badly a little later. But Sigebert, the elder son of Theobert by birth, having been let down through the wall by the help of certain citizens and having very secretly crossed the Rhine, fled away in the dress of a scholar; and coming into eastern Francia, he lay hidden for some years, unknown, among his kinsmen Godfrid and his son Genebald III, dukes of the Franks, who ruled around the Main and Würzburg. At length, after the death of King Theodoric, through the intervention of the princes, he received by the gift of King Lothar a duchy in Upper Alemannia, which is now called Helvetia; but he was not permitted to bear either the royal name or the diadem in the manner of his parents. And he remained under the subjection of the kings of the Franks. He begot a son named Theobert after his father.

In the forty-first year of King Lothar, and in the 618th year of the Lord’s nativity, in the sixth indiction of the Romans, Theodoric, king of Austrasia, died at Metz by the poison of his grandmother Brunichild.
