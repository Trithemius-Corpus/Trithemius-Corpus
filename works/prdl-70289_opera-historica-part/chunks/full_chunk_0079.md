*Annals of Trithemius.*

In the third year of his reign, Pharamund, king of the Franks, in order to restrain by laws a people uncultivated in customs and fierce in spirit, summoned to himself certain nobles of the kingdom and began to take mature counsel concerning the establishment of new arrangements. For the condition of the times did not admit in every respect the constitutions of the ancient kings; these, moreover, through the varied unrest of the nation, had for many years now come not only into disuse, but also utterly into oblivion and contempt. For this reason it seemed altogether necessary either to alter them by correction or to abolish them by novelty.

Now in that assembly of the Franks there were many men prudent in their generation. From their number four of the more eminent were chosen, to whom the king, together with the nobles, had entrusted full power for making laws. Their names were said to be these: Salagast, Wesogast, Wyndegast, and Batogast. The first of these built a village beside the banks of the river Meuse, which he called Salagastat after his own name; Wyndegast, however, founded in Eastern Francia the village Wyndesheim, named from his own name.

Therefore these four, meeting together, carefully handled the origins of cases through three assemblies, and, striking out a judgment on each matter, issued a new volume of legal constitutions. This received the name of the Salic Law from its chief author, Salagast, who was supreme priest of Jove; afterward King Clodoveus added chapters of interpretation to it.

In the same year the Vandals, Alans, and Suevi, having divided the region of the Spains among themselves in three parts for habitation, and having been separated into three kingdoms, assail one another with mutual and civil war. The Vandals, Alans, Suevi or Suedi, and also the Goths, Lombards, and Normans are equal in origin, so far as concerns the first homeland of their nation, having arisen at the beginning of their migration from the borders of Germany and the Scanzian or Scandian islands, which are now called Gothia under four or five divisions.

In the third year of Pharamund, king of the Franks, blessed Jerome the priest, renowned in signs, after fifty-six years with his books published and completed, so cast down in the chest by asthmatic suffering that he could not rise from bed unless he grasped with his hands a rope which hung from a beam for this purpose, died at Bethlehem, in the ninety-first year of his age, on the last day of September.

In the sixth year of Pharamund, in the year of the Lord’s birth 425, in the eighth indiction of the Romans, Saint Theonestus, bishop of Philippi in Greece, driven from his church by the violence of heretics, came into Italy, and then into the territories of Germany, with Albanus the priest, Ursus the deacon, and Abraham the subdeacon. Of these Albanus suffered at Mainz for the faith of Christ.

Pharamund, king of the Franks, died in the seventh year of his reign, but in the year of the Lord’s birth 426, in the ninth indiction of the Romans.

*The Forty-Fourth.*

Clodius, son of King Pharamund, succeeding his father in the same year, reigned twenty years: a magnanimous, strong, and warlike prince, who powerfully crushed the Gauls and Romans by his valor, extended the kingdom of the Franks far and wide through Gaul, and was to be feared by all nations throughout all Europe on account of his famous power.

At once at the beginning of his reign he commanded all the Franks by edict to grow long hair, so that the Franks might at first sight be distinguished from the Gauls, whom they were either going to attack, or had already previously permitted, after their defeat, to dwell with them. For this reason thereafter King Clodius himself was called the long-haired or the hairy, since he let his hair and beard grow long. But he ordered the Gauls defeated by him to have their hair shorn, as a sign of servitude. From this it will be permissible to infer that the Franks, Germans, subjected the Gauls to servitude; the Gauls, nor any other nation, ever conquered the Germans. For it is manifest, as Hunibald testifies, that from that time when the Franks first entered Germany until the death of King Clodoveus, who first received the faith of Christ, as we shall say, for not less than nine hundred forty years, under forty-seven kings and petty kings, they always both were and remained most free, nor did they ever serve any man, even for a moment. But after Clodoveus, since all Franks have remained free down to the present day, both those who are in Gaul and those who remained in Germany, no proof is needed, since this is known to those skilled in histories without envy.
