John Climacus, which in Latin means Scholasticus, abbot of Mount Sinai, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, outstanding in talent, gentle and humble, devout and most holy in life and conduct, when he was still sixteen years old, abandoned the world with its delights, and most earnestly sought and obtained permission to become a monk in the said monastery. Then after nineteen years, when he had already learned, according to the rule of monks, to fight and conquer as a solitary, with the permission of his abbot he sought the wilderness, in which for many years he lived alone with God in all holiness. Finally, when his most holy life had become known to all, by the unanimous consent of all he was elected abbot and, though resisting, was called back to the monastery. He wrote to John, abbot of Raithu, a very celebrated work, very suitable for the instruction of monks, containing thirty sermons, which is entitled:

*On the Thirty Steps*, one book. “From the good and above the good.”

*To the Shepherd*, one sermon, one book. “In the lower book indeed.”

Several letters to monks, one book.

And certain other things.

In certain places, especially where he treats of the impassibility of the mortified spiritual man, he is criticized by modern theologians, as may be seen in certain treatises in John Gerson. He flourished around the times of Constantine, Constantius, and Constans, brother emperors, who reigned in the year of the Lord 346.

Ildephonsus, bishop of the see of Toledo after Saint Eugenius, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature either, excellent in meter and subtle in talent in prose, eloquent in speech, and above all the bishops of his time fluent and eloquent, worthy of God and most holy in life and conduct. For he was endowed with constant fear of God, devout in religion, overflowing with compunction, grave in his gait, praiseworthy in integrity, singular in pastoral patience, distinguished in talent for disputation, supreme in wisdom. When he was still a boy, touched by the divine spirit, despising the things of the world and the affections of his parents, he sought the Agaliense monastery, and for many years conducted himself fittingly in it as a monk. He also built a convent of virgins in the little estate of Dehbia, which he endowed from his parents’ resources. Then, having been made abbot of the Agaliense monastery, he trained the morals of the monks, managed the property, and preserved the life. After this, by royal violence, he was brought back to Toledo, and there, after the death of his predecessor, was appointed bishop. He wrote many distinguished little works. Of these the following are reported:

*On the Holy Trinity*, one book. *On the Knowledge of Baptism*, one book.

*On His Own Weakness*, one book. *On the Progress of the Spiritual Desert*, one book.

*On the Virginity of Saint Mary*, one book. Hymns of various kinds, one book.

Annotations on the daily office, one book. Various sermons, one book.

Annotations on sacred matters, one book. Many epigrams, one book.

Annotations on the sacraments, one book. Also *On the Mass*, one book.

And many other things. When this most blessed bishop Ildephonsus had completed to the end the book on the virginity of Mary, the most pure Mother of God, whom he was always accustomed to serve most devoutly with all the zeal he could, the most sacred Lady of the world appeared to him and affirmed [unclear]. But he, wishing to honor her more highly, established that her solemnity should be celebrated each year on the eighth day before the Nativity of the Lord; this solemnity has now prevailed, so that throughout the whole Church of the faithful it is celebrated in honor of her most pure conception, on the sixth day before the Ides of December. Hence again the blessed Mother of God appeared to him and brought him a priestly vestment, which we call an alb, and that cathedral [unclear], concerning which many wondrous and glorious things are told. He flourished.

Firmianus Lactantius, once a hearer and disciple of Arnobius the rhetor, a man abundantly learned in secular letters and nobly instructed in the divine Scriptures, so that in the art of speaking he easily obtained first place after Cicero: he first taught rhetoric gloriously at Nicomedia, then at Rome under Diocletian, after being summoned by him. There, when through lack of pupils he had fallen into poverty, at last, already an old man, he was made in Gaul the tutor of Caesar Crispus, son of Constantine.
