*The Abbot of Sponheim*

Letters to various persons, one book. Various orations. Many poems. And certain other things.

He lives to this day at Citeaux and persists in the study he has begun, under Maximilian, the most illustrious king of the Romans. In the year of the Lord 1494, indiction 12.

Conrad Celtis Protucius, German by nation, born from Franconia in Germany, a man in every respect most learned, a distinguished philosopher, an eloquent orator, an eminent astronomer and cosmographer, and a poet laureate, not ignorant also of the divine Scriptures, skilled in both languages, excellent in talent and fluent in speech, wrote certain distinguished little works, both in verse and in prose, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Concerning these he formerly read the following to me:

To Maximilian, most illustrious king of the Romans, on both rhetorics of M. Tullius Cicero, an epitome, that is, a compendium, one book. “Qui in concione quip.”
On the praises of eloquence, one book. “Morine aeternis cele.”
On the art of versifying, one book. “Accepi illustriss. prin.”
On composing letters, one book. “Epistola seu apostolus est.”
On the site of Nuremberg, one book. “Cum nuper relaxandi ani.”
A Sarmatian journey, one book, to which he gave the name “volu.”
Of poems, two books. “Urse doctorum specimen.”
Of loves, which he inscribed according to the four sides, four books. “Scribere que nostrae fat alia.”
A panegyric to the duke of Bavaria, one book. “Italicas tentare lyres.”
On the life of Saint Sebald, in verse, one book. “Regiae stirpis soboles.”
The situation of Germany, three books. “Germaniam scripturus.”
Various orations, one book.

He published many very elegant letters to various persons, in verse and prose, and diverse epigrams; in cosmography, of which he is most knowledgeable, he wrote various things, which we trust will hereafter come to light. He is still living, thirty-five years of this age, and writes various works under Maximilian, the most illustrious king of the Romans, and under the supreme pontiff Alexander VI. In the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

Petrus Bonomus of Trieste, protonotary of the imperial court, a man most learned in the Latin language, both very fluent and very practiced in composing every kind of poetry, as one from whose storehouse the tuneful Muses pour forth, and for that reason very celebrated by the fame of a great name, wrote very many works in prose and in verse of various kinds. Of these there are extant especially:

Books of epigrams.
An unwearied mass of letters.

Nor does he ever cease to write. He is still living under his king Maximilian, devoted to letters and cultivating his talent with good arts. In the year in which we write these things, 1494, indiction 12.

Sebastian Ticio, otherwise Brandt, of Strasbourg, German by nation, distinguished professor of both laws, excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in the other disciplines of secular literature, by no means unskilled in poetry, subtle in talent, outstanding in speech, counsel, and action, who by his learning and literary labor wonderfully adorns Basel, the renowned city of Germany, wrote certain little works, both in verse and in prose, of by no means contemptible reading, by which he made his name widely known. Of these there are extant:

On the titles of both laws, one book. “Cum difficile.”
A Sapphic rosary of divine Mary. “Stirpis humana.”
Enigmas in praise of the Virgin, from the Old Testament. “Que parisi.”
Proof that the virginal conception was possible. “Huc adez o.”
The wonder of Mary when the angel came. “Quid tibi vis.”
The salutation of the Angel. “Missus ab excelso.”
On the [unclear] places of the blessed Virgin. “Si tu a virgo.”
The virginal compassion. “Nare patris.”
The consolation of Christ to his mother. “Quid gemis o.”
On the blows given to Christ. “Respice que tulerim.”
On the ingratitude of those eating manna. “Sorte sub hac.”
In praise of Saint Sebastian. “Quid tuas coner.”
The life of Saint Onuphrius. “Nostris carminibus.”
In praise of Saint Valentine. “Laudibus o presul.”
The beginning of a poem: “Vica.”
