*ON THE ORIGIN OF THE FRANKS. 85.*

Basina, wife of Basinus, king of the Thuringians, hearing of the prosperous successes of Childeric, leaving her husband, came to him in Gaul, and he took her as wife—forgetful of the hospitality of king Basinus, and recompensing [it] with an evil exchange.

On the first night of the marriage now at hand, Basina admonishes Childeric to abstain from intercourse, and to watch the doors, and to report to her what he should see three times in the courtyard. The king believed the woman, and, the house having been gone round three times, returned to her, saying that he had seen, first, in the courtyard of the palace, rhinoceroses—that is, unicorns—and lions and leopards; secondly, bears and raging wolves, biting one another mutually; thirdly, small dogs, attacking [each other] with alternate bites, and tearing one another in turn.

To these [things] the prophetic woman foretold to the king that there would issue from [their] intercourse offspring of various morals, diverse with time: the first, which would be noble by nature, would imitate the rhinoceroses and lions in nobility; the second, which by the habit of bears and wolves would bend [its] mind to plunder; but the third, which, by deadly biting, would imitate the rabid dogs, and would fall away from [its] pristine nobility.

In the year after these [things], Childeric led an army into the Alemanni—who are now the Suevi—and subjected them to his kingdom, and made [them] tributary to the Franks, namely in that region which is nearest to Italy, which the Alemanni or Suevi had earlier invaded.

In the third year of his resumed reign, Odoacer, a Rugian by birth, relying on the throngs of the Turcilingi, Sciri, and Heruli, going out from the farthest borders of Saxony, with a strong band of his [men] entered Gaul, and occupied the city of Angers; against whom Childeric, arriving the following day, slew Count Paul, put Odoacer to flight with the Saxons, and subjugated the city to himself. Thence moving, Odoacer—a treaty having first been made with Childeric—led [his] army toward Italy, slew Orestes, patrician of the Romans, met in battle in Liguria, obtained Pavia and all the Roman fortifications round about, and in a short time, with none resisting, subjected all Italy to his dominion. The diadem having been placed on his head, he reigned in Italy fourteen years, with no one disturbing him.

But Childeric, king of the Franks, after many [things] bravely done in paganism, like his fathers also, died, in the year of the Lord 484, in the seventh Roman indiction.

Clovis, son of Childeric, after [his] father reigned thirty years, who was the first of the kings of the Franks afterward made a Christian, as we shall say. Clovis was outstanding in form, and renowned in greatness of mind, who straightway in the beginning of his reign put to flight Syagrius, son of Aegidius the former king, whom [his] father had left as prince of Soissons, and obtained the city; and proceeding further, [he obtained] also the city of Rheims, and several others. Clovis was admonished to assume the faith of Christ, and to renounce the superstitious and vain worship of idols. But before Clotilde was his wife, he had a son from a concubine, by name Theodoric. Moreover Gunderic, king of the Burgundians, had four sons: Gundebald, Godegisil, Chilperic the father of Clotilde, and Bodemar. Gundebald, therefore, killed his brother Chilperic with the sword, and his wife, the mother of Clotilde—a stone having been tied to [her] neck—he sank, cast headlong into the water; whose two daughters, Mucurina and Clotilde, he condemned to exile. But Clotilde, Clovis asking [for her], is sent by [her] uncle into Gaul.

In the tenth year of his reign, king Clovis moved [his] army into Thuringia, to whose aid came Clodomer, duke of the eastern Franks, with twelve thousand warriors. The cause of the war was that Basina, formerly wife of Basinus, going into Gaul to Childeric (as was said a little before), had carried off with her many treasures in gold, silver, and pearls, which the son of king Basinus, demanding [them] back, when he had not obtained [them], declared war on Clovis. But he, not slothful, forestalling the king of the Thuringians in his own kingdom, conquered [him], and made [him] tributary to the Franks.
