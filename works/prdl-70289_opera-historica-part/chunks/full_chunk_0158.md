*Johannes Trithemius.*

In the eleventh year of Clovis, Theodoric, king of the eastern Goths, sent into Italy by Zeno, emperor of the East, killed Odoacer of Saxony, the tyrant, and thereafter himself occupied the Roman Empire for thirty-one years.

Clovis, king of the Franks, killed Alaric, king of the western Goths, an Arian, ten miles from the city of Poitiers, together with many others in battle, and took the city of Poitiers, with all its land and his treasures, into his own power.

The king had war with the Alemanni. And it happened that, when both armies engaged, the Franks were violently cut down and began to fail badly. When King Clovis noticed this, raising his eyes to heaven, he prayed with tears, saying: “Lord Jesus Christ, whom my wife proclaims to be the Son of God, if you give me victory over these enemies of mine, I shall believe in you and receive baptism in your name.” And while he was saying these things in prayer, the Alemanni at once turned in flight; and when they saw that their king had been killed by the Franks, they submitted themselves to Clovis, saying: “Do not, we pray, pursue the people any further; now we are yours, and we shall serve you faithfully forever.” But he, forbidding the war to continue, accepted the Alemanni in surrender and imposed tribute upon them.

Having returned into Gaul, he summoned to himself Saint Remigius, bishop of Reims; fully instructed by him in the Christian faith, he prepared himself with great humility to receive the sacrament of baptism. When he entered for baptism, Saint Remigius said with eloquent mouth: “Gently bow your neck, Sicamber; worship what you burned, burn what you worshiped.” On that day three thousand of the Franks were baptized, and in the following days many more also of the rest of the people.

At that time there reigned in the borders of Gaul, at Cologne, Sigebert, an old and wealthy man. He had an only son named Ludovicus, who, seduced by desire of reigning, had his father killed in a certain grove; because of this crime he himself, a little afterward, by a certain Frank under the direction of Clovis [unclear] three thousand, in the following days of the rest, of the people completed. When he had been killed, King Clovis, by the consent of all at Cologne, obtained the whole kingdom of Sigebert.

Ragnachar also, who was reigning at Cambrai, and also Chararic, another king, with brothers and sons, all of whom were of the race of the Franks, he caused to be killed after they had been deceived either by war or by trickery, and subjected the whole kingdom of the Franks to himself.

He held under suspicion all who reigned throughout Gaul, whether they were Franks of his own stock or foreigners, lest perhaps he should be killed by them. Therefore, after many kings had been killed, including his own kinsmen, concerning whom he feared lest they take the kingdom from him, he extended his kingdom throughout all threefold Gaul: Celtic, Aquitanian, and Belgic. Sitting one day among his courtiers he said: “Woe to me, who have remained alone like a stranger among foreigners; no one of my kin lives who could help me in necessity.” He did not say this with the intention that he was either sorry for the cruelty he had committed against his kinsmen, or grieved for those killed, but in deceit, so that if perhaps anyone of his stock were still hidden, he might be betrayed by some incautious disclosure of one of those listening.

Clovis waged many wars, which we have not written here for the sake of brevity, since they are contained more fully in Hunibald, book eighteen. He also subjected many peoples to the kingdom of the Franks, and by injury to very many greatly enlarged the kingdom. He was fifteen years of age when he began to reign; he reigned thirty years. He did many good things, and likewise evil things. His mother’s name was Basina, the legitimate wife of Basin, king of the Thuringians; he himself was born in adultery.

For Hilderic, king of the Franks, fleeing into Thuringia, was received for eight years as a guest by King Basin, his kinsman, and had secret intercourse with his wife Basina. She conceived by him and bore the aforesaid son Clovis, in the second year of his flight, which was the year 369 of the Lord’s nativity, in the seventh indiction of the Romans. But afterward, in the eighth year of his flight, he returned into Gaul; Basina followed him with the son and came into exile, in the year of the Lord 376, with the boy now seven years old. King Hilderic survived eight years and died.

Clovis succeeded his father when he was fifteen years old. He reigned [unclear] years. He died in the forty-fifth year of his age, or the thirtieth of his reign, but in the year 524 of the Lord’s nativity, in the seventh indiction of the Romans, and was buried at Paris in the church of the holy apostles Peter and Paul, which he himself had newly built with his wife Clotilde.
