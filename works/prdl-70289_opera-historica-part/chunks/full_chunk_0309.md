*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

On virgins, three books: “If, in accordance with the heavenly [unclear].”

On widows, one book: “It has happened well, brothers.”

An exhortation to virgins, one book: “You who to the great banquet.”

To a devout virgin, one book: “Hear, daughter, and see,” and one book: “Hear me now, you who.”

To a fallen virgin, one book: “Ambrose, bishop, [unclear].”

Against Symmachus, one book: “And if the increase.”

A consolatory work for Valentinian, one book: “Dalmatius [unclear] me.”

Against Auxentius, one book: “I see that you, beyond what is usual.”

On the handing over of the basilicas, two books: “You entrust me with a pledge.”

On the instruction of a virgin, one book: “This movement [unclear] for us.”

On the death of Theodosius, one book: “You have undertaken the office, sacred [unclear].”

On the instruction of priests, one book: “No ordinary [unclear].”

On circumcision, one book: “An army always with continual [unclear].”

On the synagogue, to Theodosius, one book: “To the lady sister of life and [unclear].”

The revelation of Gervasius and Protasius, two books: “Ambrose to the virgins.”

On the passion of Saint Agnes, one book: “Concerning judgments.”

On choosing a pontiff, one book: “Into the Epistles of Paul.”

On Saint Job, one book: “Although also mystical [things].”

Of hymns and prefaces, one book: “You warn well, brother.”

On the psalm “Blessed are the undefiled,” ten books: “As we are about to write into the book of the Gospel [unclear].”

Letters to various persons, ten books: “We gladly preach.”

On the Gospel of Luke, one book: “The Simonians from Simon.”

Of many sermons, one book: “On the forty-two stopping-places.”

On certain psalms, two books.

He also published some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. Finally, he is said to have made Hegesippus and certain others Latin from the Greek.

He flourished under the most merciful emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 380.

PHILASTRIUS, bishop of Brescia, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, known and familiar to blessed Ambrose of Milan, whom Saint Augustine mentions in a certain letter to Quodvultdeus, wrote for the common benefit certain distinguished little works, none of which I remember to have come into our hands. His notable work, which he wrote, is reported, with blessed Augustine bearing witness in the place cited above:

On 156 heresies, one book: “The Simonians from Simon.”

The rest I have not seen.

He flourished under the most Christian emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 380.

EVAGRIUS, monk and disciple of Saint Macarius the Egyptian, and afterwards bishop of Antioch, a man most learned from every point of view, instructed in both secular and divine writings, skilled in Greek and Latin eloquence, preserving even in the episcopate the austerity of his former life, was of such abstinence that he was held to be a miracle. He was the first to commend to writing the life of the holy fathers; from his own vein he published many little works. Of these, however, very few have come to my knowledge. The following are reported as his:

To anchorites, very useful, one book.

On the eight principal vices, one book.

Also to cenobites, one book.

An exhortatory work to a virgin, one book.

He made the Life of Antony Latin, one book: “The presbyter Evagrius.”

On the life of the holy fathers, one book.

He also wrote many other works, and translated from Greek into Latin works unknown to us. He flourished under the prince Gratian, in the year of the Lord 380.

MAXIMUS, bishop of Constantinople, an Alexandrian by nation, a distinguished philosopher and theologian, in whose praise Gregory Nazianzen published a book, enduring many injuries from the Arians and at last driven from the episcopate, wrote many things against them. He composed explanations, which among the Greeks at that time were held in great esteem. Of these the following are reported among us:

Against the Arians, one book. The rest are unknown to me.

He flourished in Greece under the prince Gratian, in the year of the Lord 380.

AMBROSIUS, a presbyter, an Alexandrian by nation, once a hearer and disciple of Didymus the Blind, from whose exposition of the divine Scriptures he learned magnificently, composed various explanations on them.

Commentaries on Job, one book.

Against Apollinaris of Laodicea, one book.
