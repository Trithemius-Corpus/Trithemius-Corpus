*Letter of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of St James, formerly of Spanheim, to the most reverend father in Christ and prince, Lord Laurentius, of the noble and very ancient family of the Bibras, bishop of the distinguished Church of Würzburg and sole duke of Eastern Francia, concerning the compendium of the first volume of the annals on the origin of the Franks.*

To the most reverend father in Christ, most excellent prince and lord, Lord Laurentius, bishop of the noble Church of Würzburg and sole duke of Eastern Francia, Johannes Trithemius, unprofitable, and abbot in name alone of the poor little monastery of St James the Apostle in the suburbs of the aforementioned city of Würzburg, offers ready service in all things, no less voluntary than owed by right.

Most reverend father, illustrious prince, and gracious lord, I have written, as your Most Reverend Lordship knows, three great volumes, the work of no small labor, on the origin, course, and particular deeds of the kings, dukes, princes, and people of the Franks, taking the beginning of my narration and of the succession of the kings from the four hundred and fortieth year before the birth of Christ, at which time, after Antenor, the great king of the people, had been killed by enemies, his son Marcomer succeeded to the kingdom and led out his people, namely our forebears, from Scythia, from the borders to the borders of Germany and Saxony, to the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is. In these three volumes I have embraced a period of one thousand nine hundred fifty-four years, in which the kings of the Franks, succeeding one another in proper order, from that same Marcomer already named down to the twentieth year of Maximilian Augustus, emperor of the Romans and Caesar, are counted as one hundred and three. There were many collateral kings, outside the direct line of genealogy, who are excluded from this number of kings. Through the whole first volume, moreover, I continue the successions of the kings of the Franks and their deeds down to King Pippin, exclusively, over one thousand one hundred ninety years, in which there were sixty-one kings succeeding one another in a direct line. Pippin was succeeded by his son Charles, surnamed the Great. After him came his son, Louis the Pious. When he died, his sons divided the kingdom of the Franks into very many parts.
