*Compendium, Book I.*

*The Franks [unclear].*
*The Eastern Franks cross the Rhine.*
*The city of Trier is destroyed by the Franks.*

The boundaries of the kingdom of the Franks at this time were greatly enlarged beyond the Meuse; they had occupied a great part of Gaul, though the Romans were unwilling. Nor did they desist from what they had begun, until at length, reckoning from this point onward, within about seventy years they subjected all Gaul to their rule.

In the times of the aforesaid king Theodomirus, Dagobert, son of Genebald, the first duke of the Eastern Franks, and successor to his father in the principate, gathered a multitude of fighters, crossed the Rhine, and, after carrying off booty, laid waste Cisrhenian Gaul with fire and sword. He plundered and burned the very ancient city of Trier, built in the times of Abraham, prince of the Jews.

But King Theodomirus was at length captured by the Romans inhabiting Gaul, together with his mother Hastila, was beheaded, and died in the tenth year of his reign, but in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 460, in the third Roman indiction.

*The Thirty-Ninth.*

*The reign of Clogio: 18 years.*
*Saint Anthony dies.*
*Julian the apostate fights with the Alemanni.*
*The Franks take the city of Cambrai.*
*The Franks hold Gaul as far as the Saone.*
*The legions of the Romans in Germany.*
*War of the Romans with the Saxons.*

Clogio, son of King Theodomirus, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for eighteen years, a spirited, strong prince, and of singular prudence. He fought many battles with the Romans and Gauls, and harshly avenged the death of both his father and his mother in the blood of very many enemies. In his first year, Saint Anthony, abbot and hermit, departed to the Lord on the seventeenth day of the month of January, in the 105th year of his age.

At the same time also the Alemanni had a war with the Romans, not far from the Rhine, in the territories of the people of Strasbourg. In that battle many fell on both sides; yet more of the Alemanni were killed than of the Romans, whose army’s commander was Julian Caesar, despiser of God and apostate.

Clogio, king of the Franks, wishing to avenge his father’s death, gathered a huge army, entered Gaul in force, and stormed the city of Cambrai, where he killed without pity many Romans whom he found there.

Advancing from there, he subjected Gaul to his kingdom as far as the river Saone. Against him the Romans, fighting more with treacheries than with strength, could do nothing.

In the thirteenth year of Clogio, king of the Franks, while the leaders with their armies were laying waste the Gallic provinces, Valentinian, Caesar of the Romans, having secretly and swiftly led legions through Sarmatia into the territories of the Germans, waged war with the Saxons in the territories of the Franks, this side of the Oder, not far from the sea. In it many on both sides fell, killed by the sword. Yet victory at last passed to the Romans, and the defeated Saxons took flight.

But King Clogio died in the eighteenth year of his reign, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 378, in the sixth Roman indiction, leaving three sons by the queen: Marcomirus, who succeeded his father in the kingdom; Dagobert, who after the death of the brother just named held the kingdom under the name of duke, as we say below in its own place; and Hector, surnamed Degenbart, from whom Pippin and Charles, afterward emperor, the Great, derived their lineage, as we have said in the second volume.

*The Fortieth.*

Marcomirus, Clogio’s elder son, succeeding his father in the aforesaid year in the kingdom of the Franks, reigned for fifteen years, a magnanimous, strong, and bold prince, who fought many battles with the Romans.

Indeed, in the first year of his reign the Romans, aided by the forces of the Gauls, stirred up war against him. He met them with the Franks and Saxons in a fearless encounter, fought bravely not far from the city of Cologne, struck down many thousands of the enemy, and divided the spoils among his helpers and friends. This war lasted four years.

But the Franks, spirited with their inborn ferocity, met the Romans for battle, just as a lion is accustomed to tear apart a little dog with extended claws, as men to be handed over at once to death and without pity. At length, after four years had passed, the Romans, considering that they could not tame the Franks and subject them to themselves, recalled by [edict?] the remnants of the legions that had survived, and sent them to other places. Yet the Franks, not ceasing on that account from harassing the Gauls, continued what they had begun.

In the ninth year of Marcomirus, king of the Franks, in the fifteenth Roman indiction, Maximus, king of Britain, sprung from the lineage of the Caesars, received the name and diadem of the Roman Empire from the soldiers stationed in England.
