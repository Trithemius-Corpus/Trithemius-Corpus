*To the Reader.*

And this, indeed, is all that remains to us from those most splendid and most carefully composed Trithemian annals of German affairs, preserved through the efforts of Christian Wechel, a printer most deserving of letters, from whose press it was published at Paris sixty years ago. Since, however, we hope, and certainly desire, that those works survive somewhere complete, or at least a good part of them, though hidden, we earnestly ask their possessors or custodians, and adjure them in the public name, that they would be willing to communicate them to the commonwealth and to posterity with the same generosity with which they see us act; meanwhile we offer our own labor, as far as it lies in us, for publishing them correctly and elegantly.

*A Compendium of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot, on the Origin of the People of the Franks, from the Last Twelve Books of Hunibald, of which Wasthaldus wrote the first six, from the entrance of the Sicambri into the regions of the Rhine in Germany. In which there is also an enumeration of the Bishops of Würzburg.*

In the year before the birth of the Lord 439, in the times of Cyrus, king of the Persians, and also of Ezra and Nehemiah, leaders of the Hebrews, in the 751st year after the destruction of the Trojans, the people of the Goths, having gone out from the island of Scandia, which is now called northern Gothia, attacked in war the Sicambri, who were remnants of the Trojans and were settled near the mouths of the Danube; they killed their king Anthenor, together with many Sicambri, and withdrew after taking spoils. Anthenor left two sons, Marcomirus and Suimon. Now it was the custom among the Sicambri that, from all the sons of the king at the time, if he had several, they should choose only one, whom they believed would be more useful and profitable for the kingdom. But if he had no son, they chose from his kinsmen one who was more suitable.

Marcomirus, therefore, the elder son of Anthenor, succeeded his father in the year before the birth of the Lord 411, and reigned over the Sicambri for twenty-eight years.

In the fourth year of his reign, when the Sicambri were being excessively harassed by the incursion of the Goths and of other neighboring peoples, they resolved to change their settlements. Accordingly, an embassy was sent to the Saxons in Germany, who themselves had come from the Trojans, and they begged them as brothers to permit them to dwell in some corner of their land, without harm to their people. The Saxons consented, promising that they would give them the best settlements, as they did. And in the seventh year of King Marcomirus the Sicambri came into Germany, in the month of April: 175,658 men most ready for war, bringing with them their wives, children, and whatever substance they had.

In the twentieth year of his reign, Marcomirus crossed the Rhine and made war with the Cisalpine Gauls; he killed many, occupied the land, and entrusted it to his brother to be guarded, as to a duke, or under a petty king. From that time, for 910 years, the Sicambri, who were afterward called Franks, never ceased from the harassment of Gaul, until at last they obtained it all with much blood of their own. And finally they subjected to their rule not only Gaul, but Alamannia, Swabia, Bavaria, Alsace, the Helvetians, Thuringia, Saxony, Burgundy, and that land which, situated between the Thuringians and the Swabians, is today called ancient or eastern Francia, and alone retains the old name of the Franks; Austria, the whole Rhine from its source to the sea, the Menapii, Batavians, Mosellani, Treveri, Metz, indeed all Germany together with the whole threefold Gaul and Spain.

It is certain that the Sicambri received their first settlements where the people of Guelders, Westphalians, Münster, and Cleves now are. Then, after crossing the Rhine, they subjected to themselves the land of the Tungri, Liège, Maastricht, Holland, and the rest. For a long time the name of Sicambri remained among them.
