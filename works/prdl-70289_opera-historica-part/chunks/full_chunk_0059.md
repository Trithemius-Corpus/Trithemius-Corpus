*Compendium, Book I.*

*The thirty-fifth.*

*Chlogio reigned for only two years.*

Chlogio, son of King Dagobert, succeeding his father, reigned for only two years. For in the second year of his reign, the Romans and the Gauls fiercely invaded the kingdom of the Franks in the Cismosanian regions and began to lay everything waste with fire and sword. But King Chlogio, having gathered what forces he could, went to meet the enemy with the help of the Germans; and, when he fought too incautiously, he was killed. Yet the Franks did not on that account shrink from the battle, but fought bravely. For Clodomir, brother of the slain king, drew his body back from the battle-line and clothed himself in his arms, so that all might think that he himself was the king, lest either his friends, once the king’s death was known, should lose heart, or the enemies, exulting, should conceive certain hope of victory. And so Duke Clodomir, clothed in the king’s arms and returned to the battle-line, since he was thought by everyone to be the king himself, inspired steadfastness in his own men and fear in the enemy. By fighting very bravely he restored the battle, turned the Gauls together with the Romans at last to flight, obtained the victory, gathered the spoils of the enemy, and freed the kingdom from destruction.

Accordingly Chlogio, king of the Franks, fell in the second year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 309, in the seventh indiction of the Romans. He left two sons, of whom the elder by birth, Helenus, was then in his twentieth year, while the younger, Richimer, was in his eighteenth. But by law the Franks were forbidden to advance anyone as king before he had completed the twenty-fourth year of his age. Hence it came about that neither of the sons of Chlogio came to the kingdom, but their uncle Clodomir was chosen as king.

*The thirty-sixth.*

Clodomir, brother of the slain King Chlogio, chosen as king by the common decree of the Franks, thereafter reigned eighteen years, a distinguished, prudent, and strong prince, who kept his people in peace and enlarged the kingdom of the Franks.

For in these times, when the Suevi were being grievously afflicted by the Romans, they resolved to call the Franks, Saxons, and Thuringians to their aid; and they trusted that they would find them willing all the more easily because they knew that they had always hated the Romans. But the Franks, otherwise ancient enemies of the Romans, after holding counsel among themselves, readily gave consent to the petition of the Suevi, judging it more wholesome to fight with their enemies in lands far from their own borders than in their own territories.

Therefore Clodomir, king of the Franks, appointed his brother Genebald as commander of the army that was to go, and entrusted to him eighty thousand fighters. The other kings called by the Suevi to their aid did likewise; the soldiers assembled into one and arranged made up a sufficiently great army. Then, having joined battle with the Romans, they fought most fiercely, killing many thousands of them, while the rest sought safety in flight; and so the Romans were driven from the territories of the Suevi.

When the Germans had gained this victory and were dividing the spoils in the camp according to custom, a dangerous quarrel arose between two soldiers, of whom one was named Adelbert, while the other was a Sueve named Doringus. The cause of the disturbance was this: that the Sueve soldier had accused Doringus of having secretly taken something or other from the common booty. When he denied it, a serious sedition arose, the Thuringians defending Doringus and the Suevi the Sueve. For each had many adherents from his own people, whose minds were stirred up. But when Doringus steadfastly denied the theft, the Sueve offered to prove the truth by duel. When the princes had permitted this, single combat was arranged between them on the appointed day; and in it Doringus, at the second encounter, was killed by the Sueve. When, on account of this matter, he had been stripped of his clothing and arms, the theft too, concerning which he had been accused, was found hidden upon him. After receiving this disgrace, the Thuringians were exceedingly angry; but, concealing their anger, they afterward held secret counsel among themselves as to how they might avenge Gunther’s fall upon Adelbert. When the plan had been settled, namely that they should kill the Sueve that same night, one hundred twenty of them conspired together concerning the matter to be kept secret. Therefore on the following night they silently rushed upon the tent in which they suspected Adelbert to be, demanding that the murderer be handed over to them to be killed. Roused by these voices, the Suevi ran to arms, firmly resolving either to defend their innocent soldier from death or to die with him. When the deed was presently reported in the camp of the Thuringians, they were greatly moved and, as if turned to fury, immediately seized arms; and, having formed a column, they rushed upon the Suevi, who suspected no such thing, raising shouts on high like men raging. But the Suevi were not idle: when they could not call the Thuringians back from the tumult with fair words, they themselves put on arms and strove to resist bravely.
