And it should be noted that three Greek authors, namely Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, Sozomen, and this Socrates, each wrote histories of ecclesiastical affairs at almost one and the same time; Epiphanius Scholasticus translated them into Latin, as they stood, and from them Cassiodorus Senator, the monk, made one continuous history, which he called the Tripartite.

Vigilius, deacon and monk, a man zealous and learned in the holy Scriptures, guardian and ruler of monastic discipline, is said to have composed certain small works for the edification of monks, which the distance of places and times has prevented from coming down to our memory. The priest Gennadius says, however, that he wrote a very useful rule:

On the Conduct of Monks, one book. He says nothing of the rest.

He flourished under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year 420.

Hosius, bishop of Corduba, before Isidore, to whom Chalcidius the grammarian is recorded to have translated Plato’s Timaeus, was indeed a learned and eloquent man. In the council of Sardica, as president, he delivered many excellent opinions, as is contained in the acts of that same synod. In his old age, however, greatly terrified by the Arian emperor Constantius, and fearing that, as a rich old man, he would either suffer exile or be condemned, he soon consented to the Arian impiety. But, as he deserved, he ended his life not long afterward by a cruel death. For after his impious defection, when Saint Gregory, bishop of Elvira, had come to Corduba by imperial decree and refused to communicate with Hosius, Hosius, angered, asked Clementinus, the vicar of the prefect of Constantius, to drive Gregory into exile. The vicar said to him: “I do not dare send a bishop into exile unless you have first deposed him from the episcopate.” Hosius, unable to endure the delay, mounted the chair as judge. He summoned Gregory by royal edict; when Gregory appeared in the sight of the multitude, and Hosius, proud in arrogance, tried to pronounce the sentence of deposition, Gregory appealed to Christ with all his strength, that he should not allow so unjust a presumption on Hosius’s part to go unpunished. And behold, while the impious Hosius was sitting and wished to deliver the sentence, his mouth turned awry, twisting his neck likewise, and, falling to the ground, he suddenly expired. From this fear seized everyone; after this no one dared touch Gregory. Hosius, while still Catholic, wrote to his sister:

On the Praise of Virginity, one book. In the aforementioned council: eighteen opinions, two books.

And several letters. He flourished under Constantius, already a very old man, by whom he was also deceived and perished, around the year of the Lord 390.

Idacius, bishop of Lemice, a city of the Spains, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and renowned in speech and eloquence, who, with Bishop Ursacius, because of the death of Priscillian, whom they had accused at Trier, was separated from the communion of the Church and driven into exile, died there. He wrote, however, works not to be despised, of which the following are reported:

Against Priscillian, one book. Chronicles from the time of Theodosius, one book.

And certain other works. He flourished under the venerable emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 400.

Leporius, priest and monk, zealous in the divine writings, but presuming too much on the purity of his life, taught dogmatically that he lived religiously by his own judgment and effort, not by the grace of God, defending the Pelagian error. But, admonished by the Gallic doctors and corrected in Africa by Saint Augustine, he yielded to the truth and later sharply attacked the heresy which he had formerly preached. Giving thanks, therefore, for his correction, he wrote:

A Corrective against Heresy, two books. And certain other works.

He flourished under the younger emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 430.

Sedulius, priest, a Scot by nation, a disciple from early youth of Hildebert, archbishop of the Scots, a man practiced in the divine writings, most learned in secular letters, excelling in verse and prose, left Scotland out of love of learning and came into France; then he travelled through Italy and Asia, and finally, leaving the borders of Achaia, shone forth in the city of Rome, renowned for his admirable learning. He wrote several small works in metre and in prose; of these I have found only the following. To Abbot Macedonius, an excellent work following the sequence of the whole Gospel, which he entitled:

Paschal Song, in metre, three books. “Paschales quicunque da.”

On all the Epistles of Paul, in prose. “Antequam Apostolica verba.”

On the Miracles of Christ, one book. “A solis ortus cardine.”

To the emperor Theodosius, two books. “Romulidum ductor cla.”

On the Greater Volume of Priscian, one book. On the Second Edition of Donatus, one book.

An Exhortation to the Faithful. “Cantemus socii domino.”
