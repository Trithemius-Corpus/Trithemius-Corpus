*The Abbot of Sponheim, book I.*

“Ventosa,” and certain other things. He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200. Book 9.

Stephen the Englishman, once the ornament and rector of the school of Paris, studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and second to none in his time in Aristotelian philosophy, taught theology gloriously for many years, and was the first to begin expounding Sacred Scripture inwardly and morally. And from this the custom grew up among us that masters of theology should explain the divine books more clearly to their pupils in their lectures in the schools. This Stephen, as I have learned, wrote the following little works:

On the five books of Moses, 5 books. A lecture on the hundred [unclear]. On Joshua, 1 book.

Also on the book of Judges, 1 book. Also on Kings, 4 books. [unclear]

On the twelve minor prophets, 12 books. He also published many other works, which have not yet come to my knowledge. He flourished about the times of the rule of King Henry VI, but the fifth emperor crowned by the pope, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Bagorotus, distinguished professor of both laws and a very celebrated interpreter, not ignorant also of other secular letters, whose authority among the ancient jurists was not small, on account of his excellent talent for teaching, also made his name known to posterity by his zeal in writing. Of his little works there survives a noteworthy summa, which he entitled:

Of Cavillations, 1 book. “Since his danger is [unclear].”

On the Order of Judgments, 1 book. “By the prayers and insistence.”

He is said besides to have written several other works, but they have not come to my knowledge.

Placentinus, an Italian by nation, a most celebrated cultivator and expounder of both laws, attained great glory by teaching and writing on Mount Pestilanus. He once held a place among the principal doctors among the professors of this art, on account of certain most commendable volumes which he composed. Of these, among others, there is said to survive:

A Summa of Judgments, 1 book. “When I was at Mantua.”

Also on the Code, 1 book. I have found nothing concerning the others.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Rofredus, Beneventan by homeland, a most renowned doctor in both laws, subtle in intellect, composed in scholastic fashion in speech, sufficiently fit, by long practice, for resolving doubts and questions of law, wrote little works not to be despised by students in that faculty, by which he consecrated his name and reputation to immortality. Of these, however, we have been able to find only a few, which we have set down below, a very useful volume containing:

Questions of Civil Law, 1 book. “If I were considering the talent.”

Questions of Canon Law, 1 book. “On all actions.”

A Summa of Law, 1 book.

Whatever more he published has not yet come to my knowledge at all.

Peter, archbishop of Sens, learned in the divine Scriptures by continual study and rich from reading the ancients, and also learned in secular letters, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, wrote little works of no small utility, by which he both cultivated his own talent and prepared material for the edification of others. He wrote very subtly, with sayings of the fathers added:

On all the epistles of Paul, 1 book. Also various sermons.

He also diligently expounded various books of the Bible, and is said to have written other treatises, both various and useful, which have not come to my knowledge.

Azo, an Italian by nation, took the origin of his life from the city of Bologna; formerly the hearer and disciple of Johannes Bosianus of Cremona, a very learned man; a most famous interpreter of civil law, a fountain of laws, an abundance of words, a lamp of law, who by his learning and knowledge easily surpassed all the jurists who had preceded him, published many little works pertaining to civil law, by which he acquired immortal praise and glory:

A noteworthy Summa of Law, 1 book. On the Old Digest, [unclear] books.

Also on the Code, 9 books.

In addition, he produced excellent apparatuses on the laws, and added additions to several summae, and wrote various things which are not unknown to learned jurists. He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230. And he left after him many distinguished disciples, nobly learned in the imperial laws as well as in other disciplines of humane letters, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, a doctor of great authority among the old professors of this faculty. He wrote certain excellent volumes in that discipline, of which I have seen only:

On the Order of Judgments, 1 book. “In the name of our Lord Jesus.” On [unclear].
