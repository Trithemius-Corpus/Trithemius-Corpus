When the Gauls joined battle with him, he overcame, routed, and destroyed them with such ease that he was thought to be not a man, but some divine power sent from heaven. Hence all the nations round about, excessively terrified, feared thereafter to engage the Franks, as though they were the fiercest monsters. In that encounter and devastation, more than two hundred thousand of the Gauls themselves fell by the sword of the Franks, together with children, infants, and women. For just as the Gauls had previously done when they devastated the Sicambri, sparing no parents, so they received from the same.

Then the Senate and People of Rome, moved with indignation, [unclear] an expedition of legions of soldiers against the Germans, under the command of Marcus Lollius. For they did not think it would be safe to engage the Franks, whom they themselves called Germans, before they had subdued the Saxons and the Doringi. Then in the twenty-fourth year of King Francus. Finally King Francus, weighed down by labors and old age, after many battles most victoriously fought with the Romans, Goths, Gauls, and other nations, and after the kingdom of the Franks had been increased not a little and its territories enlarged, died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, in the ninth year before the birth of the Lord. His wars, character, institutions, laws, and victories were written by Clodomirus, high priest of the Franks, in German verse, and by Hunibald in prose, in the Latin language; under their guidance we have treated them thus in the first volume of the annals of German and Frankish affairs which we wrote.

*Clogius the Eighth.*

Clogio, the eldest son of Francus the king, succeeding his father, reigned over the Franks for thirty years: a most cunning man, versatile in talent, an augur, a magician, and a most inquisitive astrologer. Concerning him the aforementioned high priest, counselor, and seer of the Franks, Clodomirus, arranged many astonishing and wonderful things in verse, of which we made mention in the first volume.

His mother, named Lothildis, daughter of the king of the Doringi, was the chief officiant, or priestess, of the temple of Pallas, devoted to magical arts, by the practice of which she did many marvelous things. For, as the authors already named wrote, by her songs and incantations she destroyed whichever enemies of her son she wished, made them unfortunate, and slew them through the ministry of demons, however far away they were; by sorcery she procured victory for the Franks and fear, terror, and flight for their enemies; rains, thunder, lightning, and horrible storms in the air were brought on by her spells whenever, wherever, and against whomever she wished. In the fourth year of his reign, the Romans again launched an expedition into Germany; the allied kings, meeting them energetically, did not cease bravely to drive back force with arms, until they compelled the enemies to retreat and flee. [unclear] the Franks, but called Germans, fought with Tiberius the Roman and most bravely for his people; finally, after many had fallen on both sides, the Romans departed without victory.

In the tenth year of the reign of the aforementioned Clogio, on the eighteenth day before the Kalends of January, the Lord, savior of the whole universe, Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, was born in Bethlehem of Judah from the ever-virgin Mary. In that year, as Hunibald bears witness, by the edict of Caesar Augustus, the world subject to Roman rule was registered by heads. This, apart from the meaning of the mystery, accomplished by divine providence, was not without literal significance, since the Franks supplied the cause. [unclear] and from the name of its leader Phirisia it is called Phirisia, as Hunibald holds, down to the present day.

Again after ten years, with the consent of the aforementioned King Clogio and all the princes, all the kings of Phirisia, like the other leaders, were to remain forever subject to the king of the Franks, and each year were to pay a tribute of two hundred sixty oxen; and, in the manner of the Franks’ other friends, they were to fight against any enemies whatsoever.

King Clogio of the Franks died after many brave deeds, in the thirtieth year of his reign, which was the twentieth year of the Lord’s nativity. Concerning his character, prophecies, oracles, and wars, Hunibald writes many things.
