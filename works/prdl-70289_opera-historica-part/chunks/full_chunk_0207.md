*Catalogue of Illustrious Men*

Humbert, a monk of Toul, of the Order of Saint Benedict, fellow-countryman of Pope Leo IX, a man most learned in every respect, whom the aforesaid pontiff Leo brought from Lotharingia, namely from the monastery of Toul, to Rome, and ordained bishop to preach the word of God to the Sicilians. Afterwards the holy Roman Church appointed him a cardinal prelate for itself, a most religious and most holy man. Among the other monuments of his talent he wrote many treatises against the errors of Berengar. Sent by Pope Leo to Constantinople for the extirpation of heresies, by his wonderful learning and eloquence he so far refuted and defeated in disputation Nicetas Pectoratus, a monk and heresiarch, in the presence of Constantine Monomachus the emperor and Michael the patriarch, that Nicetas at once anathematized the book which he had published against the Roman Church, and with his own hand threw it into the flames to be burned. He therefore wrote that same disputation of his in the form of a dialogue, under the names of a Roman and a Constantinopolitan speaking with one another, one book; and various letters to various persons, and certain other things which have not come to our knowledge. Lanfranc mentions this Humbert, a most learned man, in his book against Berengar. He flourished in the time of Pope Leo IX, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Bernold, priest of the church of Constance in Swabia, a devout man, studious and learned, ready in talent and distinguished in eloquence, is said to have put forth certain little works of reading not to be despised, by which he commended his name and memory to posterity; but I do not recall that any of them has come into our hands. He wrote and corrected the Roman Order for the use of the church. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Lambert, monk of the monastery of Saint Laurence at Liege, of the Order of the divine father Benedict, formerly hearer and disciple of Adelmann the Grammarian, bishop of Brescia, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and remarkably instructed in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, and most celebrated poet, distinguished in talent and eloquent in speech, whom the aforesaid excellent pontiff Adelmann commends in notable verses, wrote many splendid volumes both in verse and in prose, by which he commended his name to posterity. But of these I have been worthy to see only a few: the Life of Saint Heribert, archbishop of Cologne, one book; On the Miracles of the Same, one book; also Hymns in varied kinds of verse, one book; Epigrams, one book; letters to various persons, one book. He also composed an office for Saint Heribert and for many other saints, and arranged it with sweet melody. The rest that he wrote have by no means come into our hands. He flourished in the times of Emperor Henry III and Hermann, archbishop of Cologne, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Lambert of Liege, monk of the monastery of Eucharius and Matthias the Apostle outside the walls of Trier, of the Order of the divine father Benedict, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher, rhetorician, musician, and very distinguished poet, subtle in talent and elegant in speech. He wrote certain splendid little works both in verse and in prose; of these I have found only the following: On the Miracles of Saint Matthias the Apostle, a great work composed in verse, [unclear] books; also on Saint Agricius, one book; to Lambert his abbot he published the Life of Saint Matthias the Apostle, one book; also various sermons in honor of the saints, one book; very many letters to various persons; very elegant poems of different kinds, many and varied; and several other things unknown to us. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080.

Marianus Scotus, monk of Fulda, of the Order of our divine father Benedict, a man [unclear]. Here, from love of his most celebrated homeland, leaving his native soil, he came as a pilgrim into Germany, and first indeed became a monk in the monastery of Saint Martin at Cologne, and [unclear] for two [unclear]; summoned to Mainz by the archbishop because of his holiness and learning, he lived enclosed at Saint Martin for seventeen years and persevered for others until death. He was, moreover, a very great computist and a most diligent assessor of past time, as the very distinguished monuments which he published prove. Of these the following are extant: a notable volume of Chronicles from the beginning of the world down to his own times, [unclear] books; On the Harmony of the Evangelists, one book; also On Computation, one book; various sermons; and several letters to various persons. He died under Henry, the third emperor but the fourth king, in the year of the Lord 1086, in the ninth indiction, in the fifty-eighth year of his age, and was buried in the church of Saint Martin at Mainz, not without a great reputation for sanctity.
