Among his many children he had one son, by name Theocalus, a man most outstanding in all the wisdom of the Greeks, the Scythians, and the Germans, whom the Sicambri [made their] great pontiff.

In the eighth year, therefore, of Priam, king of the Franks—Cambra his mother and queen, and Theocalus the pontiff, commanding [it]—the Sicambri built two cities between the mouths of the Rhine, of which the one they called Neopagus, the other Neomagus, in memory of their acquired name. In these two cities, and especially in Neopagus, was the residence of the king of the Franks for a long time.

They also established in the same city of Neomagus a great and most strong temple to Jupiter, in which Theocalus the pontiff, dwelling with the priests, instructed the sons of princes and nobles in morals and knowledge, spoke prophecies to the people, and wrote in songs the brave deeds of the kings—which the youth, compelled to learn by heart, were wont to sing in the temple on feast days. Yet at certain seasons of the year the priests dwelt not in the cities but in the wilderness, whose food too was sparing, and [whose] exercise of talent [was] the continual learning of the course of the stars, and songs, and the histories of the ancients.

But king Priam, after many incursions had with the Gauls, after battles and victories, at last dies, in the twenty-sixth year of his reign, 356 years before the nativity of Christ from the Virgin Mary, leaving several sons and daughters from four wives, whom he had had at once and together.

HELENUS, the elder-born son of king Priam, succeeding [his] father, reigned among the Sicambri nineteen years, a man severe against [his] enemies—whose infants, as often as he could capture [them], he immolated to Pallas and other demons. But to his own people he was not cruel, but mild, and very humane, grievous or troublesome to no one, and therefore pleasing to all.

In the first year of his reign he had a war with the Transmosan Gauls and the Tungri, in which he laid low more than sixteen thousand of the enemy, and turned the rest to flight. He slew also, with his own hand, the first-born of the king of the Morini, by name Guedo, and subjected to his dominion a great part of the land beyond the Meuse, which he commended to his dukes to be guarded. Frequently thereafter he fought with the Gauls with varying fortune, now victor, now vanquished; of whose battles and deeds we have written more in the first volume of the annals.

At last, after many [things] bravely done with the Gauls and other enemies round about, Helenus, king of the Sicambri, dies, in the nineteenth year of his reign, 337 years before the nativity of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

DIOCLES, the elder son of king Helenus, after [his] father reigned twenty-nine years, a man tall of stature in body, harsh in morals, and, by excessive gravity, severe, bold, brave, and most warlike, who waged many battles.

In the twelfth year of his reign, a multitude of the Gothic people went out from the Scanzian islands, which are now called Gothic, and, poured into the borders of the Saxons, began to exercise a most grievous devastation, consuming with iron and fire whatever it could find. Against this most savage people, which was wont to spare no one, five kings advanced to war—namely, the king of the Thuringians, the king of the Teutons, the king of the Rugii, and Diocles himself of the Sicambri, with princes, dukes, and many warriors, whose army was numbered at two hundred and sixty thousand men.

The war is waged; a fierce battle is joined with the enemy, in which there fell of the Gothic army more than one hundred thousand warriors. But on the part of the five kings, twenty-five thousand fell, among whom Heriman also, king of the Thuringians, perished. But the Saxons, having gained the victory, divided the spoils of the enemy among [their] allies with an equal balance, and, the enemy having been put to flight afar, the kings returned to their own [lands].
