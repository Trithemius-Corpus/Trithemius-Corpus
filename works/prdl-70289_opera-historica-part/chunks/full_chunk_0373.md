*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

On the Song of Songs, 4 books. “Whoever [unclear] of his own matters.”
On contempt of the world, 2 books. “The world by an undeserved name.”
On St. John the Baptist, 1 book. “The evangelical historian relates.”
Of his revelations, 1 book. “In the year of the Lord’s incarnation.”
He wrote certain sermons on the saints whose bodies or patronages were held in the same monastery of Limburg. He flourished under Emperor Henry V, not without a reputation for holiness, in the year of the Lord 1124.

Rudolph, abbot of St. Trond in the diocese of Liège, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a German by nation, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures through constant practice, and not meanly learned in secular letters, strong in verse and prose, and an excellent musician, no less distinguished in work than in knowledge, wrote in both styles some little works not to be despised, of which there are reported: 7 books. Also a chronicle.
Against the simoniacs, 1 book.
Many poems.
He also composed many chants in praise of the saints, in sweet and regular melody. He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

Franco, the second abbot of the monastery of Affligem, of the order of St. Benedict, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in secular doctrine, strong in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote both in meter and in prose certain commendable little works, by which he transmitted his name with glory to posterity. While he was still a simple monk in the aforesaid monastery, at the command of Fulgentius his abbot he began an outstanding work, 1 book, which, after he had been made abbot, he later completed, adorned with learning and eloquence.
On the grace of God, 7 books.
On the state of future glory, in verse, 1 book.
Letters to various persons, 1 book.
Sermons on the Blessed Virgin, 1 book.
And certain other things. He was elected abbot of the aforesaid monastery of Affligem under Emperor Henry V, in the year of the Lord [unclear], indiction 13.

Gilbert, bishop of Poitiers, formerly a scholastic of Paris, surnamed Porretanus, a Frenchman by nation, a man much practiced in the divine Scriptures and magnificently learned in secular letters, subtle in talent and confused in eloquence by novelty of terms, was accused in certain matters of heresy by blessed Bernard, and, convicted in the council of Reims celebrated by Eugenius, is said to have renounced his error. There are those who excuse him, saying that the authorities introduced in his expositions were understood by some persons in a less sound way. But we, thinking with the Roman Church, confess that what has once been concluded by it may be infringed by no one. Gilbert wrote many little works, of which the following are reported:
On the whole Psalter, 7 books.
On all the epistles of Paul, 14 books.
On the Gospel of John, 7 books.
Books of Questions, etc.
On Boethius, On the Trinity, 7 books.
And certain other things.
Several sermons.
He flourished under Emperor Conrad III, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

Lucas, abbot of the mount of St. Cornelius, of the order of the divine father Benedict, learned in the holy Scriptures by continual practice and sufficiently instructed in secular letters for his state, having obtained a noble talent for expounding the divine volumes, transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity by writing various treatises. But I do not recall that any of them have come into my hands. There is reported an outstanding work of his, addressed to Milo, bishop of the church of Thérouanne, likewise a very learned man, which I have not yet seen.
On the Song of Songs, 1 book.
Several sermons and letters.
And certain other things.
He flourished in the times of Lothar, the last emperor, in the year of the Lord 1130.

Wilbert, bishop of Chartres, studious and very skilled in the divine Scriptures, and inferior to none of the doctors of his time in the disciplines of secular letters, of talent and sufficiently adorned eloquence, notable in morals and life, and a special lover and worshipper of the most blessed Mary, Mother of God, wrote an outstanding little work on this prophecy: “The scepter shall not be taken away from Judah,” 7 books.
Against the Jews, 1 book.
On the nativity of St. Mary, 1 sermon.
Approved customs.
Various sermons, 1 book.
He is also said to have written a number of other things, which have not come to my knowledge. I believe that he flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1100.
