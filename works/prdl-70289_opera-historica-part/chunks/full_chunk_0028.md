*Compendium, Book I.*

And so, intending from a fixed consideration of our mind to write an epitome of the first volume of our Annals, a compendium or breviary, we warn the reader not to censure the work before he has prudently examined it. It is the manifest mark of a rash man to judge what he does not know, and to think that no one can know what he himself perhaps does not know. I know that many have written various and divergent things about the origin of the Franks: some of them maintain that the people was native-born, while others that it was immigrant from I know not what city of the Sicambri. We believe that no one can either distinguish or reconcile their diverse opinions unless Hunibald’s compilation enlightens him. For that sound historian of the Franks flourished in human life in the times of King Clodoceus, whom Saint Remigius, prelate of the Romans, baptized, in the five-hundredth year of the Lord’s nativity; after Doracus the philosopher, Wasthald the historian, and many other very ancient writers of deeds, he wrote a distinguished work, which he divided into eighteen books. In the first six he traces the earliest origin of the people of the Franks from the Trojan destruction down to the death of King Antenor, whom the Scanzians, Goths, and Sueci or Suedi killed near the mouths of the Danube, namely in the four-hundred-fortieth year before Christ’s nativity. In the other six books he embraces the time from the death of the aforementioned King Antenor down to Faramund, king of the Franks, the forty-third of the second order. From Faramund the remaining six books are continued down to the last year of Clodoceus, the first Christian among the kings, who died in the five-hundred-fourteenth year of the Lord’s nativity. And because I have written about all these things in the first volume of our Annals, the rationale of the name Breviary requires that we here pursue brevity. Here ends the Proem.

*The breviary of the first volume of his Annals, by Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, of the Order of Saint Benedict, happily begins.*

Indeed, in the four-hundred-fortieth year before the nativity of the Lord, our Savior Jesus Christ, from Mary ever an untouched virgin, when the peoples who were afterward called Franks, but previously Scythians, and still earlier Trojans, were settled by the maritime borders of Scythia, not far from the mouths of the Danube, King Antenor ruled. Against him a most fierce people, numerous beyond number, having come forth from the Scanzian islands, which are now called Gothic, wore down his people by repeated attack, bringing arms against arms, hands against hands. Antenor, now defeated, now [unclear], was at last killed by the Goths in battle with many of his men, in the year noted above, before Christ’s nativity, as we have said, 440; his son Marcomir succeeded him in the kingdom.

*The First King.*

Marcomir, the elder son of the slain King Antenor, placed in his father’s stead, reigned twenty-eight years; and he began in almost the same year in which his father was killed. He was a magnanimous, strong, bold, warlike man, who avenged his father’s death upon the nearer Goths by repeated attacks, although he seemed far inferior to the Goths in number of fighters and in strength. Moreover, in the fourth year of his reign he held counsel with the chief men of the kingdom as to how either the Goths might be kept away from the kingdom, or, if this could not be done, provision might be made for the people in some other way, lest it perish.

At last it pleased all who judged rightly that, since they could in no way resist so immense a multitude of Goths, they should change their settlements by the will of the gods. Therefore, after the ministers of the gods had been summoned, the elders commanded that inquiry be made of the divine powers in which direction they ordered them to go. The rites suited to prophecies are prepared; sacrifices are offered by an art composed for summoning infernal powers. In the midst, before the altar of Jove, stands a frenzied priest; he twists his head about, utters songs in a murmur, gnashes tightly with his teeth, with his fingers bent back twists and shakes his arms with his hands, and, darting about, bends backward in like manner, appearing to a sane beholder like a foolish, insane man. At last, after the divine power had been summoned by song, the priest Menolpor, asked for a prophecy, gave response and began to thunder thus: “Marcomir, prince of Jove, the divinity bids itself be tranquil, and not to fear the hazards of the long journey, because he tends westward after his brothers to the mouths of the Rhine, where [unclear] Brutus, [unclear].”
