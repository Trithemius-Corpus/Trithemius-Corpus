*Annals of Trithemius.*

33.

Against him, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s nativity 393, the dukes of the Franks moved a powerful army, and, when the enemies had been gathered, they killed more than twelve thousand of the Gauls and put the rest, with Arbogast, to flight.

After this, in the year of the Lord 397, the emperor Theodosius came from the East into Gaul with a great army against Eugenius. When Eugenius learned this, he was afraid; and, coming to the Rhine, with great entreaties and promises he implored the aid of the Franks and Saxons, which nevertheless he could not obtain. And so Theodosius, coming into Gaul, joined battle with Eugenius, gained the victory, and killed him with many [unclear]. Arbogast, however, the inventor of all evils, in despair killed himself with his own sword. Theodosius, victorious, died on his return at Milan. His body was taken back to Constantinople and buried with due honor. After him his two sons succeeded in the empire: Arcadius ruled in the East, Honorius among the Romans.

In the following year, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 398, in the eleventh indiction of the Romans, died Dagobert, interrex of the Franks, son of the late most noble king Clodio, brother of King Marcomir.

*The Forty-Second.*

Genebald, the elder son of the already mentioned duke Dagobert, succeeding his father in the government of the kingdom of the Franks, was set over the people with the dukes for twenty-one years. Yet he neither wrote himself king nor was called king by others, but, content with the name of duke, usurped nothing of royal dignity for himself. He had two brothers, Marcomir and Sunno, who, together with the other dukes of the Franks, faithfully administered the commonwealth of the people and its wars by common counsel and care.

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 404, in the second indiction of the Romans, died Marcomir, duke of the Eastern Franks, who dwell at Würzburg among the Thuringians [and] Suebi, a vigorous man, but a pagan, buried, according to the superstition of his homeland, on the mountain called Frankenberg.

In the duchy of the Franks [unclear], his elder son by birth, named Pharamund, succeeded him: a prudent, bold, warlike man, who was set over his people for fifteen years, and afterward, as we shall say, was elected king of the Franks by the unanimous consent of the dukes and nobles of the people.

After these things, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 410, in the eighth indiction of the Romans, while the Franks were concealing their hatred of the Romans and Gauls, Carocus, king of the Vandals, with an innumerable multitude of people, set out from the Scansian islands, or, as they are now called, the Gothic islands, made his way through Germany and the borders of Saxony, crossed the Rhine with the consent of the Franks, and entered Gaul; after laying waste the region through [unclear], he grievously afflicted the natives of the land.

He had a mother upon whose will he depended, who said to him: “If you seek an everlasting name, hear and follow my counsel. Destroy all the buildings that other kings and princes have built; kill the men whom they spared. For you will not be able to build better or more beautiful buildings than those they long ago built, nor by piety, moderation, or mercy, by sparing those whom you have conquered, make for yourself a glorious name.” Following the evil counsel of his impious mother, Carocus crossed the Rhine, erased the city of Mainz, on this side of the Rhine, together with all its inhabitants, burned it, and utterly destroyed it. Then with similar fury he attacked Worms, and afterward Speyer, and, after killing all the citizens whom he found, overthrew them. Moving from there, he besieged, captured, and destroyed the most ancient city of Trier, on this side of the Moselle, scarcely recovering a little from the slaughter inflicted by the Franks. From Trier he moved to the city of Metz; when its wall fell by a wondrous divine command, he captured and overthrew it, after all its inhabitants had been killed. For all people of either sex and of every age whom he found on the way, Carocus, that most savage enemy of nature, killed cruelly and without mercy. Having advanced from the city of the people of Metz, the savage man entered inner Gaul. After the devastation of many cities, he finally besieged the city of Arles, where he was captured by a certain soldier named Marius, bound in chains, and led about in mockery through all the cities he had devastated. He ended his excessively impious life by a worthy death.

The Vandals, however, who had remained in Gaul with the Alans, after the death of Carocus set over themselves as king Godgifith, a man notorious for crimes.
