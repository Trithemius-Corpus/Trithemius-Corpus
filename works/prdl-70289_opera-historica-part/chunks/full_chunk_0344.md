*Abbot of Sponheim*

... converted, established rules of the orthodox faith, founded monasteries, reformed churches. He wrote to Miro, king of Galicia, on the four virtues which they call cardinal, an instructive volume which he wished to title *The Pattern of an Honorable Life*, one book, beginning: “To the most glorious and serene ...” *Moral Letters*, two books. And certain other things which I have not seen. He flourished under Theudemir, king of the Suevi, while Justinian Augustus governed the Constantinopolitan empire. In the year of the Lord 540.

Paterius, notary and secundicerius of the Roman church, a man continually exercised in the study of the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, collecting all the testimonies of divine Scripture by which the blessed Pope Gregory clarified the obscure points of his exposition, produced three books: two, namely, on the testimonies of the Old Testament, and one on the testimonies of the New Testament. He titled that volume *Testimonies*, three books, beginning: “Since the most blessed and apostolic ...” Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished at Rome at the same time when Pope Gregory presided over the universal church, and at his urging compiled the aforesaid volume, while Maurice Augustus held the empire, and after him Phocas. In the year of the Lord 600.

Columbanus, first founder and abbot of the monastery of Luxeuil, a Scot by nation, a man of outstanding holiness, merit, and learning, father of many thousands of monks and founder of several monasteries, an excellent sower of the divine word, coming from Ireland into Gaul and Germany, increased divine worship, monasteries, and servants, like a prince of the Druids of old. When he was still rather young he devoted great study to sacred letters, so that he wrote in an elegant style. *Commentaries on the Whole Psalter*, two books. And certain letters to the brothers. He died under the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 598, in the first indiction, on the second day before the Kalends of December.

John, bishop of Constantinople, a Cappadocian by nation, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, of inestimable abstinence and holiness, so intent on almsgiving and works of piety that the emperor Maurice, moved against him by zeal for avarice, ordered all poor beggars to be expelled from the city of Constantinople. He wrote in the Greek language to St Leander, bishop of Seville, of whom we spoke above: *On the Sacrament of Baptism*, one book; *Letters to Various Persons* [unclear]. And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 600.

Licinianus, bishop of Cartagena, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, after many exercises of study was killed near Constantinople by his rivals with a cup of poison under the emperor Maurice. Sudden death did not harm him, for Scripture says: “The just man, by whatever death he may be overtaken, will be in refreshment.” For then he truly begins to live when, adorned with good works, he goes through death to Christ, never to die. He wrote certain little works of reading not to be despised, of which I have not yet been worthy to see any. His little work is reported, however: *On the Sacrament of Baptism*, one book; *Letters to Abbot Eutropius* [unclear]; also *Letters to Various Persons* [unclear]. And certain other things.

Severus, bishop of the city of Malaga, once the companion and colleague of the aforesaid Bishop Licinianus, a man learned in the holy Scriptures and nobly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, keen in talent, distinguished in speech. He wrote against Vincentius, bishop of the city of Zaragoza, who had slipped from the Catholic faith into Arian depravity: *Correctorium*, one book; *On Virginity*, to his sister [unclear]; *Letters to Various Persons*, one book. He flourished in the year of the Lord 580, under the emperor Maurice, under whom he also died.

Eutropius, bishop of the church of Valencia, formerly abbot of the monastery of Servitanus of the order of St Benedict, to whom Bishop Licinianus, while still residing in the aforesaid monastery, wrote many letters, a man splendidly learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, and very well imbued with monastic disciplines. While he was still abbot, he wrote to Bishop Licinianus why, after this, chrismatic anointing is given to baptized infants: *Chrismatic Anointing*, one book; *On the Exhortation of Monks* [unclear]; and several letters to various persons. He flourished in the times of the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 600.
