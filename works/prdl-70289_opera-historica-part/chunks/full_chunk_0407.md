*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

On the Song of Songs, one book.

On the Gospel of John, one book, “In the beginning,” etc. “Of the Lamb.”

On the Lord’s Prayer, one book, “Our Father.” “Before the Lord’s day.”

A sermon in the chapter of preachers, one book, “I like a vine have borne fruit.”

Of his own positions, one book, “Blessed be God and the Father.”

Sermons on the season, one book, “The God of peace and love.”

Sermons on the saints, one book.

He flourished in his own time especially in Austria, where by teaching and preaching he was held most famous; and he wrote many other volumes.

Richard Malumbra, by native land a Cremonese, once the hearer and disciple of Jacobus de Arena, a man not moderately learned in imperial law and sufficiently instructed in secular philosophy, publicly taught law for many years in the university of Bologna, where he acquired no small praise for his learning. And although he was the most learned of all the doctors of his time, nevertheless he is read to have been condemned for heresy. He wrote many volumes in explanation of civil law.

On the Code, nine books. On the Institutes, forty-three books.

On the Digest, fifty books. Various Questions, one book.

And many other things.

He flourished under Emperor Henry VII, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Lampertinus de Ramponibus, a man most learned in both laws and nobly educated in the other arts of humanity, distinguished in intellect and speech, and notable for the practice of many virtues. In that faculty he wrote many excellent volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity with great praise. Of these are reported:

On the Code, nine books. On the Old Digest, twenty-four books.

On the New Digest, twelve books. On the Infortiatum, fourteen books.

And certain other things.

He flourished under Henry VII, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Guilhelmus de Monte Laudinio, as they report, abbot of the monastery of Poitiers, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man excellently learned in the divine scriptures, and not ignorant of secular letters, especially Aristotelian philosophy and pontifical law, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech. He published several little works of great authority, by which he made the memory of his name known to posterity. Of these the principal are reported:

On the Sacraments, one book. A Summa on Cases, one book.

Various Declarations. Moreover he published many other little works, especially in law, and arranged various glosses of the canons, concerning which I have nowhere been able to find anything.

Guido, priest of the church of Ferrara and afterward, as they report, bishop, a man practiced by long study in the divine scriptures and rich in the reading of the ancients, and nobly learned in secular letters, a distinguished philosopher and poet. He wrote, both in meter and in prose, several little works of reading not to be despised, by which he made his name known. Of these I found only one written in verse, to Pope Clement V,

on the New and Old Testament, imitating Petrus de Riga, which is entitled:

The Pearl of the Bible, one book, “A noble beginning three times.”

Of the others I have seen nothing. He flourished in the times of Henry of Luxembourg, Emperor VII, and Pope Clement V, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Petrus de Saxonia, by nation a German, of the Order of Friars Minor, learned in the holy scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, and nobly learned in canon law, distinguished in intellect, scholastic in speech, an excellent preacher of the divine word. He wrote certain excellent volumes of his intellect, which in his own time were held in esteem. Among these there survives a notable work useful to confessors:

A Summa of Cases, one book. Sermons on the season, one book.

Sermons on the saints, one book.

The rest which he is said to have composed have not come to my knowledge.

Cruslanus, by nation an Italian, by native land a Florentine, once the hearer and disciple of the physician Thaddaeus, a man indeed learned and educated in philosophy and medicine, but by no means fortunate; and for this reason, bidding farewell to the world, he entered the order of the Carthusians and usefully commended the art of the flesh to the medicine of the spirit. He had previously written some little works in his own faculty, by which he made his name known. Of these there survives a commentary.
