Johannes Gotfridi of Odernheim, pastor of the church of Saint Catherine in Oppenheim, not far from Mainz, bachelor of sacred theology, a man excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, of very celebrated reputation in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote many and various treatises, which he has not yet allowed to come to light. He also composed many elegant sermons for the cycle of the year and on various subjects. He wrote letters both to me and to others, composed with grace of eloquence. Finally, he translated many treatises of the ancient authors from Latin speech into the vernacular tongue, with no less eloquence than [unclear], if indeed charm and ornament are allowed to exist in the German language. Among these I have seen Cicero’s *On the Nature of the Gods*, which he translated at the urging of the valiant knight Frederick, chamberlain of Dalberg, no less elegantly than learnedly. He also began to translate Augustine’s *City of God* for the same Frederick, and has now completed almost twelve books. He also wrote and translated many other things, which will come to light in their own time. He is still living in good old age at Oppenheim, writing various things, under King Maximilian, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1495.

Werner Rolevinck of Laer, a Westphalian by country, sprung from the diocese of Münster, a monk of the Carthusian order, of the house of Saint Barbara in Cologne, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, subtle in intellect, devout in life and conduct. He wrote many distinguished volumes, among which the following are extant: On all the Epistles of Paul, three great volumes, which work he entitled *The Teaching of Paul*, fourteen books. *Life of Paul*, seven books. *Paradise of Conscience*, one book. On the sacrament of the altar and the value of Masses, one book. To John, bishop of Münster, *Form for God-fearing Bishops*, one book. *Form of Living for Canons and Secular Priests*, one book. On the dignity and power of priests, one book. On true nobility and its origin, one book. To Everwin, count of Bentheim, on the best manner of governing the commonwealth, one book. On the government of rustics, one book. On hospitallers and those occupied with works of mercy, one book. On fraternal correction and monastic visitation, and on those who hinder its good progress, one book. Twelve questions on those to whom it belongs to treat sacred Scripture, and on the degrees conferred in the schools, one book. Ten questions on the advancement of religious, one book. Eight questions, one book. *Fasciculus temporum*, with circles from the beginning of the world down to the times of Pope Sixtus IV, one book. On the praise of Westphalia and its conversion to the faith, one book. On the origin of the Frisians, one book. On contracts, whether they are lawful, one book. On the calendar and martyrology, one book. Twenty rules useful for prelates, one book. A long sermon on Saint Benedict, printed some time ago, which begins thus: “I in my innocence have entered.” A question whether Christ on the cross prayed for all, one book. On the excellence of Albert the Great, one book. To a certain abbot of our order who was compelled to accept the episcopate, one book. On the common brotherhood of those feasting together, one book. On the instruction of novices, one book. On the difference of passions and what a passion is, one book. On the security of the license of prelates, one book. On holy desires and the manner of conducting oneself in them, one book. On the contemplation of simple persons, one book. On devoutly paying the canonical hours, one book. Many synodal, chapter, and popular sermons, the number of which is uncertain. He also wrote almost countless letters both to me and to others. These are the things which he recently confessed to me that he had written, in the seventieth year of his age and the forty-eighth of his conversion, when I was in his cell, under Emperor Maximilian and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1495, indiction 13, on the sixth day before the Ides of May. Many other unfinished works are moreover still in his hands, to be completed in their own time.

Johannes of Keysersberg, priest and distinguished preacher of the church of Strasbourg, a man devoted to and learned in the divine writings, and excellently learned in secular letters, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, outstanding in life and conduct, an unafraid herald of truth, a lover of virtue, an enemy of vices, who has often pursued the crimes of the wicked with free voice even to the point of envy. He wrote several distinguished little works, of which, however, he has as yet allowed few to come to light. There are extant his most beautiful sermons to the people, two books.
