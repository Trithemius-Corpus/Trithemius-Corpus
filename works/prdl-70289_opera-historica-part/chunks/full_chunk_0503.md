Nor indeed should one swear it, since he is one among the few in our age who taught how to join eloquence, poetry, and the other good arts with wisdom, and that the arts without morals, but morals without letters, are of no benefit. This, indeed, his works show; in them you would not know what most to admire: the weight of the most serious and most holy thoughts; or the variety of style and poetry; or the elegance of things and words; or the skillful joinings; or the pleasing texture of little words; or the flow and harmony that repeatedly mark the verse; or, finally, six hundred other adornments, so that one might deservedly say that all graces, charms, delights, and beauties of speech have been most abundantly gathered here, although nothing either swollen or inflated is seen. Therefore, if this Burrus had written in Quintilian’s time, he would certainly not have said: “Among lyric poets Horace almost alone is worthy to be read.” For this man is most worthy to be read even by holy nuns. Indeed, since he is wholly chaste and wholly Christian, which that man is not, he sweetly breathes forth the Christian religion. Yet no less than he does this man sometimes rise full of delight, grace, varied figures, and most happily bold words, as it is easy to discern from his works listed below and issued into the light. There are certain other things printed by Apianus in the year 1507.

Iacobus Faber Stapulensis, the most celebrated philosopher of our age, the one glory of Belgium, indeed of all France. In our time he was given by a certain divine gift as a remedy for the world of letters. He rescued all parts of philosophy from the shadowy barbarism of certain sophists. And where they had already completely unlearned how to speak Latin and had gone far away from the mind of Aristotle and of the other true philosophers, he restored the majestic dignity of juridical speech and teaching, with such structure and sequence that nothing is lacking to be added, and nothing presents itself that could be removed without fault. So great is the industry, so great the art in all his works, so great the dignity in his speech, that anyone even moderately learned may, together with philosophy, most richly attain eloquence, which previously was not permitted. For he was the first among the Gauls, as Cicero among the Romans, to join philosophy, still raw and unpolished, with eloquence. For when previously he was sojourning at Paris, he endowed it with citizenship. Hence not only untrained philosophers, but even learned men think that they have gained something for speaking and for judging. For in speech he is lofty; in thoughts, weighty; in invention, choice; in composition, careful and exacting; and not only in his own works, but also in those of others. Indeed, he seems to have been born for this one purpose: to take thought for the literary arena, to restore the good arts, and to bring them back to their ancient splendor. Such is this man’s care, such his delight in study; to this all his thinking, to this his mind, to this his spirit is directed. That this might happen more freely, he not only, like Demetrius, gave over to his own people the patrimony which he had at Étaples and which was not to be despised, but he cast aside dignities, offices, benefices, and wealthy honors, thinking with Crates the Theban that such things rarely cling to virtues. Finally, to conclude everything in a short verse:

Faber has enriched our age with wisdom.

What follows are the things composed by him in the liberal arts. The first is:

A Paraphrase on Aristotle’s Logic, worked out with wonderful artistry.
