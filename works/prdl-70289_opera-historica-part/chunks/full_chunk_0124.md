Meanwhile Clodomer, secretly sending messengers to the kings of Thuringia and Saxony, called them to his aid; with his own men, once a great army of fighters had been assembled, he conceived war in his mind against the Gauls. When matters had been arranged, he honorably dismissed the messengers; and lest anything concerning the armies already sent ahead could come to their knowledge on the road, he added companions from among his own men, as if for the conduct of a safe journey as far as Gaul, with letters.

The tenor of the letters contained this: “Clodomer the Great King. We marvel at you, Gauls, that you seek to receive from us the land which our forefathers once obtained from yours by the law of war, as from their enemies, from whom they had received many wrongs, by just vicissitude. Consider that your strength is unequal to ours; cease from this rashness of yours. Since I know that you wish to come against us, we have resolved to meet you with spirits unbroken, and to defend with the sword what our ancestors acquired with the sword.” When the Gauls learned these things, they assembled a great army and, after a bridge had been built over the river Meuse, led the army across against the Sicambri, leaving a guard around the bridge lest it be occupied by their adversaries.

King Clodomer dissembled all these things as though he did not know them; and, having drawn up his soldiers, he divided the army into four parts, having in each one more than thirty-six thousand fighters. He arranged two parts into as many battle-lines. The third he placed in ambush. The fourth he sent around from the flank so that, after killing the guards, they might seize the bridge first. Therefore the Gauls came, themselves having two battle-lines; they engaged with the Sicambri not far from the river Meuse, near the fortress of Delon. But the Sicambri, like men in flight, gradually withdrew, until they had drawn the Gauls farther from the river, as far as the place of the ambush. There indeed a fierce battle was joined. For the Sicambri stood firm, fighting bravely with the Gauls. Meanwhile Clodomer’s fourth army, wheeling around, occupied the bridge. When the signal of this event had been received, those who were lying hidden burst out from the ambush and attack the Gauls, fighting fiercely. At last the Gauls took flight; since they did not know that the bridge had been occupied beforehand by the Sicambri, they hastened to retreat. And behold, suddenly they saw that they were shut in among the enemy, and almost all were miserably cut down and slaughtered, a few escaping to the woods by the speed of their horses. Hunibald wrote at sufficient length the rest of the brave deeds done by King Clodomer. King Clodomer died in the eighteenth year of his reign, in the year 224 before the birth of Christ, leaving three sons: Nicanor, Helianus, and Helenus.

Nicanor, the firstborn of King Clodomer, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-six years. In his time the Goths and Slavs again invaded the borders of the Saxons; against them, when asked by the king of Saxony, he went forth to war and returned victorious, enriched with many spoils. He waged battle with the king of the Arcadians in favor of the king of Britain, his father-in-law, whose daughter, named Constantia, he had as wife. By her he had five sons: Marcomer, Anthenor, Priam, Helenus, and Clodius. The firstborn succeeded his father in the kingdom. The second ruled the maritime regions. The third died in England. The fourth held the duchy in Germany on this side of the Rhine, between Saxony, the Rhine, and Mainz. The fifth was duke between the rivers Meuse and Rhine. King Nicanor died in the twenty-sixth year of his reign, in the year 196 before the birth of Christ.

Marcomer, the elder son of Nicanor, succeeding his father, reigned eighteen years: a prince of singular prudence and moderation, dutiful toward his subjects, severe toward enemies. For he, excellently taught by the priests of the sacrifices of Jove in all the wisdom of the ancients, in prophecies, predictions, dreams, and auguries, had no equal among the Germans. He frequently had the brave deeds of his ancestors recited before him in the temple in the palace and sung in his native songs. He waged many battles, victorious against the Romans, Gauls, Goths, and various peoples; Hunibald, the historiographer of the Franks, diligently wrote their sequence. He died in the twenty-third year of his reign, in the year 158 before the birth of Christ.

Clodius, the firstborn son of King Marcomer, succeeding his father, reigned eleven years. In his time the Gauls, with the aid of the Romans, making an incursion beyond the Meuse, greatly crushed the Sicambri. In the last year of his reign, after assembling a very great army both of his own men and of Saxons, he again fought with the Gauls, by whom, since he did not keep himself sufficiently cautiously in the column, he was killed. The Sicambri, however, notwithstanding the king’s death, pursued the war and defeated the Gauls. King Clodius was killed in the year 156 before the birth of Christ, and in the eleventh year of his reign.

Anthenor, the elder son of Clodius, reigned after his father for sixteen years. He made a truce with the Gauls for ten years; it pleased the Romans. King Anthenor was more a lover of peace than of war. He would gladly have avenged his father’s murder, but at that time he could not. This king forbade the sons of enemies to be sacrificed to the gods. He died in the year 121 before the birth of Christ, and in the sixteenth year of his reign.
