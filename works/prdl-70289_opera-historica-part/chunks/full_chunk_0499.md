Conferences, indeed, delivered both in general chapters and in observantine ones, or elsewhere, without fixed number, are partly extant and partly have been taken away, besides those which still remain unfinished in my hands; these I recall having written up to the present, in the thirty-fourth year of my age, the tenth of my assumption of the office of abbot, and the twelfth of my monastic life, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, when Alexander VI was supreme pontiff, in the year of the Lord in which I have now for the third time corrected, enlarged, and completed this catalogue, having labored on it for nearly seven years: 1493, in the twelfth indiction.

Now therefore, after many labors at last, let us put an end to our work, since, even if many men worthy of the catalogue of illustrious writers remain, it was nevertheless impossible for us to find even the names alone of all of them. But those of whom I was able to have certainty I have diligently set down, and I have knowingly omitted no one whose little works, talent, and times I could know. Nor am I unaware how many very learned men survive today, composing various things, of whom I have made no mention: not because I judged them unworthy of our catalogue, but because they either preferred to hide their little works, or they have not yet been so multiplied that they could come to my knowledge. I have done as much as I could, not as much as I wished, since, if it had been permitted, I would have brought together for myself all writers, as many as there are or ever have been. I leave to posterity this province: that they should add from their own vein where I, wearied, have stopped.

Moreover, I beseech you, enrolled fathers, who hold the heavenly kingdoms, who adorn this catalogue of ours with your names, talents, and studies: if we have written things about you that are true, just, worthy, and finally pleasing, pray for us to the tender mercy of almighty God, that he may wash away our faults, grant pardon, strengthen our holy purpose, enlighten our understanding, purify our affection, and graciously make us sharers in your joys. But if in some matters we have been able to do less than the matter itself required, grant us pardon, O most holy ones, because wherever there has been error, it is the fault of ignorance, not of will.

You also, O most distinguished men and writers, who still lead mortal earthly life, I call upon you, I adjure and beseech you: if in this catalogue we have accomplished anything less worthy of your merits, talents, or studies, ascribe it not to our will but to our inexperience, and do not deny the pardon which we ask, because, insofar as we have failed, it was the fault of those who described your little works or talents to us otherwise than the matter stands. Then we admonish and beseech the reader of this catalogue not to be readily moved against us if he finds the names, dignities, native lands, or little works of writers set down otherwise than perhaps he has read elsewhere, since no one has ever been able to reconcile the discordance of histories of a time past. Nor have we written anything that has not been confirmed in many places or by many testimonies. But, as we professed at the beginning of this work, we gladly yield to the opinion of any well-disposed corrector, an opinion which truth has made sound and charity sincere.

Lastly, rivals are indeed to be laughed at by us: those who, barking like dogs from the worst habit, condemn everything, disparage everything, tear apart everything which either they themselves have not made or have found to be contrary to their own opinions. For as soon as they have begun to read even a little, in indignation they say that it is not true, made rash judges before they have become examiners of truth.
