William of Oppenbach, a Parisian doctor, most learned in the sacred Scriptures and exceptionally learned in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, quite capable in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote On the Sentences, four books; also Various Questions, which he disputed at Paris and Cologne, one book; Sermons to the People and Various Collations to the Clergy. But he also published some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. It is established that he was a religious, but of what order he was I have not yet been able sufficiently to discover.

Gerard of Zutphen, another distinct from the first, the ornament of the Cologne school and a formed bachelor of sacred Theology, a learned and very venerable man, subtle in intellect and a very acute disputant of difficult questions. He wrote notable Questions on the Sentences, four books. He also arranged quodlibetal questions, in which he determines many matters for the instruction of students. He also published some other works, which have not come to me. He flourished around the times of the emperors Wenceslaus and Rupert of Bavaria, in the year of the Lord 1400.

John of the little town of Wittlich, of the diocese of Trier, which is about four miles distant from the city of Trier, a man judicious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not moderately learned in secular philosophy, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, who once taught sacred letters publicly in the Cologne school. He wrote On the Sentences, four books. He also published a notable lecture on the Epistles of the blessed Apostle Paul. He also publicly recited [unclear] questions to many hearers. He also published some other works, which have not come to me. He flourished under Emperor Wenceslaus, in the year of the Lord 1400.

Henry of Langenstein, called of Hesse, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and exceptionally learned in secular letters, outstanding in intellect, scholastic in speech, abundant and expansive, since, after spending many years in lecturing on Genesis, he scarcely reached the fourth chapter of the book, and completed as many volumes. For he was resolved for everything and copious in speaking and writing, so that for him it was a matter of greater difficulty to repel things worthy of remembrance that occurred to him of their own accord than to find new things to say. This man, as I discovered from a certain letter of his, I would believe to have been a canon of the church of Worms. He flourished first in the Parisian school, where he also earned the laurel of the doctorate; then, for the honor of the new planting, he betook himself to Vienna, where by teaching and writing he obtained great glory for learning. Of his little works the following are reported: On the Sentences, four books; On the Helmeted Prologue of the Bible, one book; On the Beginning of Genesis, four books; To the Bishop of Freising on Antichrist and Schism, one book; On Contemplation, one book; On the Contemplative Soul, one book; Mirror of the Sinful Soul, one book; On the Seven Canonical Hours, one book; Against the Predictor of the Thelenses [unclear], one book; On the Lord’s Prayer, one book; On the Ave Maria, one book; On the Creed of the Apostles, one book; On Contempt of the World, a notable volume, one book; Against a Certain Wycliffite Heretic, one book; To the Canons Regular, one book; On the Property of Monks, one book; On the Song of Songs, one book; Sermons for the Season and on the Saints, two books; Letters to Various Persons, one book. He also published many other works, which have not come to my knowledge. There are some who write that he was bishop of Worms, others bishop of Warmia in Silesia, and that Matthew of Krakow wrote to him seven very learned little books on predestination. The name indeed agrees, not the time; we leave the truth to be investigated by the studious. He flourished under Emperor Wenceslaus, in the year of the Lord 1400.

John Gluel, born in the imperial city of Aachen, of the order of the brothers of blessed Mary ever virgin of Mount Carmel, prior of Cologne, a man exceptionally learned in the divine writings and not ignorant of secular literature, of excellent intellect in delivering sermons to the people. Among the other works of his study he wrote a volume On the Origin and Progress of His Order, which he entitled Mirror of the Carmelites, one book; also Sermons for the Season and on the Saints throughout the cycle of the year, one book; also Sermons through Lent, one book. He also published some other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished at Cologne, where he was also buried.
