In these times the chief priest of the Franks, pontiff, elder, and prophet was named Vechtanus, born from the ancient stock of kings, eloquent in the Greek and Latin tongue; most skilled in astronomy, music, medicine, and the philosophy of the Greeks, since he had spent much time both at Rome and at Athens in the study of the writings of secular men, together with certain other fellow students of his. At length, having returned to his homeland, he taught the sons of kings and nobles, according to the ancient custom of the Franks, under an oak.

He wrote many things, in Greek poems, on the sacred rites of the gods; and he composed histories of the ancient kings, ten in number, in German, with which he exercised the talents of the young and greatly strengthened their memory. In the fifth year of King Odemar this man perished in a small river, to which, having been drowned in it, he gave his name, so that it is called in German, from Vechtanus, the Vecht down to the present day. Over his tomb King Odemar founded a new city, which he named Odemarsheim from his own name; it remains to the present in the territory of the diocese of the church of Utrecht.

*The Twenty-Sixth*

Marcomer, son of King Odemar, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-one years. He had a wife named Athildis, daughter of Marus king of Britain; by her he begot seven sons, whose names were these: Clodomer, the firstborn, who succeeded his father in the kingdom; Marcomer, who held the duchy among the Nitrians, where he founded a city of his own name, called Marcoburg; Clogio, who perished in Britain; Francus, who was duke on this side of the Rhine among the Hogii, from Confluence as far as the mouth of the river Main toward the east, between the Mainz people and the Saxon Germans, who, restoring an ancient city, named it Frankfurt. And Meroda, struck by a [unclear] in hunting, died; Nicanor, who was an island duke at the mouths of the Rhine; and Odemar, whom his father, after the death of Vechtanus, [made?] pontiff [unclear] of the Romans the tenth, whose body was buried at Odemarsheim beneath a pyramid, as he had ordained while alive.

Doracus, prophet and writer of the Franks, flourished at that time, a former hearer and disciple of Vechtanus, born from the noble lineage of kings. Afterwards, when Odemar had died, he succeeded in the highest pontificate by the election of the governors. He obtained such authority among the Franks, and was held in such esteem, that he was worshipped by all almost as some divine power, and commanded not only the common people as he wished, but also the kings of the Franks and all the nobles. Writing in verse, he continued the histories of the nation.

The Franks had peace throughout all the days in which Marcomer reigned, and under his direction they greatly increased in riches, power, and honor. Doracus writes in the annals, as Hunibald testifies, that the Romans always held the Franks suspect both in peace and in war, as future overthrowers of the empire, having long since been forewarned by the oracle of Phaedrus, and that for this reason they often conspired with all their forces for their destruction, though in vain. For afterwards under King Faramund and his successors they both obtained all Gaul by their own forces and overcame the Roman empire.

Marcomer, king of the Franks, died in the twenty-first year of his reign, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 148, in the first indiction.

*The Twenty-Seventh*

Clodomer, eldest son of the aforesaid Marcomer, king of the Franks, reigned after his father for seventeen years, a man of singular prudence, modest and most humane toward his own people. He had a wife named Hasildis, daughter of the duke of the Rugii; by her he begot three sons, Faraertus, Nicanor, and Roricus. The first of these, as we shall say, succeeded his father in the kingdom; the second was king of Frisia; the third, drowned in the water not far from Neuss, died and left his name to the river, which is called the Rur down to the present day. The place of Roricus’s drowning was between the Neocum castle [eischenanū?] and the old city which the inhabitants called Moradunum. In those times both Francia and all Germany were estranged from knowledge of the Christian faith.
