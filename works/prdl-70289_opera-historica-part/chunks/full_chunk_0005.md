He also wrote, for the edification of monks, a notable work which he entitled On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons, three books. He wrote also certain other smaller works, whose titles he did not indicate to me.

These, dearest brother Nicolaus, are the little works of Trithemius which, during the twenty-three years in which he was abbot, he composed by lamplight at Spanheim, to the honor of God, amid cares and continual occupation with household affairs, reading or writing day and night whenever he was able to steal some leisure for himself. During the years in which he presided, he brought into the library of Spanheim a very great treasure of books in every faculty, not only through the art of printing but also of manuscripts. For when I was with him for the space of [one?] year, to learn Greek and Hebrew letters from him, I counted in the library two thousand volumes which over eighty years had been properly written out with the pen on parchment and paper. I often heard from his mouth that he had spent more than two thousand florins on books; he had received these not from the substance of the monastery, which was always very meager, but, since he was known to and beloved by many princes, he spent the gifts with which he was honored by them for the most part on books. For he is a very great lover and cultivator of books, above all whom I know today living in the flesh. It is said by many, however, that his successor, who is now abbot at Spanheim, has alienated very many volumes; whether that is true I certainly do not know in the least.

In the year of the Lord 1504, when a fierce war had arisen between the kingdom and Philip, count palatine, Trithemius, from fear of the enemy, withdrew with his library and the property of the monastery to Kreuznach, a nearby town, and I likewise returned to my own affairs. But in the year 1506, in the month of October, Trithemius himself, stirred by the envy of certain men, accepted the abbey of Saint James at Wuerzburg and resigned Spanheim. There in quiet he is writing various things, whose titles, when recently asked, he sent to me as follows. To Emperor Maximilian he published a wondrous work, which he entitled Polygraphy, six books. To the same, a key to the same Polygraphy, one book. Responses to eight questions of the same emperor, one book. To the same, On the Seven Intelligences Moving the Spheres, one book. To Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg, prince elector, twenty books of various questions, orations, and propositions. To the same, On Forewarnings Permitted and Not Permitted to a Christian, two books. To the same he wrote that wondrous and laborious work of Steganography, in eight books, which that rash Bovillus, not understanding it, criticized most ignorantly and falsely. To the same prince, one book of letters. To the same, Against Malefics and All Arts and Superstitions Forbidden by the Church, five books. To the same, On the Causes and Cure of [broaduci?] and of Malefices, three books. To his brother Jakob Trithemius, The Journey of His Life, two books. To Burkhard of Hornneck, physician, On the Peculium of Nuns, one book. He wrote a history or annals concerning the origin, kings, and deeds of the people of the Franks and Germans, three large volumes, beginning the narrative with King Marcomer, who in the year 433 before the incarnation of the Lord led the people from the borders of Scythia into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine, down to the twenty-ninth year of Emperor Maximilian. To Lorenz von Bibra, bishop of Wuerzburg and duke of Eastern Francia, he wrote an epitome or breviary of those same three historical volumes, three books.
