*Johannes Trithemius*

*Clodomer reigns for 18 years. Genebald, duke of the Franks, fights against the Romans.*

Clodomer, the brother of Clogio, reigned after him for eighteen years. In his second year the Alemanni, who are also Suebi, having been severely afflicted by the Romans, called the Franks, Thuringians, and Saxons to their aid, warning and entreating them not to delay in coming swiftly to help them, considering that, if the Romans, as they had conceived in their minds, should prevail, the Franks, Saxons, and Thuringians would without doubt soon be attacked by them. When these matters had been recognized, King Clodomer appointed his brother Genebald commander of the army, and placed under him eighty thousand men. The other kings also did likewise; having been called to their aid, they joined auxiliary soldiers to the Alemanni and formed a great army, and, assembling against the Romans, fought fiercely and killed more than forty-six thousand of them; the rest sought safety in flight.

When the Germans had obtained this victory and were dividing the spoils in the camp according to their accustomed manner, there arose between two soldiers, one of whom was a Thuringian, a certain disturbance, which grew so great that a dangerous sedition followed. For each had many men of his own people adhering to him. Nevertheless, through the intervention of the authority of the dukes and princes, they ceased from tumult on that occasion. The cause of the disturbance was that the Suebian soldier, named Adelbert, had said that a Thuringian named Gunther had secretly taken away something from the booty common to all. But when the Thuringian denied the theft, the Suebian offered to prove his statement by duel. When the princes allowed this, the combat of single battle was undertaken, and the Thuringian was killed by the Alemannian in the second encounter. When his body had been stripped of its arms and clothing, the theft too of which he had been accused was found in his possession. At this shame the Thuringians were stirred in mind beyond measure. Yet, concealing their anger, one hundred and twenty men afterward held counsel apart as to how they might avenge Gunther, who had been slain, upon Adelbert the Suebian, and ordered everything to be kept most secret.

Therefore, on the following night they rushed upon the tent in which they suspected Adelbert the Suebian to be, and cried out, “Hand over to us the murderer, that he may die.” What more? They run to arms. The Suebi determined to defend their innocent soldier, or to die with him, and, rushing against those one hundred and twenty Thuringians, almost all were slaughtered. When this was announced in the camp of the Thuringians, at once driven to fury, at daybreak they rushed upon the Suebi, shouting in madness, “Ogyverrederß hoeß vwichi.” But the Suebi, not sluggish, seized their arms and defended themselves bravely. A fierce battle was joined between friends, in which more than twelve thousand Thuringians were killed by the Suebi; the rest, slipping away in flight, came to the camp of the Franks, who were distant from them by about two leagues. For the matter had become known neither to the Franks nor to the Saxons until it had been carried out, because they had pitched camp not in the same place, but far from one another.

After this, the princes and dukes of the Franks and Saxons labored with great efforts for peace and concord between the Alemanni and the Thuringians; but since both sides complained that they had been gravely injured, they could not restore full peace. They did, however, obtain truces for three years, during which the parties were to abstain from war, until in the meantime, as the disturbance of the commotion cooled, it should be allayed. From that time down to our age there remains a perpetual hidden discord between the Suebi and the Thuringians, although many do not know the cause, and they pursue one another mutually.

In the sixth year of Clodomer, king of the Franks, when the truces or agreements between the aforesaid peoples had ended, the Alemanni declared war on the Thuringians. But the Thuringians, distrusting their own resources, sent noble and distinguished messengers to King Clodomer and the nobles of the Franks, asking with great urgency that, as friends, they would deign to send forces to their aid against the Alemanni. At that time there were great movements of peoples in Britain and in all the surrounding islands. The king of the Franks, because the settlements of his people were not far distant from Britain, was in great anxiety, fearing the treacheries of the Romans, lest perhaps they should make an incursion by land and harass the borders of the Franks, as indeed they had often done before.

Therefore, after taking counsel with his men, he answered the envoys of the Thuringians in a public assembly of the magnates, saying: “Through Duke Genebald, my very dear brother, I have long understood what was the cause, friends Thuringians, of the discord between you and the Alemanni, and of those disturbances which today are at the gates. But you yourselves know very well that the Romans with the Gauls press upon us daily and observe most diligently whether by any occasion they might be able to destroy our kingdom. Therefore I do not judge it prudent for us in these perilous times to divide the people and, with our own affairs neglected, to guard those of others. If we could do this without danger to ourselves, as we have often done before, we would do it now also with friendly service.
