*ANNALS OF TRITHEMIUS*

In the following year, namely the fiftieth of his reign, and the year of the Lord’s nativity 563, in the twelfth indiction of the Romans, Lotharius himself, king of the Franks, died, leaving four sons, Heribertus, Gunthramus, Sigebertus, and Hilpericus, who again divided their father’s kingdom among themselves. The aforesaid King Lotharius built the abbey of Saint Medard, first bishop of Noyon and Tournai, at Soissons; into it he placed, with the greatest reverence and honor, the body of the same saint, translated from Tournai. But King Hildebertus, his brother, built the abbey of Saint Germanus of the Meadows, in which he deposited the stole of Saint Vincent the martyr together with his dalmatic. But the order of this abridgment now requires that we pursue the division of the kingdom.

*THE FORTY-NINTH.*

Hilpericus the younger, son of Lotharius king of the Franks, is for this reason placed by us before his brothers in this abridgment of the kings: because through him the line of kings descending in the direct line is continued down to Hildericus. He reigned for two years before his father’s death in the city of Soissons, and, succeeding his father, reigned for twenty-one years. And thus the whole time for which he reigned was twenty-four years. The name of his first wife was Andovera. By her he begot two sons, Clodoveus and Meroveus, both of whom, slain in adolescence by the deceit of their stepmother, perished, as we said in the first volume.

When Andovera had borne a child while the king was absent, and when no one was at hand to lift the boy from the sacred font for baptism, the queen mother herself, deceived by the wicked counsel of Fredegundis, fulfilled the office of godmother, and lifted from baptism the infant whom she had borne. Fredegundis, contriver of the deceit, soon reported this gladly to the king when he came, saying: “You will no longer have Andovera as your wife, O king; she has preferred to be your co-mother rather than your spouse. Now I am yours.” For this reason King Hilpericus repudiated Andovera his wife, asserting that by decree of the canons it was forbidden him to have his co-mother as wife. Thus, seduced by the crafty deceit of Queen Fredegundis, she was thrust into a monastery; and her fraudulent deceiver Fredegundis was taken by King [Heribertus?] as wife, by whom he begot the successor of his paternal kingdom, as we shall say later.

Herbertus, however, the eldest son of Lotharius, in the division of the monarchy of the kingdom of the Franks received Neustria, holding the seat of his kingdom at Paris; and he reigned for nine years, and, doing nothing worthy of memory, died in the year of the Lord 573, in the eleventh indiction of the Romans, leaving the kingdom to his brothers.

Gunthramus, the second-born son of King Lotharius, reigned at Orleans for thirty-three years, a prince excellent in his own condition, but he obtained a most wicked wife, named Austrigildis. Among the many evils that she committed, when she was sick unto death and could not be helped by the physicians, she circumvented the king by crafty persuasion, so that he confirmed by oath that he would unquestionably grant her the effect of her petition while she was still alive. “Before I die, therefore,” she said, “have the heads of my physicians, who have vainly tortured me with their medicines, cut off while I look on.” What more? Men are sent at once to bring in the physicians. When they had entered the bedchamber, the witch said: “Ah, you are not physicians, but liars, who compel me to die before my time by your medicines, though I have prepared a rich reward for you, so that, compelled to death, you may pass on before me.” Soon, that the executioner might satisfy the desire of the impious woman, by order he cut off the heads of two innocent physicians while she herself looked on and laughed as witness. King Gunthramus died in the year of the Lord 597, in the fifteenth indiction of the Romans.

Sigebertus, however, the third-born son of King Lotharius, reigned at Metz in Austrasia, and also over the Treveri, the people of [Londium?], Cologne, Mainz, and the remaining cities and provinces on this side of the Rhine, for fourteen years. He had a Spanish wife, named Bruna, whom writers have more often been accustomed to call Brunihildis, the slayer of ten kings. She was so hateful to the Franks that they said the prophecy of the Sibyl was fulfilled in her: “Bruna will come from the regions of Spain; before her sight peoples and kings will perish, but she herself will perish by the heels of horses.” By her Sigebertus begot a son named Theodobertus, who reigned after him for twenty-one years and died in the year of the Lord 599. His son, named Theodoricus, succeeded and reigned for sixteen years; he expelled Saint Columbanus from Gaul at the instigation of his Brunihildis in the year of the Lord 613, and died shortly afterwards, leaving three sons, as is read, born out of wedlock. The eldest of these, Sigebertus, was put to flight in battle by Lotharius, son of the aforesaid king, and thereafter appeared nowhere. Corbus, the second-born, was slain by the same Lotharius. But Meroveus, the third-born, whom he had in fact lifted from the font, the same Lotharius kept alive. Brunihildis, however, the slayer of ten kings, he put to death.
