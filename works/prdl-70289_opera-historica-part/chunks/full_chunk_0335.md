He is said to have flourished under Emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 480.

VICTOR, bishop of Capua, learned in the Holy Scriptures, sufficiently imbued with secular letters, an outstanding theologian and computist, keen in intellect, renowned in eloquence, is said to have written some little works on the sacred volumes, but very few of them have come to my knowledge. He refuted the error of Victorius of Aquitaine, who, at the order of Pope Hilary, writing Paschal cycles, taught that Easter was to be celebrated on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of May in the year of the Lord 455, whereas it was more correctly to be celebrated that year on the seventh day before the Kalends of May. Therefore, when this occasion arose, he wrote:

On Easter, one book. One Gospel from Four, one book.

He flourished under Zeno Augustus, in the year of the Lord 480.

CEREALIS, an African bishop, a man very skilled in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular disciplines, was asked by Maximinus, bishop of the Arians, whether he could establish the consubstantiality of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit by a few testimonies of divine Scripture, without, at least, the assertion of disputation. He replied, not with a few testimonies, as that man mockingly had requested, but with many testimonies from both Testaments, composing a distinguished work:

Against the Arians, one book. He also wrote other works:
Against the Timotheans, one book. Several Homilies to the People, one book.
And many other things.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 460.

JOHN, priest of the church of Antioch, rich in his reading of the ancient Scriptures, after he had taught grammar at Antioch for some years, began to wish to profess himself a cleric; and, made a priest by legitimate succession, he applied himself to divine readings. Having become a teacher through these, he was so powerful in delivering homilies that he had no equal in his time. He wrote a sufficiently learned volume:

Eclogues, one book. The lands of the Ethiopians.
On the Harmony of the Scriptures, one book. Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished in the year of the Lord 480, under Zeno Augustus, under whom he died.

THEODULUS, priest, an Italian by nation, a man learned in the divine writings and nobly instructed in secular letters, coming from Italy into [Caelesyria?], is recorded to have been ordained there first priest, then bishop; and by writing and composing much he transmitted his name to posterity. Of his little works I have found only a metrical work on the miracles of the Old Testament, [unclear] with the fables of the poets.

The lands of the Ethiopians.
Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

EUGENIUS, bishop of Carthage in Africa, a learned man, public confessor of the undivided Holy Trinity, suffered many injuries from the Arians under Huneric, king of the Vandals, who after his father Genseric was persecuting Christians in Africa. For with many other bishops, their tongues having been cut out, he was sent into exile. He wrote some little works full of faith and learning. Of these the following are reported:

Exposition of the Catholic Faith, one book. A Debate Held with the Arians.
Apologetic for the Faith, one book. To His Church, when he was going into exile, one letter.

He is said nonetheless to have written other things, but they have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished in the times of Augustus Zeno, in the year of the Lord 480.

FELIX, the third pope, Roman by nation, after the pontiff Saint Simplicius sat in the chair of Peter for eight years. He was a good, learned, and holy man, who, inflamed with zeal for the faith, condemned Peter and Acacius, Eutychian bishops, relegating them into exile. For this reason, holding a synod at Rome, he established many things for ecclesiastical usefulness and wrote:

Synodal Decree, one book. To Flavius Poetius, [unclear].
Several Decretal Letters, one book.

He died under Emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 493, the first indiction.

GENNADIUS, priest of the church of Marseilles, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, perfectly instructed in Greek as well as Latin eloquence, wrote many little works and translated not a few volumes of the ancient fathers from Greek into the Latin language. Concerning his own writings, however, he himself, at the end of [On Illustrious Men?], [unclear]:

On His Faith, to Pope Gelasius, one book. Against Nestorius, six books.
