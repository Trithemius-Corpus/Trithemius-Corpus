Dionysius, the thirteenth bishop of the city of Alexandria, formerly a hearer and disciple of Origen, while he was still a presbyter held the public school under Heraclas and acquired great glory for his learning. For he was a man most learned in every science, a philosopher, rhetorician, and distinguished theologian, who, agreeing with the doctrine of Cyprian and the African synod on rebaptizing heretics, wrote very many letters on that matter to various persons. Among them these are counted:

On repentance, to Fabius, one letter. On the Sabbath, one book.
To the Romans, one letter. To Hermonion [Hermammon?], one book.
To Saint Sixtus, pope, two letters. On the persecution of Decius, one book.
To Philemon and Dionysius, presbyters, two letters. Against Nepos, the Chiliast, one book.
To Dionysius after he became pope, two letters. Against Sabellius, one book.
To Novatian the heretic, one letter. To Ammon, bishop of [Berenice?], one letter.

Several letters of his to Basilides are also reported. A distinguished letter of his Against Paul of Samosata is extant, which he had written a few days before his death. He also composed many other things for ecclesiastical use, which are unknown to us. He flourished under the rulers Gallus and Volusianus, in the year of the Lord 255.

Novatian, presbyter of the Roman church, having attempted to usurp the episcopal chair against Cornelius, and being unable to do so, stirred up the first schism in the church and established the heresy of the Novatians, refusing to receive apostates who repented, and rebaptizing those duly baptized. The author of this error was Novatus, Cyprian’s presbyter. He wrote:

On Easter, one book. On Jewish foods, one book.
On the Sabbath, one book. On earnestness, one book.
On Circumcision, one book. On Attalus, one book.
On the Priest, one book. On the Holy Trinity, one book.
On Prayer, one book. Several letters to various persons.

He is also said to have written some other things besides, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the times of Emperor Philip, in the year of the Lord 260.

Malchion, presbyter of the church of Antioch, a learned and most eloquent man, since indeed he taught rhetoric in that same city for many years with the greatest distinction, published some excellent little works which in his own time seemed to contribute greatly to the defense of the Christian faith against heretics. Of these there is extant a notable work of dialogues:

Against Paul of Samosata, one book.
To Pope Dionysius from the synod, one letter.

He flourished under the rulers Claudius and Aurelian, in the year of the Lord 270.

Victorinus, bishop of Pictavium, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and trained in Greek and Latin eloquence, wrote many excellent little works, in which he splendidly exercised his talent. He was also a distinguished preacher of the divine word, and it is recorded that at last he was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ. Of his writings the following are reported:

On Genesis, one book. On Habakkuk, one book.
On Exodus, one book. On Ecclesiastes, one book.
On Leviticus, one book. On the Song of Songs, one book.
On the prophet Isaiah, one book. On the Apocalypse, one book.
