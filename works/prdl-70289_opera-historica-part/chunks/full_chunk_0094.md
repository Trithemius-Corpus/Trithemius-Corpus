*Compendium, Book I.*

The Order of Saint William, the Order of Monte Oliveto, the Order of the Slavs, the Order of Vallombrosa, the Order of Saint Paul the First Hermit, the Order of the Humiliati, with certain others, which for the sake of brevity we have passed over here. Saint Pope Gregory set forth the life of this saint.

In the tenth year of Lothar, king of the Franks, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 624, in the seventh indiction of the Romans: when Theodoric, king of Metz, demanded from Ermenfrid, king of the Thuringians, what had been promised because of the murder of his brothers, as we have said, and did not obtain it, he was stirred to anger; and, having summoned to his aid his son Theobert and his brother Lothar, kings, he launched an expedition into Thuringia, captured King Ermenfrid with his wife and children, killed him, and subjected the whole kingdom to himself. From that time the Thuringians had no king of their own.

Among the others taken captive was Radegund, daughter of the former King Berthar, whom his brother Ermenfrid, as we have said, had caused to be killed through Theodoric, king of Metz. King Lothar joined her to himself in marriage; she shone with great holiness, and afterwards, having become a nun, was resplendent with very many signs and miracles. Concerning this destruction of the Thuringians, Fortunatus the archbishop wrote more fully in verse and prose.

In the twelfth year of his reign, Lothar, king of the Franks, with his brother Hildebert, besieged Autun, a city of Burgundy, and, after King Godemar had been put to flight, powerfully subjected the whole province of the Burgundians to his dominion.

In the sixteenth year of his reign, Lothar, king of the Franks, powerfully wrested from the hand of Athalaric the king whatever his grandfather Theodoric, king of Italy, had possessed in the Gauls.

In these days Saint Nicerius flourished, from abbot of the monastery of Limoges made archbishop of Trier, a man of many virtues in Christ, whose deeds are extant. Theodoric, king of Austrasia, who ruled over the people of Metz, Trier, Liege, Cologne, Mainz, and all those on this side of the Rhine, brought many clerics from the citizens of Auvergne, whom he ordered to be enrolled in the church of Trier to serve the Lord.

In the twenty-sixth year of King Lothar, in the ninth indiction, King Hildebert rose up against this same Lothar, his brother in Christ, together with his brother’s son Theodobert; but, when their mother Clotilde acted with prayers before God that there should be no war among her sons, they were restrained by a dreadful storm, while upon Lothar not even a sign of so great a terror appeared. Compelled by manifest necessity, Hildebert withdrew from his brother in peace.

In the twenty-eighth year Lothar and Hildebert, the aforesaid brothers, having besieged in Spain the city of Zaragoza, while the citizens prayed to God and carried around the tunic of the holy martyr Vincent, accepted from them, as a gift of that martyr, his stole, and returned in peace, after the greater part of Spain had been acquired by war. At the same time Saint Maurus and Faustus, disciples of Saint Benedict, flourished in Gallican France, distinguished in conduct, learning, holiness of life, and miracles.

In the fortieth year of Lothar, king of the Franks, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 654, in the second indiction of the Romans, Queen Clotilde died, formerly the wife of the great Clodoveus, king of France; she was buried with him at Paris in the church of Saint Peter. In the same year Bucellinus and Amingus, leaders of the Franks, engaged in war with Narses, patrician of the Romans. For these two leaders, Bucellinus and Amingus, had seven years before entered Italy with King Theobert of the Franks with a strong force, and they did not cease to disturb it with varying fortunes of war. Then, sailing into Sicily, they subjected it to tribute to the Franks, and, after many spoils had been acquired, occupied it.

In the fortieth year of his reign, Lothar, king of the Franks, having gathered forces, moved into Saxony. For the Saxons, who until then had been tributary to the Franks, rose in rebellion, with the Thuringians bringing aid; they themselves bore the yoke of the Franks heavily, and with equal firmness refused all tribute. Therefore, coming into Saxony with a great army, Lothar subdued the Saxons in a severe battle.

In the forty-sixth year of his reign, Lothar, king of the Franks, pursued with an army his son Granus, long rebellious and doing many evils in the kingdom, when he was a leader in Brittany. When an engagement had taken place, he killed Duke Conober, obtained Brittany, and captured his son, whom, consumed by fire together with his wife and daughters, he reduced to ashes.
