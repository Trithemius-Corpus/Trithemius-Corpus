*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

is the title. A book without letters [unclear]: on Adam without b, on Cain without c, and so with the rest according to the sequence of the letters. But in the book whose title is *Exposition of the Virgilian Content According to the Moral Philosophers*, with marvelous industry he sought gold in the mud, so to speak, and found it, when he bent the poet’s work toward a physical interpretation. He wrote this work to a certain deacon; another, on mythologies, to Catus, a priest of Carthage; and *On the Obscure Expressions of the Ancients* to Calcidius the grammarian, already an old man, while he himself was still young and a teacher and director in a school of humane letters. He flourished in the time of Thrasamund, king of the Vandals, persecutor of the Catholic churches, under the emperor Anastasius. A.D. 500.

Ferrandus, deacon of the Church of Carthage, to whom some little works of the above-mentioned Saint Fulgentius are extant, was a man both zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and venerable no less for the holiness of his conduct than for his doctrine. He is said to have forged certain volumes of lively talent, of which only the following have come to my knowledge.

To Reginus the count.

*On the Seven Rules of Innocence*, one book. “For the toil of social life.”

To Saint Fulgentius, one epistle.

*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He flourished with Fulgentius under the emperor Anastasius. A.D. 500.

Anastasius II, pope, a Roman by nation, sat after the holy pontiff Gelasius for one year and ten months. He was indeed a learned man, but, seduced by Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, not long afterward paid the deserved penalties of his error. For when he wished to obey the necessity of nature, wretchedly struck by God, he poured out his intestines together with his excrement, as is clear in Distinction 19, “Anastasius.” And he was the second infamous pope. He wrote to the emperor Anastasius:

One epistle. “The beginning of the pontificate.”

*On the Peace of the Church*.

He is also said to have written other letters, but they have not come into our hands. He died, struck by divine judgment, when he had secretly wished to recall from exile Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, condemned and cast out by the Church, under Anastasius Augustus. A.D. 498.

Paschasius, deacon of the Roman Church, a learned and holy man, father of the poor, wholly intent on the distribution of alms, was of such virtue and holiness that Pope Gregory writes in the fourth book of the *Dialogues* that a demoniac was cured after his death by the touch of his dalmatic. Saint Germanus, bishop of Capua, saw his soul in the pains of purgatory, tormented for nothing other than that in the election of Pope Symmachus he himself had rather chosen Laurentius, and had persevered in this opinion until death, although peacefully. He wrote, as Gregory attests, a notable volume against the errors of the Greeks:

*On the Holy Spirit*, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Anastasius. A.D. 500.

Symmachus, pope, a Sardinian by nation, a learned and holy man, obtained the pontificate after Anastasius, not without discord from the party of Laurentius, and sat for fifteen years and six months. A man merciful and munificent toward the poor, he loved the clergy, enriched churches, and drove heretics from the city. In a synod held at Rome of one hundred and twenty bishops, he canonically cleared himself of the crimes charged against him, and thrust Peter, bishop of Ravenna, with his accomplices into exile. He wrote certain letters and little works not to be despised. Of these I have found:

One book. “Because of the frequent [unclear].”

*Decree of the Roman Synod*.

*The Fourth Synod Held*.

*Acts of Another Synod*, one book. “Indeed well, brotherly...”

*On the [unclear] of Clerics*.

*Letters to Various Persons*.

He died under the emperor Anastasius. A.D. 515, in the [unclear] indiction, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of August. Pope Hormisdas succeeded him.

Symmachus the patrician, Roman senator and consul, father-in-law of Boethius, a distinguished philosopher and rhetorician and a magnificent worshipper of the Christian faith, was banished into exile at Pavia together with Boethius by Theoderic, king of Italy, on suspicion of aiming at liberty; and not much later he cruelly had him slain. Since he was a learned and most eloquent man, he is said to have composed some little works, of which I was able to find only one volume, brief indeed, but elegant:

*Letters to Various Persons*, one book. “Surely you laugh because concerning...”
