Sifridus of Mainz, of the order of Friars Preachers, titular bishop of Cyrene, vicar in pontificals of the archbishop of Mainz, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, skilled in the Hebrew language, an acute disputant, and an outstanding preacher of the divine word. He wrote to Theoderic, archbishop of Mainz, *On the Usury of the Jews*, one book; *On Taxes and Redemptions*, one book; various questions; also many sermons and certain orations to the clergy. And certain other things. He died under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1473, in the sixth indiction, on the sixteenth day of the month of May. He was buried in his convent at Mainz.

Godescalcus Gresemundus of Meschede, a Westphalian by country, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and exceptionally learned in secular philosophy, sharp in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct, a profound doctor of sacred theology, who taught it gloriously for many years in the gymnasium of Erfurt. He wrote notable questions on the Sentences, four books; moreover various questions disputed in the schools, one book; many sermons and diverse conferences. And certain other things unknown to us. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1480. And almost ten years before this he died.

Stephanus Hoest of Ladenburg, priest and canon of the church of Speyer and preacher of the word of God in the castle of Heidelberg, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and exceptionally learned in secular philosophy, excellent in verse and prose, acute in intellect, composed in speech, in delivering sermons to the people no less learned than eloquent. He wrote several little works of reading not to be despised, of which, however, few have come to my knowledge. For I found a distinguished volume on the new translation of Aristotle’s Ethics, ten books; various sermons and orations to the people, clergy, and princes. And certain epigrams. Concerning the rest I have as yet found nothing. He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1471, in the fourth indiction.

Heinrich of Cologne, called de Piro, namely from his father’s house, a monk of the Carthusian order, of the house of Saint Barbara in Cologne, a man rather studious in the divine Scriptures and, before his entrance into religion, a subtle doctor of both laws and very celebrated in reputation. He wrote and lectured in that faculty many distinguished volumes, by which he commended the memory of himself to posterity. Yet few have come into our hands, namely a notable work on the Institutes, four books; *To Novices on Internal Reform*, one book; various counsels in diverse cases; quite a number of sermons on the season and on the saints. Concerning the rest nothing is known to me. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1470.

Nicolaus Donis, monk of the order of the divine father Benedict, of the monastery, as they say, of Reichenbach, a man certainly studious and learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, a distinguished philosopher and astronomer, a most vigilant discoverer and most diligent restorer of the Cosmography of Ptolemy, outstanding in intellect and clear in eloquence. He wrote volumes not to be contemned, by which he commended the memory of himself to posterity. Among these there survives a marvelous and very celebrated work, most elegantly arranged with pictures and new tables, on the Cosmography of Ptolemy, addressed to Pope Paul II, four books; also *On Wondrous Places*, one book; he also composed letters, and many, in my judgment not inelegant, to various persons. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1470.

Georgius of Peuerbach, most skilled in the knowledge of secular letters and not ignorant of the holy Scriptures, the philosopher and astronomer easily the most learned of all in his time, subtle in intellect, composed in speech, who in the gymnasium of Vienna for a long time publicly taught philosophy and the science of the stars, where, among countless other pupils, he instructed Johannes of Regiomontanus, a most learned man. He wrote *New Theories of the Planets*, one book, and several other works on astronomy, which have not come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1480.
