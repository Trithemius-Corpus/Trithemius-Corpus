*Hermannus*, who was called Contractus, a monk of the monastery of Saint Gall, of the order of our holy father Benedict, son of the Count of Veringen among the Swabians, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, the most learned of all the doctors of his time; a most celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, astronomer, poet, musician, and theologian; most skilled in three languages, Greek, Latin, and Arabic, and not ignorant of Hebrew; excelling in verse and prose, and also most devout in life and conduct. He wrote many splendid volumes, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among these the following are given: On Music, one book; On the Monochord, one book; On the Six Ages of the World, one book; On Astronomy, two books; On the Construction of the Astrolabe, three books; On the Eclipses of the Sun and Moon, one book; On Computation, one book; On the Squaring of the Circle, one book; On the Contest of Rithmomachy, one book; also On Physiognomy, one book. He composed lives of several holy fathers in a most elegant style. He also published many letters to various persons, one book, and almost countless poems of different kinds. He also composed many hymns and chants in honor of the saints with very sweet melody. Among them is that antiphon concerning the blessed Mother of God, “Salve Regina,” and that one, “Alma Redemptoris,” with many others. He also translated some volumes of Greeks and Arabs into Latin, as he himself acknowledges in a certain preface to Aristotle. Of what merit this Hermannus was before God, and how great a grace the most blessed Virgin Mary bestowed on him, I remember that I have already described in part, though in few and brief words, in the second book of the illustrious men of our order. He flourished in the times of the emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1040.

*Helperius*, a monk of the same monastery of Saint Gall, of the order of the holy father Benedict, himself also a man much learned in the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular letters; a most celebrated philosopher, astronomer, and poet, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, no less notable for his life than for his learning. He wrote many splendid little works, both in verse and in prose, but few of them have come into our hands. For I have only seen and read the very famous book which he published On Ecclesiastical Computation, and another entitled On Music, as well as certain poems. The rest which he composed have not come into our hands. He flourished at the same time as Hermannus Contractus, in the year of the Lord 1040.

*Rogerus*, a monk in Saxony of the monastery of Corvey, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular letters, distinguished in intellect and speech. He wrote the Life and Deeds of Bruno, archbishop of Cologne, brother of the emperor Otto I, one book. He also published certain other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished in the times of Otto III, in the year of the Lord 1000.

*Algerus*, from scholasticus a monk of the monastery of Corvey, of the order of our holy father Benedict, a man exercised by constant study in the holy Scriptures, rich also in the reading of the ancients, and magnificently learned in secular letters; prudent in intellect, eloquent in speech, strong in verse and prose. Among other little works of his intellect, he wrote a notable volume On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book; On the Same Matter against Berengarius, one book; also On the Grace of God and the Free Will of Man, one book. He is said to have written certain other works besides, but they have by no means come to the knowledge of my reading.

*Eckebertus*, a cleric of the church of Liege, studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and exceptionally learned in the disciplines of secular letters; subtle in intellect, distinguished in speech, excelling in verse no less than in prose. He wrote, in both styles, little works not to be despised, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among these there is extant a volume composed in elegant verse, which he entitled On Rustic Riddles, one book. And afterwards he wrote a somewhat larger work on the same subject. The rest which he composed have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1060.

*Leo*, the ninth pope, previously called Bruno, bishop of the city of the Leuci, which is called Toul, under the archbishop of Trier, by country an Alsatian, from the counts of Egesheim, sat in the chair of Peter after Damasus for seven years. He was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and exceptionally learned in the disciplines of secular letters, venerable no less in conduct than in knowledge. He wrote to Constantine [Monomachus?], emperor of the Greeks, On Extirpating Heresies, one book; to the bishops of Africa and Egypt, one book; also letters to various persons, one book; and certain other works unknown to us. He died under Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1054, in the seventh indiction, and is reported to have been distinguished by many miracles. *Hum*
