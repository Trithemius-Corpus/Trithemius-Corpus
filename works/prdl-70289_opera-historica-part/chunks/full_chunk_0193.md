*Catalogue of Illustrious Men Who Adorn Germany in Every Way by Their Talents and Lucubrations, by Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, of the Order of Saint Benedict, to Jakob Wimpfeling of Sélestat, Theologian.*

*Letter of Master Matthaeus Herbenus of Maastricht to the Distinguished Man Jodocus Beyselius, Patrician of Aachen.*

[unclear]

When recently I was leaving my paternal soil to set out for the further parts of Germany, and had come to Aachen, and had met you at home, Jodocus, most worthy of regard among friends, for the sake of greeting you, in view of our now almost long-standing association and friendship, I received instructions that on that journey I should visit certain very learned men, Germans indeed, bound to you by favor and goodwill, whom you had in the regions of the Rhine, and greet them in your name.

After therefore I had landed by boat at the place where the Nahe, flowing into the Rhine near Bingen, shows the road through Kreuznach to those going to Spanheim, soon, having left the boat, I make my way to Trithemius, abbot of that monastery. First I greet him, and then hand over your letters. Accordingly he embraces me so that I could not have been received more splendidly or more graciously by a great prelate.

But when an opportunity for walking about was granted, which for most people comes after the gatherings and refreshments of first friends, he leads me around, and immediately introduces me into his admirable library, where I behold very many Hebrew and Greek volumes. For of Latin books in every art, science, and faculty there was an immense abundance. And so I marvel at the exact diligence of one man in acquiring and arranging such varied monuments, and I am vehemently astonished; for I would not have thought that in all Germany there existed so great a number of foreign volumes. For I found there books of five languages, widely differing from one another in speech and character, in very ancient codices, which Trithemius, wakeful and assiduous, had brought together not without much toil.

In this way, therefore, most learned Jodocus, I found the library of Spanheim furnished. Nor that alone, but I also saw the walls of the whole abbatial house, which is spacious, and the vaults of the rooms most fittingly adorned with Greek, Hebrew, and Latin words and characters. Wherefore I too reckon thus with myself: if there is a Hebrew academy in our Germany, it is that monastery of Spanheim, where one can take in more learning from the walls than from the dusty and empty libraries of books belonging to many others. For whatever antiquity and learning Germany can possess in books, the monastery of Spanheim possesses, through the care of Abbot Trithemius.

I stayed with our abbot eleven days, not without exact remembrance of you. There, when one day in conversation we fell upon discussion of his book, which has been published on ecclesiastical writers, I said that I marveled that our Germany had had such distinguished writers, whom I had come to know in that book, and still had them at the present day; and I recalled that I had heard from a certain most learned Italian philosopher that he had never anywhere, in his reading, found any learned German except the one Albertus Magnus.

Then the abbot, smiling, said: “Therefore all the more is it to be believed that the Italians have deliberately passed over the praises of Germans in their writings, so that by passing over others their own glory might appear greater. I can indeed openly make known to you that there have existed far more very ancient and very celebrated German writers than you recognized in my book on ecclesiastical writers.” And bringing forth from that library of his a huge book, containing nothing except Germany’s most learned writers and men of most holy memory, he showed it to me. When I had looked at it, I began most urgently to beg and entreat him, as I do not doubt that you and any learned man would have done, that he should bring it about that our Germany should not henceforth go deprived of so great a glory of our ancestors, and that he should see to it that it be entrusted at the first possible day to the printer’s workshop. For when printed, through reading and imitation, it would bring it about that we would have more holy men and students of the good arts.

And so I prevailed upon the abbot, who in any case shows himself obliging to friends, and obtained that volume to be printed for the praise of our Germany. These are the things, most pleasant Jodocus, which I learned, saw, and accomplished with the lord abbot of Spanheim, and which, impatient of a longer delay, I resolved to write to you. Farewell. From the monastery of Spanheim, the thirtieth day before the Kalends of September. In the year of the Virgin’s giving birth 1495.
