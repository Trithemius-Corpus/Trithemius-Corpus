*The Twenty-Eighth.*

*Farabertus reigned 20 years.*

Farabertus, the elder son of King Clodomer, succeeded his father and reigned twenty years, beginning in the aforementioned one hundred sixty-fifth year of the Lord’s nativity, in the third Roman indiction. He renewed and again ratified with the Germans, Saxons, Thuringians, Theutons, Marcomanni, and Cimbri the treaty and friendship formerly contracted by King Francus, as we said above, and then confirmed by other kings, renewed, and often strengthened and continued by a new ordinance. This greatly displeased the Romans and stirred them to arms.

*The legions of the Romans into Germany [unclear].*

For in the sixth year of this King Farabertus, which was the one hundred seventy-first year of the Lord’s nativity, in the times of Lucius Aurelius Commodus Caesar, the Romans, not bearing calmly so great a concord of the Franks with the Theutons and Germans, tried with every effort to overthrow it. And so, having sent legions through Sarmatia into Germany against the Dacians, Marcomanni, Theutons, Saxons, and Franks, they brought forth a great army of their men. Against their incursion the kings of the aforesaid peoples sent out such great forces of fighters that the said Romans were compelled to withdraw without victory, with loss and confusion.

*Another expedition of the Romans into Germany, in the time of Antoninus Pius.*

In the eleventh year of King Farabertus, under the emperor Antoninus Pius, the Romans again led an army back into Germany by way of the Sarmatians, and fierce fighting went on for a long time, and many on both sides fell wounded by the sword. Concerning the circumstances, outcome, and miracles of rains in this war, which were then reported on behalf of the army of the Romans, following Hunibald we have set out a sufficient narrative in the first volume of the Annals; we judge it superfluous to repeat it here.

Farabertus, king of the Franks, after many brave deeds, died in the twentieth year of his reign, in the 185th year of the Lord’s nativity, in the eighth indiction.

*The Britons, who are now called English, receive the faith of Christ.*

*Faganus and Diuanus, bishops of the Britons.*

In the times of this reign of Farabertus, namely in the one hundred eighty-second year of the Lord’s nativity, Eleutherius, pope of Rome, at the request of Lucius king of Britain, son of Coillus, sent into that Britain itself, which is now named England, the bishops Faganus and Diuanus, so that they might announce the word of salvation to the said king, and also to the nobles and the whole people of the British kingdom of his island, and baptize them, converted to the faith of Christ, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. When they came to the appointed place, in a short time they converted not only King Lucius but also the people of the whole island to the faith of Christ.

*Priests of idols in Britain: pontiffs, 28; archflamens, 3.*

[unclear], the aforesaid apostolic preachers consecrated their temples of idols to the honor of the one God, the almighty Father, appointing as many bishops in place of the flamens, and archbishops in place of the archflamens. Gildas wrote of these matters more fully in the book which he entitled On the Victory of Aurelius Ambrosius, king of the Britons; and after him Geoffrey of Monmouth, a Briton, who, for Duke Rupertus of Claudiocestria, completed in ten books the history of the Britons and the successions of all their kings, from the first entry of the people, with Brutus from the Trojans, into that island itself, down to the six hundred eighty-ninth year of the Lord’s nativity. His contemporary Caradocus of Llancarfan also described the successions, years, times, and deeds of the kings of the Franks in lucid speech.

*Gildas and Geoffrey of Monmouth, historiographers.*

*Caradocus, writer of the Franks.*

*Sunno reigned 28 years.*

Sunno, the elder son of Farabertus king of the Franks, succeeded his father and reigned twenty-eight years. He was a strong, vigorous, and bold prince, who fought many battles against the Romans and also the Gauls. In his time, when Lucius king of Britain, whom we said had first most humbly received the Christian faith, died without children, the Romans sent the senator Severus into Britain with two legions. He fought many battles with Fulgenius, by whom at last, in the siege of the city of Eboracum, he was killed along with many Romans; his son, named Bassianus, they accepted as king.
