RAYMUND of Pennafort, a Catalan by nation, [was?] provincial and third master general of the Order of Preachers, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, ready in talent, distinguished in knowledge of canon law; by writing various things he made his name immortal, and by his little works bound the church to be perpetually, so to speak, his debtor. For at the command of Pope Gregory he compiled the Decretals of law, in 5 books: “The peace-making king, by pious mercy.”

A Summa on cases, in 3 books: “Since, as Jerome says.”

The Constitutions of his order, in 1 book. And certain other things.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.

ALBERTUS Galioti, a Parmese by homeland, a professor and famous interpreter of canons and laws, subtle in talent, cautious in counsel, scholastic in eloquence, who because of his learning and experience in affairs was held among the foremost doctors by the old jurists. He wrote certain little works in law not to be despised, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity with immortal praise. Among these there is extant a summa:

The Pearl of Questions, in 10 books: “When I, Albertus Galio.”

And Declarations of Judgments, in 1 book.

And certain other unknown works are held by various persons.

ODOFREDUS of Benevento, a hearer of Azo of Bologna, afterwards the teacher of Jacobus Balduini, a man most skilled in both laws, very subtle in talent, clear in eloquence, who left many distinguished disciples after him; on account of his learning and erudition, both in law and in the other letters of humanity, he was held in great authority among the ancients. Many little works of his are reported, by which he made his name known to posterity.

On the Formation of Libels, in 1 book: “After the work of notarial art.”

On Judicial Order, in 1 book: “In what manner Christ’s.”

On the Art of the Notariate, and other things which I have not seen.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.

JOHANNES de Blanosco, a Burgundian by nation, a man most learned in both laws, sufficiently instructed in the precepts of secular philosophers, keen and ready in talent. He wrote in plain speech little works not to be scorned by professors of this faculty, by which the fame of his name became known then to those present and afterwards to those to come far and wide. Among these there is extant a volume of great usefulness, which he entitled:

“I, Johannes de Blano.”

On the Actions of Advocates, in 1 book. But the rest of what he composed has by no means come to my knowledge.

And Various Questions. A Genoese by origin, from the city of Fribourg by origin, John by name, a German by nation, much [unclear] from the divine Scriptures; bishop of Bosnia in Hungary, of the Order of Preachers, a man [unclear] in the divine [unclear] by the consensus of the fathers, much practiced and learned in secular law, excellently skilled in philosophy, who, because of his learning and holiness of life, had been taken from the order to the episcopate; later abandoning it, he returned to his order and, after Raymond, was made general master of the order [unclear]; he did many good things. He wrote a notable work:

Summa of Confessors, in 5 books: “Since the newness of doubts.”

And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230. He was buried in his convent at Strasbourg, once shining, as they say, with many miracles.

INNOCENT IV, pope, formerly called Symbaldus, an Italian by nation, [unclear] city.

On Ecclesiastical Power, in 1 book: “It is read in Ezekiel.”

Apparatus on the Decretals, in 5 books.

Rule of the Poor Clares, in 1 book: “The form of life of the order.”
