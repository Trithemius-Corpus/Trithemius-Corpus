For in the following year, which was the ninth of King Meroveus and the 453rd from the Lord’s Nativity, in the sixth indiction of the Romans, Attila, king of the Huns; Walamir, king of the Ostrogoths; Ardaric, king of the Gepids; and other northern peoples, conspiring together as one, came forth from the borders of Pannonia, and with five hundred thousand fighters invaded the western empire and at the same time the kingdom of the Franks in the regions of Gaul. They carried out immense slaughters and spared no one.

And first, indeed, throughout all Gaul their fury, God permitting it, raged so greatly that no fortification, strength, or power could defend a city, town, or castle from their assault. Against their insolence Meroveus, king of the Franks, and Theodoric, king of the Visigoths, and Aetius, patrician of the Romans, advanced to war with the military forces of various other peoples in a common array. With them, in the Catalaunian plains, they fought from morning until night. The Franks, with their allied companions adhering to them, defeated the enemy, although Attila was not altogether destroyed.

In that battle one hundred and eighty thousand fighters were slain, among whom also fell Theodoric, king of the Visigoths, a most warlike man. When the Franks, together with the rest who had assembled against the Huns, withdrew, King Attila took his army and returned to his homeland, so that, once his army had been restored, he might continue his foolish fury and reduce Gaul together with Italy to desolation.

For in the following year King Attila entered Italy and either plundered or utterly overthrew almost all its cities. Leo, the first pope, came to him clothed in pontifical vestments, and not only obtained safety for the city and the people, but also secured that he should leave Italy behind and depart. When he was asked by his own men why he had been so gentle toward the pontiff, he answered that he had not revered him, but rather a certain old man, venerable in appearance, standing beside him, who, with sword drawn, threatened death to the king if he either saddened the pontiff or denied anything that he asked. Not long after, King Attila, having drunk to excess at his wedding, died from a stroke of apoplexy.

In the eleventh year of Meroveus, king of the Franks, and the 457th from the Lord’s Nativity, in the tenth indiction of the Romans, the Franks captured the city of Trier on the following occasion; Attila, king of the Huns, had taken it in war a few years before and crushed it with heavy devastation.

After the death of Valentinian, whom the friends of the patrician Aetius had killed in the same year, and who had ruled after Theodosius together with Marcian, Avitus was appointed emperor by the Romans; under him the city of Trier was captured. At that time there was in the city of Trier a certain senator named Lucius, who had a wife, in the estimation of many, most beautiful; Avitus languished in shameful love for her. For this reason, feigning illness, he took to his bed at an appointed time, and, as if to obtain relief from his sickness, ordered all the matrons of the city to come to visit him in succession. Among others came the wife of Lucius, suspecting no evil; Avitus immediately violated her. When the crime had been committed, the woman silently returned home to her husband. On the next day Caesar Avitus, rising from his bed healthy in body but polluted in conscience by his crime, said to Lucius as he met him: “You certainly have baths beautiful enough, Lucius, but you wash in cold water,” hinting at his wife’s foul flow, contrary to nature. But Lucius, understanding the figure, bore it gravely; and, sending messengers with letters in deepest secrecy to the Franks, he promised that if they came as quickly as possible, he would hand over the city of Trier into their power, without doubt, delay, or difficulty.

For up to that point the Trier people had, for many years past, held fast to the Romans and their kings with stubborn adherence. But the Franks, not forgetful of the slaughter and injuries which they had once suffered from Nannenus and Quintinus, princes of Trier, near the Carbonarian forest in the times of Duke Dagobert, the interking, soon gathered forces and hastened gladly to the destruction of the Trier people. When the city was taken, the Franks plundered everything and subjected it to their own kingdom.

In these times the kingdom of the Franks gradually took on great increases and expanded its borders in many ways throughout Gaul. Therefore the kings of the people, who from their first entrance into Germany had held, for nearly eight hundred and seventy years, their seats near the Rhine, where Holland now is, with the peoples who are called Gelders, Westphalians, Cleves people, and Frisians, at Nijmegen, Duisburg, and other places and castles on this side of the Rhine, transferred the dwelling place of their habitation into Gaul, rarely resting from wars, until at last they subjected all Germany and also Gaul, together with Italy, to the kingdom of the Franks.
