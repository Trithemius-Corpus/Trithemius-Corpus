*Of Illustrious Men.*

Many and various letters to diverse persons. Far greater things are expected.

JOHANNES Bartholus, Parisian, doctor of medicine, the most excellent of all in this age, and for that reason specially endowed, even by various most devoted and most expert lovers of every discipline, with the name of Apollo and Aesculapius. He is of such authority in learning among all men that you will assuredly find no one whom you could compare with him, whether you seek what belongs to his own profession or what belongs to any other doctrine. For, most accomplished in every kind of discipline, he discusses all things so learnedly, so elegantly, so skilfully and readily, and has so deep an understanding, that he alone seems to have embraced with the greatest zeal all arts, both divine and human.

ANTONIUS Sylviolus, Parisian, notable poet and orator, although he is skilled in many kinds of languages, excels nevertheless in three: French, Latin, and the common Italian tongue. By these he has won for himself, among all men and also among the most learned and noble, especially the foremost and most renowned nobles of the Amboise house of the French nation, such fame and favor as no detraction of the envious can dissolve, no devouring decay of age abolish, since already by this threefold gift of language he has sought immortality for himself, and is regarded among our countrymen as no less in this most charming fluency of the French language than Dante among the Florentines. But how great an eloquence he possesses by labor and by the light of his own talent, by what subtlety of invention he stands out, by what art of arrangement he excels, with what fertility of variety he abounds, and finally with what weight of matter and sententiae he advances, his very splendid works, several serious dialogues, [unclear] most richly. For he wrote many splendid works, especially several comedies in his own vernacular, in which he showed how various, how abundant, how fluent, how elegant, how witty, and finally how urbane he was. In Latin, however, he published the following:

One book of Elegies. One book of Epigrams.

They bear witness. He has written and writes every day other things far greater, by which he may show that the French have their poets and orators, not inferior to the Italians, indeed superior to them. If they were to expend constant and unwearied labor upon these languages, in which every Italian either flatters or catches the breeze, [unclear]. He still lives and feeds on the ethereal air, orator of the reverend father in Christ, Georges d’Amboise, archbishop of Rouen.

GUILHELMUS Copus of Basel, a most learned philosopher and very skilled in Latin and Greek, excels in this our age at Paris in the medical art, of which he is a most eminent doctor and professor. To his house not only Parisians resort as to the single remedy of health, but even those who preside over this our kingdom, having heard the man’s fame, send for him with utmost speed and summon him, and experience his aid. Alone among all the prelates of this profession he has begotten a name for himself, I do not know whether immortal or rather enviable. This Guillelmus is subtle in talent, polished in speech, eloquent in discourse, admirable in learning, to be placed after no one in knowledge of natural things. He lives at Paris under Louis the Twelfth of this name, with great expectation of him among all. For he is forging things for posterity to read, among which we already have:

The Salutary Precepts of Paulus Aegineta, clothed by him in Latin elegance.

The same man’s Prognostics of Hippocrates.

JUDOCUS Clichtoveus of Nieuwpoort, doctor, most accomplished theologian, most polished philosopher, and so notable an orator that he orders the untamed manners of men, indeed the whole mortal race, with eloquence divine rather than mortal. Born for these duties, he directs one ignorant of letters with straight reins and enlightens the foolish with polished speech. Nature the parent has marked this man with dignity, with these most noble gifts of mind, so that whatever he undertakes in this sensible world he adapts to the propagation of literary learning. For he strives with all his might to polish what is rough, foul, gaping, and barbarous, and to restore to philosophy its own brilliance; above all, he resolves in soul and mind to devote his effort to interpreting deeds and sayings. Divine utterances and knowledge of supramundane things so adorn him that, while he is still breathing mortal life on earth, they admit him to the most radiant choirs of the heavenly ones. He has left to us and to future posterity most abundant and outstanding monuments of his learning in a great number of volumes, intending at last to restore greater things. For he wrote the things which are set out in this sequence:

A commentary on the Elegances of Augustinus Dathus.

A commentary on the Introduction to Arithmetic of Severinus Boethius. A compendium On the Practice of Numbering.

A commentary on Johannes Damascenus. An excellent book On True Nobility.

Scholia on the paraphrase of Aristotle’s Physics.

E.I S
