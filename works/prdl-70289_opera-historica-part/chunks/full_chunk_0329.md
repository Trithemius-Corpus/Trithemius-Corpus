Zosimus, pope, Greek by nation, sat in the pontificate after Saint Innocent for one year and eight months; a learned man, holy, beloved of God; by word and example [unclear], eloquent in speech, whose merits of holiness the Catholic Church proclaims. He was of such great piety toward the poor that, after all his possessions had been cheerfully distributed for their use, he was thereafter called and was the father of the poor. Among other things, in quite distinguished style, he wrote [letters] of his predecessor:

*Letters*, one book. *From the report of our brother*.

*On Easter, to Hilary*, one book.

*On the Priesthood, to Esychius*, one book. *Your affection requires*, etc.

He died under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 419, in the second indiction, on the seventh day before the Kalends of January.

Boniface, the first pope of that name, succeeding blessed Zosimus, presided over the Church for three years and four months; a man of great learning, quite fit for the governance of souls, who established many things for ecclesiastical usefulness, which are found scattered in the canons of the holy fathers. For the edification of the faithful, as they say, he wrote some small works. Of these I have found only:

*Decretal Letters*, one book. *The Roman Church, to which*, etc.

He died under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 422, in the fifth indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of November.

Proba, a most elegant woman, wife of Adelphus, the Roman proconsul, a woman most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, burning with wonderful zeal, surpassed her sex by her learning. Perfectly instructed in Greek and Latin letters, and excelling in verse and prose, since she held Virgil word for word in her mind, she conceived the idea of describing the New and Old Testament from his words, as if he had intended to demonstrate it in his poems. Therefore, boldly undertaking the work stored up in her mind, carefully going through all the books of Virgil, taking now this verse, now that one, sometimes half a verse, sometimes smaller portions of verses, with wonderful industry and with the measure of the feet everywhere preserved, she described the Catholic volumes in the poet’s verses, and marked this work with a Greek term in about seven hundred verses:

*Virgilian Cento*, one book. *Long ago [I began] to profane*, etc.

Whether she wrote anything more I do not know.

Nevertheless this work is counted among the apocrypha, because, even if a devout woman undertook the study of pious things, nevertheless Virgil never considered that his little words should be referred to the honor of Christ or to our faith. For this is not a proper interpretation of the matter, but a fictive one assumed by Proba. She flourished, as some think, under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 430.

Eudoxia, wife of Theodosius the Younger, a woman most devout in the Christian religion and most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, returning from Jerusalem, brought with her the relics of the most holy protomartyr Stephen and honorably placed them in the basilica of the blessed martyr Lawrence.
