*Catalogue of Illustrious Men.*

Wolfhelm, abbot of the monastery of Brauweiler, which is nearly two miles distant from Cologne, born in the district of Ribuarien in the same diocese of Cologne, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, excellently instructed in secular letters, devout and most holy in life and conduct, renowned for the performance of many miracles, subtle in intellect, polished in speech, excellent in verse and prose. He wrote some distinguished volumes, but few of them have come to my knowledge. Against Berengar of Tours, On the Sacrament of the Altar, he published a small but necessary work addressed to Abbot Meginhard, one book; also many sermons to the brothers, various letters to different persons; likewise many elegant poems, composed in various kinds of meter; he arranged epigrams over all the volumes of his library. He is also said to have written several other works, which have by no means come to the notice of our reading. He was first a monk in the monastery of Saint Maximin at Trier, then at Saint Pantaleon in Cologne; afterward he was made abbot at Gladbach; finally he was transferred as abbot to the aforesaid monastery of Brauweiler, where he presided as the third in order. He died under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1091, in the fourteenth indiction, on the tenth day before the Kalends of May.

Theoderic, abbot of the monastery of Saint Trudo of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man devoted to and learned in the holy Scriptures, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect, composed in speech, also practiced in verse and prose. He wrote many distinguished little works in both kinds, by which he brought his name to the knowledge of many. Among these the following are reported: Life of Saint Trudo, one book; Life of Saint Bavo, one book; Life of Saint Remold, one book; various sermons to the brothers, one book; also several letters to different persons, one book; certain other works as yet unknown to us. He is said to have flourished in the time of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1100.

Conrad, monk of the monastery of Brauweiler, of the order of the divine father Benedict, in the diocese of Cologne, once a hearer and disciple of the most holy abbot Wolfhelm, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters. For the edification of the faithful he wrote volumes not to be despised, with which he usefully occupied his talent; of these the following are extant. The Life of the same Saint Wolfhelm, abbot, addressed to Eberhard of Brauweiler and Herman, abbots of Saint Pantaleon, one book; On the Miracles of the same blessed Wolfhelm, one book; sermons in honor of the saints; several other works unknown to us. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1100.

Stephen, also called Herrand, the fourteenth bishop of Halberstadt, formerly abbot of the monastery of Ilsenburg in Saxony, of the order of our most holy father Benedict, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, venerable no less for holiness than for knowledge, wrote, in the person of Count Louis, faithful to the Church, a most elegant letter in the form of a little book, in defense of obedience to the Roman Church, against Henry IV, rebel against the Roman Church, to Waltram, bishop of Naumburg, who stubbornly defended the party of Henry IV against obedience to the supreme pontiff. He also composed other letters in the same cause concerning various ecclesiastical affairs, and various treatises, but they have by no means come into our hands. He died under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1107, in the fifteenth indiction.

Rupert, abbot of Deutz near Cologne, formerly a monk of the monastery of Saint Lawrence at Liege, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man in the divine Scriptures, with the Holy Spirit enlightening him, the most learned of all the learned men of his time, not ignorant of secular literature, subtle in intellect, sweet and composed in speech, shone forth renowned no less for holiness than for knowledge. While he was placed in the aforesaid monastery at Liege, when he was still a young man, he applied the greatest zeal to divine letters; but since, because of the slightness of his ability, he made little progress, he turned himself with tears from infancy to prayer, and, the most blessed Mother of God manifestly appearing to him and promising grace, he obtained such knowledge in all Scripture that he had no equal in his time. And lest you think it a fable, he himself confesses these things about himself in many places of his works. Thereafter he had such love for sacred readings that not even while sleeping did he seem to rest from divine conversations, while his tongue and lips were moved as if he were reading something. He wrote almost infinite volumes, of which I have found the following.
