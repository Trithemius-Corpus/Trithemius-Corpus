*134 Of Johannes Trithemius*

*Of Theodoric, of the monastery of St Alban at Mainz.*

THEODORIC, a monk of the monastery of Saint Alban the Martyr at Mainz, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, outstandingly instructed in secular letters, a philosopher and most distinguished poet, subtle in talent and renowned in eloquence, is said to have written certain excellent little works, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of these the following are extant: To Richard, abbot of Fulda, On the Life and Miracles of Our Most Holy Father Benedict, one book; also On His Translation into Gaul, to the same man, one book; Letters to Various Persons, one book. He also published many varied poems. But there are several others which have not yet come into our hands. He flourished under Emperor Henry II, but the third king of this name, in the year of the Lord 1040.

WENERIC, formerly a cleric of the church of Trier, bishop of Vercelli, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, ready in talent and renowned in eloquence, wrote some little works of exercise, by which he proved his learning. In that discord which existed between Hildebrand, Pope Gregory VII, and Emperor Henry IV, he wrote, in the person of Theodoric, bishop of Verdun, while he himself was still a cleric at Trier, to the same Pope Gregory, a volume furnished with divine Scriptures: for he did not rebuke him, but, as an elder, entreated him as a father, with friendly affection, as though grieving, suggesting to him all the things which talkative rumor had spread abroad that he had done or said against right, divine law, and equity. It is entitled On the Discord of Priesthood and Empire, one book. He also composed many other things, but they have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080.

ERHARD, a monk of Corvey in Saxony, of the Benedictine order, a man most zealous in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in talent and ready in eloquence, of very celebrated reputation in delivering homilies to the people, wrote, among the other little works of his talent, a notable work On the Pentateuch, five books; also Homilies, one book. He is also said to have written many other things, and to have expounded the sacred Scriptures in many volumes; these have not come to my knowledge.

WILLERAM, abbot of the monastery of Mersburg, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man learned in the divine writings and not ignorant of secular literature, skilled in verse and prose, shone forth no less renowned in life than in learning. After he had for some time devoted his labor to the letters of philosophy in the school of Paris, he returned to his homeland, that is, eastern Francia, which is called Franconia. Made scholasticus of the church of Bamberg because of the eminence of his learning, he did not abandon the sweet fellowship of letters. At length, considering that the vain glory of the world passes away with time, scorning the age and despising worldly things for the love of God, he put on the monastic habit in the monastery of Fulda. As his merits increased, he was finally elected abbot at Mersburg. He wrote a work mixed of meter and prose on the Song of Songs, which he entitled On the Nuptials of Christ and the Church, two books; also various sermons to the brothers; and, as they say, several letters to various persons. He also composed many other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080[?].

BERNARD, a monk of the monastery of Saint Vitus of Corvey in Saxony, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man zealous and learned in the divine writings and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, subtle in talent, renowned in eloquence, skilled in verse and prose, wrote some little works of his talent, by which he made his name known to posterity. To Hartwig, archbishop of Magdeburg, he published a volume, indeed in lucid but bitter speech, against Henry IV, king but the third emperor, in which it is clearly evident how grievous and hateful the same Henry was to the Saxons. He wrote besides many varied letters, and several other things unknown to us. He flourished in the times of the same Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080[?]. He composed various things.

WILLIAM, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, of the order of Saint Benedict, in the diocese of Speyer, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher, musician, computist, and most celebrated theologian, devout and most holy in life and conduct, who was renowned for many miracles in life and death, magnificently restored by word and example the monastic discipline among the Germans, which had almost collapsed. He wrote, in lucid speech, little works not to be despised, of which the following are extant: Constitutions of His New Reform, one book; also On Music, one book; also On the Clock, one book; he made a correction of the Psalter, one book; he published various letters to various persons, among which many are said to have been to Anselm II, archbishop of Canterbury, with whom he was most familiar. Some letters of this Anselm to this William are also extant. He also published some other things, which have not come to me. He died under Emperor Henry III, but King Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1091, in the fourteenth indiction, on the fourth Nones of July. He was buried in the middle of his church.
