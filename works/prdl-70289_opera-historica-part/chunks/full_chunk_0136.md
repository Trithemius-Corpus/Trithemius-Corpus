*On the Origin of the Franks.*

...suffered no small loss from the Franks, Germans, Suebi, that is, Alemanni, and Saxons. But in the eleventh year of King Clodius, Claudius Caesar led the Roman army against the Suebi; the Suebi, meeting him not far from Lake Benacus with thirty thousand men, fought fiercely. At length the victory fell to the Romans, and more than twelve thousand of the Suebi fell, the rest seeking safety in flight.

King Clodius died in the seventeenth year of his reign, and in the year 288 of the Lord’s Nativity.

Walther, son of King Clodius, reigned after his father for eight years. In his first year Diocletian began at Rome and ruled for nearly twenty years; he was of such arrogance and pride that he ordered himself to be publicly worshipped as God.

In the first, second, third, and fourth years of King Walther there were great disturbances in Britain, which is now England. For, after Lucius, king of the Britons, had died some years before, he who first had received the Christian faith from Pope Eleutherius in the time of Suimon, son of Pharabert, king of the Franks, in the year of Christ 188, the Romans appointed Severus, a senator, in his place and sent him into Britain with two legions; when he landed, the Britons received him at the shore with war.

After he was at length slain at York, Carausius, born in Britain of the lowest rank, was declared king with the consent of the Romans and exercised much tyranny. When he at last had been killed, in the times of Walther, king of the Franks, the Romans sent Alexius in his place with three legions of soldiers. But the Britons, unable to bear the rule of the Romans any longer, themselves appointed Asclepiodotus as their king, and killed Alexius with two legions of Romans. But they besieged Gallus the prefect with the third legion at London; when he saw that he could not resist, after receiving permission to depart, he surrendered the city. Asclepiodotus began in the eighth year of Walther and reigned ten years.

The Romans were powerful in these times and ruled many nations. The Franks, however, were afflicted by the utmost hatred of the Romans; nevertheless they remained quiet, silently awaiting the prophecy of their prophets, which had not yet arrived. King Walther died in the eighth year of his reign, in the year of the Lord 296.

Dagobert, son of King Walther, reigned after his father for eleven years. He was very gentle toward his subjects; hence it came about that great and small revered him as a father, each pursuing him with great reverence and honor.

In the fourth year of Dagobert, king of the Franks, Coel, duke of Gloucester, rising against his lord Asclepiodotus, king of the Britons, induced by the promises of the Romans, killed him in war. Rejoicing at this news, the Romans sent Constantius, a senator, who had previously subdued Spain for them, a man no less prudent than bold. When Coel heard that he had landed on the shore, he sent messengers, sought peace, and promised tribute to the Romans, provided only that he might have the kingdom. Constantius agreed. These terms having been settled, Coel died within forty days; soon afterward Constantius took his daughter, named Helena, to whom no maiden in the land was equal in beauty, knowledge of the liberal arts, and skill with musical instruments. [unclear] afterward succeeded in the kingdom, and prevailed not only over the Britons but also against Maxentius for the empire of the Romans.

For Constantius, having been made emperor, made Constantine Caesar and entrusted Gaul to him. At that time the Alemanni occupied Gaul in great power; Caesar Constantine waged war with them in the fourth year of the aforesaid Dagobert, king of the Franks, at the city of Langres in Gaul, and killed sixty thousand of them. Dagobert, king of the Franks, died in the eleventh year of his reign, in the year of the Lord 307.

Clogio, son of Dagobert, succeeding his dead father, reigned only two years. For in the second year of his reign the Romans fiercely invaded the kingdom of the Franks around the Meuse and the Rhine, namely in that year in which Constantius, dying at York in Britain, commended the kingdom and empire to his son Constantine with his mother.

When therefore Clogio, king of the Franks, fought rather incautiously, he was intercepted by the Romans and killed, leaving two sons, of whom the elder by birth was then in his thirtieth year, while the younger by birth, Richimer, was eighteen. But by the law of the Franks it was forbidden for anyone to be advanced as king unless he had completed the twenty-fourth year of his age. Hence it came about that neither of Clogio’s sons could come to the kingdom, but their paternal uncle, by election of the princes, was crowned king. King Clogio was slain in the second year of his reign, and in the year of Christ 309.
