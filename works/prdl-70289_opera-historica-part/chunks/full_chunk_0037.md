From this a proverb arose among the Sicambri: whenever one man saw or heard from another anything contrary to the law, he would say, “Hort opp lieffman, kent gyn nit dy grote kunig Basan.” As if he had said, “Cease, good men, from such things; do you not know how just the great King Basanus is? If he perceived you saying or doing such things, he would without doubt kill you as transgressors.”

In the fifth year of King Basanus, the king of the Orchades, having many island petty kings as auxiliaries, declared war on the Sicambri because of the rejection of his daughter, whom King Basanus, having had her as wife, had repudiated and sent back to her father. The reason for her repudiation we described in the first volume of the annals. But King Basanus, having learned of the approach of his adversaries, advanced against them with a great multitude of fighters to the seashore, not far from the place where the Franks later built Mesoburg. Seeing from their ships so great an army of the Sicambri, the Orchades did not dare to advance onto land.

When the Gauls and Morini again broke eastward, where the Sicambri had placed settlements in the region of the Tungri, King Basanus met them with the strong hand of his men and laid them low with such slaughter that scarcely a few escaped out of many. Having won the victory, he built many castles and very strong fortifications on both sides of the river Meuse, in which he stationed garrisons of the Sicambri.

In the upper part of the river, moreover, he built a very strong castle, which from his own name he called Basanburg; in it the kings of the Sicambri and Franks after him resided for a long time, because of the pleasantness and strength of the place.

In the twenty-sixth year of his reign, King Basanus, after many battles bravely fought with various nations round about, wishing to present himself as pontiff of the gods, renewed their rites with much superstition. He appointed many priests most skilled in the worship of the divine powers, attentive to the course of the stars, trained after the manner of the ancients in prophecies, in foreknowledge, in the interpretation of dreams, in ethical and physical doctrines, and also very well practiced in composing and singing, in both languages, the songs and histories of the ancient kings and heroes only of their own people. Among these Heligastus, once the son of the pontiff Theocalus, surpassed the others in talent and practice of writing; he was in charge of educating the sons of the dukes and nobles, and described the deeds of heroes in verse.

Concerning this man Hunibaldus, historian of the Franks, writes many marvelous things; unless they are granted to have been done by the artifice of some demons, they should be thought rather to have been fabricated by some writer than performed by a man devoted to idols. He foretold to the Sicambri many future things; he presented Jupiter visibly to the priests; he brought men’s secret counsels into public view; and he disclosed to King Basanus the hidden plans of the enemy in difficult causes and affairs. It seemed that nothing escaped him of all the things that the enemies, even in most secret counsel, had discussed against the Sicambri. This matter inspired confidence in friends, but despair in enemies.

For King Basan, with all the princes of the kingdom, depended wholly on the counsel of Heligastus; what he ordered, all did; whatever he forbade, no one dared attempt. At his command the Sicambri met the enemy; and if he commanded the contrary, they remained peacefully at home. As often as he foretold victory to the Sicambri, they conquered; as often as he foretold otherwise, they were conquered. While he lived, the Sicambri prospered in all things; whenever their name was heard, it terrified enemies and made them dreadful to all throughout [unclear].

After he had spent thirty-two years in his office, on a certain solemn day of Jove, standing in the midst of the priests, hurling thunderbolts in the temple and troubling the air with his voice, resounding with frenzies in the manner of soothsayers, he suddenly disappeared, and, vanishing completely, no one was ever found who could know where he had gone. The Sicambri, thinking that he had become a god, proclaimed that he had been taken up into marriage with Diana, and they decreed sacred rites for him each year.

In the thirty-third year of his reign, Basanus moved a mighty army to the upper parts of the river Rhine, to the region called Tegaraña, which lay toward the south between the Saxons and the Cisrhenane Mainz people, under the protection of the Treviri, having its own petty king named Thaborinus. King Basanus killed him along with many others and subjugated his whole region to his own dominion. These things were done in the year 251 before the birth of Christ. The place in which the aforesaid King Thaborinus of the Tegaranes fell was called Mount Thabor from his name; it is about four miles distant from Confluentes. There Basanus, king of the Franks, founded a very strong castle, which, as a perpetual memorial of the victory, he named Mount Thaborinus; but, the word having been corrupted, it is today called Monthahur by the inhabitants.
