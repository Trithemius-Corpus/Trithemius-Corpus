*Rabanus Maurus, archbishop of Mainz.*

Rabanus Maurus of Mainz, after Saint Boniface the sixth archbishop of Mainz, a Thuringian by nationality, sprung from the city of Fulda, a disciple of Alcuin, was first made a monk of the monastery of Fulda when he was still a boy of ten years. Thereafter he turned all his study to the knowledge of the Scriptures. In all learning, both of secular and of divine writings, he became by far most learned: a distinguished philosopher, an eminent poet, an eloquent rhetorician, a most celebrated astronomer and computist, skilled in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew; in his time the Church had no one like him. Then, as his merits increased, in the thirty-fifth year of his age he was elected abbot of the same monastery by the vote of all the brethren. Presiding over its government for twenty-four years, he composed many, almost countless, volumes. At last, after the death of Otgar, archbishop of Mainz, he was consecrated bishop of the same church; sitting for nine years in the episcopate, he did many good things for the Church. Of his little works the following are reported: To Bishop Freculph, On Genesis, four books; On Numbers, four books; On Leviticus, four books; On Deuteronomy, four books; On Joshua, two books; On Judges, two books; On the Book of Ruth, one book; On Kings, to Hildewin, abbot of Saint-Denis, four books; also On Paralipomenon, four books; On Ezra and Nehemiah, three books; On Tobit, one book; On the Book of Judith, seven books; On [unclear], one book; On the Psalms, one book; On the Proverbs of Solomon, one book; On Ecclesiastes, one book; On the Song of Songs, one book; On the Book of Wisdom, three books; On Ecclesiasticus, ten books; On Isaiah the Prophet, eight books; On Jeremiah the Prophet, twenty books; also On Ezekiel the Prophet, twenty books; On Daniel the Prophet, three books; On the Twelve Minor Prophets, twelve books; On the Books of Maccabees, three books; On the Gospel of Matthew, eight books; On the Gospel of Luke, three books; On the Gospel of Mark, four books; On the Gospel of John, two books; On all the Epistles of Paul, twenty-one books; On the Catholic Epistles, seven books; On the Acts of the Apostles, one book; On the Apocalypse of John, one book; On the Faith of the Most Holy Trinity, one book; also On the Praise of the Holy Cross, a work of wondrous genius, two books; To Bishop Haymo, On the Universe, or Etymologies, twenty-one books; also On Universal Nature, one book; to Bishop Haistulf, On the Instruction of Clerics, three books; On the Divine Offices, one book; On the Ecclesiastical Computus, two books; To Emperor Louis, one book; On Questions of the Canons, one book; To Bishop Regenbald, one book; On the Blessings of the Patriarchs, one book; a Homiliary of the ceremonies of the whole year, two books; also Letters to various persons, one book. But he composed various epigrams and many treatises in humane letters which have not yet come into our hands. He died under Emperor Lothar, in the year of the Lord 855, in the third indiction, in the sixty-eighth year of his age, when the ninth year of his episcopate had been completed. He was buried in the monastery of the holy martyr Alban near Mainz, then of the order of Saint Benedict.

Strabus, a monk of Fulda, once the disciple and notary of the aforesaid Abbot Rabanus, was certainly himself a most learned man, fully instructed both in divine Scripture and in secular letters, a theologian, philosopher, and most celebrated poet, quick in talent and clear in speech. He wrote several little works in sacred volumes, by which he commended his memory to posterity. But few of them have come to my knowledge. Chiefly there are reported commentaries On Genesis, one book; also On Exodus, one book; On Leviticus, one book; and many others. This Strabus, a most studious man, was the first to compile over the whole Bible the gloss which they call the master gloss or ordinary gloss, from the sayings of the holy fathers; others afterwards enlarged it by adding certain things. He flourished under the emperors Louis and Lothar, in the year of the Lord 860.

Lupus Servatus, priest, to whom Saint Rabanus, abbot of Fulda, is read to have written eight books on Paul’s Epistle to the Romans, was a man studious and learned in divine Scripture, and learned also in secular letters. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote a book On Three Questions, that is, on free will, on predestination, and on the blood of Christ, concerning a certain superfluous assessment or redemption extending to the salvation of the impious. For over this matter there arose a certain disturbance of faith in the Church in the year of the Lord 848. Besides these he published several other works, but they have not come to my knowledge.
