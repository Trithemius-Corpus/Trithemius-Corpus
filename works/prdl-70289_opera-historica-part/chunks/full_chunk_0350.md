*The Abbot of Spanheim* ... of the Church for ten years, a most learned man, who, because of his learning and holiness of life, is rightly judged comparable to the best pontiffs. He translated the four books of the Dialogues of blessed Gregory, the great pope, from Latin into Greek, and enlightened many who were ignorant of the Latin language by their reading. He also wrote, in his own vein, works in Latin that were very elegant and useful.

Letters to various persons, one book.

Among these there are several addressed to Saint Boniface, archbishop of Mainz. He died under Pippin, two years before the martyrdom of the same Saint Boniface, in the year of the Lord 752, in the fifth indiction, on the Ides of March.

Alcuinus, or Albinus, monk and deacon, abbot of the monastery of Saint Martin of Tours, English by nation, formerly a pupil of Saint Bede the priest, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and second to none in his time in knowledge of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, was summoned from Britain by the emperor Charlemagne. He was held in such intimacy by him that he was called the emperor's beloved master; under his instruction the emperor himself strove to be initiated in all the disciplines of the liberal arts. He wrote many distinguished volumes, among which are reported:

On the Holy Trinity and the Faith, one book. To the glorious lord Charles, emperor.

To the Questions of Fredegisus, one book. To the most desired son.

To Eulalia, On the Nature of the Soul, one book. Dearest in Christ [unclear].

To Count Guido, On the Virtues, one book. To the most beloved son Guido.

Sentences on Genesis, one book.

On the Song of Songs. On Ecclesiastes, one book.

On the Gospel of John. On the Epistles of Paul, fourteen books.

Mirror of Little Ones, one book. To the venerable and in Christ.

Life of Saint Vedast the Bishop, one book. We believe in one God.

Letters to various persons, one book. In the writing of divine [unclear].

He is also said to have written many other things, which, however, have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the rule of Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 770.

Isuardus, a monk of the Order of Saint Benedict, French by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, quick in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, by order of the emperor Charlemagne, after Eusebius, Jerome, and Bede, gathered from the writings of the ancients the deeds and passions of the holy martyrs, confessors, and virgins into a single volume of no small size, which we are accustomed to entitle:

Martyrology, one book. And certain other things.

He flourished under the same emperor Charles, in the year of the Lord 790.

Paulus, monk of the monastery of Monte Cassino, of the Order of Saint Benedict, Italian by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, was held in great esteem by the emperor Charles because of his knowledge of letters. Formerly, before his conversion, he had been a deacon of the church of Aquileia and chancellor of Desiderius, the last king of the Lombards; and, captured with him by Charles and taken into Gaul, he at length became a monk. He wrote not a few volumes, both in verse and in prose. Of these I have found only the following. At the request of Aldeburga, daughter of King Desiderius, he added:

To the Chronicle of Eutropius, one book.

The Deeds of the Bishops of Metz, one book.

The Life of Pope Gregory the First.

The Life of Saint Arnulph the Bishop, one book.

The Life of Saint Benedict, [unclear].

The Deeds of the Lombards, one book. The northern region.

Many hymns in various meters, one book.

Letters to various persons, one book.

By order of Charles he arranged histories and readings throughout the whole year, suitable for the several feasts of the saints. And he arranged and wrote many other things.

He flourished in the times of the same Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 780.
