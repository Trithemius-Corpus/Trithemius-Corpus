*Additions of Certain Persons*

... What, then, would they object next? Is God, from whom every best gift and every perfect gift is from above, just as Simonides was falsely believed to begrudge metaphysics or divine knowledge to mortals, so pursuing the Germans alone with some singular envy, not favoring them, nor giving them keenness of mind, lively talent, elevated understanding, eager industry, firm memory, exercises of talent, and courses of the mind in which they may sweat and toil? Far from it; for God is without envy. For there is a pagan poet who sings: "Each man's beginnings will bring his toil and fortune; King Jupiter is the same for all."

I pass over the most learned men of Germany, who in our own time flourish in knowledge of every art and of all kinds of things, both in schools and monasteries, and in churches and the courts of princes: in regular grammar, in all dialectic, in music, in geometry, in the science of the stars, in Aristotelian philosophy, in all the mysteries of natural things, in moral disciplines, in oratory, in poetry, in all humane letters, in the most sacred laws, in pontifical canons, in divine institutions; they are most learned, outstanding in talent. They produce poems in every genre; they know histories and cosmography excellently; they write ornate treatises. I pass over these Germans of ours who are not ignorant of Greek letters. Indeed, Germans survive today who have translated Greek books into the Latin language not inelegantly.

Even if, perhaps, some Germans write sacred, humble, honorable, and most solid sentiments in a lowly style, are they therefore to be held cheap? Far from it. The old jurists, even Italians, are not to be cast aside; nor physicians, nor theologians, who set forth their old and excellent doctrines not with verbal paint and bombast, but in a calm manner of speaking and with moderate Latinity. Is the Italian Bonaventure to be rejected? In written works there is no one greater, no one more profound, no one more devout than he. Is Johannes Andreae to be rejected, or Hostiensis? or Baldus the Sicilian? Are all those men to be rejected as harsh, unpolished, rude, and shapeless, because after the manner of the scholastics they run through their teachings on a pedestrian plain? Or is no one to be judged learned unless he writes most elegantly in the Ciceronian manner? Or are all those who strive to adorn light, slippery, useless, obscene sentiments with a caparisoned style, wantonness of words, and license of composition to be judged most learned? Both are false.

Although that man is certainly more advanced who intermingles the sweetness of honeyed speech with distinguished, weighty, and profound sentiments. But we cannot all be Scipios, nor all like Nestor, from whose tongue, though he was aged and almost decrepit, speech flowed sweeter than honey. Therefore, if some of our countrymen have used not a highly polished but a doctrinal and everyday style of speech, they are not to be despised. For it was enough for them to speak in such a way that they might be understood in honorable matters. Indeed, just as for the farmer not only that land is to be cultivated which produces either the most precious spices or the most excellent gifts of Bacchus, so good men accept not only Ciceronian orations or the more polished works of noble poets, but also embrace middling works; they also read those walking in a humble buskin.

Finally, let them remember, I beseech them, those envious detractors, most bitter censors of our language, that styles of speaking change with the times, and that what is different is not immediately worse; but by the fault of human malice the old is always praised, the present is distasteful. It is sufficiently clear that even Cicero did not lack detractors, to whom he seemed swollen and puffed up, not compressed enough, exulting beyond measure, overflowing, and insufficiently antique. I would believe that our philosophers turned aside from that old and most ornate path of writing not through ignorance of eloquence, but that, after weighing the condition of the times, the subject, and the hearers, they changed the form and appearance of their speech. Let foreigners therefore cease to harass the Germans, who once described very lofty and most sacred matters under a plain and humble style.

What did that censor of the Latin language, Valla, seek? Pluto does not dare to speak Latin words. Jupiter deemed this man worthy of a part of heaven; but he fears him as censor of his own language.

Finally, to come down to the matter itself, reverend father, we add something to your Catalogue, beginning with the German Roman pontiffs, whom we do not think not excellent, nor much inferior to many others. Then we have joined the rest here and there, without order, just as they came to mind, asking pardon from the kindly reader for brevity, confusion, and an undigested, inelegant style.
