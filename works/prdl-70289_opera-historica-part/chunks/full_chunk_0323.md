And Possidonius says: “He lies who declares that he has read all of you, Augustine.” But in this work I have, not without deliberation, omitted certain treatises which are ascribed by many to blessed Augustine. Among these is the book *On Spirit and Soul*, in the thirty-seventh chapter of which Boethius is cited, who was born long after Augustine. Moreover the style itself does not savor of Augustine. Likewise the book which, under Augustine’s name, has now been printed *On True and False Penance* is falsely ascribed to him, since in the seventeenth chapter, near the end, Augustine himself is cited. The book *On the Conflict of Virtues and Vices*, which begins “The apostolic voice,” echoes the style of Pope Gregory and agrees with the older copies. Likewise the book *On the Ladder of Paradise*, whose beginning is “When on a certain day,” is in the old copies inscribed to blessed Bernard, whose spirit and phrasing it seems to express. Why should I labor over many examples? So many are the treatises and sermons falsely inscribed to blessed Augustine that a review of them would require its own volume. Nor is it mine to sit as judge and deprive of their titles works that belong to others. Let the prudent reader consider what he ought to do or hold in these matters.

The holy doctor Augustine died in the city of Hippo, when it was being besieged by the Vandals, in the year of the Lord 430, in the thirteenth indiction, in the seventy-sixth year of his age and the fortieth of his priesthood, according to Marianus Scotus, on the fifth day before the Kalends of September, in the third month of the city’s siege. While the attacks of the besieging Vandals were underway, at the very end of his days, he was replying to the books of Julian the Manichaean, and gloriously persevering in defense of the Christian faith. He made no testament, because he had nothing from which to make one, except the library of the church, which even in death he commended to the brothers to be guarded most diligently. Poor in possessions, rich in merits and learning, Augustine the bishop rested in the Lord; Possidonius, bishop of Calama, wrote his life.

Atticus, bishop of the city of Constantinople after blessed John Chrysostom, a man learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, was nevertheless blameworthy in this: that he allowed himself to be ordained in the place of a living bishop who had been unjustly expelled. From this many scandals arose in the Church of God. He wrote, as works not to be despised: *To the Queens, Daughters of the Emperors of Constantinople, to Be Guarded Most Diligently*, two books. And not a few other things.

He flourished under the rulers Honorius and Theodosius, in the year 420.

Paulinus the presbyter, disciple of Saint Augustine of Hippo, and afterward bishop of Nola in Campania, to whom many letters and various treatises of Augustine and Jerome are said to have been sent, a man most learned in the holy Scriptures and second to none in his time in secular letters, shone forth no less clearly in holiness than in learning. Concerning him blessed Pope Gregory writes in the third book of the *Dialogues* that he was of such great piety and compassion that, after he had spent all his possessions for the use of the poor and the liberation of captives, he even handed himself over into servitude for the son of a certain widow; from this at last, together with many captives, he was released on account of the merit of his life. He wrote many volumes, both in meter and in prose: *The Life of Saint Felix, Bishop of Nola*, one book; *To Severus Sulpicius*, four letters; *Letters to Various Persons*, one book; also *On the Sacraments*, one book; *On the Death of a Baptized Infant*, one book; *Panegyric of Theodosius*, one book; *Hymns in Various Meters*, one book; *On Contempt of the World*, one book; *On Penance*, one book; *On the Praise of Martyrs*, one book; *To a Fallen Virgin*, one book; *Letter-collection to Various Persons*, [unclear] books; *To a Fallen Virgin*.

He flourished in the time of the rulers Theodosius and Honorius, in the year 420. [unclear]

Niceas, bishop of the city of Romaciana, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, acute in talent, open and polished in speech, wrote little works for the edification of readers, of reading not to be despised. Among them the following are reported: *A Preparation for Candidates Who Are Being Made Ready for Baptism*, one book; *On the Errors of Paganism*, [unclear] books; *On the Faith of the One Majesty*, one book; *Against the Genealogy*, one book; also *On the Creed*, one book; *The Division of the Paschal Lamb*, one book; *To a Fallen Virgin*, one book; and certain other things.

He flourished in the time of the rulers Theodosius and Honorius, in the year 420.
