*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

A very celebrated work containing twenty-eight propositions, in which, vigorously and eloquently attacking the same number of their erroneous articles, he refuted them. He flourished in Germany, whence he came, under Frederick III, emperor, and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1490. And I believe that he is still alive.

Gabriel Biel, a German by nation, of the order of those who call themselves Brothers of the Common Life, or those living in common, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, excellently instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in talent, distinguished in life and conduct, who presided for many years over the Tübingen academy from the beginning of its foundation, where he instructed many disciples both in philosophy and in sacred letters, wrote certain little works of no contemptible authority, by which he made his name known. From these he regularly lectured in the aforesaid academy:

On the Canon of the Mass, one book, “Premised in the chamber.”
On the Passion of the Lord, he wrote one book.
Various sermons also, one book.

And certain other things. He is renowned today, an old man and advanced in age, in the highest esteem with the count of Württemberg; he is said to be writing certain things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

Georgius of Gemmingen, a German by nation, provost and canon of the distinguished church of Speyer, a learned nobleman, professor of both laws, a very celebrated interpreter, and excellently skilled in the other disciplines of the good arts, a distinguished philosopher and rhetorician; subtle in talent, eloquent in speech, excellent in verse and prose, wrote certain distinguished little works, by which, laudably exercising his talent, he also made his name known to posterity. Yet he conceals what he writes, so that he may flee the stamp of empty glory, or may correct what he has written with the file of a more mature examination. Of these, however, the following have become known to us:

On the Method of Duel, one book.
On Engines of War, one book.
On the Care of Soldiers’ Horses, one book.
Certain orations.

In addition he has produced many letters, both to me and to others; from these, how much he is capable of, both in learning and in eloquence and in experience of many things, will easily be perceived by him who reads them. He flourishes in manly age in the city of Speyer, outstanding in sacred letters and devout in conduct, an excellent cultivator of regular observance; he wrote, for the instruction of the simple, little works not to be despised, by which he made his name known. Of these [unclear] various things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Johannes Venetus, of the order of the Carthusians, of the house of St. Andrew the Apostle de Litore near Venice, a man studious and learned in the divine writings, excellent in talent, devout in life and conduct, and an excellent cultivator of regular observance, wrote for the instruction of the simple little works not to be despised, by which he made his name known. Of these there exists a distinguished work, which he entitled:

Know Thyself, one book, “Since nothing [is] more pleasant, nothing…”
On Patience and Humility, one book, “Brother most dear in Christ…”
The Mirror of the Dying, one book, “In the manner of good parents…”
The Crown of the Aged also, one book, “While the human mind…”
Various sermons, one book.
Several devout letters, one book.

And certain other things. He flourished at Venice under Frederick III, emperor, and Pope Sixtus IV, and completed the works written above in the year of the Lord 1480, indiction 13. And I am uncertain whether he still lives.

Johannes of Mylbach, a German by nation; a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and also not ignorant of secular philosophy, distinguished in talent, scholastic in discourse, who formerly taught theology gloriously for a long time in the Erfurt academy and instructed very many disciples in that faculty, wrote several distinguished little works, by which he magnificently exercised his talent and commended the knowledge of himself to posterity. From these he regularly lectured:

On the Epistles of Paul, one book.
Various sermons, one book.
In Praise of St. Jerome, one book.
Various questions.

And certain other things. He is renowned at Erfurt, an old and feeble man, a prebendary in the monastery of St. Peter, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.
