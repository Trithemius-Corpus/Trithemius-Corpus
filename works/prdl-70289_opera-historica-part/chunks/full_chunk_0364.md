*The Abbot of Spanheim* some little works in verse as well as in prose. Of these I have been able to find only a distinguished work:

On ecclesiastical computation, one book. “When with our brothers [unclear]...”
Also On Music, one book. Of the rest I have seen nothing.
He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1069.

Berthorius, abbot of the monastery of Cassino, of the Order of St. Benedict, a man zealous and learned in the Holy Scriptures, and nobly educated in the discipline of secular letters, an eminent philosopher and physician. Before his conversion he is said to have written many little works in his own faculty. But afterward changing his study, and having been made a physician of souls, he wrote for the edification of his brothers:

Elegant Sermons, one book.

He also published other various treatises, knowledge of which has not reached me.

Rogerius, a monk of the Order of St. Benedict, to whom Guimundus of Aversa, bishop, while still established in the monastery, is said to have written three books Against Berengar; a learned man, educated both in divine and in secular letters, described, for the edification of many, in a sufficiently lucid style:

The Life of Bruno, Bishop of Cologne, one book.

And certain other things. He flourished under Emperor Henry II, in the year 1040.

Algerus, from a scholastic a monk of Corbie in Saxony, German by nation, of the Order of St. Benedict, a man trained by constant study in the sacred writers, rich in reading of the ancients, and magnificently learned in secular books, cautious in intellect and sufficiently eloquent in speech, strong in verse and prose. He wrote in a very polished style a notable volume:

On the Sacrament of the Altar, two books. “Because the saints concerning the body...”
On Grace and Free Will, one book. “It must be known that free...”
And certain letters.

He is also said to have written many other things, which have not come into our hands.

Albertus, a monk of Metz, of the Order of St. Benedict, French by nation, a man skilled in the Holy Scriptures through continual exercise, and not ignorant of the arts of humanity, which we call liberal, in order to transmit his name to posterity, wrote certain little works of reading not to be despised. Of these there is said to be a short but useful work, containing the history of the events of his own time, which he dedicated:

To the Bishop of Metz, one book. And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Conrad the Younger, in the year of the Lord 1030.

Eckebertus, cleric of the church of Liege, German by nation, elegantly learned in the divine Scriptures and perfectly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters. He wrote some little works both in verse and in prose, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there survives a volume composed in elegant verse, which he entitled:

On Rustic Riddles, one book. “On the same matter, greater than others...”

And certain other things. He flourished in the times of Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Leo, Pope IX, formerly bishop of the city of the Leuci, which is called Toul, under the archbishop of Trier, sat in the chair of Peter after Damasus for seven years. Previously called Bruno, a learned and holy man. He wrote some distinguished little works, in which the grace of expression and sublimity of thought are deservedly commended. Of these, to Constantine the monk [unclear]:

Against the Errors of the Greeks, one book.
To the Patriarch of Constantinople, two books.
Letters to Various Persons, three books. To the bishops of Africa and Egypt.

He died under Henry the third king, the second emperor, in the year of the Lord 1054, in the seventh indiction, shining with many miracles.

Humbertus, a monk of Toul, of the Order of St. Benedict, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly learned in secular letters, lofty in intellect and eloquence, whom Pope Leo IX, because of his knowledge of letters, brought forward and ordained cardinal bishop. Sent by him to Constantinople to refute the heresies of the Greeks, by God’s aid he refuted and defeated Nicetas Pectoratus, a monk, in the presence of Emperor Constantine and Patriarch Michael, so that he anathematized his book which he had written against the Church of the Latins and with his own hand threw it into the fire to be burned. And whatever he said or did there in disputation, he wrote out lucidly under the name of the Roman and the Constantinopolitan:

In the Manner of Dialogues, one book. He also wrote certain other things.

He flourished under Emperor Henry II, in the year of the Lord 1050.
