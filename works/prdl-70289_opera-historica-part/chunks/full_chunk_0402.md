Nicholas IV, pope, of the Order of Friars Minor, formerly called Jerome, a Picene by nation, born from the city of Asculum, sat after Honorius IV in the chair of Peter for four years. He was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and not at all poorly instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, and no less venerable in life and goodness than in learning. He wrote a quite distinguished work:

On the Sentences, 4 books. And certain sermons.

He is also said to have published useful commentaries on several books of the Bible. He died at Rome, the Empire being vacant after the death of Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1291, indiction 4.

Boniface VIII, pope, a Campanian by nation, formerly called Benedict, sat after the resignation of Celestine V in the chair of Peter for eight years and nine months. He was indeed learned, but excessively arrogant and ambitious; the chroniclers have not kept silent about the means by which he acquired the pontificate. Since he was highly skilled in law, and saw that new varieties of cases were arising daily in the contentious forum which could not be determined by the Decretals, he composed, through three men most learned in law, new constitutions, which he wished to be called:

The Sixth Book of the Decretals, 1 book. “The sacrosanct Roman...”
Letters, several, to various persons, 1 book.

He died under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1303, indiction 1. Of him it had previously been prophesied: “He entered like a lion; he died like a dog,” etc.

William Mandagot, archdeacon of Nimes and afterward archbishop of Embrun, and finally cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, was a man most skilled in both laws, one of those who, by order of Pope Boniface VIII, published the Sixth Book of the Decretals, as is clear at the beginning of that same volume. He wrote in that faculty small works not to be despised, by which he consecrated his name to immortality. Of these I have seen only a notable work, which he wrote, while he was still holding the office of archdeacon in the Church of Nimes, to Berengar, succentor of the Church of Béziers, later bishop:

On the Election of Prelates, 1 book. “Since while elections in...”
An Exposition of the same, 1 book. “Since I am not the author of that...”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Berengar, succentor of the Church of Béziers and afterward bishop, and finally cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, to whom the aforesaid William Mandagot, while both were still established in minor offices, composed the book On the Elections of Prelates, was another of those who, at the command of Boniface VIII, composed the Sixth Book of the Decretals. A man most learned in the same juridical faculty, while he was still succentor he wrote to the same William, then archdeacon of Nimes, a notable work on the Summa of Hostiensis according to alphabetical order, which, since it is as it were an elucidation of that same Summa, he entitled:

The Eye, 1 book. On the Sentence of Excommunication, 1 book.

He is also said to have written many other works, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1301.

Richard of Siena, vice-chancellor of the Roman Church and afterward cardinal deacon of the title of Saint Eustachius, doctor of both laws, most celebrated in his time by the praise of all, was the third of those who by order of Pope Boniface VIII put together the Sixth Book of the Decretals; his name is expressly contained at the beginning of that same volume. He is said to have written some small works on law, but I do not remember that any of them have come into our hands. Let my studious reader seek them out. He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year 1300.
