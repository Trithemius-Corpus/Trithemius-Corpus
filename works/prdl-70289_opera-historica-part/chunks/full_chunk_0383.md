John Beleth, once a distinguished theologian of the University of Paris, learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in secular philosophy, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, not undistinguished among the doctors of his time, by writing and disputation brought his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. For he wrote the not unprofitable work:

A summa on the divine offices, one book. It begins: “In the primitive Church.”

Also various sermons, one book.

He is said besides to have written not a few other things, but they have not come to my notice.

Theoderic, abbot of the order of St. Benedict, German by nation, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, no less renowned for his manner of life than for his knowledge. He is reported to have composed several little works, of which I have seen only, addressed to certain abbots:

The life of Saint Hildegard, four books. It begins: “In the Roman commonwealth.”

And certain letters. He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Werner, otherwise Hirnerius, an illustrious and notable man, professor of civil law and a most distinguished interpreter, and an exceptional cultivator of humane letters, who was deservedly called the lamp of law, because he was the first to expound civil law with little glosses, and gave to others after him an unparalleled example of illuminating the law. And from this time professors of both laws have always flourished in abundance, and began to explain the volumes of his instruction down to the present day. He taught in both places, both at Rome and at Bologna, for a long time, where he had many distinguished pupils, who by their learning spread the fame and glory of their master. His glosses and splendid memorials on the laws are extant, very well known among professors of this field. He flourished in the time of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Peter, abbot of Cluny, of the order of St. Benedict, a man learned in the divine Scriptures through constant exercise, gentle in intellect, sweet and composed in speech, and quite fit for persuasion, devout and praiseworthy in manner of life, and a distinguished zealot for regular observance, making his name known to posterity by writing, composed certain devout little works for the benefit of readers. Of these the following are reported:

Accounts of many things, one book.

On the eremitical life, one book. It begins: “I have received the letters of love.”

Letters to various persons, one book.

He also published several other things, which have not come to my notice.

Peter Cantor of Paris, and afterward, as they report, bishop, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, gloriously presided for many years over the theological school at Paris and trained many distinguished pupils. He also wrote certain excellent [unclear], transmitted with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Among his little works there is found a certain notable and by no means contemptible summa, which he entitled:

The Abridged Word, one book. It begins: “The abridged word.”

Another book of distinctions, one book. It begins: “Abel is called the beginning.”

The Grammar of Theologians, one book. Various sermons.

On certain miracles, one book.

Also On the Sacraments, three books. It begins: “The people went about.”

He flourished in the time of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.
