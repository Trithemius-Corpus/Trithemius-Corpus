Hilderic, king of the Franks, after he had performed many brave deeds, died in the twenty-third completed year of his reign, but the twenty-sixth interrupted year, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 484, in the seventh Roman indiction.

*The Forty-Seventh.*

Clovis, son of King Hilderic by Basina, when he was twenty-one years of age, succeeded his father and reigned thirty years. Of these he spent fifteen as a pagan, like all the kings of the Franks before him, in vanity; and the remaining fifteen, reborn in the faith of Christ, he passed faithfully. He was a prince of outstanding beauty and distinguished by strength of spirit. Soon, at the beginning of his reign, he put to flight the son of the former Roman duke Aegidius, named Syagrius, who was ruling at Soissons, and added the city to the kingdom of the Franks.

Then, advancing, he took the city of Reims, whose church at that time was governed by Saint Remigius, a most learned bishop; and his wife, that is, the mother of Clotildis, with a stone tied to her neck, he cast into the water. But Clotildis was a faithful Christian woman, who earnestly admonished the king to believe in the Lord Jesus and to renounce the vain worship of idols. Although he despised this at the beginning, nevertheless, after fifteen years had passed in his reign, having obtained victory over the Alemanni, as we shall say in its place, he afterwards believed in Christ.

He took as wife Clotildis, daughter of Hilderic, king of the Burgundians, whom King Gundebald, his brother, had slain; and his wife, that is, the mother of Clotildis, with a stone tied to her neck, [unclear], and he divided the plunder among the soldiers according to law. Whatever in all Gaul belonged to the jurisdiction of the Romans, Clovis in a short time subjected wholly to the dominion of the Franks, as it remains even to the present day.

In the ninth year of his reign, King Clovis made war in Thuringia, because they were accustomed insolently to despise the tribute formerly imposed on them, and openly accused his mother Basina not only of adultery but also of theft. Coming therefore into the region of the Thuringians, he devastated everything with sword and fire, defeated the king, and compelled him to pay tribute.

In the eleventh year of his reign King Clovis, having gathered forces, led an army against Alaric, king of the western Goths. He met him ten miles from the city of Poitiers and killed him, together with many others; and he added all his treasures and the city itself to his kingdom.

In the fifteenth year of his reign, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 499, in the seventh Roman indiction, when the Alemanni again grew proud, and were unwilling either to be subject to the Franks or to pay tribute any longer, Clovis summoned a great army of his men and set in motion a strong expedition against the Alemanni for battle. At the beginning of the conflict the fighting was fierce and long uncertain, with Mars divided between both sides, and many fell on both sides.

At last, by the ordering of divine providence, while the Alemanni fought bravely, the Franks gradually began to give way; and when it already seemed near that they would flee, the king, suffused with tears, prayed silently within himself, saying: “Lord Jesus Christ, whom my wife proclaims to be the Son of God and able to grant all things asked to those who believe faithfully in you, if you give me victory today over these my enemies, I shall believe in you and shall have myself baptized in your name, and I shall be a faithful and devoted servant to your name as long as I live.”

And while, praying thus, with hands and eyes raised to heaven, he groaned, the help of Christ was suddenly present. At its presence the Alemanni were turned to flight; and those who before had fought immovably like a wall now suddenly fled, as the most fearful women are wont to do. The king of the Alemanni fell in that battle, together with many. But when the Franks did not cease to pursue the fleeing Alemanni more insistently, they cried out to King Clovis: “Do not pursue us any further, most victorious king, because we are yours; from now on we promise that we shall serve you forever.” Moved by these prayers, he forbade the pursuit, received the Alemanni in surrender, and returned to Gaul.

And when he had come to the palace, on the advice of Queen Clotildis he summoned to himself Remigius, the most holy bishop of the church of Reims. Having been more fully instructed by him in the Christian faith, in public confession, while all the nobles of the Franks looked on, he prepared himself with the greatest reverence and humility for the mystery of holy baptism. When he was now entering the baptistery to be washed, Saint Remigius, a most eloquent bishop, said to him: “Gently, Sicambrian, lower your neck.
