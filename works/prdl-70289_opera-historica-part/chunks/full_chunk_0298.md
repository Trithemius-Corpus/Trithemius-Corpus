And he is said to have composed many other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Marcus Aurelius, in the year of the Lord 220.

Gregory, bishop of Neocaesarea in Pontus, hearer and disciple of Origen, was a learned and holy man; he both wrote many things and, while living, shone with miracles. While still a young man, seeking the study of secular philosophy with his brother Athenodorus, he placed himself under the school of Origen, by whom he was gradually and almost imperceptibly converted to the faith of Christ together with his brother, and became a most learned and most holy man. Among his writings there survives:

A Panegyric to Origen, 1 book.
Interpretations on Ecclesiastes, 1 book.

And several letters. He flourished under the emperor Gordian, in the year of the Lord 240.

Cornelius, the twenty-second bishop of the city of Rome after Peter, a Roman by country, son of Castinus, was made pope after the glorious martyrdom of Fabian. He sat in the chair of Peter for two years, a learned and most holy man, until at last he too, crowned with martyrdom, passed, released from the flesh, to those above. He wrote several letters for the benefit of the Church. Of these I have found only the following:

To Fabius, bishop of Antioch.
On Synods, 1 letter. On Novatian, 1 letter.
To the same, on the Novatians, 1 book. On the Acts of the Synod, 1 letter.

He wrote several letters to Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, and others to other persons. He flourished under the tyrant emperor Decius, in the year of the Lord 255.

Cyprian, bishop of the church of Carthage in Africa, a learned and most holy man, taught rhetoric gloriously for many years, and was a magnificent teacher in every secular literature. Then, by the persuasion of the priest Caecilius, from whom he received his surname, he was converted to the faith of Christ and distributed all his property to the poor. Not long after, having been chosen priest, he was also ordained bishop. Since he was zealous in the divine Scriptures, he usefully published many works for the edification of the faithful. And he wrote the following volumes in the Latin language, namely:

On the Grace of God, to Donatus, 1 book. “You advise well, Donatus.”
Against Demetrianus, 1 book. “You bark at and against.”
1 book. “Many and great are they.”
On Piety and Almsgiving, 1 book. “You desired, Fortunatus.”
On the Praise of Martyrdom, 1 book. “Be zealous for what seems good.”
On Jealousy and Envy, 1 book. “About to speak of patience.”
On the Good of Patience, 1 book. “Even if among very many of you.”
On Mortality, 1 book. “The guardian of discipline, of hope.”
On Virginity, 1 book. “Peace, behold, most beloved.”
On the Lapsed, 1 book. “Cyprian to brother Caecilius.”
On the Sacrament of the Chalice, 1 book. “Since the Lord warns and [unclear].”
On the Unity of the Church, 1 book. “The evangelical precepts.”
On the Lord’s Prayer, 1 book. “That idols are not gods.”
On the Vanity of Idols, 1 book. “I had indeed promised you.”
To Antonianus on Novatus, 1 book. “The proof of the chapters.”
On the Two Mountains, 1 book. “We have received the first letters.”
To Quirinus, against the Jews, 1 book. “The Jews into offence.”
To Pope Cornelius, 8 letters. “We have learned, brother [unclear].”
On Gamblers, 1 book. Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.
To Julianus, his friend.

But he is said to have composed many other works, which have not come into our hands. The little book On the Twelve Abuses of the World, ascribed by many to Cyprian, does not have the scent of his style. At last he suffered under Valerian, on the same day on which Cornelius did at Rome, but not in the same year, namely in the year of the Lord 249, in the twelfth indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of October.
