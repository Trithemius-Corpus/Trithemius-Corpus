*Prologue of the Author.*

Often, at the insistence of many people, I have been asked that the book which I had formerly begun on ecclesiastical writers, for the sake of obtaining knowledge of books, I should at some time, when opportunity was found, bring to completion. I confess that I put it off for a long time, lest, if I presumed rashly to undertake so arduous a matter, I should provoke the hatred of rivals through my own levity. But now, when to the petition of learned friends there was added the counsel of those who by their arguments encouraged me to undertake this province, I could no longer resist their requests, especially since this little work of ours is supported by the patronage of such great men. For why should I fear the hatred of detractors, when I see that a refuge among the learned lies open to me? There will be, I do not doubt, some who will judge this labor of ours worthy of praise; there will also be some who, from a zeal of rivalry, will disparage it. What then? Are we therefore to abandon the exercises of good works, lest we provoke the hatred of rivals? Far from it. Everyone who does not imitate the wicked offends them; but [unclear]. And indeed to have displeased the wicked is praiseworthy, since they are accustomed to love nothing unless it is compared with their own morals.

Now therefore, importunate friends and exactors, receive the book composed on ecclesiastical writers, which with diligent prayers you so greatly desired to be brought to an end. I have done what I could, and have spent my ready strength on this work, although in many matters the capacity itself is less than the desire. For I, after the manner of those who see dimly, entered this forest under the guidance of the fathers who went before, and, gathering from their little works individual things suitable to my purpose, I carried them together into this bundle. I have also inserted into our catalogue many writers found by long investigation, with great toil and expense. Do not wonder, excellent reader, if in the annotations and titles of books my opinion does not always agree with yours. Who, in so manifold a variety of books, could enumerate the opinions of individuals, not to say harmonize them? You will therefore grant pardon if at times you find a volume marked in one way or another differently from what your opinion takes it to be. For it was not possible for me everywhere to express the diversities of individuals. For, as I have already said, I have followed the footsteps of the fathers, and where they disagreed I embraced what I believed to be truer. But if you find something else established by more solid truth, I shall gladly yield to your judgment.

It pleased me, moreover, to proceed by a new manner of writing, so that the reader himself might more easily find knowledge of the books from their beginnings. Beginning, therefore, from the disciples of the apostles, down to the last year of Emperor Frederick III, that is, the year of the Lord one thousand four hundred and ninety, I have drawn up a catalogue of illustrious men, in which I have recorded not the names of all, but only of those whom I found, their dignities, writings, and times, adding opposite the titles the beginnings of the volumes; but those things not seen I passed over for lack of space. The times in which certain men flourished I could not readily find; nevertheless I placed them, as seemed to me fitting, in an appropriate place. And in each defect let the knowledge of one who knows supply what is lacking, namely that he compute the times of individuals and restore those transposed to their proper places.

I confess that I have also deliberately passed over many whose writings are not easily found, nor their times. I also know at present many very learned men living, who are said to be publishing various little works; since I do not have certain knowledge about them, I prefer to pass by in silence rather than to think uncertain things. And, to use the words of Jerome, if any of those still living who write have been omitted by me in this catalogue, let them impute it not to me but to themselves, since they preferred to hide their writings rather than have them come into the light. But I believe that, content with their own writings and talent, they will not trouble themselves over the losses caused by my silence. For of those also whom I found, I wished to depict not their praises, but their books. For that study would require a rather lengthy volume.

Go now, therefore, into the city, work completed by our labor; go, and display to lovers of books the treasure which you contain. What do you fear? Why do you tremble? Why do you delay? Do not be afraid to come forth in public; do not fear the hatred of rivals, supported by the patronage of so many saints. Come then, book, of slender talent indeed, but displayer of great things. What is it that you dread, as if sluggish and useless? Go forth confidently; a thousand will praise you, while scarcely one disparages you. The love of God, for which you were written, may it, I pray, mercifully excuse you together with your author always and everywhere. Yours.
