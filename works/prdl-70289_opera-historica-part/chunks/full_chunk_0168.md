*Johannes Trithemius*

At Würzburg [it] was done in the sixty-third year from their passion, in the Roman indiction, as we said above, the fifth, with the rejoicing of the people.

*The city of Würzburg is given to Burghard, its first bishop.*

In this same year also, King Pippin, considering, at the appeal of Saint Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, the faith and holiness of the holy pontiff Burghard of Würzburg, through whose mediation he had obtained from the pope of the city of Rome the kingdom of the Franks, and because his bishopric, still new, had not been sufficiently provided for in temporal possessions, with the consent of his sons Charles, later surnamed the Great, and Carloman, and likewise of all the nobles of the whole kingdom of the Franks who were present that day at Soissons when he had been anointed and crowned king, gave by royal munificence the city of Würzburg, with all its revenues and advantages, to the cathedral church, that is, to the aforementioned Saint Burghard, bishop of Würzburg, and to his successors as their perpetual property. The new king Pippin also conferred upon the aforesaid church the castle of Karlsburg with the town called Karlstadt, and many other things. The same King Pippin also gave as a perpetual gift to the Church of Würzburg, to Saint Burghard its bishop and to his successors forever, the title, dignity, and emolument of the duchy of the eastern Franks; to them he gave the power of arranging judgment and judging according to the law and custom of the Franks, in all cases throughout the whole duchy.

In the second year of his reign King Pippin entered Saxony with a great army of the Franks; the Saxons resisted him with the utmost obstinacy. At last, however, they were defeated, and again pledged themselves to the king’s words, although they did not long keep faith. In this expedition Hildegarius, bishop of the Franks, who was also the brother of King Pippin, was killed by someone in the same year.

In the year of the Lord 755, in the eighth Roman indiction, which was the third year of King Pippin, Saint Boniface, formerly a monk and then archbishop of Mainz, suffered martyrdom in Frisia near Dokkum for the faith of Christ; his body was brought back to the monastery of Fulda and honorably buried there in the crypt.

In the sixth year of his reign Pippin, having again gathered a strong army of the Franks, moved into Saxony; battle was joined, and he killed many thousands of them, received the rest in surrender, and compelled them to promise that they would do everything whatsoever he commanded, and that each year they would give him 300 horses as tribute or as a gift of honor, in sign of most faithful subjection. When these matters had thus been arranged and confirmed according to the Saxon custom, King Pippin returned to his own lands.

In the year of the Lord 758, in the sixth Roman indiction, King Pippin of the Franks died on the eighth day before the Kalends of October, in the twenty-sixth year of his principate and the sixteenth of his kingship; his body was buried at Saint-Denis. He left a wife named Bertrada and two sons, Charles and Carloman.

Charles the Great, king of the Franks, reigned for 46 years. When he began to reign he was in the twenty-sixth year of his age. He undertook many wars and greatly enlarged the kingdom of the Franks. First he led an army against Hunold, who had invaded Aquitaine; Hunold at once fled from his face to Lupus, duke of Gascony. But Charles, sending an embassy to Lupus in Gascony, received the fugitive from him together with his wife; and Lupus himself, with all Gascony obeying him, returned.

In the year 770 King Charles held a general assembly of the princes of the kingdom in the city of Worms. Bertrada, the mother of the kings, staying with Carloman her younger son at Seltz, set out for Italy for the sake of peace; and, when the business for which she had gone had been completed, and the vow of prayer to the holy Apostles duly fulfilled, she returned to her own people. In the same year the body of Saint Othmar, once abbot of the monastery of Saint Gall, was found incorrupt and, having been taken up, was buried in the basilica of Saint Gall. Although he was abbot of that same monastery, of good and most religious conduct, he was driven out by the envy of certain perverse brothers and relegated to the aforesaid island of Stein, where he died in the year of the Lord 760, in the forty-third indiction. In the year of the Lord 761 King Carloman died; his wife fled with her children into Italy to Desiderius, king of the Lombards, from the face of King Charles, because she was conscious to herself of evil plots against him, together with others of her party who, on Carloman’s side, were always sowing discord. From then on Charles the Great alone held the kingdom of the Franks for 43 years.
