*12. Compendium, Book I.*

*The war of the Sicambri with the Gauls; the Gauls are overcome by the Sicambri.*

They declare [war] upon their enemies. Clodomir, supported by the aid of the Germans, confidently met the Gauls without delay. A very fierce battle was fought for people and kingdom; many fell on both sides. At last the Gauls turned their backs, and the Sicambri pursued them as far as the river, and laid low the greater part of the army by the edge of the sword. If they had either destroyed the bridge or seized it beforehand, as Clodomir the king, son of great Basanus, had once done, not even one of the Gauls could have escaped. After the Gauls had suffered this disaster, as long as Clodomir survived they no longer crossed the Meuse with an army, though they often committed raids in their usual fashion. King Clodomir, after many battles fought with the Romans, the Gauls, and the peoples farther up around the Rhine and the Moselle, died in the twentieth year of his reign, which was the one hundred twenty-first year before the birth of Christ.

*The Fourteenth.*

Merodacus, son of Clodomir, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-eight years. He was a high-minded, vigorous, and very bold prince; how warlike he was the Romans and the Gauls could perceive, for he often fought bravely with them, even with his own hand. For with the Saxons, Doringi, and Germans bringing auxiliaries, he led into Italy against the Romans an army of two hundred and twenty thousand fighting men; and, devastating everything round about, he came with his forces as far as Ravenna, and returned victorious, enriched with many spoils. On his return he also severely afflicted many colonies of the Romans, subjected them to very grave hardships, and made his name formidable to all.

Greatly stirred by these events, the Romans, having joined many nations to themselves, declared war on the Sicambri; the necessary preparations having been made, they assembled for battle. And lest the Saxons or Doringi bring aid to the Sicambri, the Romans had first induced the Goths and Slavs, by promised gifts, that at one and the same time these should attack the Saxons and they themselves the Sicambri. King Merodacus, therefore, being necessarily abandoned by the Saxons and Doringi, called upon the king of the northern island, whom today we call Danes; he came with one hundred thousand men as auxiliaries and increased the number of the Sicambri.

And so, in the twenty-second year of his reign, he learned that a great army of the Romans was coming against him. For Marius, prince of the Romans, sent the Roman legions with the Gauls, under Duke Theodomenus, beyond the Meuse. They boldly engaged the Sicambri, whom they themselves called Germans, and fought fiercely together; many thousands of men fell on both sides, and the Sicambri fled, having lost more than twenty thousand fighting men. After this, Merodacus, his army restored, became more powerful than the Gauls and Romans and afflicted them with much slaughter, as, following Hunibald, we have said more fully in the first volume of our histories or annals. But afterwards, when he had fought many battles with the Gauls and Romans and had enlarged the borders of his people by no small amount, King Merodacus died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, but in the ninety-third year before the birth of Christ.

*The Fifteenth.*

Cassander, the elder son of King Merodacus, succeeding his father, reigned twenty-one years. He was a very warlike man, in whose time the Gauls, with the aid of the Romans, more often crossed the river Meuse and invaded the borders of the Sicambri. Cassander, the aforesaid king of the Sicambri, gathered the armies into one and advanced to battle against them; when the clash had taken place, the fighting was fierce for a long time, and many fell on both sides. For all the days in which he ruled the Sicambri he was in conflict with the Gauls and Romans.

In these times Borbista, king of the Goths, with an innumerable multitude of fighting men, entered the borders of Saxony, devastating the region with fire and sword. Against him three very powerful kings, namely Arthari king of the Saxons, Hamer king of the Doringi, and Cassander, the aforesaid king of the Sicambri, gathered their armies into one and advanced to battle. When the clash had taken place, the fighting was fierce for a long time, and many fell on both sides. At last King Borbista turned his back, and the Germans obtained the victory. On another occasion after this, King Borbista again entered Saxony with a very great multitude of Goths, and cruelly devastated the region with fire and sword as far as the river Vistula; and, having seized many spoils of the Germans, he withdrew, nor did he presume to advance farther that day. For he knew the forces of the Germans to be unconquerable, although at the Romans’ urging he had often tested them.
