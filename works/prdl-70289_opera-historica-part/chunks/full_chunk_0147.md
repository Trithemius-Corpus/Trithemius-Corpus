*Johannes Trithemius*

In the sixth year of Genebald, duke of the Franks, Maximus the tyrant, at the third milestone from Aquileia, after being stripped of his royal garments by the emperors Valentinian and Theodosius, was beheaded and died. His son, named Victor, was killed not long afterward by Arbogast.

In the same year, after the death of Maximus became known to the Franks, they at once united and raised a strong army. With Genebald, Marcomer, and Sunno as leaders, they recovered all the land which they had lost while he was raging, whether in Germany or in Gaul, many thousands of Gauls, Romans, and Britons being slain as enemies. For the Britons, after the departure of all the nobility with Maximus, had become wholly weakened, and therefore were no longer to be feared by the Franks. The Gauls and Romans were being battered by mutual seditions; this circumstance gave the Franks the greatest hope of reclaiming the freedom of their homeland.

Therefore, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 392, the Franks, conspiring as one, scorned any longer to be subject to the tribute of the Romans. At that time Valentinian the Younger held the empire of the Romans; Theodosius had restored him after he had been driven from Italy by Maximus. Sending messengers to the Franks, he demanded the promised tribute. But when Valentinian’s envoys came to them, the Franks expelled him with disgrace in place of tribute, saying that they had been born free and were prepared even to die in defense of liberty. And at once, taking up arms, they moved against the Romans and Gauls; as has been said, after many of their adversaries had been cruelly slain, they recovered everything they had lost. In the twentieth year after they had paid tribute to the Romans, by fighting bravely with arms they reclaimed and recovered their former freedom.

For under the dukes Genebald, Marcomer, and Sunno, with a huge army, advancing from Lower Francia, which is around the mouths of the Rhine, near the sea, they soon occupied everything between the Meuse and the Rhine, after the Gallic inhabitants had either been killed or driven away in flight, and the spoils were sent back to their ancestral seats. Then, moving upward, they began to devastate the territory of the people of Cologne, striking great fear into the townsmen who were still with the Romans.

When the incursion of the Franks was heard of among the Treveri, who themselves had sided with Victor, the son of Maximus, namely that they were devastating the territory of Cologne, Nannenus and Quintinus, masters of the soldiers, to whom Maximus, when about to set out for Italy, had entrusted his son Victor and the defense of Gaul, drew together their forces and went down to aid the people of Cologne against the Franks, intending to fight if opportunity were given. But before they came to Cologne, the Franks, already laden with booty, had crossed the Rhine and returned to their own territory. They had nevertheless left behind not a few on the other side of the river, because they had conceived in their mind to return to them and proceed farther into Gaul. The first seats of the Franks, as has been said, had previously been within the three mouths of the Rhine, where Holland now is; from there on the eastern side of the Rhine toward Saxony and Frisia, where now is the inner part of Westphalia together with Frisia. To the west also beyond the Rhine as far as the Meuse they likewise once lived, where now are the people of Guelders and Jülich, and sometimes even farther, advancing to the Morini and the maritime regions.

Nannenus and Quintinus, coming with their army, attacked the remnants of the Franks that had remained in Gaul and joined a most savage battle, in which, though often defeated, they sometimes also emerged as victors. At last, however, the Franks were overcome near the Carbonarian Forest; some of them were killed, others were saved by flight.

When this had been done, it seemed useful to Quintinus that they should carry the whole army over into Francia and forestall the unprepared by an incursion. But such a plan did not please Nannenus, who did not doubt that the Franks would be stronger on their own soil.

Therefore, after Nannenus returned to Mainz, Quintinus crossed the Rhine around the fortress of [Musium?], brought the army over, and strove to bring war upon the Franks. But the Franks, not sluggish, received the Gauls manfully. In this battle many thousands of Gauls and Romans were slain by the Franks, with very few saved by flight. In that battle Heraclius, tribune of the Roman legion, fell; with him almost all who were in command of the soldiers perished by the sword and missiles of the Frankish people. For, except for a few to whom deep night and the dense hiding-place of the woods gave shelter, the army of Quintinus was destroyed, either by the sword of the Franks or by the marshes.

In the seventh year of Genebald, duke of the Franks, the emperor Valentinian died at the city of Vienne in Gaul, by the treachery and harshness of Arbogast, the master of the soldiers, by a noose. Eugenius, by the machination of Arbogast, was [unclear]. They resolved to invade Gaul, but before they could bring this to effect, Arbogast anticipated their plan: near Cologne Agrippina, having led his army across beyond the Rhine in the harsh winter, he devastated the Bacteri nearest the Rhine, the Chamanni, and others subject to the Franks.
