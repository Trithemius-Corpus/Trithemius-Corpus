*Eulogy of Johannes Trithemius, from André Thevet of Angoulême, royal cosmographer, Commentaries On Illustrious Men, book III, chapter LXXX.*

If any illustrious person has deserved to be honored and to take a place in this book of ours, that man ought chiefly, in this respect, to be esteemed who supplied and depicted for us the first designs and models of this work, and who was able in many places to aid us from his writings and memoirs. Therefore, in order to show and actually display liberality, and not to seem ungrateful, I have not wished to leave behind the natural portrait of Jean Trithemius, a person as learned and as diligent an investigator of all good sciences as any other of his time; I took it from a book printed in Germany, in the same form in which I present it to you here. He was accompanied by [purity?] of life and ardent zeal for the Christian religion, as one who, in a book on the same subject as this one, though not so complete in all its parts and native colors, drew out a brief collection of the learned men who wrote, and whose books came to his knowledge.

Now, since the life of a man, once known, can greatly stir our heart to bear affection toward him, and since at the simple report of virtue we are moved to love those whom we have never beheld with our eyes, we shall say who this Trithemius was, whence he came, and in what time he lived. The town of Trittenheim, on the Moselle in the diocese of Trier, contributed to him two graces not to be despised, namely his birth and the surname which he made so famous, in the year of the birth of our Savior 1460.

While still a child, burning with affection and love for letters, he resolved to seek farther off more solid nourishment to appease his hunger. For this purpose he journeyed through various provinces and studied in the most famous universities then flourishing in both human and sacred learning, so that in a short space of time, by continual labor, he reached the height and perfection of knowledge. For he was a subtle philosopher, an ingenious mathematician, a celebrated poet, an accomplished historian, a very eloquent orator, and a distinguished theologian, endowed moreover with many rare virtues and graces, both of body and of mind.

But since in that time, still not polished, religious men were held in great and good opinion by everyone, both for their singular devotion and for their learning, the monasteries, especially in the order of Saint Benedict, being like public schools where children were taught and where men professed to preach and explain the Scriptures more than is done at present, this Jean Trithemius, desiring to find a place very suitable and peaceful for giving himself to the contemplation of divine things, chose an abbey and monastery of the order of Saint Benedict, where he took the habit of a religious. And because he conducted himself very modestly, two years after his profession he was elected and appointed abbot in the monastery of Saint Martin in the town of Sponheim, diocese of Mainz, which office he administered with very great skill.

And although the charge of governing a number of religious men differing in manners and temperament is very burdensome, indeed as burdensome as a squadron of soldiers of diverse nations is to a good and valiant captain, and although such a charge requires that a man do almost nothing else than give it his eye, ear, and all his senses, especially considering that, although all human actions, both private and public, are subject to the tooth of slanderers, above all the life of a prelate is ordinarily picked over and exposed to the tongues of evil-speakers, whom it is impossible to satisfy, nevertheless this man, with great care, while laboring at the ordering and disposition of external affairs, did not fail to steal some hours, which he carefully employed now in reading and now in composing, as we can easily gather and infer from the works he published, which sufficiently commend to posterity his learning and admirable diligence.

Among his other books, the one which, following the example of Saint Jerome, Gennadius, and others, he entitled *On Ecclesiastical Writers* is worthy of perpetual memory and praise. In it, with very laborious research, he noted for the most part the authors, the time in which they lived, their profession, the titles of each book, and the prefaces or beginnings of them, by this labor of his providing the brush to many who later imitated him. As for the books of the *Polygraphia*, even those least well disposed toward him are compelled to admire the ingenuity of the style he used, in which he describes various [unclear].
