Sunno, king of the Franks, died in the twenty-eighth year of his reign, but in the year 213 of the Lord’s Nativity, in the sixth Roman indiction.

*The Thirtieth.*

Hilderic, the elder son of King Sunno, reigned after his father for forty years: a high-souled, prudent, bold, warlike man, who at all times stoutly defended his people no less by prudence than by arms, and powerfully extended the boundaries of the kingdom far and wide. For he was of such virtue and power that he was to be feared in no small degree not only by the Gauls but also by the Romans; to them the Frankish name was so hateful and odious that they were never accustomed to write or name that people otherwise than German.

The Franks gloried in their name; the Romans, laboring under hatred of them, begrudged them the word. Nor did the written histories of neighboring peoples, especially of the Romans and Gauls, by common consent grant the Frankish name to the people of Sicambria before the Franks, by arms and virtues, had subjected almost all Europe to their dominion. For previously, as we have said, the Romans, the Gauls, and the other hostile nations, as Hunibald testifies, moved by envy, named the Franks by no other word than Germans. For the author already mentioned says thus: Our ancestors had no less care, by common decree, for the name of their people, which they once received from their King Francus, than for the kingdom; for they thought it would be glorious for them no longer to exchange the Frankish name for another, once it had been assumed by their forefathers with the highest honor.

In these times there flourished under King Hilderic [unclear], also [unclear], [unclear] Hildegast, seer and philosopher, [unclear], most learned of all, who in his own time said, did, and wrote marvelous things. And he was of such esteem and authority among his own people that all always obeyed his command.

On the birthday of Hilderic, king of the Franks, standing in the temple of Jove at Neopagus, before a multitude of the Frankish people, Hildegast, philosopher, counselor, seer, father of kings, elder, most learned of all, because in his own time he said and did marvelous things, after prayers poured out before the image of that very god for the obtaining of a prophecy, suddenly began to rave, as is the custom of the frenzied, with a song of [unclear], and, with head tossed and hands bent back, he prophesied thus, not using verses composed according to the law of meters, but rhythms without measure, uttered very confusedly in prophetic frenzy; Hunibald made their interpretation thus:

Victory will come to the Franks from the western God,
whom the old Dardanian ancestors worshiped.
Whatever the Gaul possesses, whatever all Germany possesses,
all will yield to your right, warlike Sicamber.
When he gives you first a king from the name,
whom the leaders, then without a king, shall appoint,
then, with him victorious, the Frank shall reign without end.

He also added thus concerning the empire of the Romans:

Behold, the great God will give the brothel-camps to the Frank,
and the lion will break the eagle, crushed together with the serpent.

Hunibald explains this meaning concerning the standards of the Franks, which they used in battles, which we call the insignia of princes.

The kings of the Franks, he says, from that time when they began to have war with the Romans, on the advice of the pontiffs of their people, took these new arms or insignia: after throwing away the three frogs which they had once brought from Scythia on a white fleece, they depicted a lion with the forepart of its body on a fleece of blue color, its feet stretched out for seizing, its mouth open in the manner of one roaring, whose lower part of the body ended in the tail of a serpent; with this it seemed strongly to bind, press, and choke the neck of an eagle depicted beneath on a golden fleece with wings spread.

The Romans had the eagle as their sign when fighting. Desiring to suppress and annihilate their proud and arrogant power by strength and at the same time prudence, the Franks took the lion for one part and the serpent for the other. For by the lion the Franks mystically designated their strength, but by the serpent their wisdom and prudence.
