*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.
He also composed many other works, both in verse and in prose, which have not come down to us. He flourished under King Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1049.

Adam, abbot of Persema, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man learned in the divine Scriptures through constant exercise, rich in reading the ancients, and sufficiently trained in knowledge of secular letters, of very celebrated reputation in delivering homilies, transmitted his name with praise to posterity by composing various treatises. There are extant his devout and useful works:

*Sermons to the Brethren*, one book.
*On the Saints and Various Subjects*, one book.

He is also said to have explained certain treatises of sacred Scripture, in which he showed the abundant vein of his talent; but they have not come to my knowledge.

Anselm, bishop of Lucca, a man most learned in the divine writings and not meanly instructed in secular letters, the tireless co-worker of Pope Gregory VII and an outstanding defender of him against Guibert, who had been set under him by Henry, so that he defended all his deeds, sayings, or writings against Emperor Henry by the authority of law and of the Scriptures. He left very famous monuments of a great talent, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there are extant:

*Against Guibert the Antipope*, one book.
*On the Psalter*, also one book.
And *Several Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080.

Willeram, abbot of Mersburg, of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, practiced in verse and prose, after he had for some time devoted himself to the study of letters at the University of Paris, returned to his homeland and was made scholasticus of the church of Bamberg. At length, considering that the glory of the world passes away with time, and despising the world, he put on the monk in the monastery of Fulda for love of Christ; and finally, as his merits increased, he was consecrated abbot in the monastery of St. Peter near Mersburg, and by writing made his name known to posterity. Of his little works I have read only a volume, composed with fine variety in mixed prose and verse, on the marriage of Christ and the Church:

*On the Song of Songs*. It begins: “When I look upon the studies of the elders...”
*On Jeremiah the Prophet*, one book.
And *Several Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

Of the rest I have seen nothing. He flourished under Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1070.

Franco, scholasticus of the church of Liège, a German by nation, a man magnificently learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature, also outstanding as an astronomer and computist, and venerable no less for religion than for knowledge. He is said to have composed some treatises on the sacred Scriptures, which have not yet come to my knowledge. He wrote to Hermann II, archbishop of Cologne, a subtle and excellent work, a volume in mixed prose and verse, composed with fine variety, on the marriage of Christ and the Church:

[unclear], one book.
*On the Squaring of the Circle*, one book.
And many other works.

He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

*The Customs of Cluny*, one book.

Bernard, a monk of Corbie in Saxony, of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man nobly learned in the divine writings and very skilled in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech. He wrote to Hartwin, archbishop of Magdeburg, in a clear indeed but bitter discourse, a volume:

*Against Henry IV*, one book.
And other works which I have not seen.

He also wrote other things. He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Ivo, bishop of Chartres, formerly provost of St. Quentin of Beauvais, of the order of regular canons of the divine father Augustine, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and a most careful interpreter of the canons of the holy fathers and general councils, and revered no less for holiness than for knowledge, magnificently reformed his order, which had long since collapsed. After Burchard, bishop of Worms, he wrote from the canons of the saints a concise decretal collection, which jurists used before the time of Gratian, and which he entitled:
