Hildovinus, formerly abbot of the monastery of Lobbes, of the order of St Benedict, was first bishop of Liège; then, driven from there, he came to Hugh, king of Italy, to whom he was joined by a line of kinship. By him he was made bishop of Verona, and finally, after Lambert, was made archbishop of Milan. He ordained Ratherius the monk as bishop for the church of Verona. He was a man sufficiently learned in the divine Scriptures. While he was still abbot, at the request of the brothers, he wrote [unclear], one book; Sermons to the Brothers, one book; and certain other things. He flourished under Hugh, king of Italy, in the times of Conrad, emperor of the Romans and Germans, in the year of the Lord 920.

Ratherius, formerly a monk of the monastery of Lobbes, after Hildovinus bishop of Verona, was a man learned in the divine Scriptures and very skilled in the arts of humanity, which they call liberal; simple in spirit, gentle in disposition, and scholastic in speech. With Bishop Hildovinus he came to King Hugh in Italy, and after Hildovinus’s transfer to the archiepiscopate of the church of Milan, he was appointed bishop of the city of Verona. Not long afterward he was expelled from there by Hugh, for the reason that he had admitted Arnold, duke of Bavaria, Hugh’s enemy, into Verona; driven out, he was banished to Pavia. At length, having returned to his church, again [unclear]; I do not know whether they will be able to please readers no less than to profit them. Of these, the following are reported: On His First Exile, one book; another, concerning the second, entitled Phrenesis, one book; another, which is called Inefficax, one book; On the Body and Blood of the Lord, one book; Against the Anthropomorphites, one book; Agonisticon, that is, of Preliminaries, six books; also a Confession, one book; Life of St Ursmar of Lobbes, one book. He is also said to have written some other things, which have not come to my knowledge. And finally, as Sigebert the monk writes, he was made bishop of Liège, and was also driven from there. He flourished in the times of Henry, the first emperor of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 950.

Stephen, formerly a cleric of the church of Metz, bishop of Liège after Franco, was a man learned in the divine Scriptures, magnificently skilled in secular letters, and venerable no less for holiness than for knowledge. He wrote several little works of no contemptible usefulness, through which his name became known to posterity: To Hermann, archbishop of Cologne, Life of St Lambert, one book; To Rupert, bishop of Metz, one book; and certain other things. He also wrote the chant on the Holy Trinity from Alcuin, which we use on the octave of Pentecost; on the finding of St Stephen; and the nocturnal chant on St Lambert, bishop and martyr. All these, together with certain others, he composed in an authentic and sweet melody. He flourished in the times of Louis, son of Emperor Arnold, in the year of the Lord 910.

Radulphus of Flavigny, a monk of the order of St Benedict, was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, in his time, most learned in all letters of humanity; subtle in talent, profound in judgment, sweet, adorned, and eloquent in speech; acute and very distinguished in expounding sacred volumes. He wrote two great and distinguished volumes: On Leviticus, twenty books, “When at one time there was speech concerning Judah”; On the Epistles of Paul, one book; History of the Franks, one book; also a brief Chronicle. Besides these he published many other things, which had not come to my knowledge. There are some who write that he was a monk of the monastery of Fulda in Germany.

Smaragdus, abbot of the monastery of St Michael, of the order of our lord father Benedict, a man [unclear], acquired a reputation for his knowledge and conduct. For he wrote many distinguished little works. Of these I have found only the following: On the Rule of St Benedict, one book, “When the crowds of very many”; Diadema of Monks, one book, “This little book”; On the Whole Psalter, one book; On the Epistles and Gospels throughout the Year, two books; also Several Sermons, one book. But concerning his other little works I have nowhere been able to find anything. It is said, however, that he published many commentarial treatises on the divine Scriptures. But he expounded Donatus Major and explained many authors of secular letters.
