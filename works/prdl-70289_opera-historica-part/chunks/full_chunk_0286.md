*A reply-letter of the Reverend Father Lord Johannes de Trittenheim, Abbot of Sponheim, to the venerable and most learned man Father Albert Morderer, giving the reason why he placed many teachers of secular letters among the ecclesiastical writers.*

Brother Johannes de Trittenheim, abbot in Sponheim, of the order of Saint Benedict, sends greeting to Brother Albert Morderer, of the Observant Order of Minors, his friend. You say, most beloved Albert, that you wonder that I have placed teachers of secular letters, who wrote nothing ecclesiastical, among illustrious men and ecclesiastical writers, since Jerome, Gennadius, Isidore, Sigebert, and the remaining assessors of doctors are found not to have done this at all; you judge it unworthy that men intent on secular disciplines, who did no labor for the edification of the universal Church, should obtain a place among ecclesiastical writers. I do not wonder that you wonder; others wonder too. Therefore, by answering you I shall satisfy all who hold the same opinion.

And first, indeed, why others did not do what you wonder that I have done is no concern of mine; but why I have done what they did not, I shall briefly relate. Indeed, teachers of the good arts who remain in the Catholic faith, philosophers, orators, and poets, who have written anything useful and honorable in their faculty, I by no means judge unworthy of the catalogue of illustrious men, since I know that they adorn the Church by their studies. For I think that no one can be called sufficiently learned in the divine Scriptures if he is ignorant of the discipline of secular letters. But how shall the theologian learn it, if there is no one who has handed it down? Who will not know how much a Christian and faithful philosopher benefits the Church, who destroys the errors of the gentiles, lays bare arguments and fallacious syllogisms, and confounds the doctrines of unbelievers? Read the histories of the ancients, and you will find [unclear] establishing the faith by words. How often have orthodox philosophers destroyed the cunning of heretics by disputing, overcome the gentiles, and conquered schismatics? Why say many things? Every school of theologians now takes its beginning, for the student, from philosophy, so much so that one cannot be a theologian who has not first been a philosopher among them.

What shall I say of orators, without whose instruction antiquity used to judge a theologian unfruitful, since he could not persuade others of the good things he had learned? For the rhetorician unlocks the figures of the Scriptures, clarifies tropes, dissolves knots, shows colors, unravels doubtful matters, and makes clear what is in the shadows of words. When he assumes the office of an orator, he will easily destroy the allegations of heretics and convict their errors. Finally, who can worthily pursue the praises of good orators, if he carefully weighs how much power there is in persuasion? O if only all the theologians of our time had the coloring of orators, how much fruit they could produce in the Church of God by their exhortations. A theologian-orator is said to have the minds of his hearers in his power; by his persuasions they are easily moved to those things which are holy and just.

Nor do we cast aside Christian poets, whom we do not doubt come closer to our theory, who under the shadow of a thing done, or, that is, show us briefly what must be done or avoided. Nor are we ignorant how great an injury poets today suffer from certain undigested and crude theologians, who condemn all poets in one sentence, asserting that they are liars and foul-speakers, as though those who imitate the poems of the ancient poets could not be truthful and good. Far be it that a good and modest poet cannot exist, who in this art had the holy fathers Augustine, Jerome, Prudentius, Bede, and innumerable others as predecessors. But it is not my office, nor is this the time, to refute their sloth.

Therefore, to close the letter, I have judged that philosophers, orators, and cultivators of the other good arts who are faithful and Christian, and who have written something useful, should be counted among ecclesiastical writers for three reasons. The first is that I thought their little works could contribute not a little to the knowledge of the holy Scriptures. The second is that it seemed likely to me that they wrote some ecclesiastical things which escape my notice. The last is that, by an occasion given them through us, they may understand that henceforth, as they are to write ecclesiastically, they should adorn the place they have received with worthy labors. Do you therefore think that such great men, so useful to the Catholic Church, should be rejected? Far be it from us that we should inflict upon her that now rash injury, by which we might be thought provoked by zeal for rivalry, not by reason of piety. Finally, that proverb commonly tossed about belongs to us: Knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man.

These things, then, I wished to answer briefly in this book, so that you might know that what you previously wondered had been done was not done without cause. Farewell, remembering us before God in your prayers. From Sponheim, the fourth [unclear] of April. In the year of the Lord 1492.
