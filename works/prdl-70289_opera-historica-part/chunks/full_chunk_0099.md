*Compendium, Book I*

All these were descended from the ancient kings of the Franks: he was of King Anthenor; he was of King Clodius; he was of King Nicanor; he was of King Cloodomir; he was of King Basangot; he was of King Diocles; he was of King Helenus; he was of King Priam; he was of King Antenor; he was of King Marcomir, who led the Franks from Scythia into Germany. But Pippin the elder begot Charles Martel; and he begot Pippin the younger, mayor of the palace of the Franks, and king after Childeric. Thus from King Marcomir the first down to King Pippin, son of Charles Martel, mayor of the palace, father of Saint Gertrude, there are counted fifty-one generations over the course of one thousand fifty-nine years.

But King Lothar, in the forty-first year of his reign, which was the six hundred twenty-eighth year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the first Roman indiction, appointed Dagobert, his son by Queen Bertrude, king of Austrasia, subjecting to him Metz, Trier, Cologne, Liège, Tournai, Arras, Tongeren, Thérouanne, the Morini, and whatever lay between the western sea, the English sea, the northern sea, and also the Rhine. He very earnestly entrusted him to Saint Arnulf, bishop of Metz, and to Pippin, count of Namur and most noble duke of the Brabanters, to be instructed and directed. Dagobert established the seat of his kingdom, according to the custom of the old kings of Austrasia, in the aforesaid city of Metz, and governed the kingdom of the Franks well, both by himself and through dukes and counts.

In the following year the Saxons rose in rebellion and refused the customary tribute to the Franks. Therefore King Dagobert, having gathered the forces of his men, moved his army against the rebels and fought with Berthulf, duke of the Saxons, by whom he was gravely wounded in the first encounter. When his father Lothar, who was then in the Ardennes, heard this by a swift messenger’s report, he immediately crossed the Rhine at Neuss and hastened to his son’s aid. The battle was renewed; hard and fiercely it was fought on both sides. At last, however, the Saxons, after their duke Berthulf had been slain by the Franks, were defeated and sought safety in flight.

But King Lothar, pursuing the fugitives with the Franks, laid them low in a miserable slaughter, sparing no one, but killing with the sword all who exceeded the length of his sword. The people who remained throughout Saxony he brought back under subjection to the Franks, and imposed on them a tribute of five hundred oxen.

After this, in the six hundred and first year of the Lord’s Nativity, in the fourth Roman indiction, the aforesaid Lothar, king of the Franks, died in the forty-fourth year of his reign, leaving by his first wife, who was called Bertrude, a son named Dagobert, who succeeded to the kingdom. After his first wife had died he had taken a second, named Sighild, who bore Heribert.

*The Fifty-First.*

Dagobert, the elder son of King Lothar, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks in the year of the Lord’s Nativity noted above, reigned three years while his father was still living; thus the whole time during which he reigned was seventeen years.

Heribert, however, Dagobert’s brother by the same father but born of another mother, as has been said, received in the division of the kingdom Aquitaine, Toulouse, and the other cities contained between the Pyrenees and the Loire as the possession of his inheritance, on this condition, namely, that in the other kingdoms of the Franks he should claim no right for himself. He established the chief seat of his kingdom at Toulouse. In the fourth year after this, having seized an occasion for war, he received the Gascons into surrender.

King Dagobert constructed anew from the foundations the monastery of Saint Denis the martyr, which he enriched with many estates, goods, and possessions, though not without injury to certain churches, which he despoiled by force of many things, as if all things acquired for the services of Saint Denis, both by right and by wrong, were owed to him. He built the wholly most beautiful church of the same monastery, which was roofed with solid silver; into it he transferred from the village of Catulliacum the bodies of the aforesaid martyr and his companions and placed them in a golden shrine. He most beautifully adorned the walls of the church itself on the inside with tapestries very fittingly interwoven with pearls and large pearls, and gave the place to be inhabited by monks of our order.
