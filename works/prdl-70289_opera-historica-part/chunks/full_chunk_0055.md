These insignia, or arms, although they are improperly so called, all the kings of the Franks thereafter used down to Clovis the Christian, who is read to have adopted, in place of the lion, three lilies sent down from heaven, as will be said in its proper place.

At length, when the kingdom of the Franks was divided into two by the sons of Louis the First, the emblem of the lilies remained with the Gallic, or western, Franks; but the eastern or German kingdom of the Franks retained its own arms of the lion and the serpent’s tail. Since that kingdom has the Roman Empire annexed to itself, the earlier insignia having been brought into oblivion, it has been accustomed to use only the eagle, the mark of the empire, from that time of division down to the present day.

*Hildegast, teacher of the noble Franks in every science.*

The aforesaid Hildegast also, the wise man of the Franks, taught the sons of the nobles to sing in their mother tongue, with citharas and various other musical instruments, the deeds of their ancestors in songs; he advised the people to live by civil customs, to build houses, and to go about more cultivated in dress; and he ordered them to lay aside ferocity. For indeed the Franks until then cared little for cultivation or adornment, both in buildings and in clothing. Hildegast made all these things more cultivated, and most wisely instructed them to crush and subdue the pride of the Romans not only with arms, but also with possessions and manners. From that time the Franks began to build larger and more beautiful houses, divided into various chambers. For whatever they had built in earlier times was contemptible in its excessive simplicity and rustic, since, because of their frequent changes of settlement, they had been more accustomed to live in fields than in cities. Hence the Germans, taking up a proverb, whenever they saw something made or painted with ancient simplicity, were accustomed, praising antiquity in this way, to say: “That is a good old Frankish work.”

*The Franks had become more cultivated in manners and buildings.*

Hunibald records the remaining marvelous sayings, writings, or deeds of Hildegast. In the twenty-fourth year of Hilderic, king of the Franks, Alexander, son of Mammaea, Caesar Augustus of the Romans, is killed at Mainz, a city of Germany, in a military disturbance, in the thirteenth year of his reign.

But Hilderic, king of the Franks, the first of that name, after many brave and magnificent deeds, at length dies in the fortieth year of his reign, and in the two hundred fifty-third year of the Lord’s nativity, in the first Roman indiction.

*The Thirty-First.*

*Bartherus reigned 48 years.*

Bartherus, son of King Hilderic, succeeding his father, reigned eighteen years. In his time the Romans waged war with the Goths, who, crossing the river Ister, were devastating their cities and towns. The Romans killed thirty thousand of them under the command of Decius Caesar and his son. Many of the Romans also fell, among whom the very son of Decius Caesar [unclear]. But Decius Caesar, a most wicked persecutor of Christians, while fleeing, was drowned in the whirlpool of a certain marsh; his corpse could never be found by his men.

In the tenth year of King Bartherus, when the Romans under the wanton rule of Gallienus were sluggish in idleness, the Franks, thinking that a suitable time was at hand to avenge the insults committed against them, with the assistance of the Germans, Saxons, Bavarians, and Thuringians, gathered a great army of warriors; and, entering the borders of their enemies and devastating everything with fire and sword, they reached Ravenna in a hostile incursion, and, enriched with booty, returned to their own lands.

In the following year, which was the eleventh of King Bartherus, the Alemanni, having assembled their forces, led a strong army into Gaul; and, after laying waste the region with sword and fire, they returned to their own lands with much spoil and booty. In the same year they set an expedition in motion against the Romans in Italy, and inflicted many losses on them, avenging the manifold injuries which they had received from the same.

In the thirteenth year of King Bartherus, which was the two hundred sixty-sixth year of the Lord’s nativity, in the fourteenth Roman indiction, the Franks, with the aid of the Germans and Saxons, assembled an immense army; and, after crossing the Rhine and the Meuse, under the leaders Antharius and Luther they entered Gaul, laying waste everything in hostile fashion. Antharius the leader was the son of Bartherus, the aforesaid king of the Franks; and Luther was the son of Marbod, king in Saxony. Beginning from the region nearest to the Tungrians, and from there proceeding hostilely through the Bellovaci beyond the Seine, they reached as far as Spain; and, surrounding the noble city of Tarragona with a siege, they destroyed it utterly.
