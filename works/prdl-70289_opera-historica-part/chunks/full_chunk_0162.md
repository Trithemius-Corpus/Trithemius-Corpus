*Johannes Trithemius.*

He sent books of dialogues, in the year of the Lord’s nativity [unclear]. At that time there were kings of Bavaria, under the power of the kings of the Franks, and they paid them tribute every year.

In the year of the Lord 602, in the fifth Roman indiction, which was the sixteenth year of Lothar, Garibald, the aforesaid king of Bavaria, died; in his place the aforesaid king of the Franks, Lothar, appointed Tassilo king.

Bertrudis, wife of Lothar king of the Franks, bore Dagobert; after her death he married Sigehild, who bore Heribert. These will be discussed in their proper place. The father had entrusted Dagobert to Saint Arnulf, bishop of Metz, to be raised and instructed; at this time Arnulf was held in great esteem. This Arnulf, of the race of the Franks, was count of Namur; from count he was made bishop of Metz, and from bishop a monk and hermit, in the opinion of all most holy. The matter prompts me to trace his genealogy further back, because he came both from the blood of kings and because several of his descendants were raised to the kingship.

For in the year of the Lord 343, Clogio, king of the Franks, begot Marcomer the king and Dagobert the duke. Dagobert begot Genebald. Genebald begot Marcomer and Sunno. Sunno begot Diocles. Diocles begot Meroneus. Meroneus begot Leontius. Leontius begot Heribert. Heribert begot Priam. Priam begot Sigebert. Sigebert begot Nicanor. Nicanor begot Ansegis. Ansegis begot Arnulf, count of Namur in Austrasia, afterwards bishop of Metz and hermit, of whom we are speaking.

Arnulf begot Ansegis, count of Namur and mayor of the palace of the Franks, or count palatine. Ansegis begot Pippin, mayor of the palace and duke of Brabant, father of Saint Gertrude, whose wife Ida built the monastery of Nivelles in Brabant at the urging of Saint Amandus, or, as usage has it, in Brabant. This Pippin died in the year of the Lord 657, under Dagobert king of the Franks. Pippin begot Grimoald and Charles, surnamed the Strong, mayors of the palace. Charles begot Pippin, Carloman, and Grifo. Pippin, from count palatine or mayor of the palace of the Franks, was made king after the deposition of Hilderic, and begot Charles the Great; these will be discussed later.

In the year of the Lord 611, Dagobert, whom his father Lothar had already previously made king of Austrasia, declared war on Berthold, duke of the Saxons, because he refused to pay tribute to the Franks. Having gathered his forces and crossed the Rhine, he fought with him, and at first was defeated and seriously wounded. When his father Lothar, who was then in the Ardennes forest of the diocese of Cologne, heard this from a swift messenger reporting it, he hastened to his son’s aid. After he had crossed the Rhine at Novellus, battle was again joined. Berthold, or, as others hold, Berthulf, duke of the Saxons, was killed. The Saxons fled after their duke was slain. King Dagobert pursued them and killed all males who exceeded the length of his sword, and compelled the people to pay a tribute of 500 oxen.

After these things, in the year of the Lord 631, in the fourth Roman indiction, Lothar the king, son of Helperic and father of Dagobert, died in the forty-fourth year of his reign.

Dagobert, surnamed the Great, son of Lothar, reigned after his father’s death for thirty-three years. His mother’s name was Bertrudis. He had two sons, Sigebert and Clodoveus, or, as others write, Lodovegus, whom we today would rather call Ludovicus in German.

When Sigebert himself, a little infant of forty days, was baptized by the bishop, and during the prayer Amen was to be answered by those standing around, and all were silent because their thoughts were distracted, the infant himself answered Amen in a clear voice.

Heribert, Dagobert’s brother, who was reigning at Toulouse in Aquitaine, lifted him from the sacred font; this Heribert, that is, after his brother was now reigning, later died together with his son Hilperic, and the monarchy returned to Dagobert.

The Saxons, sending envoys, asked Dagobert to deign to remit their tribute to them, namely the enormous oxen which they were compelled to give every year to the kings of the Franks. The king consented for a time, on this condition: that the Saxons themselves should wage war against [unclear], which they did.

King Dagobert made his son, the said Sigebert, king of Austrasia, that is, of the people of Metz, Cologne, and Liege, placing the diadem upon him and commending him to Saint Cunibert, archbishop of Cologne, and to Adelgis, count palatine of Namur, so that he might be governed by their prudence.
