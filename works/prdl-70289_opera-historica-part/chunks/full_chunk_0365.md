*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

ANSELM, a monk of Reims, of the Order of St. Benedict, a Frenchman by nationality, a man having a mind trained in the divine scriptures, wrote certain small works of his own study, which have not yet come into my hands. And among other things he gathered into one volume the deeds of Pope Leo IX performed throughout Gaul in councils and assemblies, in order to make known with how great authority he held a synod at Reims, or in other cities; with how great industry and subtlety he examined ecclesiastical cases; with what apportionment he corrected sinners; and how divine power worked together with him, as was sufficiently clear in one case of the Synod of Reims, where, when the bishop of Freising acted contumaciously against apostolic authority, by God’s vengeance he suddenly became mute before the eyes of all. He wished this work to be entitled:

*The Itinerary of Pope Leo*, one book.

He flourished under Henry III, king, in the year of the Lord 1050.

LANFRANC, archbishop of the church of Canterbury in England, formerly abbot of the monastery of Caen, of the Order of St. Benedict, an Italian by nationality from the city of Pavia, a man most learned in the divine scriptures, and in secular letters, especially in dialectic, by the opinion of all the most learned of his time, famous no less for holiness than for knowledge, left outstanding monuments of his intellect. Of these I have found only the following, a distinguished work:

*On All the Epistles of Paul the Apostle*, four books. *Commentaries on the Psalter*, one book.
*Against Berengar*, one book, “Lanfrancus misericor.”
*The Deeds of Count William*, one book. Of the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1060.

PETER DAMIAN, formerly a monk of the holy father Benedict, bishop of Ostia, cardinal of the most holy Roman church, a man devoted to the divine scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, gentle and keen in intellect, pleasing and polished in speech, sufficiently capable in persuasion, shone forth as distinguished and famous no less for holiness than for learning. Following the example of the great bishop Gregory, through love of solitude and quiet he abandoned his episcopate, and after he had governed it strenuously for some years he returned to his monastery, thereafter spending his life with his brothers in tranquility of mind. But when he was reproved by the supreme pontiff with a charitable rebuke as to why, being a useful and suitable man, he had relinquished the episcopate through love of private salvation, he issued apologetic writings to the Roman pontiffs who succeeded one another, Nicholas II, Alexander II, and Gregory VII; in these, humbly excusing his action by authority, reason, and example, he appeased their minds.
