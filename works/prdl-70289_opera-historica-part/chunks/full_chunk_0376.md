*The Abbot of Sponheim*

On the praises of Saint Mary, one book.
Sermons on the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book.
And several other works, one book.

He flourished under Frederick, the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1160.

PETER the Dialectician of Paris, called Abelard, a Gaul by nation, a man most learned in secular philosophy and learned in the divine Scriptures, delighting in novelty of terms and attempting to examine things too lofty for himself, was overwhelmed by glory, fell into several errors, and, through the action of the holy abbot Bernard, was condemned with his depraved teachings at the council of Sens. From this time secular philosophy began to defile sacred theology with its useless curiosity. This Peter wrote some little works which, if he had not mixed errors into them, could have served the utility of posterity. Among them there exists a volume, shadowed over with dialectical obscurity and varied with many opinions, which he wished to entitle:

His Theology, one book. Know Thyself, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Conrad, the third emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

ADELPHUS, abbot of the order of Saint Benedict, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures by long practice and not ignorant of secular letters, an indefatigable cultivator of regular observance, so that by word and example he might show himself a good shepherd to the sheep of Christ entrusted to him. He is said to have forged several little works for their utility, in which a rich vein of talent commends ornate eloquence. Of these there survive elegant works:

Sermons to the brothers, one book. Against the Saracens, one book.

The other things which he is said to have composed have not come to my knowledge.

WILLIAM, abbot of Saint-Thierry and afterward, as is said, a monk of Clairvaux under the divine father Bernard of the Cistercian order, a man zealous and learned in the divine writings, distinguished also by his life and by the holiness of his conduct, and for that reason singularly beloved by him, published little works not to be despised in reading. Of these only a few have come to my knowledge. He began to write, but, overtaken by death, did not complete:

The Life of Saint Bernard the Abbot, one book. Incipit: “About to write a life for the service.”
The Mirror of Faith, one book. The Enigma of Faith, one book.
Sentences of Faith, one book. To Theophilus on the Nature of the Soul, one book.
On Contemplating God, one book. On the Nature and Dignity of Love, one book.
On the Sacrament of the Altar, one book. Meditations for Novices, one book.
Against Peter Abelard, one book. On the Song of Songs, unfinished, one book.
On Paul’s Epistle to the Romans, one book. And certain other works.

There are some who say that this William, while he was still abbot of Saint-Thierry, wrote that well-known letter to the Carthusians whose beginning is “To the brothers of Mont-Dieu, an eastern light,” which is ascribed by many to blessed Bernard. He flourished under Conrad, the third emperor, in the year of the Lord [1140?]. And before Bernard, when he had completed one book on his life, he died; Arnold, abbot of Bonneval, added four other books to it.

CONRAD, a monk of the monastery of Hirsau on the borders of the Swabians, of the order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, of the diocese of Speyer, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and very skilled in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, musician, and distinguished poet, composed many very excellent little works under the name of the Pilgrim. In these the ornate arrangement of the opinions and the cultivation of graceful speech declare their author inferior to none of the ancients. Of these only the following have come to my knowledge: a distinguished work of dialogues under the name of the pilgrim priest and Theodora, a virgin consecrated to God, which he entitled:

The Mirror of Virgins, one book. Incipit: “When I am about to confer with you, O Theo-.”
On the Gospels throughout the Year, one book. Incipit: “Because in the letter of the Old Testament.”
On the Life of the Spirit and the Fruit of Death, one book. Incipit: “Since every page of divine.”
The Matricularius, one book. Incipit: “Since it is manifest [unclear].”
On the Praises of Saint Benedict, in verse, one book. Incipit: “Like light the sun.”
Didascalon, one book. Incipit: “Because I see myself for a long [unclear].”
On Music and the Tones, one book. Incipit: “Music is, according to someone’s [unclear].”

Of the rest I have seen nothing. There was another declaimer of the order of preachers who, according to the custom of the moderns, composed sermons to the people under the name of Peregrinus, much inferior to our man in eloquence and also later in time. Whatever this Conrad wrote resounds with Ciceronian eloquence, which the other could not achieve. He flourished under Conrad, the third emperor, in the year of the Lord [1140?].

ARNOLD, abbot of Bonneval, of the Cistercian order, once of blessed Bernard the abbot
