As the hour was passing, Pope Vigilius handed the volume to the venerable Surgentius, the primicerius, to be placed in the archive, so that he might read it through at a time of leisure. All the learned men earnestly begged His Holiness to order the book to be read before them in its entirety. When he had ordered this to be done in the church of Saint Peter in Chains, a great crowd of religious men and noble laymen assembled; and, as Arator himself recited in clear words, both books were heard four times, since on one day only half a book was read, because of the continual repetitions which the listeners requested with manifold favor. Where now, in these times, is such zeal for letters? Where is love of saving knowledge? Our religious and nobles, clerics also and pontiffs, heap up not books but revenues; not letters, but avarice do they cultivate. Now Antimachus reads many poems, and, as individuals withdraw, scarcely a single Plato is found and remains. But of these matters we are now speaking out of season. Arator flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

Victor, bishop of the Cummenian church in Africa, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, was banished into exile in Egypt by the emperor Justinian and Eutychius, bishop of the city of Constantinople, for his defense of the Three Chapters. Summoned back again from there to Constantinople, when he resisted the emperor and the bishop, detractors of the same Chapters, he was sent to be guarded in a monastery of the same city; and, remaining under the same condemnation, as they say, he died. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote, from the beginning of the world down to the first year of Justin the Younger, a very useful history concerning deeds and ecclesiastical wars: *History*, one book. *For the Defense of the Three Chapters*, and certain other works. He flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

Gregory, the first pope from the monks of the order of Saint Benedict, a Roman by nation, sat after Pope Pelagius in the chair of Peter for thirteen years and six months. He was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and indeed most highly instructed in secular letters, prince of theologians, splendor of philosophers, and light of rhetoricians, whole and most holy in life and conduct. When he was explaining the divine Scriptures, the Holy Spirit several times appeared to him in visible form, and in visible teaching revealed to him the secrets of their mysteries.
