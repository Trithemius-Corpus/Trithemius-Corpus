*To the Bishop of Worms.*

For indeed no one, unless foolish, readily accuses what he knows has been approved by the judgment of greater men. The innocence of the sheep rests safely when the compassion of a good shepherd watches over it. Since, therefore, the examples of the ancients are incentives to virtue for us, it is fitting that we imitate the holy conduct of those whose praises we sing, so that, while we profit from them and others from us, the order of the ecclesiastical hierarchy may continue inviolate. And although I am too slight a man to trust that anyone might take pleasure in my little works, nevertheless I have resolved humbly to offer what I have into the Lord’s treasury, in which the widow’s two coins are rightly preferred to the riches of Croesus.

Accordingly, when recently, at the urging of friends, I had gathered together with much labor a book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, as the work itself bears witness, I resolved, lest I stray from the footsteps of the ancients, to submit this labor of mine to some most learned man, eminent in ecclesiastical dignity, by whose authority and goodwill it might pass through the teeth of the envious without being bitten. And when much thought had arisen in me as to whom I should [dedicate?] this same little lucubration so that it would be more welcome, many presented themselves to me as I hesitated, men concerning whose learning and goodwill no small promised hope was strong; among them you, most reverend father, appeared to me as preferable, more eminent, and more learned, you who surpass all in learning and exceed everyone in erudition. In you the nobility of a high lineage shines forth, episcopal authority stands eminent, integrity of conduct is seen, and every kind of knowledge of the liberal arts is praised. Could we find such a man anywhere besides you, in whom

*Dedicatory Letter.*

all these things come together at once? Among the Germans, most reverend father, I know no one equal to you, in whom such lineage, dignity, and erudition shine forth. I do not speak into your ear; let the Almighty be witness; nor do I aim to seem a flattering panegyrist, since all Germany knows you as a man most skilled in every respect. The learned honor you; nobles and princes respect you. You are to us what Plato is said to have been to Antimachus. Whoever is approved by your eloquence is deservedly judged learned. By public report, which does not lie, we have learned how deep a knowledge of sacred letters abounds in you, how penetrating an interpretation of law, and finally how great a charm, sweetness, and grace in speaking and delivering orations, so that we do not hesitate to compare you with the most learned men whom we have mentioned in the catalogue. The speeches you are said to have delivered bear most clearly witness to this; your letters bear witness; many epigrams bear witness. Indeed, the most illustrious fame of your name has become known with praise not only to our Germans but also to Italians and Frenchmen.

Hence it entered my mind that I should inscribe to you, so great a bishop, the work *On Ecclesiastical Writers* submitted to you, and dedicate it to your name, so that I might rest secure under the shadow of your pontifical eminence. But while I behold in you the splendor of flashing light, I am struck with awe, drawn back, and wholly shaken by fear of your great majesty, lest perhaps I, wholly inferior to you, a lofty and most learned father, in knowledge, merit, and dignity, should displease you in the very matter by which I presumed to please. For I know your many occupations; I am not unaware of your sacred studies, which I ought not to have troubled with my trifles.

*Dedicatory Letter.*

But your learning gives me courage, which your kindness and humanity render lovable to all, since it recoils from no one’s letters and judges no one unworthy of its address. Therefore I approach your Highness, although I am the lowest of men; I humbly submit the present little work to you; I appoint you its judge and defender. Accept, I beg you, most reverend and most learned father, this lucubration of ours, such as it is; examine it, judge it, defend it; and, if you judge it worthy, follow it with kindly favor. And you who turn over the writings of the ancients in continual reading, deign also, when there is leisure, to inspect their catalogue.

I, however, have not yet shared the present little work with anyone, nor shall I share it until I know that it has been approved by the testimony of your authority. Friends await it; printers are waiting for it; but I judge the opinion of your Fatherhood to be preferred to all. May your most merciful Fatherhood, I pray, pardon me for having presumed to bring my [trifles?] to ears occupied with difficult matters, because I have such confidence in your humanity, gentleness, and kindness that it is scarcely possible to doubt the benevolent reception of our catalogue. And I shall count it among the greatest delights if I learn that you take pleasure in my little lucubrations. Farewell, most reverend father; keep me, your most devoted servant, commended to you together with the volume sent. From Spanheim, on the sixth day before the Kalends of May, in the year of the Lord 1492.
