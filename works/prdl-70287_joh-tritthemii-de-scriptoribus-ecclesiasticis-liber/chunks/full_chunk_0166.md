Richard de Bury, bishop of Durham, chancellor of Edward III, king of the English, to whom there is extant a letter of Francis Petrarch asking about the island of Thule, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, a singular and very celebrated lover of books, founded, with much expense and labor, a notable library in the University of Oxford, which with incredible zeal he established there for the public benefit of students. He wrote a very fine treatise on the love of books and the establishment of the said library, which has now been printed, and which he entitled in Greek:

*Philobiblon*, one book.
Several letters, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Charles IV, in the year of the Lord 1350.

John Walsgram, an Englishman by nation, of the order of the brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly trained in secular philosophy, subtle and acute in intellect, adorned with the master's insignia in the Parisian school, was very celebrated in lecturing and disputation. He wrote certain distinguished volumes by which he made his name known. Of these there is extant the notable work:

On the *Sentences*, four books.

The other things which he is said to have composed have not yet come to my knowledge.

Various questions, one book.

John de Saxo, of the order of Friars Minor, learned in the divine Scriptures, and nobly trained both in canon law and in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, an excellent preacher in delivering sermons to the people, and very famous in his time, published certain little works of reading not to be despised, which are necessary for the instruction of simple priests. Of these I have found only:

A summa of law, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Charles IV and Pope Clement VI, in the year of the Lord 1359.

Thomas of Argentina, a German by nation, of the order of the hermit brothers of Saint Augustine, after Gregory of Rimini prior general, a man most learned in the holy Scriptures and an outstanding cultivator of secular philosophy, distinguished by his life and the honesty of his conduct, who wonderfully raised up his order by word and example, wrote several distinguished volumes on the Scriptures, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there is extant the most brilliant work:

On the *Sentences*, four books. “The abyss gave forth its voice.”
Constitutions of his order, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Charles IV, in the year of the Lord 1350.

*The Abbot of Sponheim*

John Brammart of Aachen, a German by nation, of the order of the brothers of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, prior provincial of Lower Germany, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and, according to the custom of the scholastics of this time, a very celebrated expounder of secular philosophy, distinguished in intellect and speech, an excellent teacher in delivering sermons to the people, wrote for the benefit of the faithful little works not to be despised; by exercising his talent in them, he transmitted the memory of his name to posterity. Of these there are extant:

On the *Sentences*, four books.
Various sermons, one book.

He is also said to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Henry of Erfurt, a German by nation, long exercised in the divine Scriptures and not moderately learned, and excellently trained in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, wrote for the edification of the Church several volumes, by which he made his name and reputation known to many. Of these there is reported a work of histories:

On memorable deeds, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Charles IV and Pope Clement VI, in the year of the Lord 1350.

John of Erfurt, a German by nation, a Thuringian by homeland, of the order of Friars Minor, an enlightened and skilled doctor in the divine Scriptures, nobly and excellently trained both in both laws and in secular philosophy, published several little works of great authority, in which he also entrusted the force of his most excellent talent to posterity. Of these I have seen only a great volume, which he entitled:

Table of law, one book. “As it is written,” two.
A summa on cases, one book. “Asked by the brothers that...”

Of the others I have seen nothing.
