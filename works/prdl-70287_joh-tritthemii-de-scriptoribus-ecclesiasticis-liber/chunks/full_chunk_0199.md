*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

Johannes Gritsch, a German by nation, from Basel as his native city, of the Order of Friars Minor, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, sufficiently instructed both in canon law and in secular philosophy, outstanding in ability and distinguished in eloquence, of very celebrated reputation in delivering sermons to the people, wrote works not to be despised for preachers of the word of God, by which he commended the memory of his name to posterity. Of these the following are reported:

Sermons for the Temporal Cycle, one book.
Sermons on the Saints, one book.
Sermons for Lent, one book. “When you fast,” etc., [unclear]
On the Passion of the Lord, one book.
And certain others.

He flourished, as his own people say, in the time of Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1430.

Johannes Nider, of the Order of Friars Preachers, a German by nation, a man devoted to and learned in the divine writings, sufficiently learned in scholastic philosophy, of excellent and distinguished ability in delivering sermons to the people, an inquisitor of heretical depravity and a very keen investigator of the little women whom the common people call witches, wrote in plain speech several volumes, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these the following are reported:

On Ants Morally Treated, five books. “Often traversing those things which”

Mirror of Perfection, one book.

Lenten Sermons, one book. “Be converted to,” etc. And many other things.

Nicolaus Siculus, abbot of [Monacen?], of the Order of Saint Benedict, sprung from the city of Catania, called de Tudeschis, first auditor of the causes of the lord Pope in the Rota, afterward archbishop of Palermo, and finally cardinal of the holy Roman Church, the most celebrated jurist of all, subtle and acute in intellect, ready and firm in counsel, clear and composed in speech, who deservedly is called the lamp of the law, wrote many distinguished volumes, by which he made his name known to posterity; of these the following are reported:

On the Decretum, unfinished, one book.
On the Decretals, five books. “Since every reason of the supreme”
On the Clementines, one book.
For the Council of Basel, one book. “Since the truth of words”
Judicial Process, one book. “The peace-making king of all”
One Hundred and Four Opinions, one book. “The contingency of the fact is such”
Various Disputations, one book.
And many other things.

He flourished in person at the Council of Basel, in the year of the Lord 1435. And on behalf of the authority of that council, before the princes at Frankfurt, he publicly read the aforementioned treatise and delivered an elegant oration there.

*The Abbot of Sponheim*

Ludovicus Pontanus of Rome, an Italian by nation, a very brilliant jurist, and no less devoted to the holy Scriptures than to secular philosophy, excellent in ability, concise and weighty in judgment, published volumes not to be despised for those wishing to make progress in the legal faculty, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of their number I have seen only the work entitled:

Notable Singulars, one book. “The word is understood entirely”

Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished in the time of the most excellent Emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1430.

Johannes Noblet, of the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Mary Ever-Virgin of Mount Carmel, a Frenchman by nation, of the Paris convent and school, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular disciplines, since before entering religion he had been, by art, practice, and profession, a salutary physician of sick bodies, wrote several distinguished little works, of which the following are reported:

On the Sentences, four books.
On the Canonical Epistles, seven books. “Noble in the gates is her husband”
Centiloquy of Riddles, one book.
Various Sermons, one book.
And many other things.

In addition, on many books of the Bible he wrote marvelous and elegant commentaries [the text breaks off].
