*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

... finally cardinal of the Roman Church, a man highly skilled in both laws, one of those who, by order of Pope Boniface VIII, issued the Sixth Book of the Decretals, as is clear at the beginning of that same volume. In that faculty he wrote works not to be despised, by which he preserved his name for immortality. Of these I have seen only the notable work which he wrote while he still held the office of archdeacon in the church of Nîmes, to Berengarius, succentor of the church of Béziers and afterwards bishop:

On the election of prelates, book 1. “Because while elections in ...”
Exposition of the same, book 1. “Since I am not the author of that ...”

Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Berengarius, succentor of the church of Béziers and afterwards bishop, and at last cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, to whom the aforesaid Guilhelmus Mandagoti, while both were still established in minor orders, composed the book On the Elections of Prelates, another of those who, at the command of Boniface VIII, compiled the Sixth Book of the Decretals, a man very learned in the same legal faculty, while he was still succentor, wrote to that same Guilhelmus, then archdeacon of Nîmes, a notable work on the Summa of Hostiensis according to alphabetical order; since it is, as it were, a clarifying glossary of that same Summa, he entitled it:

The Eye, book 1.
On the sentence of excommunication, book 1.

He is also said to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1309.

Richardus de Senis, vice-chancellor of the Roman Church, and afterwards [unclear], in the praise of all [unclear] very celebrated in his time, the third of those who compiled the Sixth Book of the Decretals by order of Pope Boniface VIII, whose name is expressly contained at the beginning of that same volume, is reported to have written several works in law; but I do not remember that any of them have come into our hands. Let the studious reader inquire after them.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

Dinus Mugelanus, professor of both laws and a notable expounder, subtle in intellect, and very ready and acute in disputing and resolving doubtful matters and questions of law, who by teaching and writing acquired great glory for himself among all the doctors of Italy, published many distinguished works in his faculty, among which the following are reported. By command of Lord Pope Boniface VIII he was the first after the archdeacon to write elegantly:

On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, book 1.
On the Code, 10 books.
On the Old Digest, 24 books.
On the New Digest, 12 books.
On the Infortiatum, 14 books.
Many Consilia, book 1.

Besides these he also published not a few other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

He also wrote On Interest; On Prescriptions; On Successions from Intestacy; On the Method of Arguing; Contrary Glosses and their Agreements.

He flourished under Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1300.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

Johannes Duns, a Scot by birth, of the Order of Friars Minor, once a hearer of Alexander of Hales the Englishman at Paris, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and very learned in Aristotelian philosophy, and so profound that his writings are penetrable [unclear], and for that reason also less commonly used, published certain instructive volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there are extant:

On the Sentences, 4 books. “Desiring ... Around the prologue ...”
Quodlibets, book 1. “All difficult matters ...”
On the Knowledge of God, book 1. “Because the highest ... for us ...”
Questions on the Metaphysics, book 1.
Sermons on the season, book 1. “There shall be signs,” etc. “In this ...”
Sermons on the saints, book 1.

He is said to have written commentaries on the Gospel and the Apostle, and to have composed various other treatises, which have not come to my knowledge.

He died in the time of Emperor Albert, in the year of the Lord 1398, in the sixth indiction, and was buried at Cologne among the Friars Minor.

Simon, monk of Hasnon [unclear], of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man most devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, so put into practice the counsel of our divine lawgiver Benedict, by which the monk is commanded to love the divine Scriptures, that nothing in this life was sweeter to him than to read the sacred writings and to turn over with diligent study the works of the holy fathers. He abridged:

The Moralia of Pope Gregory, 10 books.
On the Song of Songs he wrote sermons, book 1.
The vision of a certain convert, book 1.
Several sermons, book 1.
