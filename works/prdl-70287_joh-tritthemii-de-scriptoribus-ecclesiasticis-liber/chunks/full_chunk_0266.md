At the end he also makes mention of Martin Luther, the heresiarch. He flourished under Emperor Charles V, in the year of salvation 1531.

Bernardus Saracenus, a Venetian, a learned man, was the first of the more recent writers to write explanations and corrections on Plautus. He lived at the time of Georgius Valla.

[Bertrucius?] of Bologna composed a method for recognizing diseases, both particular and universal, in which he briefly explains the causes, signs, differences, and accidents of diseases.

Brigitta, a most holy widow, accustomed to divine converse and united to God by the perfection of inward charity, and also very famous for the prophetic spirit and for miracles, founded, at Christ’s command, a new religious order, called by her own name. She wrote on her prophecies and revelations a distinguished work, most edifying for all orders of the Church, and in every respect consonant with Catholic doctrines. She flourished in Sweden and at Rome around the year of the Lord [1350?].

Alisius of Piacenza, a canon regular, a learned and pious man, composed several excellent books, of which those set below report: two books. On Free Will against Luther, two books. And certain other works known to students.

Carolus Aretinus, formerly chancellor of Florence, learned in both languages, wrote certain comedies, from which Albertus de Eyb selected some sayings in his Poetic Pearl.

Carolus Verardus of Cesena, papal chamberlain, wrote the Baetican History, addressed to Raphael Riario, cardinal-deacon of St George, in several dialogues, about 20. He flourished while Innocent VIII presided, in the year of the Lord 1492.

Catharina of Siena, a nun of the Order of St Dominic, was the cause, through letters written to Gregory XI and by undertaking a journey, that he returned to Rome. Her writings, together with her prudence in speaking, can be compared with the great learning of theologians.

Christodulus, a monk, a man of most holy life, composed nine sermons against the Jews, which are extant in Greek at Rome in the Palatine library.

Christophorus Richerius Torichneus of Sens, royal chamberlain and secretary-chancellor of France, wrote five books on Turkish affairs, addressed to Francis, king of the French. He is still flourishing in the year 1546.

*Chronicle*

Chromatius, bishop of [Aquileia?], a man of rare integrity and, what is rare, as blessed Jerome testifies, a most learned bishop, composed a dissertation on the fifth and sixth chapters of Matthew, and an elegant declamation on the same fifth chapter.

Claudianus, priest of the church of Vienne, a man skilled in speaking and subtle in disputation, composed three books On the State, or On the Substance, of Souls, in which he pursues the intention of showing that there is something incorporeal besides God. Thus Gennadius.

Claudius of Auxerre, very skilled in scholastic theology, wrote a Summa and commentaries on the epistles of Paul.

Commodianus, while among secular writings he was also reading ours, received the occasion of faith; and so, having become a Christian and wishing to offer some gift of his studies to Christ, the author of his salvation, he wrote in a middling style, as if in verse, against the pagans. And because he had touched upon the writings of our fathers, he was better able to destroy their doctrines than to establish ours. Hence, when treating against them concerning the divine promises, he discoursed in a sufficiently mean and coarse sense, so to speak, striking amazement into them and despair into us. Following Tertullian, Lactantius, and Papias as authorities, he pursued sound moral doctrine, and especially love of voluntary poverty, very well, and impressed it upon students. He flourished before Gennadius.

Conradus Collin of the Order of Preachers, formerly prior and distinguished governor of the convent of Cologne, from his tender years imbibed no small learning in scholastic theology. He composed very lucid commentaries on the Prima Secundae of St Thomas Aquinas. He also wrote four books against Luther’s Epithalamium, and a seventh book on opinions or quodlibetal questions, and he would have published certain other works had not hostile death taken so great a man from us. He flourished in the year of restored salvation 1536. He died in the year 1536.

Conradus à Mure, first cantor and canon of the church of Zurich, a man of great knowledge, wrote the Fabularium, where he reviews the fables of the poets in alphabetical order, explaining chiefly the names of men and gods which are found among the poets, in a fairly large volume, which is preserved in manuscript in the library of the greater church at Zurich.

He also testifies that he wrote in verse on the natures of animals, emended the New Graecismus, and wrote several other things, which, as I found them at the end of the Fabularium, I shall append here.
