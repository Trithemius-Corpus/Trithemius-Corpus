*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

He was an instructor; in a simple and open style he composed some little works, the reading of which is not to be despised by devout and simpler brothers. Of these I have found only the following:

A lengthy work on the miracles and various visions that occurred in his time in Germany, which, in imitation of Saint Pope Gregory, he entitled: Dialogues on Various Matters, 12 books. Incipit: “Gather up the fragments, lest...”

On the Sunday Gospels, 2 books. Incipit: “The cluster of Cyprus, beloved...”

Sermons to Novices, 4 books. Incipit: “Our Lord and Savior Jes...”

Besides these he published several others, which have not come to my knowledge.

William, a monk of the monastery of the blessed martyr Dionysius at Paris, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a Frenchman by nation, a man skilled in sacred Scripture through constant study, and outstandingly learned in the disciplines of secular letters, distinguished in talent and eloquence, and an eminent knower of the times, transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity by writing and teaching various things. Of these there is extant:

A volume of Histories, 3 books.

And numerous letters to various persons, 4 books.

He is also reported to have published commentaries on sacred Scripture, which I have not yet seen.

James of Vitry, bishop of Acre, afterward cardinal of the holy Roman Church, bishop of Tusculum, a man learned in sacred Scripture and not ignorant of secular letters, notable in character and life, was of excellent talent in delivering sermons to the people. Gloriously preaching the cross against the Albigensian heretics, he marked many of the faithful against them by apostolic authority. He wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which he made his name known to posterity, namely:

Eastern History, 1 book.

Life of Mary of Oignies, 5 books.

Against the Saracens, 1 book.

On the Holy Women of Liège, 4 books.

Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.

Besides these he composed some others, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1220.

Bonaguida of Arezzo, a man most learned in both laws above all others in his time, of great authority among the old professors of this faculty, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, wrote for the benefit of students of law certain excellent volumes, by which he made himself useful to those present and known to those to come. Of these there is extant a noble summa, which he entitled:

Introductory Work for Advocates, 2 books. Incipit: “Since of the office of advocacy...”

Pearl of Law, 1 book. Incipit: “When after the invasion...”

Declarations of Various Cases. But he is said to have composed certain other works, which have not come to my knowledge.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

Peter de Vineis, a German by nation, scribe and chancellor of Emperor Frederick II, a learned and eloquent man, but chiefly reprehensible in this: that, adhering to that Frederick, by his favor he foolishly barked against the Roman Church. At length, when he had offended him in certain matters, he was duly rewarded by him and lost the light together with his eyes. He wrote certain little works of his talent, of which I have found only:

Letters in the Person of the Emperor, 6 books. Incipit: “The pontiffs have gathered...”

On Imperial Power, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the same Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230.

Anthony, called of Padua, but Spanish by nation, born from the city of Lisbon, of the Order of Minors, disciple of Blessed Francis, a man most learned in sacred Scripture, since he taught gloriously for so long a time and left after him distinguished disciples; he was of such sanctity that, living and dead, he shone with many miracles. He was an exceedingly excellent preacher, so much so that the supreme pontiff was accustomed to call him the ark of the covenant. And Saint Francis was accustomed to call him his bishop. Among the other little works of his talent he wrote very elegant and most ardent:

Sermons also on the Saints, 1 book. Incipit: “There shall be signs, he says...”

He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1230. And because of the abundance of miracles after his death, he was immediately enrolled in the catalogue of the saints by Pope Gregory IX, together with Saints Dominic and Elizabeth, landgravine of the Thuringians.

Jordan of the Order of Preachers, after Saint Dominic, as they say, master general, a German by nation, a man practiced in sacred Scripture and sufficiently learned, an outstanding preacher of the word of God, venerable no less for sanctity than for learning, by his example and honey-sweet doctrine instructed many in piety. He is said to have written some little works, the reading of which is judged not useless for declaimers and devout brothers.
