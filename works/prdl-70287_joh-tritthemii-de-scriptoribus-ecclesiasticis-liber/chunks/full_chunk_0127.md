*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

Several exhortatory letters, one book.
Sermons to the people, one book.
On the institution of the order, one book.
He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, at the same time also as Blessed Dominic, in the year of the Lord 1220.

Rupert of Bobbio, professor and expositor of both laws, most sagacious in interpretation, subtle in intellect, and brilliant in speech after the scholastic manner, who among the older professors of this science was held to be among its chief doctors, transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity by teaching and writing, as an example for others in learning and an aid to advancement. For he wrote a notable Summa, which he entitled:

On the order of trials, *Because paternal piety*, one book.
Questions and determinations, one book.

And not a few other works, which nevertheless have by no means come into our hands.

Bernard Dorna, a Provençal by nation, once a hearer and disciple of the very famous man Azo of Bologna, a most learned professor and expositor of both laws, moved by the example of his most learned teacher, devoted himself wholly to letters and wrote various little works, by which he dedicated his name to immortality. Of these there is extant a distinguished volume:

On the formulation of legal petitions, one book.

And other works which I have not seen.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

Accursius, an Italian by nation, born in the city of Florence, once a hearer and disciple of Azo, a man most skilled in both laws and not least learned in the other disciplines of secular letters, subtle in intellect and renowned in eloquence. How great his authority is at this time among professors of law, no one even moderately learned is unaware. He wrote many excellent little works, and was the first to gloss the whole civil law:

On the Old Digest. On the New Digest, twelve books.
On the Infortiatum, fourteen books. On the Code, twelve books.
On the Institutes, four books.

He also published not a few other works, which are very well known to students of law.

Johannes Fichardus relates in the Lives of the Jurists that he flourished in the year of the Lord 1226, at which time, as Accursius himself reports, he also composed glosses on the volume of the Authentics. He taught at Bologna, where, leaving behind his son Franciscus Accursius, himself also a jurist and professor, but far more obscure than his father, he died.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

Upertus of Bonacurso, an Italian by nation, a man skilled in both laws, not ignorant of secular philosophy, ready in intellect and clear in speech, who among the older expositors of law was of no obscure reputation, composed in that faculty certain excellent little works, of which I do not remember any having come into our hands. There is reported to be his:

On the preludes of cases, one book. *Since by imperial mandate*.
And certain works on trials.

We believe, however, that both those and his other works are not unknown to learned jurists.

John of Abbeville, archbishop of Besançon, cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, a Frenchman by nation, a man most learned in the divine scriptures, a very famous interpreter of those same sacred letters, who in the Parisian studium taught theology excellently for many years, and was most outstanding in delivering sermons to the people. For that reason especially, from a Parisian master he was made archbishop of Besançon. He wrote very useful and distinguished works:

Sermons on the temporal cycle, two books.
Sermons on the saints, three books.

He also published many other works, which have not come to my knowledge. He wrote the said sermons for the cycle of the year: first, to be sure, briefly expounding the short Gospel and apostolic readings; afterward, however, he published other sermons, both on Sundays and on feast days, so lengthy, introducing so many testimonies of the scriptures, that they could scarcely be committed to memory. Yet, with new ones now coming after them, they have in part fallen into little esteem.

Raymond of Penyafort, a Catalan or Provençal by nation, third master general of the Order of Preachers, a man learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, ready in intellect, distinguished in knowledge of canon law, made his name immortal by writing various works, and bound the Church to himself by his little works as a perpetual debtor, if I may so speak. For at the command of Pope Gregory IX he compiled:

The Decretals of law, five books. *The peace-making king, with pious* [unclear].
A Summa on cases, six books. *Since, as Jerome says* [unclear].
