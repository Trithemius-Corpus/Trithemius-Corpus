*Ecclesiastical [Writers], Addition II.*

The *Poetic Elucidarius*, not the former one, but an entirely different work, and so enlarged and polished that a studious youth could lack nothing among those matters that pertain both to the understanding of poets and to oratory.

Romulus Amasaeus, a man learned in both languages with few equals and endowed with a distinguished intellect, translated Xenophon’s seven books on the expedition of Cyrus the Younger.

I hear that Pausanias has been translated by him, but has not yet been published.

He flourished in the year of Christ 1543.

Rusticus the deacon, a man, as is clear from the books which he wrote, well practiced in divine letters, composed an excellent book against the Acephali.

He defends the definition of faith of the Synod of Chalcedon, through a dialogue between an orthodox man and a heretic.

Salmonius Macrinus of Iuliodunum, a Gaul by nation, by profession a distinguished poet, and chamberlain of King Francis of Valois, excelling greatly in keenness of intellect, composed and sported with many splendid poems, among which these are counted:

Six books of Hymns.

Three books of selected hymns by the same.

One book of Poems.

Four books of Poems or Odes.

He flourished, as has been said, under Francis of Valois, King of the French.

Santes Pagninus of Lucca, of the Order of Preachers, apostolic preacher, a man learned in Hebrew beyond measure, published most complete Institutions of the holy language, divided into four books. But this is the index of the other works which he published:

An Isagoge to the sacred writings, one book.

An Isagoge to the mystical senses of Sacred Scripture, eighteen books.

An abridgment of the Hebrew Institutions.

A new translation of both Testaments from the Hebrew and Greek truth, almost word for word.

Added to these is a book on the interpretations of Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek names.

A Treasury of the Holy Language, or Hebrew Lexicon.

He wrote Hebrew Meditations on Psalm 50.

He flourished under Clement VII, Supreme Pontiff.

Saxo Grammaticus the [Zealander?], a man wholly devoted to writing histories, wrote sixteen books of Danish History to Andreas, bishop of Denmark, three hundred years ago, with such elegance of diction and such variety of deeds performed that they may seem able to vie with all antiquity.

*In the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers.*

Sebastianus Aquilanus, a follower of Galen, a man most expert in medicine, composed for Lodovico de Gonzaga, bishop of Mantua, a book full of learning on the French disease; all physicians have borrowed from this author the treatments of it which they use in our time.

By the same, a question on sanguine fever.

Phoebadius, bishop of Agen in Gaul, a man not moderately versed in sacred and humane letters, published against the Arians a book excellent indeed in my opinion. They say that there are other little works of his, which I have not yet read.

He flourished in the time of St. Jerome.

Sophronius, a very learned man, while still a boy composed the Praises of Bethlehem, and recently an outstanding book on the overthrow of Serapis.

He also translated into Greek, in elegant speech, my little works On Virginity to Eustochium and the Life of Hilarion the monk; also the Psalter and the Prophets, which we translated from Hebrew into Latin. These are the words of St. Jerome.

The same man rendered from Latin into Greek St. Jerome’s Lives of Ecclesiastical Writers.

By the same, a paraphrase on Aristotle’s books On the Soul.

Likewise on the little book On Memory, the following [unclear].

Stephanus Doletus of Orléans, a Gaul by nation, wrote not a few works within a few years, for the instruction of the Latin language, in a most correct style.

Among these, the following are known to me:

On Naval Matters, to Badius, one book.

A Dialogue on Ciceronian Imitation, for Christophe de Longueil, against D. Erasmus.

Two Orations against Toulouse.

Two books of Letters.

Two books of Poems.

One book of Letters of friends to Dolet himself.

Other Poems, four books.

On the Deeds of Francis of Valois, King of the French, two books.

Two volumes of Commentaries on the Latin Language.

A Formula of the More Illustrious Latin Expressions.
