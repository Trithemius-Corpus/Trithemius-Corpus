*Ecclesiastical [Writers], Addition II.*

Aegidius Maserius of Paris, a man by no means moderately versed in the liberal arts, wrote commentaries on the poems of Valerius Flaccus Setinus Balbus, the *Argonautica*, in 8 books.

Aelius Antonius Nebrissensis, Spanish by nation, highly learned, made himself famous in his age by certain excellent books, of which these are extant:

*On Cosmography.*

A lexicon of civil law, against certain notable errors of Accursius.

Commentaries on the satires of Aulus Persius.

Exegeses on some passages of sacred Scripture.

Expositions on the Paschal work of Coelius Sedulius.

*On Grammar.*

Aeneas the Christian Platonist wrote a golden dialogue on the immortality of the soul and on the resurrection of bodies, which is entitled *Theophrastus*. It was translated from Greek into the Latin language by Ambrosius Camaldulensis. He flourished around the times of the emperors Zeno and Anastasius.

Agapetus, deacon of the great and most holy Church of God, wrote in Greek 72 paraenetic sentences to Justinian Caesar, which the Greeks call the royal schedule; for it discusses the office of a king. He died about the year of salvation 538.

Agathius Guidacerius, royal professor of sacred theology at Paris, wrote a *Mirror*, whose subject will be learned from the author’s own letter, which is approximately as follows:

Although, being bound by the public lectures on the holy Scriptures in their original languages, I have not yet completed the commentaries which I had undertaken to write on the holy tongue, nevertheless I wished at present to publish at least its first precepts. Wherefore, behold, from the most approved Hebrew teachers, Moses Kimhi, *Pethah Debarai*, the *Michlol*, and others, in whose writings I think I have done enough to write Latin commentaries, since I was born of Christian parents and from tender childhood, as they say, was instructed in Latin and Greek, and not in Hebrew, we now give this first little book, on letters, on points, on the quantity of syllables, on accents, and finally on the perfect method of pronunciation, in which all the beauty and sweetness of this heavenly language consists. We shall soon give, with Christ’s help, if I understand that these things are pleasing to you, a second book on the eight parts of speech; and thereafter we shall proceed to work out and publish those matters which are most copiously handed down in the very learned David Kimhi’s *Michlol*, for the practice of this art’s *dikduk* (that is, subtle consideration; for thus the Hebrews call that which we call grammar).

Agnellus the bishop wrote a letter to Arminius on the principle of faith, in which he chiefly refutes the Arian heresy by the consubstantial and coeternal nature of God the Father and the Son. Its beginning is as follows: Brother Martin demanded that letters be sent to your Charity, etc.

Alanus Porretanus, a learned man, wrote rules, or maxims, of heavenly law, that is, theological axioms, with interpretation.

Alardus Aemstelredamus, a Hollander by nation, corrected all the works of Rodolphus Agricola and illustrated them with learned scholia.

The same man wrote an epitome on Rodolphus Agricola’s book *On Dialectical Invention*.

He also described Ecclesiastes according to all the passages of Rodolphus Agricola.

There are also extant certain other small works of the same man, namely *Sacred Similitudes*, and a paraphrase of the letter of Hippocrates of Cos to Damagetus, which he rendered paraphrastically from Greek into Latin.

Many epigrams, letters, prefaces, annotations, and little commentaries on various other authors by him also survive, not yet known to us through printing.

His too is the translation from German into Latin of a little book on the miracles performed through the venerable Eucharist at Brussels in Belgium.

Albertus Crantz, a remarkable lover of more refined literature, described Sweden, Denmark, and Saxony.

Albertus de Ferrarijs of Piacenza wrote on the canonical hours. Likewise on the celebration of Masses, on the first chapter of the third book of the Decretals, which begins *Presbyter*.

Albertus Florentinus under Nicholas V wrote excellent books on architecture, as Aeneas Sylvius testifies in the *Description of Europe*, chapter 58.

Albertus Pius, count and prince of Carpi, and a zealous imitator of the Ciceronian style, wrote a paraenetic letter, in the form of a book, to Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam.

Albertus Pighius, of Kampen by homeland, German by nation, wrote on the discovery of the equinoxes and solstices, to Dom Franciscus Molinis, abbot of Saint Maximin, secretary and councillor of the king of the Franks, etc.

Likewise on the method of celebrating Easter and on the restoration of the ecclesiastical calendar, to Leo X, supreme pontiff.

A defense of astrology by the same Pighius, and a certain book against prognosticators, has been published.
