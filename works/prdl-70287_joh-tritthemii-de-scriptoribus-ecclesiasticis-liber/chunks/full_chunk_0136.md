At the command of Pope Urban IV he composed the office for the feast of the Body of Christ. He commented on all the books of Aristotle and Porphyry. He also published many other works, both on divine scriptures and on secular philosophy, which have by no means come into our hands.

He died under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1274, in the second indiction. And he was canonized by Pope John XXII.

Thomas of Cantimpre, a Brabanter by nation, of the Order of Friars Preachers, a certain disciple, as they say, of Albert the Great, a devout man, excellently learned both in divine scriptures and in secular letters, ready in talent, scholastic in eloquence, is said to have written certain distinguished volumes, by which he brought his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there is extant a work on mystical bees, which he entitled The Universal Good, one book. It begins: To the reverend in Christ...

On the nature of things, one book.

The life of Saint Christina, one book.

And other works which I have not seen.

He flourished under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1270. And there are those who write that he had knowledge of the Greek language and translated the books of Aristotle that are now in use in the schools.

Alfonso, king of Castile and Leon, a philosopher and astronomer most excellent among all men of his time, subtle in talent and distinguished in speech, an outstanding lover and cultivator of letters, after a long vacancy of the Roman Empire, had been elected king of the Romans against Richard, brother of the king of England; and since he was a good and prudent man, when Richard died in the fifteenth year of his election, with a view to peace he voluntarily yielded before Pope Gregory X and freely renounced every right that he had had in his election. He wrote very useful and elegant works:

*Abbot of Spanheim*

Astronomical tables, one book.

Letters to various persons, one book.

He flourished in the time of the vacant Empire, in the year of the Lord 1270.

Peter, of the Order of Friars Preachers, a Gaul by nation, and provincial prior in France of the same religious order, a man learned in divine scriptures by long study and not ignorant of secular philosophy, acute in talent and ready in speech, was apt in delivering sermons to the people. Hence, for the use of readers, he composed, through the cycle of the year, sermons not to be despised:

Sermons for the season, one book.

Sermons also on the saints, one book.

But he is said also to have written some other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

Peter of Tarentaise, of the Order of Preachers, and, after Gregory X, canonically consecrated pope under the name Innocent V, sat in the supreme pontificate for only five months. Certainly he was a man most learned in divine scriptures and nobly instructed in Aristotelian philosophy, who taught theology excellently for many years and acquired great praise for his learning. He wrote many distinguished little works, by which he made his name known both to those then present and to those who would come afterward. Of these there are extant:

On the Sentences, one book. It begins: Do you know the order...

On all the epistles of Paul, one book. It begins: To inquire into the principles of things...

On the Psalms also, one book.

John of Ascoli, from Ascoli, of the Order of Friars Minor, minister general after Saint Bonaventure, a man learned in divine scriptures and an outstanding declaimer of sermons, was sent to Constantinople by Pope Gregory X. By his exhortations he brought the Greeks back to obedience to the Roman Church, and persuaded them to come with him to the Council of Lyons. He wrote, for the edification of the faithful, works not to be despised:

Sermons for the season, one book.

Sermons on the saints, one book.

Several letters to various persons, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished while the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1170.
