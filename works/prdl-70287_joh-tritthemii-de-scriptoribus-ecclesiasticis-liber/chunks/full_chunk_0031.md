*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

He is said, moreover, to have written some other works, which have not come to my notice. He flourished in the times of the emperor Philip, in the year of the Lord 250.

Malchion, presbyter of the church of Antioch, a learned and very eloquent man, since indeed he taught rhetoric in that same city for many years with the greatest success, published some distinguished little works, which in his own time seemed greatly useful for the defense of the Christian faith against heretics. Of these there survives an excellent work of dialogues:

Against Paul of Samosata, one book.

To Pope Dionysius, from the synod, one epistle.

He flourished under the rulers Claudius and Aurelian, in the year of the Lord 270.

Victorinus, bishop of [Pictavium?], a man very learned in the divine Scriptures, furnished with Greek and Latin eloquence, wrote many distinguished little works, in which he magnificently exercised his talent. He was an eminent preacher of the divine word, and at last is read to have been crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ. Of his writings the following are reported:

On Genesis, one book. On Habakkuk, one book.
On Exodus, one book. On Ecclesiastes, one book.
On Leviticus, one book. On the Song of Songs, one book.
On the prophet Isaiah, one book. On the Apocalypse, one book.
On Ezekiel, one book. Against All Heresies, one book.

And many others. He flourished in the times of the rulers Valerian and Gallienus, in the year of the Lord 270.

Pamphilus, presbyter of Eusebius of Caesarea, gathered so many books at Caesarea out of love for the Scriptures that in all the world at that time no library was more celebrated. Indeed he copied out a large part of Origen’s books with his own hand and placed them in that same Caesarean library. From him Eusebius obtained the surname Pamphili. But before Eusebius began to write, he wrote little works of authority not to be despised, of which I have found only:

Against the Mathematicians, one book.

An Apology for Origen, one book.

He is also reported to have written certain letters full of faith and learning. At last, after blessed studies and many labors for the church of God, he was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ at Caesarea in Palestine, under the persecution of Maximian, who ruled around the years of the Lord 240.

Anatolius of Alexandria, bishop of Laodicea in Syria, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures and second to none in secular letters in his own time, an eminent rhetorician, astronomer, and geometer, left many distinguished little works of his talent; yet very few of them have come to our notice. There survives his notable volume, entitled:

On Easter, one book. On Arithmetic Instruction, ten books.

He flourished under the rulers Probus and Carus, in the year 285.

Three sermons by the same author, On Death, in Greek, are preserved in Italy, namely at Rome, as some will have it.

Archelaus, bishop of Mesopotamia in Syria, a learned man and a keen disputant, wrote some little works in the Syriac language, which among the Latins are rarely found. At the end of [Persia?] he disputed vigorously against Mani the heresiarch, and confounded all his arguments, although he could not call him back from error. He wrote that same disputation:

Against Mani, one book.

Other works, however, are unknown to us. He flourished under the rulers Aurelian and Probus, in the year of the Lord 280.

Lucian, presbyter of the church of Antioch and afterwards bishop of Nicomedia, a learned and eloquent man, was of excellent talent in delivering homilies to the people, and venerable no less for his life than for his knowledge of the Scriptures. Under the persecution of Maximian he was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ, and was buried at Helenopolis in Bithynia. He wrote, among other things:

On Our Faith, one book.

And various other treatises, sermons, homilies, and letters. He flourished under the emperor Maximian, in the year of the Lord 240.

Pierius, presbyter of the church of Alexandria, a man of such learning and eloquence that he was called the younger Origen, was most excellent in delivering homilies to the people. Under Theonas, bishop of the Alexandrians, shining most brightly in life and doctrine, he was an eminent cultivator of voluntary poverty for the love of Christ, and an admirable teacher of the people. He wrote countless treatises, which, however, have not come to our notice. There survives his very long treatise, delivered to the people on the vigil of Easter:

On the prophet Hosea, [unclear].
