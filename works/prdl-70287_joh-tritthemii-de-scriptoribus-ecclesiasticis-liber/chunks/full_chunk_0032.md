Phileas, bishop of the city of [unclear] in Egypt, a noble and very learned man, distinguished no less for his character than for his knowledge of the Scriptures, after many labors and various treatises published for the benefit of the faithful, at last under the emperor Maximian was made a martyr for the faith of Christ and flew to the heavenly ones. Of his writings there is extant a disputation held

Against an Unjust Judge, one book. On the Praise of Martyrs, one book.

Other things that he wrote are unknown to us, although many are reported by the fathers. He flourished under the emperor Maximian, in the year of the Lord 240.

Arnobius the priest, an eminent philosopher and rhetorician, while he was still a pagan teaching rhetoric, was called by divine mercy and began to profess himself a Christian. And when, approaching them, he sought to be baptized, they did not believe him, fearing that a man swollen with worldly eloquence was trying to mock the sacraments of the faith; and therefore they postponed their decision. But Arnobius, seeing that no trust at all was being placed in him, as proof of his sincerity wrote and offered to the bishops an excellent volume:

Against the Nations, seven books.

Also On the Psalter, one book.

On the Teaching of Rhetoric, one book.

He also published many other things, which have come to my knowledge. He taught rhetoric in Africa under the emperor Diocletian. He had many excellent and noble pupils, who became very learned men. He therefore flourished around the year of our Lord Jesus Christ 500.

He also wrote brief annotations on some passages of the evangelists.

John Climacus, which in Latin means Scholasticus, abbot of Mount Sinai, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, excellent in talent, gentle and humble, in life and conduct devout and most holy: when he was still sixteen years old, he left the world with its pleasures, and very earnestly sought, and obtained, to be made a monk in the said monastery. Then, after nineteen years, when he had already learned, according to the rule of monks, to fight and conquer as a solitary, with the permission of his abbot he sought a desert place, in which for many years he lived alone with God in all holiness. Finally, when his most holy life had become known to all, by the unanimous consent of all he was elected abbot, and, though reluctant, was recalled to the monastery. He wrote to John, abbot of Raythu, a very celebrated work, and one very suitable for the instruction of monks, containing thirty sermons, which he entitled

On the Thirty Steps, one book.
