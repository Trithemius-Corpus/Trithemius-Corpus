Maximus, bishop of Constantinople, an Alexandrian by nation, a distinguished philosopher and theologian, in whose praise Gregory Nazianzen published a book, having suffered many injuries from the Arians [unclear], and at last driven from his bishopric, wrote much against them. Of these there survives an excellent work addressed to Gratian:

Against the Arians, one book. The rest are unknown to me. He flourished in Greece under the emperor Gratian, in the year of the Lord 380.

Ambrose, a priest, an Alexandrian by nation, formerly a hearer and disciple of Didymus the Blind, learned the divine Scriptures magnificently while he expounded them, and composed various explanations of them, which at that time were held in great esteem among the Greeks. Of these the following are current among us:

Commentaries on Job, one book. Against Apollinaris of Laodicea, one book.

He flourished under the emperors Theodosius and Arcadius, in the year of the Lord [400?].

Petronius, bishop of Bologna, a monk in manner of life, very learned and eloquent in the divine Scriptures, and revered no less for holiness than for knowledge, because he shone with many miracles in life and after death, as is read in his history, wrote, among other works:

On the Life of the Holy Fathers, one book. On the Ordination of a Bishop, one book.

And other works unknown to us. He died under Theodosius and Valentinian, in the year of the Lord 383, in the thirteenth indiction.

Pachomius (Gennadius calls him Pachumius), abbot and a most magnificent founder of the monks of his time, founder of many monasteries, and a man of most holy manner of life, shining with many miracles in life and after death, since he was a learned man and instructed in the divine Scriptures, wrote many letters for the edification of the brothers; of these the following are reported:

To Cornelius the abbot, a letter. To the provosts of the monasteries, a letter. To Syrus the abbot, a letter. To the brothers working outside, a letter.

Also a Rule for Monks, one book.

He flourished under the emperors Valentinian and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 390.

Aurelius Clemens Prudentius, a Christian poet, a man most learned in secular literature and sufficiently skilled in the divine Scriptures, excellent in meter and prose, having been held for fifty-seven years by the loves of the world, read and expounded many little works of the poets, and from his own vein wrote many profound poems.

But at last, in old age, having rejected the vanity of the world, he betook himself to the sacred Scriptures, and in verse and prose expounded many hidden things of both Testaments. He wrote many little works; among these, with the measure of the meter supplied, I found the following:

Against Symmachus the Idolater, one book. “Paul, herald of God.”

On Compunction, one book. “The faithful old man first.”

On the Divinity, one book. “God is three supreme things.”

Against the Patripassians, one book. “There are very many, but.”

Against the Sabellians, one book. “Yield, profaner.”

Against the Jews, one book. “These things, if Jewish, if in.”

Against the Homuncionites, one book. “There are some who [unclear] with Jewish [unclear].”

Against the Phantastics, one book. “There is reward for the work [unclear].”

On the Origin of Sins, one book.

On the Hexaemeron, six books.

Of Various Hymns, two books. “The winged messenger of day [announces] the light.”

Praises of Saint Lawrence, two books. “Blessed martyr, look forth.” “Romanus, brave man of Christ.”

Praises of Romanus against the Gentiles, one book. “The ancient [unclear] of shrines.”

Of Histories, one book. “Eve was a dove.”

On the Nature of the Soul, one book. “Here a doubter meets.”

On the Resurrection of the Flesh, one book. “I ask for my own in Christ.”

On the Martyrs, one book. “Written things are in heaven.”

He also wrote not a few letters.

The Hamartigenia, one book. “Whatever errs [that] souls.”

On the Hope of Pardon, or his prayer, one book. “Brothers, Ephebus the digger.”

Against the Marcionites, one book. “O [unclear] parent.”

Peristephanon, one book. “Whither does [unclear] hurl you.”

Enchiridion of the New and Old Testament, which some also call [Diptychaion?].
