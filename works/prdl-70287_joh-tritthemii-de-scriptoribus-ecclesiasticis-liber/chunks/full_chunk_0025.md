Theodotion, by origin an Ephesian, once a pupil of Tatian the philosopher while he was still faithful, a man certainly most learned from every source, and the third interpreter of the divine Scriptures, besides the translation of the Scriptures which he made Latin from the Hebrew, also wrote certain other little works, which however have not yet come into our hands. He flourished in the times of the emperor Commodus, in the year of the Lord 180.

Rhodon, an Asian by race, once a hearer and pupil of Tatian, most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, is said to have written many works in the Greek language, copies of which are rarely found among our people. Of these, however, the more important are reported to be those which we have set down:

Against Marcion, 3 books. To Callition, 1 book.
Against the Cataphrygians, 1 book. On the Hexameron, 6 books.

He flourished under the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 200.

Clement, presbyter of the church of Alexandria, hearer and pupil of Pantaenus the Stoic, once a Christian philosopher, who after him held the ecclesiastical school at Alexandria for many years, was a devout, learned, and most eloquent man. He wrote many distinguished volumes, of which these are reported:

Stromata, that is, Varieties, 1 book. On Disputation, 1 book.
Against the Nations, [unclear] books. On the Salvation of a Rich Man, 1 book.
The Pedagogue, 3 books. On Detraction, 1 book.
On Easter, [unclear] book. Against Those Judaizing, [unclear] books.
On Fasting, 1 book. On Ecclesiastical Canons, 1 book.

He also published many letters and various treatises, whose titles we do not know. He flourished in the times of the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 200.

Melciades, whom Rhodon remembered in his work which he composed against the Cataphrygians, Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, the mad prophets, a man most learned in secular letters and exercised by constant study in the divine Scriptures, wrote not a few little works for the edification of the faithful, of which the following are reported:

Against the Nations, 1 book. Against the Jews, 1 book.
Against the Nations, 1 book. An Apologetic for Our Faith, 1 book.

Apollonius, a certain learned and most eloquent man, distinguished no less in life than in eloquence, a most valiant overthrower of heretical depravity, composed many things for our faith, and often refuted the madness of Montanus in words and writings, as is clear to those reading that notable volume which he is said to have composed with the greatest charms of eloquence:

Against Montanus, 1 book.

But he published several other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 180.

Serapion, formerly an anchorite and the eighth bishop of the church of Antioch, a man of keen intellect, very studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, venerable no less for his life than for his knowledge, is reported to have fashioned several little works for the [unclear] of believers, in which he shows himself a learned man and a most vigorous defender of the Christian faith. Of their number there are:

To Domnon Judaizing, one book.
To Carinus on Montanus, 1 book.
On the Gospel under the Name of Peter, 1 book.
Of brief but numerous letters, one book.

He was ordained bishop of the aforesaid city under Commodus, ruler of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 193, in the first indiction.

Apollonius, a senator of the city of Rome, a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, venerable no less for sanctity than for knowledge, when before the senate he had been denounced as a Christian and refused to deny Christ, received sentence of death, since an old law among them prevailed that Christians were not to be released without denial, once they had been brought before their judgment. Among other things he wrote:

An Apologetic for Our Faith, 1 book.

When he had recited this in order before the senate, he was beheaded. He flourished at Rome under the rulers Commodus and Severus, in the year of the Lord 190.

Tertullian, presbyter, by origin a Carthaginian of the African province, his father being a centurion, a proconsular man, a man of keen and vehement intellect, most learned both in the divine and in secular writings, since indeed at Carthage he gloriously taught rhetoric for many years, wrote almost countless little works in the Latin language, in which he strongly crushed the errors of the heretics. The most blessed martyr Cyprian held this man’s writings in such veneration that he let no day pass without reading them; for he was accustomed to say to his secretary, “Give me the master,” meaning Tertullian.
