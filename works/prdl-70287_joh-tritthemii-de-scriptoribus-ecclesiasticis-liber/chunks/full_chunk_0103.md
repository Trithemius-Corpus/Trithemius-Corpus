Alphanus, archbishop of Salerno, formerly a monk of the monastery of Cassino, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man nobly learned in the divine Scriptures and perfectly instructed in secular letters, a philosopher and theologian of very celebrated reputation, wrote certain little works of profound meaning, by which, displaying his talent, he also made his name known to posterity. Of these there survive:
*On the Union of the Word of God and Man*, one book.
*On the Four Humors of the Body*, one book.
*On the Union of the Soul*, one book.
He also composed other works in every branch of literature, which have by no means come down to us.

Campanus, a Lombard by nation, a philosopher and astronomer, in the opinion of all the most celebrated man of his time, sharp in intellect, scholastic in speech, not ignorant also of the divine Scriptures, an outstanding calculator and computist, published not a few little works of his talent, the reading of which will not be contemptible to rulers of churches. Of these I have read only the following: a great and distinguished work,
*On the Ecclesiastical Computus*, one book. “One of...”
*On the Construction of the Quadrant*, one book. “You ought to know that the circle...”
Also a *Calendar*, one book.
He also composed many other works in astronomy, which have not come into our hands. He says that he flourished under King Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Helpericus, monk of the monastery of St. Gall, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a German by nation, a man much trained in the divine Scriptures and very learned in secular letters, an outstanding philosopher, poet, and astronomer, subtle in intellect and composed in speech, wrote several little works both in meter and in prose; of these I was able to find only the distinguished work,
*On the Ecclesiastical Computus*, one book. “With our brothers ado...”
Also *On Music*, one book. I have seen nothing of the rest.
He flourished under Emperor Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1069.

Berthorius, abbot of the monastery of Cassino, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man devoted to and learned in the holy Scriptures, and nobly instructed in the discipline of secular letters, an outstanding philosopher and physician, is said before his conversion to have written many little works in his own field. But afterward, changing his study and being made a physician of souls, he wrote for the edification of his brothers:
*Elegant Sermons*, one book.
He also published various other treatises besides, knowledge of which has not reached me.

Rodericus, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, to whom Guimundus, bishop of Aversa, while still living in the monastery, is said to have written three books against Berengar, a learned man and trained in both divine and secular letters, described in a very lucid style, for the edification of many,
*The Life of Bruno, Bishop of Cologne*, one book.
And certain other works.
He flourished under Emperor Henry II, in the year of the Lord 1040.

Agerius, from a scholastic a monk of Corbie in Saxony, a German by nation, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man practiced by constant study in the holy Scriptures, rich in ancient reading, and magnificently learned in secular letters, cautious in intellect and sufficiently eloquent in speech, strong in verse and in prose, wrote in a very urbane style a distinguished volume:
*On the Sacrament of the Altar*, three books.
*On Grace and Free Will*, one book.
And certain letters.
He says, however, that he wrote several other works also, which have not come into our hands.

Libertus, a monk of Metz, of the order of Saint Benedict, French by nation, a man nourished by constant practice in the holy Scriptures and not ignorant of the arts of humanity, which we call liberal, in order to transmit his name to posterity, wrote certain little works of no contemptible reading. Among them there is said to be a brief but useful work, containing a history of the deeds of his own time, which he dedicated
*To the Bishop of Metz*, one book.
He flourished in the times of Emperor Conrad the Younger, in the year of the Lord 1030.

Eckbertus, a cleric of the church of Liège, German by nation, elegantly learned in the divine Scriptures and perfectly instructed in the disciplines of secular literature, wrote several little works both in meter and in prose, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of later men. Of these there survives a volume composed in elegant meter, which he entitled
*On Rustic Riddles*, one book.
Another larger book *On the Same Matter*, one book.
And certain other works.
