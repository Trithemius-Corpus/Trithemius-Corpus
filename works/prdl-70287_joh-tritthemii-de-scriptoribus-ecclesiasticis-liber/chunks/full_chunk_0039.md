*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

Many homilies, namely twenty-nine.

In addition he composed other treatises, sermons, and letters for various [unclear].

He flourished under Valentinian and Valens, in the year of the Lord 380.

He also wrote an Exposition on Isaiah; likewise On the Soul, On Natural Principles and Causes, Several Chapters on the Divine Nature, On Synods, and Origen’s Philocalia.

Ephrem, a monk and distinguished inhabitant of the desert, and deacon of the church of Edessa, ordained priest by blessed Basil, because of his humility never approached to perform the divine mysteries, although he was held worthy before God and men. Nevertheless, so far as pertained to his office, in admonishing the people he delivered countless homilies. He, as is read in the Lives of the Holy Fathers, was seen to have received from an angel a book written in golden letters; whence immediately on the next day, and thereafter, with the Holy Spirit speaking in him, he had such grace in declaiming that he found no one like himself in his time. He wrote in the Syrian tongue, for the instruction of readers, certain devout little works, of which the following are found among the Latins, namely:

On the Holy Spirit, one book.
On Compunction of Heart, one book.
On Contrition of Heart, one book.
On Repentance, one book.
On the Struggles of the World, one book.
On the Day of Judgment, one book.
On the Resurrection, one book.
On the Blessedness of the Soul, one book.
On Thoughts, one homily.
On Severe Preaching, one homily.
On the Celebration of the Mysteries, one homily.
Lament over the City of Edessa, one book.

He also published not a few other works, especially sermons and homilies to the people, which have not yet come to my knowledge.

He flourished under Valentinian and Valens, a very close friend of blessed Basil, as you may find in his Legend, in the year of the Lord 380.

He flourished, younger than his brother, under the emperors Valentinian and Valens, and composed various works, in the year of the Lord 380.

Gregory, from monk made bishop of Nazianzus, a man of such great learning, integrity, and eloquence that not even his adversaries dared in any way to contradict the things that were said by him, so that for the confirmation of authority it was enough that Gregory had merely said something. He, cultivator and lover of the monastic way of life, when on account of the excellence of his learning and the holiness of his life he was called to the episcopate, fled and hid himself, preferring to be abject in the house of the Lord rather than to be exalted among the crowd with perishable honors. But almighty God did not wish so bright a light to be hidden under the bushel of the aforesaid way of life, since it could be extended to the salvation of many. Found therefore by divine indication, he is led by the people to the episcopal see; and immediately, in excuse for his flight, a solemn homily is composed, in which he shows that the rule of souls is the art of arts. On account of his distinguished learning and holiness of life, he was called Gregory the Great before the blessed pope of the same name. He wrote many little works in the Greek language, of which the following are extant:

He published many sermons and homilies, and various treatises on diverse subjects, since they have not come to my knowledge. At last, worn out by old age, when he perceived that he could no longer be equal to the governance of the people, after another bishop had been ordained in his place, he returned to the pursuit of the solitude he had left behind.

He flourished under Valentinian and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 390.
