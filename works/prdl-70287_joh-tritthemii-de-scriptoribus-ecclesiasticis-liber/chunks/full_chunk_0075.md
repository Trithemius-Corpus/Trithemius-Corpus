He flourished under the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord 600.

Justinian the Great, emperor, nephew through his sister of the elder Justin, who reigned afterward for 38 years, a man of great and excellent talent, deservedly gained a place among the ecclesiastical writers. He both published a most distinguished volume against the heretics and, through the hands of John the patrician and the consuls Theophilus and Dorotheus, with marvelous brevity corrected the Roman laws, which lay in more than two thousand books. For, reducing to twelve books all the constitutions of the emperors that were contained in many volumes, he called it the Code. Again, reducing the laws of the individual magistrates and judges, which had extended to nearly two thousand books, as we have said, to the number of fifty books in three volumes:

The Code of the Digests, or Pandects, 50 books.

The Institutes, 4 books. “The imperial majesty...”

Epitome of the Laws, one book. “O...”

On the Incarnation of the Lord, against Eutyches, 3 books.

Against the African Bishops, one book. “These things which necessarily...”

Who would doubt that he also wrote other works, especially many elegant letters, which have not come into our hands? At last, in his old age, having become mad and foolish, he died in the year of the Lord 565, in the thirteenth indiction. Justin the Younger succeeded him.

Dionysius, a Roman abbot, surnamed, as is said, Exiguus, a man certainly great and very renowned in talent and knowledge, learned in the holy Scriptures, and nobly instructed in the discipline of secular letters, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence, an outstanding computist and calculator of times, wrote some little works in the Latin tongue. Of these I have found only the following:

The Great Paschal Cycle of 532 Years, one book. “O...”

On the Reckoning of the Paschal Feast, one book. “The reckoning of the Paschal feast...”

I have found nothing about the rest. He translated from Greek into Latin the life of Saint Pachomius the abbot, also the book of Gregory of Emesa on the condition of man, the writings of Proterius, bishop of Alexandria, to Pope Leo, and certain other things. He began his cycle in the year of the Lord 532, which was the last year of the great paschal cycle; once completed, it always returns to the same point.

*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

He therefore flourished under Justinian the Great, emperor, in the year of the Lord 540.

Facundus, bishop of Hermiane, sufficiently learned in the holy Scriptures, is said to have written some volumes for the edification of readers. Of these there has come down to us only a great and excellent work, composed in an elegant style and furnished with great authorities of the ancients:

In Defense of the Three Chapters, 12 books.

He flourished under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

Justinian, bishop of Valencia, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of human letters, had from one mother three brothers who were bishops, namely Justus, Nebridius, and Elpidius, learned and erudite men who wrote various little works, which nevertheless have by no means come into our hands. He himself, among other things, composed for a certain man named Rusticus:

On Five Questions, one book. And certain other things.

He flourished in Spain under the emperor Justinian, in the year of the Lord 540.

Justus, bishop of the church of Urgell, brother of the aforesaid bishop Justinian, himself also a man practiced and learned in the holy Scriptures, of excellent talent in expounding the divine and canonical volumes, wrote a sufficiently elegant and commendable volume in a scholastic style:

On the Song of Songs, one book. And many other things.

He flourished with his brother under the emperor Justin, in the year of the Lord 530.

Apringius, bishop of the church of Pax in Spain, learned in the divine Scriptures and most erudite in secular letters, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, expounded the sacred Scriptures so gloriously and profoundly that, in comparison with him, the ancient writers seemed not to have expounded them. Of his little works there exists a notable volume, with subtle meaning and illustrious style:

On the Apocalypse of John, one book.

On the Song of Songs, one book.

He also published many other works, which have not come into our hands.
