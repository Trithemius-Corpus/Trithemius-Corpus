Odo, bishop of Cambrai, a man learned in the Holy Scriptures, nobly educated in humane letters, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, notably distinguished, in no slight degree, in composing and delivering homilies to the people after the manner of the ancients, wrote a disputation which he had had with a certain Jew concerning the mystery of the Lord’s incarnation, in dialogue form, marked with the first letter of his own name and of the Jew disputing with him:

To Wilbodo the monk, 1 book.

On the Canon of the Mass, 1 book.

On the Unjust Steward, 1 book. “There was a certain rich man.”

Several homilies and letters.

And he composed not a few other things, which have come to my knowledge.

Eadmer, a monk of Canterbury in England, of the Order of St. Benedict, at one time a hearer and disciple of Archbishop Anselm, a man learned through long practice in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, wrote little works not unworthy of reading, by which he both benefited the edification of the faithful and made his name known to posterity. Among these there is a volume on the discord which existed between William, king of the English, and the aforesaid St. Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury:

On Ecclesiastical Liberty, 1 book.

The Life of the same Anselm, 1 book.

And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Gratian, a monk of Bologna or Classe, of the Order of St. Benedict, by nation, as some will have it, Etruscan, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and burning with wondrous zeal, prompt in intellect and clear in eloquence, also distinguished in life and conduct, after Ivo, bishop of Chartres, wrote a notable volume of decrees, from the statutes, sanctions, and writings of the ancients, which teachers of canon law use in the schools down to the present, approved by Pope Eugene III. Its title is:

The Concord of Discordant Canons, 1 book. “The human race by two.”

He also composed certain other things.

He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120. [unclear]

Hugh, a monk of Corbie, of the Order of St. Benedict, called de Folieto, a man rich and learned in the Holy Scriptures through reading of the ancients, clear in intellect and sweet in eloquence, and no less venerable in conduct than in knowledge, made his name known to posterity by his writings. For he wrote an excellent volume, for all religious and God-fearing people not so much useful as delightful, divided into four little books, headed as follows:

On the Material Cloister, 1 book. “On the ordering of the cloister.”

On the Spiritual Cloister, 1 book. “The cloister of the soul contains.”

On the Cloister of the Soul, 1 book. “In the heavenly Jerusalem.”

On the Cloister of Paradise, 1 book.

And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Richard, a monk of St. Victor at Paris, of the Order of the Regular Canons of St. Augustine, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect, scholastic in eloquence, exceptional in morals and conduct, wrote many and almost innumerable little works, by which he greatly benefited the usefulness of believers and consecrated his name to immortality.
