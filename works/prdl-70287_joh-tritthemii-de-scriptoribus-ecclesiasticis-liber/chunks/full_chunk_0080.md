John, bishop of Constantinople, a Cappadocian by nation, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, of inestimable abstinence and holiness, so intent upon almsgiving and works of piety that he ordered all the begging poor to be expelled from the city of Constantinople. He wrote in Greek to St. Leander, bishop of Seville, of whom we spoke above:

On the sacrament of baptism, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.

And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 600.

Licinianus, bishop of Cartagena Spartaria, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, after many exercises in study, was killed at Constantinople by his rivals with a cup of poison, under the emperor Maurice. Nor did sudden death harm him, as Scripture says: “The just man, by whatever death he has been overtaken, shall be in refreshment.” For then he truly began to live, when, adorned with good works, he went through death to Christ, never to die. He wrote certain little works of reading not to be despised, of which I have not yet deserved to see any. His little work is reported, however:

On the sacrament of baptism, one book.
To Abbot Eutropius, one book of letters.
To various persons also, one book of letters.

And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 590.

Severus, bishop of the city of Malaga, sometime associate and colleague of the aforesaid bishop Licinianus, a man learned in the holy Scriptures and nobly instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, sharp in intellect, distinguished in speech, wrote against Vincentius, bishop of the city of Zaragoza, who had slipped from the Catholic faith into Arian depravity:

A Correctory, one book.
On Virginity, to his sister, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.

He flourished in the year of the Lord 580, under the emperor Maurice, under whom he also died.

[unclear] Eutropius, bishop of the church of Valencia, formerly abbot of the monastery of Servitanum, of the order of St. Benedict, to whom Licinianus the bishop wrote many letters while he was still dwelling in the aforesaid monastery, a man magnificently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, and especially imbued with monastic disciplines, while he was still abbot wrote to Bishop Licinianus:

Why, after these things, the anointing of chrism is given to baptized infants, one book.
And several letters to various persons.

*The Abbot of Spanheim*

He flourished in the times of the emperor Maurice, in the year of the Lord 600.

John, bishop of Girona, formerly a monk of our order, a Goth by nation, from the province of Lusitania, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and distinguished also in morals and conduct, was learned and renowned in the Greek and Latin tongues. When as a young man he sought Constantinople out of love of learning, after seven years had passed he returned to Spain. King Leovigild, when he wished to draw him over to Arian impiety and could not, condemned him to a very harsh exile for ten years. Then, recalled by the most Christian king Reccared, he founded a monastery, where by word and example he showed his brothers the path of regular discipline. He wrote:

An Exhortatory to Monks, one book.
Additions to the Chronicle, one book.

He also composed many other things which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Reccared, the most Christian king of the Visigoths in Spain, in the times of the emperors Maurice and Phocas, in the year of the Lord 600.

Maximus, bishop of the city of Zaragoza, learned in the divine Scriptures and not moderately instructed in secular letters, ready in intellect and polished in speech, excellent in verse and [unclear], published in both styles sufficiently distinguished volumes, which have not yet at all come to the knowledge of our reading. There is reported, however, his notable volume and pleasing work:

On the Deeds of the Goths in Spain, one book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Phocas, in the year of the Lord [610].

Jonas, monk and disciple of St. Columbanus the abbot, and companion of his pilgrimage, a Scot by nation, a zealous man, learned and distinguished in knowledge, venerable no less for religion than for doctrine, at the brothers’ request and for the edification of many, wrote in composed discourse:

The Life of Saint Columbanus, one book.
The Life of Saint Attala the Monk, one book.
The Life of Saint Eustasius the Abbot, one book.

He flourished under the emperor Heraclius, in the year of the Lord 630.
