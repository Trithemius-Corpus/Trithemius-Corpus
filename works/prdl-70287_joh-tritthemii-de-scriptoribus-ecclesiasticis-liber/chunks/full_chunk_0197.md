Paulus de Castro, a jurist, the most celebrated of all men of his time, a man of great learning and eloquence, who for many years taught civil law gloriously at Siena, Florence, and Padua, having been hired at a public salary, composed many little works on the laws, known and dear to the professors of this faculty; by these he left to posterity the memory of his most famous name. Among them the following are reported:

On the Code, 9 books. On the Old Digest, 24 books. On the New Digest, [unclear] books. On the Infortiatum, 14 books. On the Institutes, 4 books. Most elegant Opinions, 1 book. And several other things.

He died in the times of the emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1437, in the fifteenth indiction.

Ambrosius Florentinus, general abbot of the Camaldolese order, once a pupil of Chrysoloras, the Byzantine philosopher, when he was teaching Greek literature at Venice, a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular studies, a most lucid philosopher, rhetorician, and theologian, left to posterity many distinguished monuments of his talent. He was the nuncio and envoy of Pope Eugenius to the Council of Basel, where he delivered a solemn oration on his behalf before the fathers. He wrote a distinguished volume:

Letters to Various Persons, 1 book. Various Orations. And several other things.

He also translated into Latin from Greek the four books of Blessed Dionysius the Areopagite, in a more elegant and easier style; also the original Life of Blessed John Chrysostom, addressed to the emperor Sigismund; likewise Basil On Virginity, addressed to Cardinal Gabriel, afterward Pope Eugenius IV; likewise Laertius On the Life of the Philosophers, addressed to Cosmas de’ Medici. But he also rendered the three books of Saint John Chrysostom to Stagirius, the afflicted monk, in lucid and adorned speech. And several other things still unknown to us.

He also translated Ephrem the Syrian. He himself wrote On the Sacraments; On [unclear] the Holy Spirit.

He flourished in the times of the Councils of Constance and Basel, both of which he adorned by his presence, in the year of the Lord 1430.

Caietanus, by country a Vicentine, a canon of Padua, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, notably instructed in secular letters, a distinguished philosopher and physician, a most celebrated theologian, who publicly taught sacred scholastic letters in the school of Padua, acquired great praise for his learning. He wrote, in both branches of literature, as they report, several little works, by which he left to posterity the memory of his care. Among them these are reported:

On the Physics, 8 books. On the books On the Soul, 4 books. On the book On the Heaven and the World, 4 books. And several other things.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Sigismund and Pope Eugenius IV, in the year of the Lord 1450.

Henricus Gulpen, abbot of the monastery of St. Giles in Nuremberg, of the order of the holy father Benedict, a German by nation, a distinguished jurist, exceptionally learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular philosophy, and no less venerable for his religious way of life than for his knowledge of the Scriptures, whose readiness of talent and prudence in counsel Johannes Nider, having experienced them, commends in his little work On the Ants, wrote elegantly on the seven distinctions:

On Penance, 1 book. On Consecration, 1 book. On the Passion of the Lord, 1 book. And certain other things.

He was personally present at the Council of Constance, when he was still a secular master of arts studying law, in the year of the Lord 1417.
