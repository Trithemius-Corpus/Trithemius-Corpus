Gaius, under Zephyrinus, bishop of the city of Rome, that is, under Antoninus, son of Severus, held a very notable disputation against Proculus, a follower of Montanus, accusing him of rashness in defending the new prophecy; and in the same volume, enumerating only thirteen letters of Paul, he says that the fourteenth, which is said to be to the Hebrews, is not his; and even among the Romans down to the present day it is not regarded as being, as it were, by Paul the apostle.

Gaspar Sasgerus, otherwise Schatzgerus, a Minorite, having early acquired no slight learning in sacred Scripture, drew his pen, as befits a soldier of Christ, against the pestilential offspring of the Lutherans. And although he showed himself a strong antagonist in many works, he did this especially in two books, which are entitled On the Dissolubility of Marriages and On the Sacrifice of the Mass.

I know that the man labored over many other works, but I do not have the titles of the books; therefore I would rather play the Angerona in this matter than ascribe false titles of books.

He flourished under Emperor Charles V.

Geminus, priest of the Church of Antioch, composed a few monuments of his talent, flourishing under the emperor Alexander and Zebennus, bishop of his city, especially at that time when Heraclas was ordained pontiff of the Alexandrian church.

Gemma Frisius, physician and distinguished professor of mathematics among the Louvain scholars, wrote a method of arithmetic, and certain things on the description of places and on finding their distances.

Likewise, booklets on the use of the astronomical ring, recently enlarged by the same man. He also wrote a chart, which they commonly call a map, more elegant than those which we have seen up to now.

He is still distinguished under Emperor Charles V, 1546.

Gennadius Scholarius, a man well learned in divine letters, wrote certain excellent little works. Those which follow are extant with the illustrious man Diego, for thus the Italians call Jacobus, Hurtado [unclear] de Mendoza, the emperor’s ambassador among the Venetians.

On the First Worship of God.

On the Time and Mode of the Essence of Intelligible Beings and Immortal Souls.

To Those Who Suffer Scandal.

And very many others.

At what time he lived is not certainly established.

George, a certain Greek monk, distinguished in knowledge of histories, wrote Chronicles from the creation of the world down to the reign of Nicephorus Botaniates, under whom he flourished; these exist in manuscript at Venice in the library of Saints John and Paul.

George Collimitius Tannstetter, royal and imperial physician and mathematician, taught at Vienna in Austria after Andreas [Stiborius?]. Many works of his are extant, concerning which these are reported:

An Artifice on the Application of Astrology to Medicine, and on their agreement.

Other canons on the reckoning of critical days.

On the same matter, Nine Axioms on investigating critical days from the figure of the heavens and the point of time at which the sick man began to suffer.

A compendium on the natures and properties of the planets.

Final canons on the administering of drugs and phlebotomy.

A Judgment of Storms.

A table for finding the hourly portion.

The Judgment of Albohazen Haly.

Of the Son of Abentagel, on the physician ignorant of astrology.

Certain scholia by the same man on the second book of Pliny’s History of the World.

He is said to have written very many other things.

George Wicelius, a German, a man, besides his other innumerable gifts of mind, learned even to a marvel, highly skilled in the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin languages, excelling in outstanding talent and renowned for rare learning, throughout his whole life does and meditates nothing except that, both by writing and by teaching publicly in the schools from the pulpit, he may instruct and educate the holy people of Jesus Christ.
