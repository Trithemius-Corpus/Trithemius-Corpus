Armandus de Bellovisu, of the Order of Preachers, wrote an explanation of difficult terms, both of theology and of philosophy and logic.

Arnoldus Peraxylus Arlenius, a man learned beyond his years even in youth, is still, unless I am mistaken, in the household of the most noble Lord Diego Hurtado de Mendoza, ambassador of the imperial majesty among the Venetians, a singular pillar of the literary commonwealth, and one who has deserved exceedingly well of every kind of study and wisdom, while he has diligently sought out ancient authors, especially Greek ones, in the libraries of Italy, and restored them to the light and use of mortals. We expect from him, if God prolongs his life, certain treatises from Plutarch on morals, not yet translated by anyone; also seven books of Dio Cocceius’s Roman History, rendered purely into Latin speech; and the commentaries of Olympiodorus, the great Platonic philosopher, on Aristotle’s Meteora.

From the sacred writers Chrysostom, Theodoret, and others, he will give most beautiful sermons, commentaries, and orations hitherto unseen. In addition, he has resolved to publish certain little notes on Lactantius Firmianus, in which he attempts to refute the trifling superstition and vanity of the ancients, and to establish that our own doctrines are much more certain and true by law.

Arnoldus de Villanova, by nation a Frenchman from Provence, or, as others will have it, a Catalan, since Villanova is a town four miles distant from the city of Girona in Catalonia, was most devoted to astronomy, and certain men asserted that he had been a heretic. Sent by King Frederick from Sicily to the pope, he died at sea and was buried at Genoa.

He expounded very many volumes in every branch of learning, from the excellence of his own intellect, without a teacher.

A certain principal work of his on medicine is in circulation.

He also wrote an elegant letter on alchemy to the king of Naples.

Arnoldus Haldrenius of Wesel, a man conspicuous for singular holiness of life and exact knowledge of sacred scripture, and most skilled also in the three languages, Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, equally strong in verse and in prose: he was of such humility that the various works, as we have learned from his friends, which he both composed and corrected, he did not publish to the public, apart from these few:

While still rather young he corrected Macrobius, adding, so far as could be done, those things that were missing from the Greek sources.

He applied the same labor to restoring Diomedes, the ancient grammarian, and likewise certain others less well known.

He published a little book on the Ten Commandments of the Decalogue, on that Gospel saying: If you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments.

Likewise another, against the hagiomastiges; and others, which, as we have said, still lie hidden.

He wrote several prefaces or letters, placed before various authors partly in his own name, partly in another’s.

Also many epigrams, either purely Greek or translated from Greek into Latin. Among these the chief are those in Heresbach’s translation of Herodotus; when the translator, for I know not what reason, unless perhaps terrified by their majesty, had left them untouched in translating, this Arnoldus of ours, for the sake of public usefulness, rendered them into Latin no less splendidly than they had formerly been in Greek.

His too are those summaries prefixed to the individual distinctions of the commentary of Dionysius the Carthusian on the four books of the Sentences, expressed almost in individual distichs, although several of these, especially in Books I, III, and IV, left in passing by Haldrenius, were supplied by a certain Noviomagus, as the copy of Arnoldus preserved up to now in the Carthusian house at Cologne clearly bears witness to this very fact.
