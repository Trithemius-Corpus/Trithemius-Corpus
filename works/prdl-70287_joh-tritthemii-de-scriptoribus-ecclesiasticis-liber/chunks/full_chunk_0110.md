Peter Alphonsus, converted from Judaism to the faith, formerly called Moses, whom King Alphonsus, lifting him from the sacred font, distinguished with his own surname, a man learned in the holy Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, as proof of his sincere faith wrote, under the name of Moses the Jew, which he had been, and of Peter the Christian, which he had begun to be, a distinguished dialogue:

Against the Jews, one book. *To the one and eternal First...*
On Knowledge and Philosophy, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1100.

Honorius, priest and scholasticus of the church of Autun, a man most devoted to the holy Scriptures and very learned, and also nobly instructed in secular sciences, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, wrote many excellent little works. Of these he himself, in the book of illustrious men which he entitled *On the Lights of the Church*, listed the following: a very necessary work on Christ, on the Church, and on the future life, which he entitled

The Elucidarium, three books.
On Free Will, one book.
The Seal of Saint Mary, one book.
The Mirror of the Church, or of Sermons, one book.
A Stumbling-Block concerning Priests, one book.
A Summary of Histories, one book.
The Jewel of the Soul, on the divine offices, six books.
On the Image of the World, two books.
On the Song of Songs, one book.
On the Lights of the Church, one book.
On the Body of the Lord, one book.
The Ladder of Heaven, on the degrees of visions, one book.
The Key of Physics, on the natures of things, one book.
On the whole Psalter, one book.
On the Pope and the Emperor, one book.
A Dialogue from the little works of Augustine, one book.
Refreshment of Tables, on feasts, one book.
The Food of Life, on the saints, one book.

In addition he published many other works, whose titles I have not yet been able to find. He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1120.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

[unclear] Sigebert, monk of Gembloux, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man from his youth most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, and also, in secular letters, second to none in his time in the territory of Liege, practiced in verse and in prose, wrote many volumes in each style, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. He is reproved, however, in that, adhering to the emperor Henry, persecutor of and rebel against the Roman Church, he wrote letters and little works against Hildebrand, Pope Gregory VII, which did not befit his position. What he wrote, indeed, he plainly declared at the end of his *Illustrious Men*. When he had been placed in the monastery of Saint Vincent at Metz to instruct boys, he wrote in heroic meter:

The Life of Saint Theoderic, bishop, one book.
The Passion of Saint Lucy, in verse, one book.
On the Translation of the same, one book.
The Life of King Sigebert, one book.
The Passion of the Theban Martyrs, one book.

Marcellus, [unclear], one book.

He also published other works after these, which he did not mark together with the little works written above. He flourished in the times of Emperor Henry III, the fourth king, in the year of the Lord 1110. He brought his Chronicle down to the year 1111.

[unclear] Calixtus, the second pope, a Burgundian by nation, brother of the duke of Burgundy, previously archbishop of Vienne, sat in the pontificate after Gelasius for six years; a man learned in the divine writings and not ignorant of secular letters, an especial lover of Saint James the Apostle, in whose honor he raised the church of Compostela to an archiepiscopate. He wrote in composed speech and with the greatest diligence:

On the Miracles of Saint James, one book. *Calixtus, bishop, servant...*
Letters to various persons, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1120.
