For the rhetorician opens up the figures of the Scriptures, makes tropes clear, unties knots, shows colors, explains uncertainties, and makes the shadows of words clear. When he assumes the office of an orator, he will easily destroy the allegations of heretics and refute their errors. Finally, who could worthily recount the praises of a good orator, if he carefully weighs how much power there is in persuasion? O would that all the theologians of our time cultivated oratory: how much fruit they could produce in the Church of God by their exhortations. The theologian who is an orator is said to have the minds of his own hearers in his power; at whose persuasions, toward those things that are holy, [unclear]. Far be it that a poet cannot be good and truthful, one who has had as his predecessors in this art the holy fathers Augustine, Jerome, Sedulius, Prudentius, Bede, and countless others. But it is not my office, nor the business of this time, to rebuke their sloth. Therefore, to close the letter: I have thought that philosophers, orators, poets, and faithful Christian cultivators of the other good arts, who have written something useful, should be counted among ecclesiastical writers, for three reasons. First, because I judged that their little works could contribute not a little to the knowledge of the holy Scriptures. Second, because it seemed likely to me that they had written some ecclesiastical works which are unknown to me. Last, because they should understand that an occasion has been given them by us, so that, writing henceforth ecclesiastically, they may adorn the place they have received with fitting labors. Do you therefore think that such great men, so useful to the Catholic Church, should be rejected? Far be it from us to inflict upon them so rash an injury, by which they might believe that we were provoked by zeal of rivalry, not by the reason of piety. Finally, we must beware of that proverb commonly tossed about: Knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man. Thus I wished to answer you briefly, so that you might know that what you previously wondered at had not been done without cause. Farewell, remembering us before God in your prayers. From Spanheim, the fourth day before the Nones of April, in the year of the Lord 1492.

*Letter of the Venerable and Distinguished Father in Christ, Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, to the Most Reverend Father in Christ, Lord Johannes von Dalberg, Bishop of Worms.*

To the most reverend father and lord in Christ, Lord Johannes von Dalberg, by divine providence Bishop of Worms: Brother Johannes, called abbot in Spanheim, of the order of Saint Benedict, offers the little that he is.

It was formerly the custom of the ancients, most reverend father, if they had fashioned some new work, to dedicate it to eminent men, so that it might receive lasting strength and firmness, and so that the poisonous tongue of rivals, beaten back by the light of majesty, could nowhere succeed in fastening the tooth of wicked biting. Nor were they deceived in the opinion that proceeded from humility, since among learned prelates a kindness friendly to subjects must always be lifted up with praises, a kindness which rewards the studies of the good and restrains those of the wicked. Imitating as far as we can this example of antiquity, men of our own time also are accustomed to submit the works they write, dedicated to prelates of churches and to more learned men, so that, approved by their authority, they may be able to go forth securely into public. For those things go forth into the light without fear which are supported by the testimony of a bishop.
