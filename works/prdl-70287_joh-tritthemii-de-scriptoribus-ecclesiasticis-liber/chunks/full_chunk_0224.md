Peter Scotus, German by nation, from Strasbourg by homeland, a canon of Saint Peter there, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures, most learned in both laws, and very well instructed in the other arts of humanity, a very celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, and poet, excellent in talent and distinguished in life and conduct, wrote certain modest and most elegant little works, both in verse and in prose, by which he commended himself to posterity. On Saints John the Baptist, John the Evangelist, and Chrysostom, he wrote a beautiful and pleasing work in elegiac verse, one book. The Praises of John Gerson, one book. Most elegant letters, [unclear], one book. In addition, he composed poems and various pieces, not contrary to the reader’s compunction, but properly apt and fitting, in various kinds of meter. He died at Strasbourg, buried in the church of Saint Peter, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1491, in the ninth indiction, and in the thirty-first year of his age. Having been perfected in a short time, he fulfilled many times, and he was taken away lest wickedness alter his understanding.

Nicasius de Voerda of Mechelen, German by nation, Brabantine by homeland, deprived of his sight from the third year of his age, and for that reason also naturally and completely ignorant of the alphabet of all letters, displayed, as it were, a great miracle, a second Didymus of Alexandria in our time, since he became most learned in every doctrine and science, both divine and human. For in the school of Cologne, where he also died, he publicly taught law: the books of both laws, which he had never seen, he learned by hearing, retained in memory, and recited openly. In the University of Leuven he first received the degree of master in arts; afterwards he governed schools for some time in Mechelen; then, having returned to Leuven, he was appointed licentiate in sacred theology, and by a singular apostolic dispensation was ordained priest. He preached publicly, heard confessions, served at the altar, and, though blind, recited the Gospel before all, using his mind as a book; only he did not celebrate the divine rites at the altar by himself. Lastly, in the academy of Cologne, by the agreement of all the doctors, he was made doctor of canon law, and also a careful interpreter of the imperial laws.
