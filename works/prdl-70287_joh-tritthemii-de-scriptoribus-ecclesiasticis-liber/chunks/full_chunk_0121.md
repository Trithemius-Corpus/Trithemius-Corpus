Moreover, Eugenius III, in the council of Trier, with Saint Bernard present, publicly approved before all her writings and little works when they had been read aloud; and again strengthened her by letters, that she should persevere in the undertaking she had begun. She also wrote other things. In all her little works, however, Catholic doctrine shines forth, such as either confirms faith or instructs conduct; nor did she ever say or write anything that could be called into doubt. And although she was ignorant of the Latin language, nevertheless, through the revelation of the Spirit of God, she dictated everything in Latin and fittingly, while notaries took it down. We have seen many letters of Conrad III and Frederick I, emperors, and of the bishops of Mainz, Trier, Cologne, Bremen, Jerusalem, and of almost innumerable others, who, from different regions of the world, stirred by the fame of her holiness and revelations, commended themselves in writing to her prayers, and sought answers to various questions.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

They sought them. She died under Emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1180, in the eighty-second year of her age, in the thirteenth indiction, on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of October.

Albert, patriarch of Jerusalem, a learned and most holy man, who gave to the Carmelite brothers, while they were still living in the Holy Land, the rule which those same brothers, now settled in Europe, profess everywhere after it was modified and approved by the Apostolic See, at the urging of Pope Innocent III described, in a lucid and truthful style:

The State of the Holy Land, one book.
The Rule of the Carmelites, one book.

He flourished under Emperor Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1190.

Innocent III, pope, a Campanian by nation, previously called Lotharius, presided over the Church after Pope Celestine III for eighteen years. He was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, since from his boyhood he had devoted himself to letters in the school of Paris, and he grew into a man no less upright than learned. He left not a few little works of his talent, of which, under the name of Lotharius the Levite, or also of Pope Innocent, the following are current:

On the Usefulness of the Human Condition, two books. “Why from the womb of the mother…”
On the Seven Penitential Psalms, one book.
On the Office of the Mass, two books. “There are chiefly three things in which…”
On the Sacrament of Baptism, one book.
On the Cloister of the Soul, one book.
The Ancient Decretals, one book.
Several Sermons, one book.
Letters to Various Persons, one book.

He flourished at the time of the discord which existed between Philip, brother of Henry, and Otto, duke of Saxony, in the year of the Lord 1200.

G[unclear], most learned in the divine Scriptures, and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, having ascended the theological chair in the school of Paris, shone forth with distinction for a long time, and among all the doctors of that time was celebrated with the greatest fame and reputation. He wrote a very distinguished and useful volume, or:

Summa of Theology, one book. “As the Apostle says, Faith…”
On the Divine Offices, one book.

He is said also to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.
