Nicholas of Cusa, bishop of Brixen and cardinal priest of the Holy Roman Church of the title of St. Peter ad Vincula, a German by nationality, from the diocese of Trier: a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, easily the foremost of the theologians in his time, and outstandingly learned in secular letters; a very celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, and mathematician; subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech; skilled in three languages, Hebrew, Greek, and Latin; most upright in life and conduct, and singularly devout; a sincere and fervent lover of the salvation of souls. When he was sent as legate into Germany, he did and established many things useful for the reformation of the Church, and especially of our order. *The Abbot of Sponheim*

Near Cusa, the village of his birth, on the banks of the Moselle opposite Ürzig, he built a very famous hospital in honor of St. Nicholas the bishop; he richly endowed it with estates and revenues, and adorned it with many books in every branch of knowledge, Greek, Latin, and others.

He died under Emperor Frederick III in the year of the Lord 1464, in the twelfth indiction, on the eleventh day of the month of August, in the sixty-fifth year of his age. He was buried at Rome; his heart was sent to the aforesaid hospital and is buried there in the middle of the choir beneath marble.

Johannes Rode, abbot of the monastery of St. Matthias the Apostle outside the walls of Trier, of the order of the divine father Benedict, a German by nationality, from Trier by homeland: a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, outstandingly learned in canon law, devout in morals and conduct, a very celebrated restorer of regular observance, from whose labor and foundation the most recent Bursfelde reform of our order in Germany began. He once devoted himself to letters in the school of Heidelberg, where, becoming a bachelor of sacred theology and a very learned licentiate of canon law, he seemed already in mind and body to despise the world. Then, having been made a canon of the church of Metz, dean of St. Simeon, and official of the court of Trier, he despised all things for love of Christ and, to everyone’s amazement, entered the Carthusian order outside Trier. After some years he undertook the office of prior, although unwilling and refusing it. But Otto, archbishop of Trier, seeing that this man was learned, experienced, and zealous, had him brought out of the charterhouse by authority of the supreme pontiff and appointed him abbot in the aforesaid monastery; through him he reformed many monasteries in his diocese.
