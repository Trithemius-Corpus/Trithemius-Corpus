Yet it is our part, not only concerning Origen, but concerning any writers whatsoever, to think with the holy Roman Church and to despise superfluous opinions. He published, moreover, almost infinite volumes, of which I have seen only the following translated into Latin:

On Genesis, 16 homilies. “In the beginning God made...” “It seems to me one...”

On Exodus, 15 homilies. “As on the last day...” “As on the last days...”

On Numbers, 28 homilies. “In Numbers not all...” “The Lord gave a name...”

On Joshua, 26 homilies. “God for doing good...”

On Judges, 9 homilies. “As long as King Uzziah...”

On the beginning of [Idida?], 2 homilies. “Not from the beginning only...”

On Isaiah the prophet, 9 homilies.

On Jeremiah the prophet, 4 books.

On Ezekiel, 14 homilies.

Likewise on the Gospel of Matthew, 26 homilies.

On the Song of Songs, 4 books. “As formerly among the people...” “Concerning the Song of Songs...”

On the number 150. “Blessed is the man,” this psalm.

Against Heretics, 5 books. “Adamantius said, who...”

Against Celsus the philosopher, 8 books.

Periarchon, 4 books. “All who believe...”

On the Epistle to the Romans, 15 books. “Because the rest of the Apostle Paul...”

On the beginning of John, 1 book.

He also composed many other works, which have not come into our hands. There are those who write that they have read six thousand treatises of Origen, a number both large and unknown to the Latins. Nor is there any doubt that many little works of his have lately been translated, which have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading. He died an old man and poor, buried at Tyre, under the emperors Valerian and Gallienus, in the year of the Lord 261, the ninth indiction, in the seventieth year of his age.

Ammonius of Alexandria, the most celebrated of all men of his time, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote in the Greek language certain distinguished volumes, by which he left the memory of himself to posterity; of their number the following are extant:

On the Harmony of Jesus and Moses, 1 book.

Of the Evangelical Canons, 1 book. “In the first canon four...”

One from Four, 1 book. “Since indeed many...”

And certain other things. The most impious Porphyry, a pagan philosopher and the bitterest enemy of Christians, falsely accuses him of having denied the faith and become a heathen, though it is established that he remained a devoted Christian until death. He flourished under Alexander, son of Mammaea, in the year of the Lord 230.

Ambrose, a hearer of Origen, first a Marcionite and afterward corrected by Origen himself, an outstanding confessor of the Christian faith, also deserved to be promoted to the office of deacon. He was certainly a most learned man and a zealous lover of books; with him furnishing the expenses, notaries, and copyists, our Adamantius composed almost infinite volumes. He was so great an admirer of his studies that day and night he urged him on, in words and in writings, to write something new, so that in a certain familiar letter he is named by Origen as the taskmaster of the work. He used to say that he never went to bed in Origen’s presence, nor took food, unless first one of the brethren who was always present had sounded forth in the letters of the sacred offering. He wrote, concerning his conversion, giving thanks, to Origen:

Epistles, 1 book.

And certain other things. He flourished with Origen in the year of the Lord 230.

Tryphon, a hearer and disciple of Origen, to whom some of Origen’s letters are extant, a man most learned both in divine Scriptures and in secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote in the Greek language certain distinguished little works, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these the following are extant:

On the Red Heifer in Deuteronomy, 1 book.

On the Quality of Sacrifices, 1 book.

On the Dove and the Turtle-Dove, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Alexander, in the year of the Lord 230.

Minucius Felix, an advocate, Roman by nationality, a man most learned in secular letters and zealous in divine readings, excellent in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote in the Latin language certain distinguished little works, by which he commended the memory of himself to posterity.
