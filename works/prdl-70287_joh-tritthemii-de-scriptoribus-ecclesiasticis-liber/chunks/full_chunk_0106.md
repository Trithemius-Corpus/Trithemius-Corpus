Bernoldus, priest of the church of Constance in the Swabian nation, a German, a devout man, studious and learned in the holy Scriptures, and sufficiently instructed in the discipline of secular letters, brilliant in talent, polished in speech, left for the studies of his own people some little works, which nevertheless have by no means come to the knowledge of our reading. He is said, for the benefit of the ecclesiastical commonwealth, to have composed a volume which he entitled:

The Roman Order, one book.

He flourished in the times of Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1060.

Marbodus, bishop of Rennes, much devoted to the divine Scriptures, learned from reading the ancients, and nobly educated in the disciplines of secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, wrote in both styles not a few volumes, which can please readers no less than profit them. Among these there stands out an outstanding work which he composed in elegant verse with a threefold sense:

On the Song of Songs, one book.

The Passion of the Theban Martyrs, in verse, one book. And certain other works.

He flourished in the times of Henry III, in the year of the Lord 1050.

Marianus, a monk of Fulda, of the order of St. Benedict, a Scot by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, an outstanding historian and computist, leaving his own country, came into Germany as a pilgrim for the love of Christ. First, having become a monk at St. Martin's in Cologne, he was enclosed out of desire for solitude, and remained there for two years. Afterwards, however, he was transferred to Fulda, and remained enclosed there for [unclear] years, until at last, on account of his learning and holiness, by command of the bishop of Mainz and the abbot of Fulda, he moved to Mainz. *Abbot of Sponheim.*

He died under Henry IV, in the year 1086, in the ninth indiction, in the fifty-eighth year of his age, and was buried at Mainz, at St. Martin's in the greater church, not without a reputation for sanctity.

Wenericus, bishop of Vercelli, formerly a cleric of the church of Trier, a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in the discipline of secular letters, made his name known to posterity by writing, although his little works have not come to the knowledge of our reading. In that discord which existed between Hildebrand, Pope Gregory VII, and Henry, the third emperor or fourth king, he wrote, in the person of Theoderic, bishop of Verdun, while he was still a cleric at Trier, to that same Pope Gregory a volume instructed by the divine Scriptures, not rebuking him, but beseeching an elder as a father, and, with the friendly feeling of one grieving, suggesting to him all the things which talkative rumor had spread abroad that he had done or said against the right of laws and the propriety of equity. He entitled it:

On the Discord of the Kingdom and the Priesthood, one book.

He flourished under King Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1080.

Adam, abbot of Perseigne, of the order of the divine father Benedict, learned in the divine Scriptures by continual exercise, rich in reading of the ancients, and sufficiently educated in the knowledge of secular letters, of very celebrated reputation in delivering homilies, transmitted his name with praise to posterity by composing various treatises. There are extant his devout and useful:

Sermons to the Brothers, one book.

On Saints and Various Subjects, one book.

He is also said to have explained certain treatises of sacred Scripture, in which he showed the abundant vein of his talent; but they have not come to my knowledge.
