86 *On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

*On the Moralized Meaning of Virgil*, one book. Incipit: “Indeed he was seeking [unclear].” *On the Immensity of the Son of God*, one book. *On the Passion of the Lord*, one book.

He is said also to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge. For he was trained in both Greek and Latin speech. It should be noted that the title of the book *On Adam without a* is *The Book without Letters*, namely *On Adam without a*, *On Abel without b*, *On Cain without c*, and so with the rest according to the sequence of the letters. But in the book whose title is *Exposition of the Virgilian Content, according to the Moral Philosophers*, with marvelous industry he sought, and found, gold in the mud, so to speak, while he inclined the work of the poet toward physical reasoning. He wrote this work to a certain deacon. Another work, the *Mythologies*, he wrote to Catus, a priest of Carthage, and *On the Obscure Expressions of the Ancients* to Chalcidius the grammarian, already an old man, when he himself was still young and a teacher and rector in the school of humane letters. He flourished in the times of Thrasamund, king of the Vandals, persecutor of the Catholic churches, under Emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Ferrandus, deacon of the church of Carthage, to whom some little works of the above-mentioned Saint Fulgentius are extant, a man both devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, and no less venerable for holiness of conduct than for doctrine, is said to have composed certain volumes of lively talent. Of these, only the following have come to my knowledge:

To Count Reginus:

*On the Seven Rules of Innocence*, one book. Incipit: “For the labor of social life.” This book is extant among the [Ebirbacenses?].

To Saint Fulgentius, one letter. *Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He flourished with Fulgentius under Emperor Anastasius, in the year of the Lord 500.

Anastasius II, pope, a Roman by birth, sat after the pontiff Saint Gelasius for one year and ten months. He was indeed an erudite man, but, seduced by Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, not long afterward paid the due penalties for his error. For when he wished to obey the necessity of nature, struck by God, the wretched man poured out his intestines with his feces, as is clear in Distinction 19, *Anastasius*. And this was the second infamous pope. He wrote to Emperor Anastasius:

*On the Peace of the Church*, one letter. Incipit: “The beginning of the pontificate.”

He is said to have written other letters too, but they have not come into our hands. He died, struck by divine judgment, when he had secretly wished to recall from exile Acacius, bishop of Constantinople, condemned and cast out by the Church, under Anastasius Augustus, in the year of the Lord 498.
