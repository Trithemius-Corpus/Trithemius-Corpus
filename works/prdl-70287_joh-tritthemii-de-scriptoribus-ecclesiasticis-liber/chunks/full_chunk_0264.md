*On Ecclesiastical [Writers], Addition II*

Instructions on Ptolemy’s *Apotelesmata*, one book.
A playful Dialectic; and he composed very many other works, a man worthy of immortality, under Charles V, emperor of the Romans.

Augustinus Steuchus of Gubbio, a canon regular of Saint Saviour, distinguished in every kind of wisdom, produced elegant annotations on the Pentateuch of Moses. Besides these he composed other works, of which he has the following:

On perennial philosophy, ten books, together with a little book on the name of Gubbio, his own city.
Against the Lutherans, [unclear] books.
The same man’s *Cosmopoeia*, or exposition on the fabric of the world from the three chapters of Genesis.
The same man wrote very learned commentaries on forty-seven Psalms.
He is still alive and is meditating many things under the most unconquered emperor of the Romans, Charles V, and Pope Paul III.

Avitus, a priest, a Spaniard by birth, translated into Latin Lucian’s book, written in Greek, on the revelation of the tomb and relics of the body of Saint Stephen, and, with his own letter added, gave it to the westerners through Orosius the priest.

Bachylus, bishop of Corinth, regarded as illustrious under the emperor Severus, wrote, in the person of all the bishops who were in Achaia, a book full of learning on Easter.

Bacciauius, a man of Christian philosophy, unencumbered and ready, deciding to devote himself to God, also chose pilgrimage in order to preserve his integrity. He is said to have published pleasing little works, but of them I have read only one, on faith, in which he satisfies the pontiff of the city against the querulous slanderers of his pilgrimage, declaring that he undertook the pilgrimage not from fear, in the manner of men, but for the sake of God, so that, going out from his land and from his kindred, he might become a co-heir of the patriarch Abraham.

Baptista Fulgosus wrote an incomparable work, arranged in nine books, on the memorable sayings and deeds of emperors, princes, dukes, bishops, priests, private persons, etc.; drawn partly from all the historiographers who exist anywhere, and partly illustrated by examples that occurred in his own age, of which there is the greater part, so that he can deservedly be called another Valerius Maximus, both on account of his native eloquence and on account of the variety of matters which he inserts everywhere.

*In the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers*

Barlaam, a Calabrian, a monk of Saint Basil, flourished at almost the same time as Francesco Petrarca. Learned in both languages, he gathered into one volume many things that pertain to the disciplines. Certain Greek little works of the same man are extant in Italy, namely: several letters; Logic, five books (elsewhere six); Geometry.

An arithmetical demonstration, or geometrical demonstration, on numbers.
A theological speculation on the discovery of the feast of Easter.
Sermons against the Latins on the Holy Spirit are extant at Rome.
Little works by the same man and by various others on the procession of the Holy Spirit are there also.

Bartholomaeus Argentarius, an Italian by nation, a physician by profession, indeed an excellent and very learned man, proficient in both languages, collected an outstanding work of medical matters from Galen’s books for the use of students.

He is still illustrious in the year of salvation 1546, while Charles V, the most unconquered emperor of the Romans, is reigning.

Bartholomaeus Arnoldus of Usingen wrote a compendious summary of the whole of logic.

The same man’s *Little Book of Natural Philosophy*, or interpretation by figures and tables, in an epitome of the *Physics*, completed in three treatises. To these is appended a question on the quiddity of continuous quantity.

Bartholomaeus Cocles of Bologna, a distiller of medicine, surgeon, physiognomist, chiromancer, and geomancer, collected a revival of chiromancy and physiognomy from many, indeed almost innumerable, authors.

Bartholomaeus Latomus of Arlon was for a long time royal professor at Paris, and is now chief counsellor of the most reverend archbishop of Trier. He devoted much labor to students, both by dictating and by writing. Certain proofs of this distinguished talent are extant; of these the following are reported:

An oration on the praises of eloquence.
A funeral oration at the obsequies of Richard, archbishop of Trier.
A congratulatory poem on the coronation of the king of the Romans, addressed to Charles V Caesar and King Ferdinand, the august brothers.
An oration on his departure for Italy.
An epitome of the commentaries on the dialectical invention of Rudolph Agricola.
