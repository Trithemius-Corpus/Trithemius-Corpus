Simplicianus, bishop of Milan after St. Ambrose, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and a lover of poverty, by many letters urged Augustine, while he was still a presbyter, to exercise his talent and devote himself to expositions of the Scriptures, so that he might seem like another Ambrose, a promoter of Origen’s work. Hence Augustine also resolved many questions and letters addressed to him. But he himself also wrote useful letters to Augustine and to various others, which are found partly here and there.

He flourished under the rulers Theodosius and Honorius, in the year of the Lord 420.

Isidore the Elder, [unclear] of Corduba, a man very skilled in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, ready in talent and sufficiently eloquent in speech, distinguished no less for the conduct of his character than for knowledge of the Scriptures, whose learning among the ancients was held at a great price, wrote no few little works on the divine volumes. Of these I have found only an excellent work which he published for St. Orosius, the Spanish presbyter: *On the Four Books of Kings*, four books.

Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished under the rulers Theodosius and Honorius, in the year of the Lord 420.

Orosius the presbyter, Spanish by nation, once a hearer of blessed Augustine, was a man learned in the divine writings and most skilled in secular letters, very renowned in life and conduct. Blessed Augustine had sent him, when he came to him from Spain to learn the question concerning the nature of the soul, to St. Jerome in Bethlehem of Judea. After staying there for a little while, he learned clearly the question for which he had come, and, returning to Hippo, carried with him the relics of Stephen the protomartyr. To this most studious man there are extant several little works of blessed Augustine and various resolutions of questions of the Scriptures. He himself also wrote the following volumes:

*An Apologetic against the Pelagians*, one book. *On the [unclear] of the World, a Chronicle*, seven books, “I have obeyed your instructions.” *On the Reason of the Soul*, one book, “When among you, heavenly [unclear].”

John, bishop and patriarch of Jerusalem, studious and learned in the holy Scriptures, but too intent upon Origen’s teachings, stirred up against himself the writings of the fathers, namely of the most holy presbyter Jerome and of Bishop Epiphanius, as is found in their letters sent to him. But John himself, giving account of his study, says that he imitated not the faith of Origen, but his talent. For chiefly on this account he wrote a noteworthy work: *Against the Detractors of His Study*, one book; and certain letters to various persons, and certain other things.

He flourished in the times of the emperors Arcadius and Honorius, in the year of the Lord 400.

Maximus, bishop of Turin, most learned in the holy Scriptures, second to none in his own time in delivering homilies to the people, was distinguished not only in doctrine but also in life.
