74 *On Ecclesiastical Writers*

On the Apocalypse of John, to Castorius, five books. But the book *Apocalyptica* which he composed has not yet come to my knowledge.

He flourished under the emperor Theodosius II, in the year of the Lord 440.

Vtilius, bishop of a certain city in Africa, though I have not been able to discover the name of the city, was certainly a man very learned in the sacred Scriptures and, in my judgment, sufficiently instructed in secular disciplines, profound in understanding, sweet and adorned in speech. He is said to have written many little works, but I have seen one notable work, which he wrote to the above-mentioned bishop Primasius, entitled:

*On the Parts of the Divine Law*, one book. “To the holy and most blessed bishop ...”

Of the rest I have seen none.

He flourished at the same time as Primasius, in the year of the Lord 440.

Proterius, bishop of Alexandria, a man most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, a distinguished theologian and computist: in the times of Leo Augustus, the successors of the heretic Dioscorus, after a sedition had been raised by Timothy, cruelly slew him on his journey, and set up that same Timothy in his place as an idol for the Alexandrian bishopric. He wrote, among other things, to Saint Leo the pope:

*On the Celebration of Easter*, three letters.

To Hilary, on the same matter, four letters. “Since your holiness ...”

Several letters to various persons. And certain other things.

He flourished under the emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 460.

Paschasinus, Sicilian bishop, learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly instructed in secular letters, reverend no less for his way of life than for his knowledge, is said to have presided in place of blessed Pope Leo the Great at the Council of Chalcedon, in which, together with the holy fathers to the number of two hundred, he condemned the heretics Eutyches and Dioscorus. Eutyches was a priest and abbot of a very celebrated monastery of Constantinople; he asserted that Christ the Lord was only God and not man, and wrote various things in support of his error, all of which were condemned together with their author. Paschasinus, however, wrote to Pope Leo:

*On the Question of Easter*, two letters. “The writings of your apostolate ...”

To the same, *On the Miracle of the Font*, one letter. “Not one day alone ...”

The acts of the Council of Chalcedon, [unclear] book[s]. “The rules of the saints ...”

He flourished under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 450.

Leo, the first pope of that name, a Tuscan by nation, after Sixtus III sat in the chair of Peter for twenty-one years. He was a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and so celebrated and eloquent in declaiming homilies to the people that he had no superior in the Roman Church, as his most famous sermons will testify. He was the Cicero of ecclesiastical diction, the Homer of sacred theology, the Aristotle of the arguments of faith, the Peter of apostolic authority, the Paul in the Gallican pulpit. His sermons pierce the heart, clothe the soul, and confirm the catholic faith in sweet, brief speech. Nor is it any wonder: bearing the fire of divine love kindled in his breast, what else would he speak but burning words? He wrote many little works; of these I have found only the following, namely:

Against Eutyches the priest, two books.

*On the Nicene Creed*, one book.

Testimonies to Leo Augustus, one book.

To the questions of Bishop Januarius, one book.

On the Passion of the Lord, twenty-four homilies, one book.

Many homilies to the people, one book.

To Bishop Flavian against Eutyches, one book.

To Leo Augustus against Eutyches, one book.

To Bishop Turibius against the Priscillianists, one book.

To Rusticus, bishop of Narbonne, one book.

Many letters to various persons, one book.

Of the rest I have found nothing.

He died at last, after many labors for the benefit of the Church, under Leo Augustus, in the year of the Lord 461, in the fourteenth indiction, on the fourth day before the Kalends of July.

*The Abbot of Spanheim*

Peter, bishop of Ravenna, a learned and most holy man, shining in life with many miracles, was of excellent talent in declaiming homilies to the people, and by word as well as example converted many to the path of truth. He wrote many things for the edification of the faithful, among which are reported:

Several sermons or homilies, one book.

Two copies of these sermons exist in the library of Eberbach.

To Eutyches the priest, one letter. “Sad, I read your sad letter ...”

He also wrote several other letters.

He flourished under Marcian Augustus, in the year of the Lord 450.
