Jacobus of Teramo, a canon of Aprutium, archdeacon of Aversa, a very celebrated jurist, an excellent doctor of secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, clear and well-composed in speech, whose authority among the professors of this faculty was great in his time, published in that discipline certain outstanding volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have found only the dialogue *On the Redemption of the Human Race*, commonly called *Belial*, which the author entitled:

*The Consolation of Sinners*, 1 book. “And afterwards through the tree of knowledge.” He is said to have written *On the Clementines*, 1 book.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Wenceslaus and of the new Pope Boniface, in the year of the Lord 1390.

Wilhelmus of Oppenbach, a German by nation, an eminent doctor learned in the sacred Scriptures, and nobly exercised in scholastic philosophy, sharp and vigorous in intellect, well fitted for disputing and untying questions of the Scriptures, wrote in the theological faculty little works not to be rejected, by which he made his name known in the Parisian school. Of these I have found:

*On the Sentences*, 2 books.
*Disputed Questions*, 1 book.

*Of Ecclesiastical Writers.*

He is also said to have written sermons and certain other treatises, and to have worked out readings in the schools, which have not come to my knowledge.

Henricus of Langenstein, called “of Hesse,” a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, nobly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect, abundant and expansive, since, after spending many years in lecturing on Genesis, scarcely reaching the fourth chapter of the book, he completed as many volumes. For he was ready in all things, copious in judging and writing, so much so that for him it was a matter of greater difficulty to keep away things worthy of remembrance that occurred to him of their own accord than to find new things to say. This man, as I found from a certain letter of his, I should believe to have been a canon of the church of Worms. He flourished first in the University of Paris, where he earned the laurel of the doctorate; then, for the adornment of the newly planted University of Vienna, he betook himself there, and both by teaching and by writing acquired great glory for his learning.
