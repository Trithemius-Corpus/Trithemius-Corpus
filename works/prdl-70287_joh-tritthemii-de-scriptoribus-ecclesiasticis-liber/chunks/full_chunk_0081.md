Isidore the Younger, bishop of Seville after Leander, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, second to none in his time in secular letters, subtle in intellect, clear in judgment, composed in speech, excellent in verse and prose, no less distinguished for holiness than for learning, shone forth. He is called the Younger to distinguish him from the elder, the bishop of Corduba, of whom we made mention above, or also from another bishop of Seville who preceded him. He is said to have written many other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the emperor Heraclonas, son of Heraclius, in the year of the Lord 630, in the time of Sisebut, king of the Goths in Spain. Because of his most holy life and conduct he was inserted into the catalogue of the saints; his feast is celebrated on the eighteenth day before the Kalends of February.

Caesarius, from a monk of the monastery of Lerins bishop of Arles, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures, gentle in intellect, sweet and adorned in speech, graced by a wonderful brevity of sentences, most holy in life and conduct, since from his youth he had been instructed in monastic disciplines and had been abbot of Lerins for a long time, wrote certain distinguished little works of reading not to be despised, which are very necessary and useful for monastic religious persons and persons dedicated to God. Of these there are extant:

On grace and free will, one book.

Of sermons to monks, one book: “I fear in Christ [unclear].”

Some of these sermons are extant among the Eberbach monks.

A decree of three councils of Arles, three books. I have found nothing about the rest.

In the third sermon, which is on the struggle of the monk against the vices, he acknowledges that those to whom he is speaking are professed monks of the order of the rule of our most holy father Benedict.
