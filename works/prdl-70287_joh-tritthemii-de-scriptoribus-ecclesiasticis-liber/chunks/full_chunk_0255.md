Sylvester of Prierio, a Bolognese, of the Order of Preachers, a venerable doctor and certainly a most renowned expounder and likewise very keen defender of the doctrine of Saint Thomas Aquinas, flourished in Lombardy around the times of Pope Innocent VIII. For he wrote a Defence of the doctrine of Saint Thomas on the questions of Johannes Capreolus, abridging the writings and demonstrating more clearly than light the irrefragable doctrine of the most holy doctor, dissolving from that doctor’s own sayings all the subtleties of his opponents. He also wrote an Introduction to Logic.

Also a Summa of Sermons, which is called the Golden Rose.

Also a Summa on cases of conscience.

Also on the four books of the Sentences.

He continued writing down to these times, living at Bologna, that is, to the year of the Lord 1512.

Dominicus of Flanders, a most renowned doctor, of the same Bolognese order, in the same times as the preceding, wrote Metaphysical Questions. Questions on the Soul. Also on the books of the Posterior Analytics. He died while Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, presided in the chair of Peter.

Paulus Soncinas, of the same order, in these times wrote Metaphysical Questions. Also on the predicables and categories. He died at the same time as, or about the same time as, the preceding.

A certain abbot of Ursperg, whose proper name is hidden, wrote a volume of Chronicles. Its beginning is from Ninus, great king of the Assyrians, down to Frederick II, emperor of the Romans. The last year is counted by him as 1229 from the salvation of the world. Ursperg, moreover, is a monastery of the Premonstratensian institute, almost halfway between Ulm and Augsburg of the Vindelici.

Abba Abraham and Abba Patritius, holy fathers [unclear], translated the ascetic sermons of Isaac, the Syrian monk, into the Greek language. This work is extant at Rome in the Vatican Library.

Adrianus Barlandus, a Zeelander by nation, a man of rare learning, published several dialogues, together with a little work on the notable towns of Lower Germany.

Five books of the same author’s old and recent jests, illustrated with scholia, are extant. And a little work on all the bishops of Utrecht.

Also a poem on the location and pleasantness of Louvain.

The same man described the instruction of a Christian man in aphorisms.

He also composed concise institutes of the art of oratory.

He also selected proverbial verses from Virgil with the greatest zeal.

He wrote many other things unknown to us.

He flourished at Louvain around the beginning of the reign of Charles V, 1520.

Aegidius Bellamera, a remarkably learned man, wrote counsels in law, of which 148 survive at Rome in the Palatine Library.

The same man published several other little works, namely those which follow:

On plurality of benefices. On the Roman Rota.
