Guido, fifth prior of the Charterhouse, a man skilled by long study in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, gentle in disposition and distinguished in speech, and venerable no less for his manner of life than for his learning, is said to have composed several little works, by which his name has been endowed with immortality. Of these there is extant an excellent work, written in lucid discourse:

On the life of Hugh, bishop of Grenoble, one book.
On the institutions of the Carthusians, one book.
And certain other things.

He corrected with diligent study the letters of the most blessed father Jerome, which had been corrupted by the excessive fault of scribes, to the standard of truth, and brought them together into one. He died under Lothair the Younger, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1157, in the fifteenth indiction, on the sixth day before the Kalends of August.

Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris, formerly a scholastic, a man most studious in the divine Scriptures and nobly learned, subtle in intellect and distinguished in speech, transmitted his name to posterity by writing with such great glory that, down to the present day, the school of theologians, wherever it is practiced, names and regards him with singular veneration as the Master. For with discerning study he wrote, from the sayings of the holy fathers, a commendable work:

Four books of Sentences. “Desiring something concerning…”
On the whole Psalter, one book. “Since all prophecies…”
“Seeking the beginnings of things…”
On all the epistles of Paul, four books. “Seeking the beginnings of things…”
Various sermons, one book.

He also composed not a few other things, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord [unclear].

Eckardus, first abbot of the monastery of St. Lawrence, which is called Vraugia, in the diocese of Würzburg in the Hercynian Forest, of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, inferior to none among the learned Germans of his time, excelling in verse and prose, wrote little works not to be despised in reading, by which he showed the liveliness of his intellect. Of these I know only the excellent work, composed in a double style in imitation of Boethius, very useful and necessary for the instruction and consolation of monks and of all the faithful who fear God, which he entitled:

The Lantern of Monks, one book.

He composed certain elegant sermons and various letters to different persons. He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord [unclear]. He was buried in the middle of the church of the aforesaid monastery.

Engelbert, abbot of the monastery of Admont, of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man very studious and learned in the divine writings, and sufficiently instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, distinguished in intellect and speech, and no less worthy of reverence for his manner of life than for his knowledge, made his name known by writing and expounding various things, and both by word and by example did no slight service to the edification of his brothers. For he wrote:

Elegant sermons, one book.
On vices and virtues, one book.

But he also published several other things, which have by no means come to my knowledge.
