*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

Stephen, also called Herrand, the fourteenth bishop of the church of Halberstadt, formerly abbot of the monastery of Ilsenburg, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech, wrote little works not contemptible to read, but few have come into our hands. He wrote, in the person of Count Louis, a most elegant and useful letter to Waltram, bishop of the church of Nienburg, who was defending Henry, a rebel against the Roman Church:

A Defense of the Pope, one book. “As a good man concerning go[od]...”
And several letters to various persons, one book. And certain other things.

He died under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1107, in the fifteenth indiction, and was honorably buried at Reinsburch.

Bernard, first abbot of Clairvaux, of the Cistercian Order, a Burgundian by nation, a noble and holy man, who shone with many miracles in life and after death, learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect, adorned in speech, since indeed in his writings he neglected nothing that could contribute to the adornment of discourse, published many splendid little works, of which the following are reported:

Anselm, scholasticus of Laon, very studious in the divine Scriptures, skilled through long practice, and nobly learned in the disciplines of secular letters, acute in intellect, scholastic in speech, distinguished no less in life than in knowledge, explained the Scriptures of both Testaments in a new kind of exposition, by an interlinear and marginal gloss from the writings of the Fathers. He also wrote certain other little works which have come to me. There is reported:

Commentaries on the Psalter, one book. And certain letters.

He flourished under Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1110.

Hugh, priest and monk of Saint Victor at Paris, of the Order of the regular canons of Augustine, abbot, as they report, in the same place, a Saxon by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, inferior to none of the ancients in secular philosophy, who, like a second Augustine, was regarded as a most celebrated doctor in his time, subtle in intellect, adorned in speech, and venerable no less for his manner of life than for his learning, wrote almost infinitely many little works, of which few have so far come into our hands:

The Sum of His Sentences, one book. “A difficult thing indeed...”
