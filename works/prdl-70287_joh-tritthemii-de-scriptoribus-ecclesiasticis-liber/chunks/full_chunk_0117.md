*Abbot of Spanheim.*

...according to the custom of modern writers, he composed sermons to the people under the name of Peregrinus, much inferior to our man in eloquence, and also later in time. Whatever this Conrad wrote, however, echoes Ciceronian eloquence, which the other could not do.

He flourished under the emperor Conrad III, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Arnold, abbot of Bonneval, of the Cistercian order, once the close friend of the blessed abbot Bernard, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and also not ignorant of secular letters, ready in talent and distinguished in eloquence, no less conspicuous in conduct than in doctrine, made his name known to posterity by writing. For after William, abbot of Saint-Thierry, had died, [he wrote] the work on the Life of Saint Bernard, 4 books; The Deeds of Illustrious Men; and certain letters.

He flourished under the emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Richard, monk of Cluny, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, and in secular letters reputed in his time the most learned of all, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, by writing and composing various treatises transmitted his name with great glory to the knowledge of posterity. Yet I do not recall that any of them has so far come into our hands. His distinguished and famous work is, however, reported to be:

History and Times, 1 book.
Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1160.

Ganfred of Auxerre, monk of the Cistercian order, once a hearer and disciple of Peter Abelard, but lest he should also become an imitator of his error, betook himself to the teaching of Saint Bernard of Clairvaux. Under his discipleship he became distinguished in learning and religion, and composed little works of reading not to be despised, by which he made his name known also to posterity.

Against Peter Abelard, 1 book.
On the Apocalypse of John, 1 book.
Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Frederick I, in the year of the Lord 1180.

Peter, cleric of the church of Reims, called de Riga, a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and in secular letters the most learned among all the doctors of his time, an outstanding grammarian and philosopher, excellent in verse and prose, wrote not a few volumes in both styles, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these I have seen only the notable metrical work on the Bible, which he entitled Aurora, as follows:

On Genesis, 1 book. It begins: “The beginning of the world, what sort...”
1 book. “These twelve of the fathers...”
1 book. “But the voice of the Lord [to] Moses...”
1 book. “God addresses Moses...”
1 book. “These are the words of God...”
1 book. “After Moses, Joshua...”
1 book. “The Hebrew sins, he says...”
1 book. “To Ruth hastens...”
3 books. “Dwelling on Mount Ephra...”
1 book. “Origen alone, with...”
1 book. “Solomon reigned...”
1 book. “After those subject to him...”
1 book. “You who read the history...”
2 books. “Certain people [assign] the book of Job to Moses...”
1 book. “To Eustochium and Paula...”
1 book. “As far as under the Ethiopians...”
1 book. “Great Alexander...”
1 book. “He shone under Herod...”
1 book. “In the nineteenth year of Tiberius...”
1 book. “These things concerning the new and old law...”
1 book. “[unclear].”

He also published many other things which have not come to my knowledge. In the aforesaid little works he expounded the historical books of the Bible with marvelous brevity, carefully observing the law of meter, and explained each item according to the historical and allegorical sense.

Joachim, abbot of the monastery of Fiore, of the order of Saint Benedict, originally from Calabria, a man zealous and practiced in the divine Scriptures, who, being regarded in his time as a prophet, even attempted to foretell future things. But it seems to me, if I may speak without prejudice to his own opinion or that of his followers, that he spoke more from conjectures drawn from the Scriptures than that he was a prophet. For, to be silent meanwhile about the rest, he dreamed that the emperor Frederick would be an enemy of the Church, whereas we all know that he persevered until death as peaceable, and subject and faithful to the Roman pontiff.
