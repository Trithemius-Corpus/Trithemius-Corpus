*T*itus, bishop of Botra, otherwise Bostrinus, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently instructed in secular letters, sharp and forceful in talent, is said to have fashioned many little works, by which he transmitted the memory of his name with glory to posterity. Of these, the following are given in number:

Against the Manichaeans, [unclear] books.
A Commentary on the Gospel, one book.
And many others.
He flourished in the year of the Lord 340, and died under the emperor Valens.

Damasus the pope, Spanish by nation, sat after Liberius in the chair of Peter for eighteen years; a man most learned in divine matters and excellently instructed in secular letters, a very celebrated rhetorician and poet, and most skilled in composing verses; subtle in talent, devout in life and most holy, very dear to the priest Saint Jerome. He wrote various little works in both genres, of which the following are extant:

Deeds of the Supreme Pontiffs, one book. Its opening: “The blessed apostle Peter.”
On Faith against Heretics, one book. Its opening: “Since after the Nicene council.”
Letters to Various Persons, one book. Its opening: “Damasus the bishop.”
Various poems.
And other works.
He flourished under Valentinian and Valens, in the year of the Lord 370.

Apollinaris, bishop of Laodicea in Syria, a man devoted to and learned in the divine writings, and by far most learned in grammatical instruction, sharp and forceful in talent, clear in speech, wrote certain distinguished volumes in defense of the Catholic faith, by which he commended his name to posterity. Of these there is extant a very celebrated work:

Against the mad Porphyry, thirty books.
And certain other works.
He flourished under the emperor Gratian, in the year of the Lord 380.

Didymus of Alexandria, blind from early childhood and on that account wholly ignorant even of the elements, furnished so great a marvel of himself that he learned perfectly dialectic and geometry, which especially require sight. In the divine Scriptures he was most learned, the teacher of blessed Jerome and very close friend of Saint Antony; sharp in talent and more distinguished in life. He wrote the following works in Greek:

Against the Arians, seven books.
On Doctrines also, one book.
On the Canonical Epistles, seven books.
On the Holy Spirit, one book.
On the Book of Saint Job, one book.
On Hosea, to Jerome, three books.
On Isaiah the prophet, ten books.
On the Gospel of Matthew, one book.
On Zechariah, to the same, five books.
On the Psalter also, one book.
On the Gospel of John, one book.
And certain other works.
He flourished as an octogenarian under the emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 395.

Optatus, bishop of Milevis, African by nation, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, outstanding in talent, devout in life and Catholic, is said to have written certain distinguished little works in defense of our faith, in which he magnificently exercised his abundant talent. Of these there is said to be extant a large and notable volume, which he wrote:

Against the Donatists, six books.
And several letters.
And many other works.
He flourished under the princes Valentinian and Valens, in the year of the Lord 370.
