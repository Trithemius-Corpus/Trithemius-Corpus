*Abbot of Spanheim.*

On the Marriage of Wisdom, 4 books. “I sought a bride for myself.”
On the Twofold Logic, 2 books. “And John was clothed.”
To the Rectors of Boys, 1 book. “Allow the little children to come.”
Sermons in the Council of Constance, 1 book. “Walk while you have the light [unclear].”
Sermons to the Clergy, 1 book. [unclear]
Sermons to the People, 1 book.
Dialogue on Any Subject, 1 book.
Several Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.
He also published many other smaller works, indeed, but very useful, of which certain knowledge has not come down to us.

He was personally prominent in the Council of Constance, in which, laboring gloriously for the extirpation of heresies, at last, after it was dissolved, he was deprived of home, country, relatives, friends, dignities, and his own possessions for the defense of the truth, and was sought by many plots. Hence, compelled by necessity, he fled to the city of Lyons, and there after a few years he died, under Emperor Sigismund and Pope Martin V, in the year of the Lord 1429, in the seventh indiction, in the sixty-sixth year of his age, on the fourth day before the Ides of July, and was buried there with honor among the Celestines.

Vincent Ferrer of Valencia, of the Order of Friars Preachers, to whom some letters of John Gerson are extant, a man most learned in the divine scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature, subtle in talent and distinguished in eloquence, devout in morals and conduct, and the most celebrated preacher of sermons of all in his time, shining with miracles while alive and after death, wrote elegant works:

Sermons on the Season, 1 book. “Blessed is he who comes in...”
Sermons on the Saints, 1 book. “He is rich toward all who...”
Sermons throughout Lent, 1 book.
Certain Letters.

He was personally prominent in the Council of Constance, in the year of the Lord 1418. After his death, because of his frequent miracles, he was canonized by Pope Callistus III, in the year of the Lord 1455, in the third indiction. His feast is in April [unclear].

Boniface Ferrer of Valencia, a Catalan by birth, twenty-fifth prior of the Carthusian Order, brother of the aforesaid Blessed Vincent the Preacher, a man learned in the divine scriptures and an outstanding doctor of canon law, not unlike his holy brother in life and conduct, governed the Grande Chartreuse vigorously by word and example. Among other things he wrote a noteworthy treatise on why few in the Carthusian Order have been canonized and why they do not publicly perform miracles:

To Boniface of the Same Order, 1 book.
On the Approbation of the Order, 1 book.
Certain Letters. And many other things.

He was prominent in the times of Emperor Sigismund and Pope Martin V, in the year of the Lord 1420.

Lupus of Oliveto, a Spaniard by birth, prior general of the Order of Hermit Monks of Saint Jerome, a man devoted to and learned in the divine scriptures, and an outstanding restorer of his order, wrote several excellent little works by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these are reported:

From the Sayings of Saint Jerome, The Rule of His Order, 1 book.
Sermons on the Saints, 1 book.
And certain other works.

This rule, newly compiled in thirty-three chapters, Martin V confirmed.

He was prominent at the time of the Council of Constance, in the year of the Lord 1420.

Dominic of San Gimignano, a most excellent jurist, and nobly learned both in the divine writers and in the other secular disciplines, subtle in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote certain excellent volumes on canon law, by which he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there is extant a notable and very celebrated work, divided into two volumes:

On the Sixth Book of the Decretals, 2 books. “Because the first gloss at the end...”
And various declarations.

He also wrote a Repertory in law, and commentaries on the second and third books of the Decretals.
Counsels in law by the same author.

He flourished at the same time as Paulus de Castro and John of Imola.

The rest, however, which he is said to have composed, have not come to my knowledge.

Paul of Saint Mary, a Jew by birth, a Christian by faith, first bishop of Cartagena and afterward bishop of Burgos in Spain, archchancellor of the king of Castile and Leon, a man most devoted to and very learned in the divine scriptures, and a master of sacred theology, having been converted to the Catholic faith in his mature age, advanced so far in life and doctrine that among all the doctors of Spain he had no equal in his time.
