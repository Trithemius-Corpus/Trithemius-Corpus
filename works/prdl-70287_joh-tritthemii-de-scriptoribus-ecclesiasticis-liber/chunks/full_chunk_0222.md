Johannes Consobrinus, Portuguese by nation, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature and canon law, excellent in talent, of very celebrated reputation in delivering sermons to the people, first in England and then in Portugal uttered great and effective denunciations against the enemies of the faith; by disputing, preaching, and writing he reproved and refuted very many heresies and errors. At the urging of the provost of the royal college of blessed Mary of [Amhten] in England, he wrote a distinguished work in three parts, on contracts and exchanges, which he entitled

On Commutative Justice, 4 books. Various Sermons, 1 book.
And certain others.

He abbreviated the Conflatum of Franciscus Maro in a pleasing compendium.

He flourished in the year of the Lord 1475. And under Pope Sixtus IV he died at Lisbon, poisoned for the faith.

Henricus, surnamed Prudens, of the order of the Carthusians, prior of the house of the valley near Bruges in Flanders, a man devoted to and learned in the divine Scriptures, renowned in talent, devout in life and conduct, and singularly dedicated to God, wrote for the instruction of religious certain distinguished little works, by which he made his name known to posterity. Among these there is extant a very celebrated work, especially suited to devotion, divided into three parts according to the distinctions of states, namely beginners, the progressing, and the perfect, in the manner of a dialogue, in which speakers are introduced: an angel and a monk, Jesus and the heavenly Father, Mary, the loving mother-in-law of holy souls. He entitled it

Tetralogue of Devotion, 5 books.
And certain others.

He died in the new house of St. Sophia of Constantinople near ’s-Hertogenbosch, in which he had held the office of prior for some years, under Emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1484, in the second indiction.

*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

Franciscus Diedus, a noble Venetian patrician, a man exceptionally learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, a philosopher and rhetorician, distinguished in doctrine and eloquence, a very celebrated interpreter of pontifical and civil law, and an indefatigable lover and defender of the Venetian republic, wrote certain distinguished little works both in verse and in prose, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among these the following are reported:

History of Saint Roch the Confessor, 1 book.
Orations and various letters,
And certain others.

He died at Verona under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1483, in the first indiction.

Rudolphus Agricola of Groningen, Frisian by nation, a man most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, a philosopher, rhetorician, and the most celebrated poet of all of his time, skilled in Greek and Latin speech and not ignorant of the Hebrew language, excellent in talent, eloquent in speech, once the teacher of Johannes Camerarius of Dalburg, bishop of Worms, likewise a most learned man, easily obtained first place in the Heidelberg school in teaching and writing, even among all doctors of whatever faculty. He wrote certain most excellent little works of his talent, by which he consecrated his name to immortality and left it to posterity. Among these the following are extant:

Life of Francesco Petrarca, 1 book.
Poem on Saint Anne, 1 book. “Anna, parent of the highest [unclear]”
A Most Subtle Dialectic, 3 books.
Various Letters and Orations, 1 book.

In addition he produced various and most elegant poems, in which he showed his almost divine talent. He translated the Psalter from Hebrew, and certain works of Plato from Greek. Having begun to translate the volumes of Dionysius the Areopagite, he was forestalled by an untimely death and left them unfinished. He also wrote other things.

He died at Heidelberg, his mind most fully directed toward God, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1485, in the third indiction. Hermolaus Barbarus, patriarch of Aquileia, wrote this epitaph for him:
