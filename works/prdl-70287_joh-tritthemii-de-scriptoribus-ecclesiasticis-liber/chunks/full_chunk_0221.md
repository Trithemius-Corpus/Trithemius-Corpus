Georgius Molitoris of Nuremberg, a German by nation, a man long practiced in the divine Scriptures and excellently learned, and not moderately instructed in scholastic philosophy, which they call Aristotelian, keen in intellect, distinguished in life, who for many years publicly taught theology in the academy of Erfurt and won great praise for his learning, wrote certain excellent little works of his talent, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Among these the following are reported:

On the Sentences, 4 books. On the Passion of the Lord, 1 book.
Many Sermons, 1 book.
Various Questions.
And several other works.

He died under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1484, in the second indiction.

Bartholomaeus, otherwise Baptista, surnamed Platina, by birthplace a Cremonese, once an apostolic abbreviator, a man most learned in every respect, a very celebrated philosopher and rhetorician, subtle and forceful in intellect, eloquent and pleasing in speech, steadfast and strong in spirit, suffered many calamities at the hands of Pope Paul II, so that, stripped of all his goods and dignity, after suspension on the rack he was most cruelly thrust into prison and detained until the death of Paul himself. Soon freed by Sixtus, in imitation of Eutrandus, deacon of Pavia, who suffered similar things from Berengar, king of Italy, he wittily marked Paul’s character and vices by antiphraxis. For he wrote to Pope Sixtus IV:

On the Deeds of the Roman Pontiffs, 1 book.
On the Natures of Things, 1 book.
Letters to Various Persons, 1 book.
And certain other works.

His also is the Panegyric in praise of Bessarion, bishop of Sabina, etc.; an Oration to Paul II, Supreme Pontiff, on establishing peace in Italy and on declaring war on the Turks; On Honorable Pleasure, 10 books; On the False and True Good, 5 dialogues; Against Loves, 1 dialogue; On True Nobility, 1 dialogue; On the Best Citizen, 2 dialogues; [unclear]

*The Abbot of Sponheim*

He died at Rome of plague, under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, in the year of the Lord 1481, in the fourteenth indiction.

Nicolaus de Wachenheim, a German by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in intellect and clear in eloquence, who in the academy of Heidelberg for almost fifty years, teaching, lecturing, and disputing, was held in the highest esteem as though the prince of theologians, wrote little works of reading not to be despised, by which he also commended the memory of his name to posterity. Michael of Milan, of the Order of Friars Minor, devoted to and learned in the holy Scriptures, and not ignorant of secular literature, subtle in intellect, distinguished in life and conduct, of very celebrated reputation in delivering sermons to the people, wrote, for the use of those who devote themselves to declamations, little works not to be despised, by which he also made his name known to posterity. Of these there are extant:

Sermons on the Temporale, 1 book. Sermons on the Saints, 1 book.
Sermons for Advent, 1 book.
Sermons on Penance for Lent, 1 book.
Sermons on Sins, 1 book. Sermons on the Ten Commandments, 1 book.
And certain other works.

He flourished under Emperor Frederick III in the year of the Lord 1480. And he died under Pope Innocent VIII.

Sixtus IV, pope, a Ligurian by nation, born from the maritime city of Savona, formerly called Franciscus, minister general of the Order of Friars Minor, sat after Paul II in the chair of Peter for thirteen years; a man learned in the divine Scriptures and an outstanding disputant, a lover of the clergy, a singular promoter and benefactor of religious, especially of those whom an observant life commended.
