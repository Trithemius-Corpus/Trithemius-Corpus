*Abbot of Sponheim.*

A summary of the Passion of Jesus Christ, one tract. “At night Jesus ...”

On the Ascension of the Lord, among the sermons of Meffret, one sermon. “Rise up, Lord ...”

On the sermons of Meffret concerning the immaculate conception of the Virgin Mary, one tract. “[unclear] ...”

Very many sermons, both to the clergy and to the people, one book.

Various orations and letters, one book.

Also many other things, of which no mention is made here, one book.

Moreover, he divided many books of various authors into treatises and chapters, prefixing arguments to each, by which the writer’s intention in them can clearly be recognized. Among these are Cicero’s books On Duties, On Friendship, On Old Age, and On the Dream of Scipio. Likewise Aristotle’s books of logic; the same author’s books On the Soul; the books of the Metaphysics. Likewise the books of Ephrem the deacon; John Chrysostom’s books On Compunction of Heart; Saint Augustine’s books On Contrition of Heart; the same author’s book of letters; and almost all the books of Saint Ambrose the bishop. He is still living in the charterhouse of Basel, composes various works, and by his example urges many, through writings, to holy studies, under Maximilian, most merciful king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Angelus de Clavasio, an Italian by nation, of the Order of Friars Minor of Saint Francis, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, a very celebrated jurist, outstanding in intellect, distinguished in speech, devout in life and conduct, is said to have produced certain excellent little works by which he made his name known far and wide. Of these there is extant the notable Summa on cases, very useful and necessary for simple confessors, and no less for learned but occupied men, now dispersed everywhere, which is entitled from the name of its author:

The Angelic Summa, one book.

Additions to the same, one book. “Of an angelic spirit ...”

And certain other things.

He completed this Summa under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV. And I hear that he is still alive, while Maximilian governs the kingdom of the Romans and Germans, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494, in the twelfth indiction.

Jason de Mayno, Milanese by country, ducal senator, a man most learned in both laws, very learned both in the divine Scriptures and in the other secular disciplines, a distinguished philosopher, a very celebrated orator, subtle in intellect, eloquent in [unclear], outstanding in counsel and action, is said to have produced certain excellent little works, by which, exercising his talent, he also commended the memory of himself to posterity. But apart from the oration which he is said to have delivered at Rome before Pope Alexander VI, and which is also circulated in print, I do not recall that any of them have come into our hands. Yet, as I have learned for certain, he wrote elegantly:

On the title De actionibus in the Institutes, one book.

Also on the Code, nine books.

On the Digest he wrote various works, fifty books.

Orations and letters.

And certain other things.

He is distinguished today among the Milanese princes, held in the highest esteem, under Maximilian, most illustrious king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1493, in the eleventh indiction.

Laurentius Burellus of Dijon, of the Order of the Brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently taught in secular letters, outstanding in intellect and distinguished in speech, practiced in meter and prose, wrote in both kinds certain excellent volumes by which he made his name known. Of these the following are reported: a very celebrated work in praise of Elijah, patriarch of the Carmelites, which he entitled:

The Heliad, one book.

On the illustrious men of his order, one book.

And many other things.

He is still living, held in great esteem among his own, writing and composing many things under Maximilian, unconquered king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Christophorus Landinus, an Italian by nation, a citizen of Florence, a man especially learned in secular letters and not ignorant of philosophy or of sacred Scripture, for many years presided over the chair of the Muses and by public office taught poetry, distinguished in intellect. He wrote works held in great esteem by all, especially:

Commentaries on Publius Vergilius Maro.

The Camaldulensians.

Ethics.

He is said still to flourish among his Florentines, whom he adorns with the flowers of every literature.
