He died under Frederick, the third emperor, and Pope Paul II, in the year of the Lord 1471, in the fourth indiction, on the twelfth day of the month of March. He was buried, not without a great reputation for sanctity, in the common cemetery of the brothers of the aforesaid house of Roermond. Finally, he reduced the books of John Cassian to an easier style for the sake of the simpler brothers; the preface of this work begins thus: “Declaration,” etc.

And these very many and very distinguished works of so great a man were printed for the public use of the Church about twelve years ago at Cologne by various printers, at the instance of the Cologne Charterhouse.

Bessarion, Greek by nation, cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, commonly called the Nicaean, and patriarch of Constantinople, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher and orator second to none in either language, wrote certain very distinguished volumes of his genius, by which he transmitted his name to posterity with immense glory. Of these, the following are reported: against Mark, bishop of Ephesus, who held erroneous views on the Holy Spirit in the manner of the Greeks at the Council of Florence, he wrote an eminent and very distinguished work, which he entitled *Confutation*, one book.

He also founded at Venice a notable library, very richly furnished with many books both Greek and Latin, over which Marcus Antonius Sabellicus, a most learned man, was placed at no small stipend; of him we shall speak later.
