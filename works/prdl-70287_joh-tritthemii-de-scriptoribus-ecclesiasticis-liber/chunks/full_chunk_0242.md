*Abbot of Spanheim.*

Henricus Northeimensis, German by nationality, a man studious and learned in secular letters, not ignorant of the divine Scriptures, subtle in talent, eloquent in speech, excellent in verse and prose, wrote certain little works of reading not to be despised, by which he commended his name to posterity with glory. Of these I have seen only that distinguished work which he composed in verse and prose for Frederick, margrave of Meissen and duke of Saxony, entitled Dinetromachia:

On the conflict of virtues and vices, one book. “Tu mea laurigeros.”

Concerning the rest that he composed, nothing has come to my knowledge. He lives even to this day, writing various things, under Maximilian, most illustrious king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Aquinus, of the order of the Friars Preachers, deriving the origin of his life from Sweden, a man studious in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular literature, a celebrated philosopher, a distinguished mathematician, excellent in talent, polished in speech, in imitation of Boethius wrote a most commendable work on the proportions of numbers and sounds, one book.

Certain letters.

And certain other things.

He lives to this day, held in esteem by Otto, duke of Bavaria; he writes various things, under Maximilian, most unconquered king, in the year of the Lord 1494, the twelfth indiction.

Albertus de Prusa, Polish by nationality, a member of the college of the University of Cracow, a man studious and learned in the divine writings, also excellently instructed in secular letters, a theologian, philosopher, and distinguished astronomer, praised in verse by Conrad Celtis for his knowledge of the stars, outstanding in talent and renowned in eloquence, wrote several little works of reading not to be despised, by which he made his name known. But few of them have come to my knowledge.

On the Theoricae of Georgius Burbachius, one book of commentaries.

On the ephemerides of Konigsperg, one book.

And certain other things.

He is still living in the University of Cracow, composing various things under Maximilian, king of the Romans, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494, the twelfth indiction.

Theodericus de Ofenbruck, German by nationality, of the order of the Friars Minor of the province of Cologne, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures, a most famous declaimer of popular sermons, whose life all who know him proclaim at this time to be most holy: in delivering sermons to the people he is illumined by God with such grace, and in short moves his hearers with such compunction and sweetness, that he has had no equal among the Germans. Hence he has also been appointed general preacher throughout the whole province. Recently, when he was with me at Cologne, he read to me a notable little work, suited to devotion, which he had composed at the request of Hermann, archbishop of Cologne:

On the Passion of the Lord, one book.

Manual for simple people, one book. “Quia sicut Augustin.”

On interior exercise, one book. “Qui perseveraverit.”

And certain other things.

He is still living in the province of Cologne, vigorously applying himself to the office he has begun, under King Maximilian and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Bartholomaeus Coloniensis, German by nationality, a man excellently learned in secular letters, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, a distinguished philosopher and poet, whose poems even the most learned poets both admire and extol with praises, wrote in both styles some little works worthy of celebration, by which he made his name known. Of these I have so far seen only a little work on the praises of philosophy, composed in verse, which he entitled:

Sylva of poems, one book. “Cur Plutoni aurum prae.”

Defecta Diogenis, one book. “Quisquis avet cynice.”

A mythological letter.

Of the others I have seen nothing.

He lives to this day at Deventer, composing various things, under King Maximilian and Pope Alexander, supreme pontiff, the sixth, in the year of the Lord in which we wrote these things, 1494.
