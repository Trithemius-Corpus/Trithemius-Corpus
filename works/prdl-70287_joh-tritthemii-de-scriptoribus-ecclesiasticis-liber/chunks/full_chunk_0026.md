And although he erred in some matters, nevertheless he wrote well on many, as his volumes attest. Of these I have seen: *On the Patience of God*, one book, “I confess to the Lord God”; *Against Praxeas*, one book; *Against Marcion*, five books; *Against the Jews*, [unclear]; *Against All Heresies*, one book; *On the Prescriptions of Heretics*, one book; *Against Hermogenes*, one book; *Against the Valentinians*, one book; *On the Flesh of Christ*, one book; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, one book; *On the Soldier’s Crown*, one book; *To the Martyrs*, one book; *On Repentance*, one book; *On the Veiling of Virgins*, one book; *On Women’s Dress*, one book; *On the Adornment of Women*, one book; *To His Wife*, two books; *On Persecution, to Fabius*, one book; *To Scapula*, one book; an *Exhortation to Chastity*, one book; *On Monogamy*, one book; *On the Pallium*, one book; *On Spectacles*, one book; *On Fasts*, one book; *On Ecstasy*, one book; *Against Apollonius*, one book; *On Modesty*, one book.

He also wrote many other things and various treatises, which have not yet come into our hands at all; and he lived studiously even to old age and senescence. He flourished in the times of the emperor Severus, in the year of the Lord 200.

Origen, who is also Adamantius, a priest, Alexandrian by nation, son of the martyr Leonides, once a disciple of Clement of Alexandria, was a man of great and excellent talent, so that in zeal and learning he never had his equal. In the tenth year of Severus Pertinax, when a persecution was set in motion against the Christians and his father Leonides was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ, he was left poor with his widowed mother and six brothers, being about seventeen years old.

*The Abbot of Spanheim*

For the treasury had seized all their substance because of their confession of Christ, so that they no longer had anything from which they might live. And although the number of years was reckoned slight in the young man, nevertheless his learning was already wonderful, and his constancy in the Christian faith admirable. For he had resolved to die bravely with his father, the faithful Leonides, for the faith of Christ, had it not happened that, by his mother’s stratagem, he was preserved for the usefulness of the Church, since he was to become a great teacher. For when, early in the morning, he had resolved to run to his father at the place of contest on the preceding day, his garments were taken away by his mother during the night, and the doors of the chamber were locked, and he was forced to remain at home.

When, however, he had grown into a man, he resolved to lead the apostolic life, and, proclaiming the word of God in the region around him, he went about barefoot for many years. Finally, he never slept on a bed stuffed with feathers; he abstained completely from wine and meat, except in illness; he was intent on serving God devotedly with fasts, vigils, prayers, and continual studies and expositions of the divine Scriptures. Also, according to Christ’s counsel, he was accustomed to wear only one tunic, and, having become a despiser of all worldly things, he took no thought for the morrow. Out of love of chastity, so that he might dare to preach safely to women, he had himself castrated, although in this deed zeal remained without knowledge.

Because of his learning, eloquence, and apostolic life, Mammaea, the most Christian mother of the emperor Alexander, when she was seized with a desire to hear him, came from Alexandria to Antioch, and heard his honey-sweet doctrine with great admiration. He was so intent upon the study of the divine Scriptures that he never took either food or sleep unless one [unclear] of the brothers had first delivered a reading of the Scriptures while all listened. He knew the New and Old Testament by heart like another Ezra, and when already an old man, contrary to his age, he learned the Hebrew language; and with wonderful zeal he brought together into one all the editions of the interpreters, besides the Septuagint.

In dictating editions of the Scriptures he had so admirable a memory and subtlety that, with incredible speed of utterance, he wore out seven notaries and the same number of girls, very swift in writing, besides other young men, all at the same time writing different things from his mouth. And although he was held, and was, so great and distinguished a teacher among all ecclesiastical men, nevertheless he is marked by great errors, by which either he himself was foolish, or he was corrupted by others inserting false things into his writings.
