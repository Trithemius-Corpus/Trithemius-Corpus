*Abbot of Spanheim.*

Gabriel Biel, German by nationality, of the order of those who call themselves Brothers of the Common Life, or those living in common: a man learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in talent, distinguished in life and conduct. From the beginning of its foundation he presided for many years over the university at Tubingen, where he instructed many pupils both in philosophy and in sacred letters. He wrote certain little works of no contemptible authority, by which he made his name known. Among these he ordinarily lectured in the aforesaid university:

On the Canon of the Mass, one book. *Praemissa in cubiculo*.
On the Passion of the Lord, he wrote one book.
Various sermons also, one book.
And certain other things.
Likewise an epitome and collection on the four books of the Sentences.

He is renowned today, an old man of advanced age, in the highest esteem with the count of Wurttemberg, and is said to be writing certain things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Pope Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

George of Gemmingen, German by nationality, provost and canon of the distinguished church of Speyer, a learned and noble man, professor of both laws and a very celebrated interpreter, and excellently skilled in the other disciplines of the liberal arts; a distinguished philosopher and rhetorician, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, excellent in meter and prose. He wrote certain distinguished little works, in which, laudably exercising his talent, he also made his name known to posterity. Yet he conceals what he writes, so that he may avoid the semblance of vain glory, or may correct what he has written with the file of more mature examination. Of these, nevertheless, the following have become known to us:

On the Method of the Duel, one book.
On Engines of War, one book.
On the Care of Horses and Soldiers, one book.
Certain orations.

Moreover, he has published many letters both to me and to others; from these, how much he is capable of, both in learning and in eloquence and in experience of many matters, is readily perceived by anyone who has read them. He is renowned in manhood in the city of Speyer, intent upon sacred letters and writing various things, under Maximilian, king of the Romans, and Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, in the year of the Lord 1494.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

[unclear] John the Venetian, of the Carthusian order, of the house of Saint Andrew the Apostle de Littore near Venice: a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, outstanding in talent, devout in life and conduct, and an eminent cultivator of regular observance. He wrote little works for the instruction of the simple, not to be despised, by which he made his name known. Among these there is an excellent work, which he entitled:

Know Thyself, three books.
On Patience and Humility, one book.
The Mirror of the Dying, five books.
The Crown of Old Men also, one book.
Various sermons, one book.
Several devout letters, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished at Venice under Emperor Frederick III and Pope Sixtus IV, and completed the works written above in the year of the Lord 1480, in the thirteenth indiction. And I am uncertain whether he is still living.

[unclear] John of Muhlbach, German by nationality: a man zealous and learned in the divine Scriptures, and also not ignorant of secular philosophy, distinguished in talent, scholastic in speech. Formerly, in the university of Erfurt, he taught theology gloriously for a long time, and instructed very many pupils in that faculty. He wrote several distinguished little works, in which he exercised his talent magnificently and commended knowledge of himself to posterity. Among these he ordinarily lectured:

On the Epistles of Paul, one book.
Various sermons, one book.
In Praise of Saint Jerome, one book.
Various questions. And certain other things.

He is renowned at Erfurt, old and weak, a prebendary in the monastery of Saint Peter, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.
