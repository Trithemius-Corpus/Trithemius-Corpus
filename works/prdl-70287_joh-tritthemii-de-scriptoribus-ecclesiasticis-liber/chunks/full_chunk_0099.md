*Abbot of Spanheim.*

He flourished in the times of Otto I, in the year of the Lord 970.

Robert, king of France, noble, brave, just, learned, and religious, deservedly receives a place among the ecclesiastical writers, since he composed chants and responsories in sweet and regular melody for the honor of the Catholic Church. In humane letters, especially in music, he was very learned, and of such great devotion that he sang the canonical hours in church with the clerics, and outside, at his own house, read them with his own people. He was of such great piety and compassion toward the poor that, sometimes stripping himself of his own garments, he covered their nakedness many times. A book never left his hands, so much so that, while sitting on the tribunal amid the noise of judgments and cases, he at the same time had the psalter open; hence he is also read to have shone with miracles after his death. His is that well-known Sequence which is sung on the days of Pentecost concerning the Holy Spirit, namely, “Veni sancte spiritus.”

A responsory on St. Peter, namely, “Cornelius centurio.”

On the Nativity of the Lord, namely, “Iudaea et Hierusalem” [unclear].

And that Alleluia, namely, “Eripe me de inimicis.”

And many other things which I do not know.

The responsory “Cornelius centurio,” while the supreme pontiff was celebrating Mass at Rome, he offered with great devotion to St. Peter on the altar at the time of the offertory. The ministers of the altar, hurrying up, thought that the wealthy king had offered gold of great weight; but, finding a little sheet written and noted, they were amazed, praising the prince’s talent and devotion. Hence the Pope decreed, in memory of the most holy king, at the request of the clerics, that this responsory should thereafter be sung in honor of St. Peter.

He flourished under the emperor Otto II, in the year of the Lord 980.

Ammonius, a monk of the monastery of Fleury, of the Order of St. Benedict, a Gaul by birth, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, is said to have composed some small works both in meter and in prose, but they have by no means come into our hands. After the monk Adrevaldus he wrote one volume:

On the miracles of St. Benedict, book 1.

On the affairs of the Franks, or on the origin and deeds of the Franks, book 1.

He flourished under the emperor Otto III, in the year of the Lord 990.
