*On Ecclesiastical Writers, Addition II.*

On the prescription of five feet, one book.
On magistrates and civil and military offices, one book.
Parergon of law, three books. The process of law.
An apology against Ludovicus. On presumptions.
On things entrusted. On single combat.
Commentaries on the title *If a fixed sum is claimed*.
An opinion on the matter of the duel, taken from book 5 of the responses.
On book 1 of the second book of the Code, *On judgments which*;
on C. 4 of the second book of the Decretals, *On trials*. And many other things, by which he endowed himself with immortality.

Andreas, archbishop of Rhodes, wrote a demonstrative apology, from the writings of St Thomas, to Bessarion of Nicaea, on the divine essence and operation; it is extant in Italy.

Andreas Barbatias, most skilled in the laws, wrote many distinguished little works; among them the following are reported:

On [z.?] ff. *novi*.
Four volumes of opinions. On the Clementines.
On the first [book] of the Infortiatum.
On cardinals.
On book 2 of the Decretals, rubric *On witnesses*, chapter *Testimonium*.
On book 2 of the Decretals, rubric *On sureties*, chapter *Dilectorum*.
On book 2, chapter, rubric [unclear], chapter *Quae admit*.
On [z.?] C. *De fideicommissis*, law *Cum acutissimi*.

Andreas Brentzius of Padua translated into Latin Hippocrates’ book *On the Nature of Man*, and also the same author’s book *On Dreams*, with a preface to Sixtus, the supreme pontiff, in which he discusses dreams at length. To this little book is appended an epistle by the same author to Ni. Gupalatinus, in which he explains the words of Hippocrates by which he assigns the internal parts of the body to the Moon, the middle parts to the Sun, and the external parts to the Stars; and why Hippocrates commands supplication to be made to the Sun, to celestial Jupiter, to terrestrial Jupiter, to terrestrial Minerva, to Mercury, to Apollo, to Earth, and to the Heroes.

Andreas Camutius, a physician in Cisalpine Gaul, published certain propositions in philosophy and medicine, to which he promises that he will respond, and a certain little work in which he attempts to reconcile philosophy and theology. He flourished in the year of the Lord 1545.

Andreas, bishop of Crete, wrote several notable sermons, which are entitled:

A sermon on Lazarus four days dead.

*430 In the Abbot of Sponheim, On Writers.*

Sermon 2 on the divine resurrection of the Lord. And thereafter he published other homilies on the feasts of the saints. All these are preserved in Greek in Italy.

Andreas ab Exea, doctor and interpreter of both laws in the Academy of Valencia, wrote a little book on the treasury and fisc, and on the prefecture of the accounts of both.

Andreas Gratiolus of Tusculum, from Benacus, translated into Latin the commentaries of Eustratius, bishop of Nicaea, on the second book of Aristotle’s *Posterior Analytics*.

Also the concise expositions of an uncertain author on the same work.

Andreas Janus Lascaris Rhydacenus excerpted certain things from the histories of Polybius on the military affairs of the Romans, and especially on the laying out of camps. He rendered the same into the Latin language.

Greek and Latin epigrams by the same man.

Andreas à Lacuna, a Spanish physician of Segovia, wrote on the method of recognizing, preventing, and driving off pestilential fever.

There is also extant by the same man an anatomical method, or contemplation on the dissection of the human body.

I also translated not a few works, concerning which the following are given:

A little book of Aristotle *On Virtues*, translated from the Greek and illustrated with many scholia and examples.

From the commentaries *On Agriculture*, formerly ascribed to the divine Emperor Constantine, the last eight books, in which the nature, habits, and nurture of very many animals are described, translated into Latin according to the authority of very ancient codices.

One book of Galen of Pergamum *On Philosophical History*, turned into Latin.

By the same interpreter, that work of Aristotle *On the Nature of Plants*.

He poured Aristotle’s book *On the World* into Latin.

He also wrote other little works, namely, on the regimen of diet and exercises to be observed, especially in old age.

And *Europe Lamenting Herself*.

Andreas de Novo Castro, skilled in scholastic theosophy, published an excellent volume on the four books of the Sentences.

Andreas Stiborius, a Bavarian, canon at Vienna in Austria and a distinguished professor of mathematics, wrote many books, of which only the following have come down to us:

A book of astronomical instruments of the first and second movable [heaven], with their proper old canons.
