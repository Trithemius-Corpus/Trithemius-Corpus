*To the Ecclesiastical Writers, Addition II.*

Whoever you are, if you wish to enter blessed rest,
and so enjoy everlasting peace with Christ,
you must listen to the words of God and follow them.
Therefore, while it is granted to live here, learn to die.
Death is a passage to life, if you believe in him
who, seized by love of us, bore death.

Arsenius, archbishop of Monemvasia, collected apophthegms of philosophers, emperors, rhetoricians, and poets.

The same man collected Greek scholia on seven tragedies of Euripides, namely on Hecuba, Orestes, the Phoenissae, Medea, Hippolytus, Alcestis, and Andromache.

There is also a little book compiled by him, containing various witty poems, and several fables and poetic explanations, and certain passages from the Stromata of Clement concerning the thefts of writers.

And he is said to have collected many other things. He flourished under Charles V, the most unconquered Roman emperor.

Asianus, bishop of Sardis, a man of no common learning, delivered to the emperor Antoninus an Apologeticum for Christians, both pious and catholic.

Audbertus, wonderfully practiced in divine letters, published certain most learned homilies, which Alcuin the Levite collected.

Augustinus Dathus of Siena, philosopher and orator, highly skilled in both sacred and profane literature, composed various little works, of which the following are extant:

On the immortality of souls, ten treatises.
Latin orations, 222. Letters, 3 books.
A fragment of the history of Siena, 3 books. A little book of the history of Plumbino.
A work of elegances, On the Flowers of Eloquence.
A discourse On Pleasure.
An introductory little book On the Order of Learning, addressed to his son Nicolaus.
A little book addressed to his dearer pupils, against the common opinion of many concerning the nine words, namely: Manes, Lucus, Bellum, Officium, Parca, Ludus, Ocium, Coelum, Eumenides.
A little book On the Genius and the [unclear] genialis.

*438 On the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers.*

The first lecture, when he had begun publicly to explain Virgil’s Aeneid.
A little book On the Blessed Life.
Plato’s book On the Bird Halcyon, translated from Greek speech into Latin.
A treatise On the Seven Virtues, namely faith, hope, charity, also prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance.
On the sacraments of bread and water, 1 book.
On the marriages of the saints, 1 book.
A little book of Flosculi, which explains in alphabetical order the force and usage of several words.

Augustinus Dodo, a Frisian, canon at Saint Leonard’s in Basel, once first collected the works of Saint Augustine; for the individual works he had learnedly prepared arguments and judgments on the works, since he was most studious, but plague carried him off at an untimely age in the year in which the work was to be begun, namely [unclear].

Augustinus Iustinianus of Genoa, of the Order of Preachers, bishop of Nebbio, arranged an Octaple Psalter in a handsome order, and rendered both Testaments from the Hebrew, Greek, and Chaldean truth into purer Latin expression.

He flourished under Leo X, Supreme Pontiff.

Augustinus Niphus, philosopher of Sessa, translated many little works of Aristotle and wrote not a few commentaries on them. He wrote a book On the Infinity of the First Mover, addressed to Cardinal Dominicus Grimanus.

And another book On the Immortality of the Human Soul, against the little book of the philosopher Petrus Pomponatius, and the following other little works:

On the Active Sense, 1 treatise.
On the Beautiful, 1 book.
On Auguries, 1 book.
On the Calamities of Our Times, 1 book.
On Courtly Affairs, 1 book.
On the Comparison of Arms and Letters.
On the Profit of Enmities.
The Apology of Socrates. Aristotle’s Rhetoric.
On the French Disease, 1 book.
On the [unclear] figures of the stars, 1 book.
On critical or decretory days, 1 book.
On the false prediction of a flood, which was spread abroad from the conjunction of all the planets that will occur in Pisces in the year 1524, 1 book.
