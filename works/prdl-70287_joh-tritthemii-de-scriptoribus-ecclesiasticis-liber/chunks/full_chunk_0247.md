*On Ecclesiastical Writers, Addition I*

Jerome of Ferrara, of the Order of Preachers, a most ardent declaimer and a man of much learning, was exceedingly erudite; relying on this, he embraced ambition, damnable in religious men, which he ought most earnestly to have strangled in himself. This contagious plague indeed proved to be the cause of his ruin and most bitter death, and no small scandal to his order. For since he attempted many things, presumed much, and raised his horns against the leading men of his profession, he stirred up no small schism throughout all Italy. It could not be extinguished except by the death of his accomplices, a loss indeed to be lamented in so great a man, if he had applied those gifts of mind which he displayed to edification, and not to the ruin and destruction of himself and his own people; since, filled with every kind of learning, he would have brought great aid to Christian letters. Alas, that crafty tempter, going about and always seeking whom he may devour, begrudged us this one man. He wrote one Lenten book, book 1.

A commentary on the penitential Psalms.

He also commented on many prophets.

He also issued a prophecy, in which he foretold many things about this our age, the kingdom, and the disturbance.

At length, condemned under Alexander VI, in the year of salvation 1499, he was burned in the Florentine marketplace with two companions.

Bernardinus Gadolus of Brescia, a Camaldolese abbot, a man of good letters, exceedingly learned in philosophy and canon law, renowned for holiness of life and religion, left us many monuments of his learning, in which he shows himself most erudite. He wrote commentaries on all the books of the Bible.

On fleeing the world and embracing religion, one treatise.

Against pride and ambition.

Also many letters.

And certain other things scattered here and there.

Having ended his life, he is honorably buried in the Camaldolese monastery.

Marius Sanutus, a Venetian patrician, not only a man learned in good letters, but also skilled in administering the commonwealth, was very powerful in eloquence, as can easily be discerned from his little works. For he wrote the following:

*On the Abbot of Sponheim, On Writers*

On the magistrates of the Venetians, book 1.

On the lives of the Venetian princes, book 1.

On the Gallic War, several books, both in Latin and in the vernacular.

He wrote certain other things.

Philippus Beroaldus, a Bolognese by birth, most skilled in both languages, and easily the foremost of all grammarians, rhetoricians, and orators of his age, left no ordinary testimonies of his very fertile genius.

Orations delivered in the exposition of various authors.

A panegyric to Lodovico Sforza.

One oration on the marriage of the Bentivoli.

The story of Gisippus and Titus.

Also that of Galesus and Simon.

One proverbial oration.

One little book on the sayings of the seven sages.

Symbols of Pythagoras, book 1.

On the best state, book 1.

On happiness, book 1.

A declamation of the philosopher, physician, and orator.

A declamation of the drunkard, whoremonger, and gambler.

A lamentation on the Lord’s Passion.

A poem on fortune.

A poem on two lovers.

A poem on Cupid, the god of love.

A poem against a slanderous woman.

Various epigrams.

On earthquake and plague, with notes from Galen, a little work.

Annotations on Servius; annotations on civil law.

Commentaries on Propertius; on Suetonius.

On the Tusculan Questions; on Apuleius.

Annotations on Titus Livy; on Cicero’s Rhetoric.

On Horace Flaccus; on Persius.

On Silius Italicus.

He wrote many other things which have not yet reached us. He died at Bologna, not without great loss to the republic of letters, in the year of the Lord 1510.
