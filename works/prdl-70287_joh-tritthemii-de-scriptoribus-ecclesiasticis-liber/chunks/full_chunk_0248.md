Ambrosius Calepinus of Bergamo, of the Order of Saint Augustine, flourished in this age, a most zealous investigator of divine and human letters. Hence by his own industry and great labor he made common to us a very large work, collected from almost countless authors, on the knowledge of words and the interpretation of Greek and Latin prepositions, with the attestation of authors. In it he explained, learnedly enough and elegantly, not only the words that contribute very much to literature and to the knowledge of poets and orators, but also those that pertain to physicians, philosophers, and also ecclesiastical doctors; and, having explained them, he left them to be read in a truer light than in certain other similar books. These certainly brought no small assistance to learned and unlearned alike. For by this it has come about that things which among the barbarians had already become barbarous are made Latin, while those that among the learned were being called into doubt now lie open, the cloud of uncertainty having been dispelled.

*419 On the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers.*

Petrus Burrus, a Belgian, by country a man of Bruges, canon of Amiens, distinguished in divine and human letters, flourished at Paris under Charles VIII, king of the Franks. There he grew to such renown for good disciplines, virtues, most approved morals, piety, and religion, that very many, and especially the family of the Gandincourts, wished to use this one man alone as teacher for the instruction of their children and of very many other more noble youths. Nor indeed is it at all to be wondered at, since he was one among the few in this our age who taught how to join eloquence, poetry, and the other good arts with wisdom, and professed neither arts without morals nor morals without letters. His works indeed show this; in them you would not know what to admire most: the weight of his very grave and most holy sentiments, or the variety of his style and poetry, or the propriety of things and words, or his skillful joinings, or the charming diction and texture, or the ever-recurring flower and harmony of his verse, or finally hundreds of other ornaments, so that you would rightly say that all the graces, charms, delights, and elegances of speech have been most abundantly granted here, while nevertheless nothing swollen or inflated is observed. Therefore, if this Burrus had written in Quintilian’s time, he certainly would not have said that, of the lyric poets, Horace alone is almost worthy to be read. For this man too is most worthy to be read even by holy nuns. For since he is wholly chaste, which that other is not, and wholly Christian, and sweetly redolent of the Christian religion, he nevertheless rises at times no less than that other, full of delight and grace, and most happily bold in varied figures and words, as it is easy for anyone to discern from his works written below and published.
