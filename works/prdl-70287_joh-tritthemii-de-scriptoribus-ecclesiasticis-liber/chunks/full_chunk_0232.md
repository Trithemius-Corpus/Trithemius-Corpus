*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

On “Blessed are the undefiled,” sermons, one book.
Sermons on the season, one book.
Sermons on the saints, one book.
And certain other works.

He flourished at Utrecht under Frederick III, emperor, and Pope Innocent VIII, in the year of the Lord 1490. And I believe that he is still living.

Georgius Valla of Piacenza, a man most learned in every respect, a philosopher, rhetorician, and very celebrated poet, excellent in intellect and eloquent in speech, who now adorns all Italy with his learning, teaches both languages most flourishingly at Venice; he wrote many distinguished volumes, by whose elegance and usefulness he may deservedly feel that all learned men are indebted and obliged to him. Of these the following are extant:

On orthography, two books.
On the quantity of syllables, one book.
On the figures of orators and poets, one book.
On Cicero, On the Universe, one book.
On the same author, On Fate, one book.
[unclear]
On the Topics, a book of the same author, one book.
“Long since foreseen by me” [incipit].
On the Rhetoric to Herennius, one book.
On Juvenal, one book.

Also of the very acute Josephus, On the Resurrection. Also the book of Brisso on dialectic. Moreover the treatise of Galen the physician on the sects of physicians. The same author’s Introduction to Medicine. Also four books of the Problems of Allendrus. And many other works.

Johannes Camerarius of Dalburg, bishop of Worms, German by nation, a very celebrated jurist, most learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, once a hearer of Rudolf Agricola, most skilled in both languages, a very distinguished rhetorician and poet, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, notable for much experience of affairs, magnificently illustrating the nobility of his family by his character and learning, has written and is writing, both in verse and in prose, certain little works of excellent talent, which he has not yet allowed to come to light, because, being occupied with the affairs of the commonwealth, he has not been able to bring them so far to completion as had been his intention. Yet some of these have become known to us:

A Sapphic poem
On the Death of Rudolf Agricola, one book.
Also On Coinage, one book.
An Oration before Pope Innocent VIII, one book.
Various poems and orations.
And several most elegant letters.

He is distinguished today, in his manly age, in the chair of the church of Worms, a bishop worthy of God, and he is composing various works, under Maximilian, most clement king of the Romans, and the supreme pontiff Alexander VI, in the year of the Lord in which we are writing these things concerning him, 1494.

Baptista Guarinus of Verona, son of the most learned man Guarinus, whom we mentioned in his proper place, a man certainly not inferior to his father, but most learned in every literature and discipline of the liberal arts, a philosopher, orator, and poet most celebrated among all, most skilled in the Greek and Latin tongues and very eloquent in both, wrote and translated many distinguished little works, by which he commends the force and keenness of his almost divine intellect both to those present and to posterity. Of these the following are reported:

A very beautiful work of dialogues
On the Sect of Epicurus, one book.
On the Order of Teaching, one book. [unclear]
On Governing a Kingdom, one book.
On the Letters of Cicero, one book.
On Ovid’s Fasti, one book.
On Lucan’s Pharsalia, one book.
On the poet Catullus, one book.
Letters to various persons, [four?] books.
Also several orations, one book.
And poems of various kinds.
