Guido de Baysio, archdeacon of Bologna, a man most skilled in both laws, acute in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, whose authority among the professors of this science has endured greatly down to the present day, wrote many distinguished volumes, by which, so to speak, he rendered the memory of his name immortal. Of these only a few have so far come to my knowledge. For I have found only the following: a very large and highly notable volume, which he entitled Rosarium:

On the Decree, three books. “Reverend in Christ ...”
On the Decretals, five books.
He flourished under Emperor Adolf, in the year of the Lord 1290.

Thaddaeus, a Florentine by nation, a most skilled doctor of the medical art, who taught that science excellently at Bologna for certain years, hired at a public salary, gathered an almost incredible treasure and acquired a great reputation for his experience. For when he was called by some prince, he wished to have no less than fifty gold coins as his daily wage. Once called by Pope Honorius IV, he refused to come until one hundred gold coins per day were appointed for him; when the pope had been cured of a most serious illness, he bestowed ten thousand gold coins on him. He wrote:

On the Aphorisms of Hippocrates, one book.
On the Prognostics of the same, one book.
On the Work on Acute Diseases of the same, one book.
On Galen’s Techne, one book.
On the Isagoge of Johannitius, one book.
On Preserving Health, one book.
Various Counsels, one book.
And certain other works.
He flourished under Emperor Rudolf, in the year of the Lord 1280.
And at last, an octogenarian, he died at Bologna.

John of Parma, born from the territories of Bologna, minister general of the Order of Friars Minor, a man learned in the divine scriptures, devout in life and conduct, and an outstanding lover of holy poverty, who from the Parisian school, where he had lectured on the Sentences, was elected general in his absence, is said to have written many and various treatises, by which his name has come down to our memory. Of these I have found only:

On the Sentences, four books.
On the Conduct of Religious, two books.
He flourished while the Empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1260.

John Christophori, of the Order of Friars Preachers, exercised in the scriptures by long and continual study and nobly learned in secular philosophy, ready in talent and scholastic in speech, is said to have fashioned many little works on the holy scriptures; their publication made him, useful to those then present, known to those to come. For over a long time he expounded the divine writings publicly by lecturing and explained them with subtle understanding. From his commentaries the following volumes are reported, namely:

On the Gospel of Matthew, one book. On Mark, one book.
On Luke, one book. On John, one book.
On all the Epistles of Paul, fourteen books.
And many other works.
