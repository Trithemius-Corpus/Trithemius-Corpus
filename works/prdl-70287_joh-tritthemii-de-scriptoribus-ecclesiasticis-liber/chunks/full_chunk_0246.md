Felinus Sandeus, of the most noble family of the Sandei, a native of Ferrara, a most accomplished doctor of both laws, and not ignorant of the other good letters, first at Ferrara, then at Pisa, in the public academies of those cities, professed canon law to the great admiration of all. Summoned to Rome by Innocent VIII, while he was occupied in the college of the Rota, where difficult affairs of the greatest importance are treated, both there and in the consultations of the supreme pontiff, nevertheless, since he was of almost divine talent and of most accomplished prudence in both laws, he published many little works and commentaries in that field. And these indeed so clearly, so learnedly, so exactly, that he untied all the knots of this discipline, restored obscure matters to the light, and, with right judgment applied, rendered doubtful matters easy and accessible; in this he brought no small assistance to our jurists. What he published is appended:

First, on the books of Gregory’s Decretals. On constitutions.
On rescripts. On the office of the ordinary. On majority and obedience.
On truce and peace. On joinder of issue. On proofs.
On witnesses. On compelled witnesses. On the credibility of instruments.
On presumptions. On oath-taking. On exceptions.
On prescriptions. On sentences and res judicata.
On appeals. On accusations and inquisitions.
Additions to the Decree, to the work begun by the abbot.
One treatise on the right of patronage.
A sermon on plenary indulgence, whose theme is: Today you will be with me in paradise, etc.
In history, one little book on the Neapolitan kings.
There are also other commentaries of his.
He flourished at Rome under Innocent VIII and Alexander VI.

*On Abbot Spanheim, Concerning Writers*

Matthaeus Bossus, a native of Verona, of the order of the canons regular of Blessed Augustine, a man exceptionally learned in every kind of erudition, but still more learned in elegance and gravity of speech, was held in great esteem among all, both because of the office of preaching and teaching, in which he was long engaged, and because of the various works composed by him, in which he so adorned and polished ecclesiastical doctrine with oratorical pigments that you will find nothing more refined or more elegant. He wrote the following little works:

A Dialogue on Enduring Adversities, divided into two books.
On Conducting Magistracy and Cultivating Justice, to Giovanni Ludovico Bosso, jurisconsult and golden knight, two books.
One book of orations. Books of letters to various persons.

He published certain other things; in all of them, although he overflows with varied learning and is everywhere brilliant, nowhere did he belch forth the smoke of arrogance, but, as befits a religious man, he was everywhere most devoted to holiness and divine wisdom.

He died at Padua in the year of the Lord 1502.

Antonius Cornazanus, a native of Ferrara, a most brilliant orator and poet, so polished, copious, and sweet, especially in poetry, that many think he received poetry from the mouths of angels. For among other things he sang the life and death of the Virgin Mother of God so devoutly, piously, copiously, brilliantly, and holily that all proclaim that the poetry in them is not mortal, but altogether divine. He inscribed and dedicated that work to Hippolyta, queen of the Neapolitans, while she was still a virgin.

He published certain elegies and various poems.
He wrote in prose the life of Bartolomeo Colleoni, the great commander of the Venetians.
Also very many orations, and certain other things.

Georgius Merula, a native of Alessandria, an orator and philosopher most learned in both languages, always served letters well by teaching and writing. He wrote, in many volumes, the deeds of Ludovico Sforza and of his ancestors.

Commentaries on Juvenal.
On Statius and very many others.
He published certain centuries.
Many books of letters.
