*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

John Maronis, English by nation, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and subtly instructed in secular philosophy, outstanding in ability, and an excellent disputant of scholastic questions, composed certain distinguished little works commending his talent, by which he made his name known even to posterity. Among these there is said to be a notable work, which he entitled:

Table of Originals, 1 book.
Against the Wycliffites, 1 book.
And certain others.

He flourished in the school at Oxford, where by teaching, disputing, and writing he earned great praise from all, at about the time when the errors of John Wycliffe were condemned by the archbishop of Canterbury.

John Golein, Norman by homeland, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, prior of the convent of Rouen, a man studious and learned in the divine writings, and sufficiently instructed in secular letters, subtle in ability and clear in eloquence, wrote little works of no contemptible authority, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of later generations. Among these there survives a notable work:

On the Sentences, 4 books.
On the Office of the Mass, 1 book. “Sing to the Lord a song.”
Various Questions.

He also published several other works besides, which have by no means come into our hands.

Henry of Dolendorp, of the order of the brothers of Blessed Mary ever Virgin of Mount Carmel, a man learned in the divine Scriptures through long practice, and perfectly instructed in the disciplines of worldly philosophy, which they call Aristotelian, acute in ability and very well suited for disputing and unraveling scholastic questions, wrote for the instruction of readers certain excellent volumes, of which I have found only:

On the Sentences, 4 books.

Of the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Charles IV and Pope Urban V, in the year of the Lord [1370?].

Gallus, abbot of the monastery of Aula Regia in Bohemia, of the order, as they say, of the Cistercians, German by nation, a man skilled in the divine Scriptures through long practice, and rich in the reading of the ancients, an excellent contemplator of divine things, subtle in ability, open and composed in eloquence, also distinguished in the conduct of regular life, published for the edification of his brothers certain excellent volumes, by which he sent his name to the knowledge of posterity. Among these there survives a great and notable dialogue between father and son, which he entitled:

Pomegranate, 3 books. “Son, as you approach the service...”
Sermons to the Brothers, 1 book.

There is today great use of this volume, the Pomegranate, among the truly devout religious, because it is suitable for beginners, those advancing, and the perfect.

Richard, archbishop of Armagh, primate of Ireland, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy and canon law, distinguished in ability, scholastic in speech, of excellent industry in delivering sermons to the people, wrote several little works of his talent, by which he transmitted his name to posterity. When at one time he was preaching to the people on the matter of mendicancy, he set forth nine conclusions; offended by these, the brothers of the mendicant orders appealed before the people to the Apostolic See. Therefore, when both the archbishop and the brothers appeared at Avignon before Pope Innocent VI and the cardinals, in the year of the Lord 1347, on the eighth day of the month of November, Archbishop Richard publicly set forth, read, and pronounced [unclear] of those propositions, 1 book. “Judge not according...”
He also wrote Sermons, 1 book.
Questions on the Sentences, 1 book.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the times of Emperor Charles IV and Pope Innocent VI, in the year of the Lord 1370.
