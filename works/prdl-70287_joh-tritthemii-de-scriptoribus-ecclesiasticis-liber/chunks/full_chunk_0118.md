But the treatise which he wrote against Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris, was condemned in a general council, as is clear at the beginning of the Decretals, “We condemn.”

He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Rupert, a monk of the monastery of St. Remigius in the diocese of Reims, of the order of the holy father Benedict, a Frenchman by nation, a man trained by constant study in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of the secular disciplines, distinguished in intellect and clear in speech, wrote several little works of his own ability, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these I have seen only the distinguished History of the deeds done in the Holy Land by the Christians in the time of Urban II against the infidels, one book. “Among all histories...” Of the others I have seen nothing.

He flourished under Henry V, in the year of the Lord 1120.

Roswitha, a noble woman, born in Saxony, a nun in the monastery of Gandersheim, which is not far from Hildesheim, was renowned for remarkable talent and learning, and admirable in both kinds of writing. For, exhorting consecrated virgins to chastity and continence, she wrote in Latin six Comedies.

A panegyric on the deeds of the Ottos, in hexameter verse.
In praise of the Blessed Virgin, in elegiac and hexameter verse, [unclear]. “Only hope...”
Likewise the Passion of St. Dionysius in elegiac verse.
Likewise on St. Gangolph and several other saints.
She also had knowledge of the Greek language.

She was contemporary with John the Englishman, who earned the papacy by his learning.

Udechinus, priest of the church in Logenstein, a German by nation, a man learned in the holy Scriptures through long practice, sufficiently instructed in the disciplines of secular letters, went to Jerusalem with many others under the emperor Conrad III in hope of eternal reward. While they often fought with the Saracens in naval battle, he shone forth, beloved for his great devotion and constancy on behalf of the faith of Christ. After returning to his homeland, at the request of Cuno, abbot of the monastery of St. Disibod, then of our order, he wrote in a fairly elegant style, in the manner of a letter or little book, the History of that same pilgrimage, one book. “At your command, father...”
Letters to various persons, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished under Conrad III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1140.

Rupert, bishop of Lincoln, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures, among all the learned men of his time most skilled in the disciplines of secular letters, a philosopher, astronomer, distinguished calculator, easily the foremost theologian of his time, subtle in intellect and clear in speech, transmitted his name with glory to posterity by writing many and various works. Of his little works few have come into our hands. These, however, are reported:

A Summa of Theology, one book.
On the books of Dionysius, four books.
The Summa which is called Numerale, one book. “Those who rule well...”
On the Celestial Sphere, one book. “Our intention in this treatise...”
On the Ecclesiastical Computus, one book. “Computus is the science of number...”
A beautiful Calendar, one book.

He published many other things besides, which have not come to my knowledge.

John, bishop of Chartres, by homeland of Salisbury, formerly cleric and disciple of blessed Thomas, archbishop of Canterbury, a man learned in the divine writings, nobly instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect, eloquent in speech, composed with fine grace, excellent in poetry and prose, and no less venerable for religion than for knowledge, wrote many splendid volumes by which he made his name known to posterity, but few of them have come into our hands. I have read the distinguished work on the trifles of courtiers and philosophers, which he chose to title Polycraticon, eight books. “Most delightful then in many...”
