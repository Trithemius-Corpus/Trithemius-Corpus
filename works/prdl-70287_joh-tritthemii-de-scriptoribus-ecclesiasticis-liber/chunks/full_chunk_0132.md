He is also said to have written many other things, sermons, letters, and various treatises, which have not yet come into our hands.

He flourished with great piety and learning under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

Eustathius, who is Bonaventura, born at Bagnoregio, minister general of the Order of Friars Minor, and afterwards bishop of Albano, cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, formerly a hearer and disciple of Alexander of Hales, a man most learned in the divine scriptures, nobly instructed in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect, distinguished in eloquence, devout and most holy in life and conduct: when his teacher Alexander contemplated his intellect, his natural gift, and the purity of his life, he used to say: “In Brother Bonaventura Adam does not seem to have sinned.” He wrote many profound and most devout little works, in which, bringing forth burning words, he no less kindles the reader’s affection in the love of Christ than he enlightens the intellect with holy teachings. For he easily surpasses all the doctors of his time in the usefulness of his works, if you attend to the spirit of divine love and Christian devotion speaking in him. He is profound, not wordy; subtle, not curious; eloquent, not empty; bringing forth words that are flaming, not puffed up. Hence he is read more safely, understood more easily by one who loves, more sweetly retained the more often he is read, and more fruitfully. Many set forth doctrine; many, though few, preach devotion; by writing books they have taught both. But Bonaventura surpassed many, and few [equaled him], since his doctrine instructs devotion, and devotion instructs doctrine. Therefore, if you wish to be called devout, be attentive to his little works. These are:
