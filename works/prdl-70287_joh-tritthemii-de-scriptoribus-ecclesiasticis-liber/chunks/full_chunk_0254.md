William de Mara flourished at this time, a Neustrian by nation, born in the deserted district of Coutances, begotten by parents of noble lineage and most holy life, now canon and treasurer of the cathedral church of Coutances, a distinguished orator and poet. When he was still a boy, the distinguished theologian Doctor Jean Boucard, formerly bishop of Avranches and royal confessor, took him up to be educated in learning, on account of the excellent gifts of mind which he perceived in the boy. Nor did he only see to having him instructed by others, but also taught him himself whenever he had leisure, taking no little delight in the cultivation of so fertile a field. And he hoped so much from his excellent natural ability that he did not hesitate to endow him with a benefice in tender age, and certainly would have given greater things if he had lived longer. Finally William himself went off to the famous study at Paris, and in a short space of time advanced so far that no one was held more celebrated than he in the art of oratory or poetry. Hence Robert Briconnet, formerly archbishop of Reims and chancellor of France, hearing the reputation of so great a man, summoned him to himself as secretary. When this Robert died, Charles VIII gave this William to his chancellor, Guy de Rochefort. For Charles loved William himself because of the not unpolished rhymes which he knew how to compose most elegantly in the French tongue, and also because of his skill in writing in every kind of letters, in which he found no one equal to him. Afterwards the most reverend cardinal of Saint-Malo, Guillaume Briconnet, obtained this man from Guy de Rochefort to be his confidant; with him he remained for a long time. But at last, weary of the misery of courts and ill bearing the loss of time, he withdrew to the study at Caen, where he was granted the dignity of rector, and afterwards the doctoral laurel in both laws. And in that study he professed law for many years, and at length was taken up as canon and treasurer of the cathedral church of Coutances, where he resides, serving God alone and good letters, with which he is to enrich posterity. These are the testimonies of his learning which now exist:

The Conflict against the Chimera, to Jean de Ganay, great chancellor of France, a truly excellent and holy work, in which he elegantly attacked those three chief vices by which the whole world is infected and poisoned, namely pride, lust, and avarice.

A little work on the most holy eucharist, to Guillaume, bishop of Coutances.

A paraphrase on Musaeus, On Hero and Leander, to Nicolas de Cerisay, dean of Saint-Germain of Paris.

One book On Loves, to the same man.

One book On Praises, to Jean Briconnet, president of the royal accounts.

One book On Reproaches, to Charles Guillart, president of the Parlement of Paris.

One book of dirges and epitaphs.

One book On Divine Things.

Besides these he wrote many things in prose discourse which have not yet come to light.

Matthew of Evreux, a Norman, of the Order of Friars Preachers, a very profound investigator of the Scriptures, a man certainly full of religion and letters, made an excellent postil on the Pentateuch, Isaiah, and very many other books of the Old and New Testament, which is kept deposited in the common library of the convent of Evreux of the aforesaid order, of which he had been an alumnus. He flourished, however, as appears from certain writings of his, around the times of Charles VI, king of the Franks. When his books had been scattered in gatherings, Brother Robert Baiguard, doctor of theology and confessor of the Most Christian king of the Franks Charles VII, of the same order and convent, gathered them together again and deposited them in the common library of the same house.

Guy of Evreux, a compiler of excellent sermons, is believed to have flourished around these same times, a brother of the same order, who also, besides the rules of Tyconius, gave certain very apt instructions for grasping the meaning of sacred Scripture, showing from ignorance of what matters it often happens that one errs in the exposition of sacred doctrine; these are found gathered together among the rest of his works in the libraries of certain convents.
