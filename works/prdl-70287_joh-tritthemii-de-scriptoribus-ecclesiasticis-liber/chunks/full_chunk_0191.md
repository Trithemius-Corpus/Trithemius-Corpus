*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

[Leonardus Aretinus?], first secretary to Innocent VII, Gregory XII, Alexander V, and John XXIII, Roman pontiffs, down to the Council of Constance, and then summoned by the Florentine senate, was appointed general chancellor; in that office he remained until the end of his life with great fidelity and authority. Among other things he wrote an outstanding work of Dialogues, 1 book; A History of His Own Time; On the Good Deeds of the Florentines, 2 books; On the Praises of Florence; A History of the Goths, 4 books; A History of the Punic War, 2 books; The Life of Aristotle, 1 book; On the Life of Cicero, 1 book; A Volume of Letters, 1 book.

He also composed some other works most elegantly, which have not come to my knowledge. Moreover he translated many things from Greek into Latin: namely, the treatise of the blessed priest Basil On the Instruction of Pupils; also, from Plutarch, the lives of Tiberius and Gracchus among the Greeks, and of Paulus Aemilius, Cato the Younger, and Sertorius among the Romans. He likewise translated Xenophon’s little book On the Tyrant, Plato’s Gorgias and Phaedo, and certain letters, Aristotle’s Economics, and the books of the Ethics and Politics. And other things which have not come to me.

He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1420. He died, however, under Frederick III as emperor and Eugene IV as pope, in the year of the Lord 1443, in the sixth indiction, on the seventh Ides of March, in about the seventy-fourth year of his age.

Poggio the Florentine, a philosopher and eloquent orator, perfectly trained in Greek and Latin speech, and for many years a most eloquent writer of apostolic letters, is said to have composed some little works of excellent talent, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Lorenzo Valla was this Poggio’s most bitter rival, and wrote various things against him. He himself published: On the Situation of the Region of India, 1 book; Against Lorenzo Valla, 2 books; Letters to Various Persons, 1 book; Various Orations, 1 book; and many other things.

He rendered Dionysius Siculus and many other Greek authors into Latin. Moreover, at the Council of Constance he delivered very many funeral orations. The filthy work which he entitled Facetiae I have judged rightly to be excluded from this catalogue of illustrious men, since the reading of it offends the devout, harms the unwary, and infects the carnal.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1420.

Lorenzo Valla, a Roman patrician, by far the foremost of the grammarians of this age, a most outstanding philosopher, orator, and theologian, subtle in intellect, sharp, and vehement, who, preferring Latin eloquence to Greek, composed many works for its adornment, and, being most skilled in Greek and Latin speech, published little works not to be despised in reading, by which he transmitted his name with praise to the knowledge of posterity. Among these the following are reported: Elegances, 6 books; On the Reciprocity of sui and suus, 1 book; A Book of Many Letters, 1 book; also On Quintilian, 12 books; Dialectic, 1 book; Panegyric On the True Good, [unclear] books; On Free Will, 1 book; Apologue against Poggio, [unclear] book; Against Antonio da Rho, 1 book; and many other things.

“When often with me the elders,” 1 book. “You alone, Johannes,” 1 book. “When I was undertaking, Paul,” 1 book. “I would very much wish, Garzia,” 1 book. “I hear that Poggio another,” 1 book.

He translated into Latin Homer, Thucydides, and several others.

He flourished in person at the Council of Constance under the emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1429. Concerning him someone says as follows: What did that most biting censor of the Latin language, Lorenzo Valla, beget for himself by his dreadful and exacting reproof? What praise, I ask, did he obtain after death other than that, according to his merits, that fine epigram about him is read and proclaimed everywhere?

Now after Valla, dead, sought the shades,
Pluto does not dare to speak Latin words.
Jupiter would have deemed him worthy of heaven’s honor,
But he himself fears him as censor of his tongue.

What he thought about the Donation of Constantine, by which Constantine endowed the Roman church, his book beautifully shows; would that so great a man had never published it.
