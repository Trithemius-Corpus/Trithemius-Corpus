This man, having been innocently sent to prison by Emperor Louis (I know not on what suspicion), composed the hymn *Gloria laus*. When, on Palm Sunday, as the emperor was passing by near the prison, he sang it in a loud voice, it so pleased the emperor that he immediately permitted him to go away free. Among other things, he wrote in various meters:

One book of hymns.
One book of letters.

He flourished under the rule of Louis, in the year of the Lord 840.

*Einardus* (otherwise Eginhartus), chancellor of the emperor Charles the Great, German by nation, and afterward a monk of the order of the divine father Benedict, a man most learned in secular letters and not ignorant of the divine scriptures, wrote little works of reading not to be despised, of which the following are reported:

Having imitated Bede, who abridged the Hebrew psalter by excerpting from it all verses containing words of prayer, he himself also abridged the Gallican psalter, excerpting from it all verses containing words of prayer. He also wrote, both in his own person and in that of the emperor:

One book of letters to various persons.
One book on the life of the emperor Charles.
And certain other things.
One book of the history of the times.

He flourished under Charles and Louis his son, in the year of the Lord 820.

*Johannes Scotus*, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, formerly a hearer and disciple of Bede, the most illustrious monk and doctor, a colleague of Albinus, coming with him from Britain into Gaul, was held in esteem by the emperor Charles the Great. Since he was a man most learned in all the scriptures, he published in clear language very distinguished little works, of which these are reported:

Five books on the Gospel of Matthew.
One book on human duties.
One book on the division of nature.

He also composed many other things, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished in the times of the emperor Charles the Great, in the year of the Lord 800.
