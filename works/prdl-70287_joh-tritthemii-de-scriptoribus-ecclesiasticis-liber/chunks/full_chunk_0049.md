*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

*On the Instruction of Monks*, one book. If he wrote anything more, I do not know. He flourished under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 420.

John Cassian, a Scythian by nation, formerly a hearer and disciple of blessed John Chrysostom, bishop of Constantinople, and ordained deacon by him, after some years was expelled by the same man for I know not what cause. Consecrated priest at Marseilles, he founded two monasteries, one of men and one of women, in which a great fervor of regular discipline remained for many years. With experience as his teacher he wrote several little works, in which the grace of eloquence is apparent even in the translator. Among these are counted: at the request of Leo the archdeacon, afterward bishop of the city of Rome, he wrote against Nestorius the heretic:

*On the Incarnation of the Lord*, seven books.
*On the Institutes of Monks*, four books. “On the institutes and [unclear]”
*On the Eight Capital Vices*, eight books. “The fifth, with [unclear] helping us”
*Conferences of the Holy Fathers*, twenty-four books. “When in the desert of Scete”

In these conferences of the saints there is useful and necessary instruction for monks; but because he is censured by blessed Prosper on certain points concerning freedom of the will, his little works are counted among the apocrypha. At length, after the publication of many books, he died at Marseilles, not without a reputation for sanctity, while Theodosius and Valentinian were reigning, in the year of the Lord 455.

Lucian, priest of the church of Jerusalem, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and most holy in life and conduct, to whom in the times of Honorius and Theodosius almighty God revealed the place of the tomb and relics of the most holy protomartyr Stephen, is said to have written some little works. Of these there survives a lengthy letter:

*On the Discovery of Saint Stephen*, one book. “Lucian the priest”

Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He wrote that revelation itself in the Greek language; Avitus, a priest Spanish by birth, translated it into the Latin tongue and, with his own letter added, sent it through Orosius to the easterners to be read. He flourished under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, around the year of the Lord 410.
