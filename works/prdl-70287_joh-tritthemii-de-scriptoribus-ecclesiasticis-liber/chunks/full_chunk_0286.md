*Ecclesiastical [Writers], Addition II.*

On the Orator, Brutus, The Perfect Orator, and the Rhetoric to Herennius. He also published annotations on Cato, Varro, and Columella, and commentaries on Aristotle’s Rhetoric. He is still living in the year of the Lord 1546.

Philippus Decius of Milan professed publicly in many places and for many years, more than any other jurisconsult ever in Italy, namely for about 65 years. He left most learned writings in both laws, and besides these four books of counsels, and commentaries on the rules of law, and on the first and second of the Old Digest and the first and [unclear] of the Code.

Philotheus, patriarch of Constantinople, a man learned in Greek beyond what can be said, famous for the keenness of a happy intellect, published a notable book against the writings of the philosopher Gregoras. By the same Philotheus: [unclear Greek].

Encomia of Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian, and John Chrysostom, and on All Saints.

Epitome of Christ Incarnate.

These and certain other works are preserved in Greek at Rome in the Vatican Library.

Photius, patriarch, a man diligent in collecting the books of authors, wrote in Greek two volumes of description and enumeration of the authors, as many as he himself had read.

By the same, a purgation and defense addressed to the Council.

Against those of old Rome, thirteen chapters.

Other works also circulate under his name, which are in Greek, namely:

Letter to the eastern thrones,

On the procession of the Holy Spirit,

On the Holy Spirit,

On the Seven Synods, one book.

Pinytus of Crete, bishop of the city of Gnossus, wrote to Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, a very elegant letter, in which he teaches that peoples must not always be nourished with milk, lest, as little children, they be overtaken by the last day, but that they should also feed on solid food, so that they may advance to spiritual old age.

He flourished under Marcus Antoninus Verus and Lucius Aurelius Commodus, around the year of the Lord 170.

Polycarp, disciple of the apostle John, and ordained by him bishop of Smyrna, was the foremost man of all Asia. He wrote to the Philippians a very useful letter, which is read even to this day in the assembly of Asia.

*On the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers.*

He also wrote to the great Dionysius the Areopagite, and to other churches.

Polydorus Virgilius of Urbino, collector for the Roman pontiff in England and archdeacon of Wells, wrote three books of dialogues on prodigies, and also a work of Adages, both sacred and profane.

By the same, twenty-six books of English History, and eight books On the Inventors of Things.

He flourished in the year of salvation 1499.

Pomerius, a Moor by nation, ordained priest in Gaul, in answer to the questions and requests of Bishop Julian and the priest Verus, replying in the manner of dialogues, with dialectical art and a style suited to his talent, composed eight books on the nature of the soul, its quality, and the special character of its resurrection in this life for the faithful, and the general resurrection of all human beings. I remember that I once read his dictation to a certain man named Principius, an exhortation on contempt of the world and the passing away of things, and another entitled On Vices and Virtues. He is said to have written other things too, and still to be writing, which have not come to my knowledge. He is certainly living to this day, in a manner of life worthy of God, suited to his rank and profession. Thus Gennadius.

Proclus, bishop of Constantinople, a man possessing exact knowledge of sacred letters, wrote a letter on the faith to the venerable and holy bishops, priests, and archimandrites throughout Armenia.

Very many words by the same are reported by Epiphanius himself.

He flourished under the consulship of Theodosius for the fifth time and Valentinian for the fourth.

Prosper Calanus, from Sarzana, a city of Etruria, a very learned man, practiced medicine for many years at Rome and then at Bologna, and indeed by the best and most exact method. He shared with students the lucubrations entitled:

A Commentary on Preserving Health.

A Paraphrase on Galen’s book On Natural Faculties.

An Enchiridion on Guarding against Poisons.

Eighteen commentaries, through which he himself gave counsel to the same number of illustrious and powerful men concerning various illnesses.

A Paraphrase on Galen’s little book On Unequal Distemperament.

Very many letters, sent to various friends on the cure of certain illnesses.

He flourished under Clement VII, supreme pontiff.
