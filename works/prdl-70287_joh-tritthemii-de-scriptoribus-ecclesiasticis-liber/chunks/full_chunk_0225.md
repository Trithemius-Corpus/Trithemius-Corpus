*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

On the Sentences, 4 books. Various sermons, 1 book. Various questions.

He wrote to me some not inelegant letters, in each one of which, at considerable length, he wove together the whole history of his fortune; and, when I had asked him some time before, he humbly confessed that he had made the aforesaid little works.

He died at Cologne, buried in the greater church, under Frederick III, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1491, in the tenth indiction, on 26 August.

Christophorus Porsena, a Roman, prior of St Albina in the City, of the order of the hermit brothers of St William, a man most devoted to the divine Scriptures and very learned, highly skilled in the Greek and Latin languages, since from earliest youth he had imbibed those literatures in Greece itself and under Greek teachers, so that he seemed to have been born from Greece; subtle in intellect and eloquent in speech; wrote, among other things, 1 book:

Letters to various persons,

and many other works.

He translated into Latin Origen’s eight books Against Celsus the Philosopher, dedicated to Pope Sixtus IV; also moral sermons of the blessed John Chrysostom, twenty-five in number, which he dedicated to Marco Barbo, cardinal of St Mark. He also translated quite finely certain little works of Theophylact, archbishop of Bulgaria, with others, and gave them to the Latins to read.

He also translated the volumes of the following writers:

Procopius of Caesarea, On the Gothic War, 3 books.

And Agathias Scholasticus of Smyrna, On the Gothic War and other foreign histories, 5 books.

He also translated certain works of Athanasius.

He died at Rome, carried off by plague, under Frederick III, emperor, and Innocent VIII, pope, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1486, in the fourth indiction.

Hermolaus Barbarus, Venetian by nation, patrician by family, archbishop and patriarch of Aquileia, and at last cardinal-bishop of the most holy Roman Church; a man devoted to the divine Scriptures and most learned in secular letters; a very celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, poet, astronomer, and geometer; highly skilled in Greek and Latin speech; subtle in intellect, eloquent in expression, devout and distinguished in life and conduct, and most worthy of every ecclesiastical honor. From adolescence he devoted zeal and effort to good letters and holy morals, and proved such and so great a man that he left no one his superior. Beginning to write from the eighteenth year of his age, he composed many most elegant little works, by which he commended his memory to posterity. Of their number the following are reported:

On Celibacy, 1 book.

Castigations on Pliny, 1 book.

Against the Barbarian Philosophers, 1 book.

On Marriage, in verse, 1 book.

Questions of Geometry, 1 book.

The Agreement of Astronomy and Medicine, 1 book.

Precepts for Writing History, 1 book.

Compendium of Philosophy, 1 book.

Compendium of Galen, 1 book.

Letters to various persons, 1 book.

Multiple Orations, 1 book.

More than twelve thousand verses of poems, 1 book.

And many other works.

The Castigations on Pliny, a great work which he began as Venetian legate at Rome and completed as patriarch, he dedicated to Pope Alexander VI; in it he healed nearly five thousand wounds of copyists. Once, in the year of the Lord 1486, when he was passing through Cologne on his way to Mainz, at the request of Theodericus Flas, physician of Neuss, he composed a little book, How Far Astronomy Agrees with Medicine. To Marcus Antonius Sabellicus, the historiographer, he published a book on the precepts for writing history. He also interpreted Aristotle’s Rhetoric, the whole Dialectic, and Dioscorides; and he composed elegies, letters, and metrical orations, up to twelve thousand verses of poems, as we have said. He translated into Latin the responsive treatise on the Catholic faith by the patriarch of Constantinople, to the question of Mahomet the great Turk; also Plutarch On Isis and Osiris, and the same author’s Why the Oracles Ceased; to Arnoldus Bostius, the Carmelite of Ghent, a certain little work of Dionysius the Areopagite. He translated many other things as well. His work On Marriage contains about six hundred verses, in which he argues most elegantly whether it is expedient for a wise man to take a wife, concluding that it is not. He died, not without the very great loss of the studious, under Frederick III, emperor, and Alexander VI, pope, in the year of the Lord 1495, in the thirty-ninth year of his age, in the second indiction.
