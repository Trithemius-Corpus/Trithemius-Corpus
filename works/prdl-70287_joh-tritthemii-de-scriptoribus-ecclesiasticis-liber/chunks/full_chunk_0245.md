*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

*Peroration.*

Now therefore, after many labors at last, let us set an end to our work; for although many remain who are worthy of the catalogue of illustrious writers, nevertheless it was impossible for us to find even the bare names of them all. Those, however, concerning whom I was able to have certainty, I have diligently recorded; nor have I knowingly omitted anyone whose little works, talent, and times I could have known.

Nor am I unaware how many most learned men survive today, composing various works; of these I have made no mention, not because I judged them unworthy of our catalogue, but because they either preferred to conceal their little works, or they have not been so widely multiplied that they could come to my knowledge. I have done as much as I could, not as much as I wished, since, had it been permitted, I would have brought together for myself all writers, however many there are or ever have been. I leave this province to posterity, that they may add from their own vein where I, wearied, have left off.

Moreover I beseech you, enrolled Fathers, who hold the heavenly kingdoms, who adorn this catalogue of ours with your names, talents, and lucubrations, that if we have written true, just, worthy, and finally pleasing things about you, you may entreat the mercy of almighty God on our behalf, that, our faults being taken away, he may grant pardon, confirm our holy purpose, illuminate our understanding, purify our affection, and mercifully make us sharers in your joys. But if in some matters we have been able to do less than the thing itself required, grant us pardon, O most sacred ones; for wherever there has been error, it is the fault of ignorance, not of will.

You also, O most distinguished men, writers who still live a mortal earthly life, I address you, I adjure you and beg you: if in this catalogue we have done anything less worthy of your merits, talents, or lucubrations, ascribe it not to our will but to our lack of skill, not refusing the pardon we ask; for wherever we have failed, it was the fault of those who described your little works or talents to us otherwise than the matter stands.

Next we admonish and beseech the reader of this catalogue not to be readily moved against us if he finds the names, dignities, native lands, or little works of writers set down otherwise than perhaps he himself has read elsewhere, since no one has ever been able to reconcile the discordance of histories of time past. Nor have we written anything that had not been confirmed in many places or by testimonies. But, as we professed at the beginning of this work, we willingly yield to the opinion of any benevolent corrector, which truth shall have made solid and charity sincere.

Finally, however, we must laugh at our rivals, who, like dogs barking from the worst habit, condemn everything, disparage everything, tear apart everything that they themselves have not made, or that they have found to be contrary to their own opinions. For as soon as they begin to read even a little, grumbling, they say that it is not true, having become rash judges before they are examiners of the truth. But what have we to do with those who disparage nothing but the good, and praise nothing but the bad? Therefore envious dogs are to be despised, not taught. We, however, although we think it glorious to displease the malicious, consider it much more glorious to have pleased good and learned men; by them, wherever there has been error, we are gladly corrected; once corrected, we are fruitfully amended. Let the love of writings excuse us, for whose sake we have taken up a heavy burden with fearless neck.
