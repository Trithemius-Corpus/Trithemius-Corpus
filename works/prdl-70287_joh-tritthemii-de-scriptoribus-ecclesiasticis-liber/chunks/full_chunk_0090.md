Hincmar, archbishop of Reims, formerly a monk of Saint-Denis of Paris, a man exceptionally learned in the divine Scriptures, and in secular letters second to none in his time, conspicuous for wisdom and uprightness of conduct and most eloquent, wrote several little works of authority not to be despised, of which I found only the following: In the person of the emperor Charlemagne, he wrote, very instructively,

To the Church of Ravenna, one book.
The Life of Saint Remigius, bishop, two books.
To Walo, bishop of Metz, one book.
Many Letters, one book.
Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished under Charlemagne and Louis his son, emperors, in the year of the Lord's nativity 800. And he wrote many doctrines.

Amularius Fortunatus, archbishop of Trier, by nation [unclear], a learned and most eloquent man, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence, was sent by the emperor Charlemagne to Constantinople to the emperor Michael, and with admirable industry and his eloquence confirmed peace and friendship between them, as is contained in the deeds of the people of Trier. Among other things he wrote:

On the Ecclesiastical Offices, one book. Satis et nimium quidem.
To Louis on the same matter, four books.
On the Mysteries of the Mass, one book.
Letters to Various Persons, one book.

He died under the emperor Louis, in the year of the Lord 813, in the sixth indiction, in about the sixteenth year of his episcopate.

Angelomus, monk of Luxeuil, of the Order of Saint Benedict, a man endowed with a noble talent for expounding the divine Scriptures, so that, in comparison with him, the ancients seem not to have expounded those which he himself expounded; not ignorant of secular writings either, at the command of Drogo, bishop of Metz, who was the son of Charlemagne and brother of the emperor Louis, wrote a most excellent work, the like of which I do not remember ever having read elsewhere in the holy Scriptures:

On the Books of Kings, four books. Igitur cum aquam pluribus.
To Lothar on the Song of Songs, one book. Priusquam augustale decus.
On the Divine Offices, one book.
And certain other things.

He flourished under Louis and his son Lothar, in the year 850.

*The Abbot of Spanheim.*

Rabanus Maurus, sixth archbishop of Mainz, formerly abbot of the monastery of Fulda of the Order of Saint Benedict, a German by nation, from the city of Fulda in Buchonia, once a pupil of Albinus the Englishman, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly taught in secular letters, a philosopher, rhetorician, astronomer, and most subtle poet, to whom, that I may speak without envy, Italy bore no equal, nor Germany his peer. For in the monastery of Fulda, while still a young man, he put on the monk, and advanced wonderfully both in morals and in knowledge; hence, consecrated fourth abbot of the same monastery, he presided for twenty-four years over the sheep entrusted to him, by word and example, and composed almost innumerable little works of Scripture. At last, offended by the wickedness of his monks, who, because they said he was too intent on the Scriptures and neglected temporal affairs, the devil working this in them, took displeasure against him, being scandalized in that in which they ought especially to have been edified. Therefore, giving place to wrath and not consenting to remain any longer with ungrateful sheep, he left the monastery and its inhabitants, and moved to Louis the emperor, son of Charles, with whom he also remained many days. But the monks, moved by repentance, sent an embassy to him and asked that he return to the monastery, but they did not obtain it. Therefore he remained with the emperor until the death of Otgar, archbishop of Mainz; appointed in his place, he presided over the aforesaid church of Mainz for nine years. Having also held a synod, he established many things for the benefit of the faithful. While still established in the monastery of Fulda he wrote many volumes, both in verse and in prose, especially on the holy Scriptures, all of which he explained from beginning to end.
