Letters, two books. Thirty-five homilies at the beginning of Lent. Ethics. Expositions on Isaiah. Two manuscript sermons on Jeremiah are extant at Bologna in the library of San Salvatore. At Rome, in the papal library, four Greek volumes are preserved. A homily on προστυχὴ, [unclear], from the third up to the thirty-eighth.

At length, after many labors and holy exercises, through the deceit of Theophilus of Alexandria and of the empress Eudoxia, he died in exile in Pontus, on the Kalends of February, in the year of the Lord 411, in the ninth indiction.

Severianus, bishop of Gabala, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures, and an admirable teacher in delivering homilies to the people, was of incredible eloquence; whence he was frequently summoned by the emperor Arcadius to Constantinople by the aforesaid blessed bishop John, to preach. To him Saint John commended his people when he was being driven into exile, venerating not only his doctrine but the merit of his life. Severianus, however, commending himself to the people more than was fitting, supplied kindling for discord between him and John. But when the patriarch returned from exile, they returned to the bonds of brotherly love, as the homily delivered above in the church of Constantinople testifies. Of the writings of Severianus, a most learned man, few are possessed among the Latins; still fewer have come into our hands.

On the Epistle to the Galatians, one book. On baptism and Epiphany, one book. In the church of Constantinople, one homily. On the advent of the Lord, [unclear]. On the death of the innocents, one homily: “To what end zeal tends, to what [unclear].”

There are extant among us certain other homilies of his, brief indeed, but very useful. Concerning his other little works, though many are reported, I have as yet found nothing. He flourished under Arcadius and Honorius, at the time of John Chrysostom, in the year of the Lord 406.

Vitellius Afer, defending the Donatist schism, a man indeed well exercised in the divine Scriptures, would have composed doctrine useful enough if he had remained in ecclesiastical unity. But because he denounced true Catholics as persecutors and, as it were, betrayers, he was deservedly despised together with his volumes. The following volumes from his writings are extant:

Against the nations, one book. That the servants of God are hated by the world, one book. A complaint against our own people, one book. He is said to have composed many other volumes besides, which have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under Constans, son of Constantine the Great, emperor, in the year of the Lord 360.

Theodotus, presbyter of the church of Antioch, a learned and eloquent man, and practiced in the reading of the ancient authors, wrote against the Apollinarists and Eunomians a most elegant volume, in which by very clear reasoning and authority he refuted the depraved doctrines of the heretics. He entitled this magnificent and notable work:

On the incarnation of the Lord, fifteen books.

He is said to have written many other things also, which have not come to my knowledge.

Acrobius, presbyter, and afterward hidden bishop of the Donatists in the city of Rome, while he was still a presbyter in the Church of God, wrote to the confessors and to the virgins of Christ a volume, moral indeed, but very necessary, of doctrine; in it he chiefly deals with and treats [unclear]. To show that there is one principle, and that nothing material was created from any other source than God, he wrote a sufficiently elegant and notable work, which he entitled On the natures of primordial things, one book.

He flourished first among our people at Rome, afterward among his own Donatists, the Montenses of Africa.

Heliodorus, once a presbyter, studious and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, sufficiently sharp in disputation and of a vehement intellect in the scholastic manner, [unclear].
