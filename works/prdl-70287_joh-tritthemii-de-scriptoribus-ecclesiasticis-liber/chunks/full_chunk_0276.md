*On Ecclesiastical Writers, Addition II.*

On the death of Cardinal Federico Fregoso, one homily.
He also wrote very many letters.
He flourished under Emperor Charles V, in the year of salvation 1530.

Jacobus Spiegel of Schlettstadt, German by nation, skilled in both laws and in languages, by many nights of study commended himself to posterity as one to be admired; among these works, the following are reported under his name:

An Explanation on Johannes Reuchlin's Progymnasmata.

An exposition on the poem of Gianfrancesco Pico on the mystery of the Lord's Cross, recently come down into Germany.

Scholia on the twelve books of Richard Bartholinus's Austrias and on the poet Gunther's work on the deeds of Emperor Frederick.

Scholia on the four books of Antonius Panormita, On the Sayings and Deeds of Alfonso, King of Aragon.

A Lexicon of Civil Law.

He is still living in the year of the Lord 1546.

Jacobus Tusanus, French by nation, public professor of the Greek language at Paris, translated certain parts of Theodore Gaza's grammar into Latin.

Likewise the Greek passages which are found in Angelo Poliziano's books.

He also translated the Greek letters of Guillaume Budaeus and added scholia from Budaeus's own replies.

He flourishes under Francis of Valois, king of the French, 1546.

Jacobus Peretz of Valencia, bishop of Christopolis and a most observant member of the order of the Hermits of Saint Augustine, wrote expositions on all the Psalms of David.

Likewise on the official Canticles, or, as he himself calls them, the ferial and evangelical together, and the remaining ecclesiastical canticles.

To these were added treatises against the Jews, and an exposition on the Song of Songs of Solomon.

Janus Parrhasius, also called Neapolitanus, of Cosenza, Italian by nation, flourished and taught at Milan around the year of the Lord 1500. He wrote a compendium of rhetoric excerpted from the best authors of both languages.

Commentaries by the same man on Claudian's work On the Rape of Proserpina.

Janus Vitalis of Palermo, a man exquisitely learned in divine scripture, wrote hymns to the Holy Trinity. Likewise The Triumph of Ferdinand Francesco d'Avalos of Aquino, the great marquis of Pescara.

The Laments of the same man on that same commander, and a certain poem entitled Archangelus. Some epigrams of his are also extant.

*In the Abbot of Sponheim, On Writers.*

Idacius, a distinguished Spaniard, a very great defender of the Catholic faith against the Arians, wrote a book against Varimadus the Arian. There was also added an explanation of certain very difficult passages concerning the Trinity.

Ignatius, deacon and skeuophylax of the Great Church of Constantinople, who was metropolitan of Nicaea, a grammarian, wrote lives of the holy and blessed patriarchs Tarasius and Nicephorus, funeral elegies, letters, iambics, against Thomas Antartes, the so-called [unclear], and many other things.

Pope Innocent VIII wrote two letters to Angelo Poliziano: one in which he thanks him and sends him two hundred gold pieces for the dedication of the translated Herodian.

The other to Lorenzo de' Medici, in commendation of Poliziano himself.

Joachimus Perionius of Cormery, French by nation, a monk by profession, translated into Latin certain books of the ancient Greeks, especially of Aristotle, with such polish and elegance of style, rivaling Cicero, that no one seems to have taken this praise away from him. Day by day we expect more monuments of so great a talent; but the works we have seen up to now were all written at Paris, where the author resides. He is still flourishing in the year of salvation 1546.

Johannes Atrocianus, German by nation, a learned Catholic man of our time, published certain grammatical commentaries on the common book of Macer On the Powers of Herbs.

An elegy by the same man on the Peasants' War.

Some more select epigrams.

No Evangelical, a little book written in verse. He is planning many other things under Emperor Charles V.

Johannes Baptista Egnatius of Venice, a priest and public professor of humane letters in his native city, wrote three books on the Roman princes or Caesars, from Caesar the Dictator to Constantine Palaiologos, and then from Charlemagne to Emperor Maximilian.

Likewise annotations on the lives of the Caesars whom Suetonius mentions.

On the Origin of the Turks, one book.

Observations on Ovid.

A panegyric on the king of the French.

Gleanings, which interpret some of the more obscure passages of authors.
