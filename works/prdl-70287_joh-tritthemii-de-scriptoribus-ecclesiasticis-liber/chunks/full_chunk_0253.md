Christophorus Longolius, a Parisian, a most ingenious philosopher and a very distinguished orator of our age: as mother nature adorned him with the greatest gifts of mind and marked him out with a natural equipment for investigating the natures of things, so fortune bestowed on him most abundantly the vastest accumulations of goods and a most pleasing dexterity. Reflecting in his mind that kindly nature had breathed into him the noblest gifts of the soul, he so increased nature’s gifts by labor and practice that he emerged as the most distinguished philosopher and most copious orator of our age. He lectured on Pliny in the renowned academy of Poitiers with an inexpressible structure of style, so that he seemed, without distinction, to belong to all, not relying on mortal eloquence but rather strengthened by a variety of thoughts drawn from the inner chambers of the heavens. The Parisian academy, nurse of the disciplines, trained him not inelegantly in morals, in oratorical savor, and in philosophical letters; nevertheless, not satisfied with Gallic eloquence, he hastened to Italian households, judging that he would attain greater things there. There, instructed by ready experience of affairs and polished eloquence, he at last returned to the borders of Gaul, where he gave testimonies of his eloquence and learning. For scarcely passing his twenty-fourth year, consulting posterity with an innumerable variety of volumes, he published monuments of his learning and left them to his followers in this arranged order:

An oration on the praises of Saint Louis, king of the Franks, and of the nation of the Gauls, addressed to Francis of Valois, the Dauphin.

Nearly innumerable orations and letters.

Various elegies and epigrams.

Commentaries on Pliny, which he learnedly and elegantly interpreted publicly at Poitiers, to the highest admiration of all.

Certain writings on civil law, while he was publicly professing it at Poitiers.

Johannes Stephanus flourished in this our age; by nation an Italian, bishop of Vercelli, cardinal of Bologna, a most exacting cultivator of literary learning. The more fortune raised him to the most excellent and eminent dignities, the more nature adorned him with nobler gifts of mind. He, indeed, always placed all things, even the most illustrious heights of dignities, after literary leisure and the investigated natures of things.
