*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

Various orations to the clergy, one book.
And certain other things.

At great expense and with long labor he collected the works of Johannes Gerson, many of which he at last brought to us from France, dividing them into three parts in a very fine order, and compiling a very useful table; he gave that whole work to the press. He is still living in the German city of Strasbourg, and composes various works, under Maximilian, the most illustrious king of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 1494.

Johannes Picus, an Italian by nation, count of Mirandola, lord of Concordia, a man most devoted to every kind of good literature, and, just as most noble in distinguished blood, so also in almost divine talent, surrounded by a great household throng trained in all sorts of arts, with whom he exercises himself in the arena of letters and studies, Latin as well as Hebrew and Greek; most worthy and deserving, one whose journey the Muses should follow; the most learned philosopher of our age and a very keen disputant: he wrote, in a most elegant style, some works which our age will both approve and, above all, admire, by which he has not unworthily made the memory of his glorious name everlasting. Of these there are extant the works that have already appeared:

The *Heptaplus* on the beginning of Genesis.
Likewise, nine hundred philosophical conclusions.
An apology.
Likewise, on the Psalms, one book.
And several other works which have not yet presented themselves to our sight.

He is still living today among the Italians, amid the flock of his talented young men, and he does not cease either to write higher things or to do good to posterity, under Alexander VI, supreme pontiff, and Maximilian, king of the Romans, in the year of the Lord in which we write these things, 1494.

Johannes Capnion, otherwise Reuchlin, a German by nation, from the town of Pforzheim in Swabia, an excellent doctor of imperial laws, counselor of the illustrious count Eberhard of Württemberg, both most eloquent and wholly upright in law and faith, a most diligent investigator of all the most ancient philosophy and literature, a most learned interpreter of the three principal languages, namely Hebrew together with Chaldean, Greek, and likewise Latin; also especially renowned in French and in our more polished vernacular tongue; and most learned both as secretary in divine writings and in secular letters. Indeed, in every kind of learning he is foremost at this time, since for many years he has most earnestly penetrated the secret mysteries of the Greeks and Hebrews.

*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

Worthy indeed to devote himself to letters alone, and not also to the noisy popular court. For not only Italy, but not even our own age, could set before us anyone equal to so great a man. He wrote a number of works, by which he commended his memory to those now living and to posterity. To the bishop of Worms, Camerarius Dalburg, he wrote a dialogue, in which he explains all the secret, wonder-working words of Greek, Hebrew, and Christian philosophy, which he entitled

*Capnion, or On the Wonder-Working Word*, five books.
*On the Preliminary Exercises of the Orator*, one book. It is from the ancients.
*On the Diversity of the Four Idioms of the Greek Language*, one book.
A funeral oration on the death of Rudolf Agricola.
A book of epigrams and elegies, one book.
Epistles and scholastic orations, many without number.

Besides these, he translated for our common use many small works from Greek into Latin: Xenophon’s *Apology for Socrates*; Nestorius the heretic; the *Confession* of Cyprian; Proclus on the Blessed Virgin; some dialogues of the dead by Lucian; by the same author a dialogue on the council of the gods; the *Monomachia* of Homer’s *Iliad*, on the duel of Paris and Menelaus, in the German language, in verse; Epiphanius on the life and death of the prophets; Maximus Tyrius, the Platonic philosopher, in the oration, *If God Made Good Things, Whence Do Evils Come?* He is living still today, devoted to letters, under King Maximilian and his own prince Eberhard of Württemberg, whose illustrious name that distinguished Capnion makes still more illustrious by his talent, in the year in which we write these things, 1494.

Conradus Leonorius, commonly called de Leonberg, a German by nation, a monk of the monastery of Maulbronn, of the Cistercian order, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, exceptionally learned in secular subjects, a philosopher, orator, and poet not to be dismissed, skilled, as they say, in the three principal languages, subtle in talent and eloquent in speech, wrote some small works in verse and prose, by which he made his name known. Of these there is extant a volume of

Letters to various persons, one book.
Various orations. Many poems. And certain other things.

He is living still today among the Cistercians, and persists in the study he has begun, under Maximilian, the most illustrious king of the Romans, in the year of the Lord 1494, in the twelfth indiction.
