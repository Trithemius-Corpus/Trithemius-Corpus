Maurice, bishop of Paris, very studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and nobly instructed in secular philosophy, since before his episcopate he taught theology gloriously for many years at Paris, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, was of excellent zeal in delivering sermons to the people. He is said to have composed certain distinguished little works, by which he made his name known to posterity. Of these there are said to be, throughout the cycle of the whole year, both on Sundays and on feast days:

*Sermons to the People*, one book.

*On the Care of Souls*, to priests, one book.

But the rest which he is said to have written have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1190.

Papias, a Lombard by nation, a man most learned in secular letters, the most renowned grammarian of all in his own time, fully instructed in Greek and Latin speech, and also not moderately exercised in the divine Scriptures, wrote in a twofold style, in both literatures, certain excellent little works, of which few have come to my knowledge:

*On the Order of Speaking*, two books.

*On the Words of the Latin Language*, three books. It begins: “All in this book [unclear]”

*Letters to Various Persons*, four books.

Concerning the rest I have found nothing.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

John Beleth, once a distinguished theologian of the school of Paris, learned in the divine Scriptures and sufficiently instructed in secular philosophy, ready in intellect, scholastic in speech, not undistinguished among the doctors of his time, by writing and disputing brought his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. For he wrote the not useless

*Summa on the Divine Offices*, one book. It begins: “In the primitive church”

Also various *Sermons*, one book.

In addition he is said to have written not a few other things, but they have not come to my knowledge.

*The Abbot of Spanheim*

Theoderic, abbot of the order of St. Benedict, a German by nation, a man studious in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, distinguished no less in manner of life than in knowledge, is said to have composed some little works, of which I have seen only, addressed to certain abbots:

*The Life of Saint Hildegard*, four books. It begins: “In the Roman commonwealth”

And certain letters.

He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Werner, otherwise Irnerius, an illustrious and notable man, a professor and most renowned interpreter of civil law, and an eminent cultivator of the other humane letters, who was deservedly called the lamp of the law, because he was the first to expound civil law with little glosses and, without example, gave to others an example of illuminating the law. And from this time the professors of both laws always shone in abundance, and they began to explain the volumes of their instruction down to the present day. He taught in both for a long time at Bologna, where he had many and excellent disciples, who by their learning spread the fame and glory of their master. His glosses and distinguished monuments on the laws are extant, which are very well known among the professors of this field.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Peter, abbot of Cluny, of the order of St. Benedict, a man learned through constant exercise in the divine Scriptures, gentle in intellect, sweet and well-composed in speech, and sufficiently fit for persuasion, devout and praiseworthy in manner of life, and a distinguished zealot for regular observance, making his name known to posterity by writing, composed certain devout little works for the usefulness of readers, of which these are reported:

*Revelations of Many Persons*, one book.

*On the Eremitic Way of Life*, one book. It begins: “I have received the letters of your affection”

*Letters to Various Persons*, one book.

He published some other things also, which have not come to my knowledge.
