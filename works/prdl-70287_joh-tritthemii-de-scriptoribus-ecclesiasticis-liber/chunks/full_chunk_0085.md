He also published many other works besides, which have not come to the knowledge of our reading. His little works, even while he was still living and always writing new ones, were of such authority that, by ordinance of the prelates of England, they were read publicly in the churches. And when in the heading of the homilies they wrote, according to custom, the name of the author, “of the venerable priest Bede,” since they could not proclaim him otherwise while he was alive, the title once assigned from the beginning has endured down to the present day, so that Bede is called “venerable” rather than “saint.” For while he was alive it was not permitted to call him otherwise, even if he was holy. There are those who invent other reasons for this title “venerable”: some from the inscription of the epitaph, others dreaming that Bede was blind. But they are wrong, since Bede was not blind, nor do we know that such an epitaph was inscribed on his tomb. And indeed, if I were not consulting brevity, I would very easily refute these ravings. He died under the emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 732, in the fifteenth indiction, in the seventy-second year of his age, on the day before the Kalends of June.

Gregory, the second pope, a Roman by birth, sat in the chair of Peter after the pontiff Constantine for sixteen years and nine months, a man certainly learned and most holy; he ordained Boniface, a monk, English by nation, as bishop of Mainz. He wrote certain little works full of learning, of which the following are reported:
To the Questions of Justinian.
Against Leo Augustus, one book.
To John, bishop of Constantinople, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.
He died under the emperor Leo, in the year of the Lord 734, in the fourteenth indiction, on the Ides of February; Gregory the Third succeeded him.

Boniface, the first archbishop of Mainz, a Scot by nation, a monk of the order of St. Benedict, when he was a boy of five years was offered by his parents to a monastery in England which is called Mischele. There, devoting himself to letters and morals, he became both a learned and most holy man. After he had been ordained priest, out of love of pilgrimage for Christ he came to Germany; and there, preaching the word of God, he was ordained bishop of Mainz by Pope Gregory the Second, and by his successor Gregory the Third he was raised to archbishop, primate of Germany. Since he was a learned man, he taught the people committed to him not only by word but by writings. Of his little works I have found only:
Letters to various persons, one book.
The Life of Certain Saints, [unclear] books.
He died in Frisia while preaching, crowned with martyrdom, in the times of the emperor Pippin, in the year of the Lord 755, in the eighth indiction, in the thirty-sixth year of his episcopate, on the Nones of June. His body blessed Lullus, whom he had ordained archbishop after himself, caused to be brought back to the monastery of Fulda, situated in Buchonia, and to be honorably buried.

*The Abbot of Sponheim*

Verus, bishop of Hispalis in the province of Baetica, where Isidore once held the episcopate, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, sharp in intellect, brilliant in speech, distinguished no less in life than in learning, wrote for the edification of the faithful, especially of the church of [Aransica?] in the Gauls, renowned for virtues and miracles:
The Life of Bishop Eutropius, one book. And certain other things.
He is said to have flourished in the times of the emperor Pippin, in the year of the Lord 760.

Walafrid, abbot of St. Gall, as is said, a German by nation, a man devoted to the divine Scriptures, in secular letters the most learned of all in his time, renowned for eloquent talent, strong in verse and prose, a monarch of regular discipline, father of many monks, wrote for the edification of all the faithful who read devoutly:
The Life of St. Gall in prose, one book. The same afterward in meter, one book.
On the Divine Offices, [unclear] books. Many letters to various persons.
And he is said to have written some other things which have not come into our hands. He flourished in the times of Augustus Tiberius, around the years of the Lord 700.

Gregory, the third pope, presided over the universal Roman Church after Gregory the Second for [unclear] years, a pious, merciful, and most holy man, very learned both in secular letters and in the divine Scriptures, distinguished in Greek and Latin eloquence. Having held a synod at Rome, he excommunicated the emperors Leo and Constantine, who despised the images of the saints, and deposed them from imperial rule. He also wrote, among other things, to the same emperors:
An Admonition, one book. To the same, one book.
Against the same, [unclear] books. Letters to various persons, one book.
