He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Pilleus of Modena, professor of both laws and a very celebrated interpreter, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, distinguished in learning and cautious in counsel, was held in the highest esteem among the older jurists. By writing and teaching various things in that faculty he transmitted his name with glory to the knowledge of posterity. Among his little works there is found a certain notable and not negligible *Summa*, which he entitled:

*On the Order of Trials*, 1 book. It begins: “The name of Christ having been invoked.”

Of the other things that he wrote, however, nothing has come to my knowledge.

John Bosianus, by country a Cremonese, a most famous expounder of laws, teacher of Azo of Bologna, the very distinguished jurisconsult, a man of great learning and reputation, who appeared first among the expositors of law and by his example gave many both spirit and form for study, is said to have written certain excellent little works in that faculty, by which he gave his name to immortality. Among these there is extant a brief *Summa* on the Pandects, which is entitled:

*Ventosa*. A lecture on the Code, 9 books.

And certain other things.

He flourished under Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 120[unclear].

Stephen the Englishman, once an ornament and rector of the school of Paris, studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, and in Aristotelian philosophy second to none in his time, taught theology gloriously for many years, and was the first to begin to expound Scripture scholastically and morally. And from this the custom took root that masters of theology should more clearly open the divine books to their students in their lectures in the schools. This Stephen wrote, as I have learned, the following little works:

On the five books of Moses, 1 book.
On Joshua, 1 book.
Also on the book of Judges, 2 books.
Also on Kings, 4 books.
On the twelve minor prophets, 2 books.

He published many other things besides, which have not yet come to my knowledge.

He flourished around the times of the rule of Henry VI as king, but as the fifth emperor crowned by the pope, in the year of the Lord 120[unclear].

*Abbot of Sponheim*

Bagorotus, distinguished professor of both laws and a most celebrated interpreter, not ignorant also of other letters, whose authority among the ancient jurists was not small, on account of his excellent talent for teaching and his zeal for writing, also made his name known to posterity. Among his little works there is extant a notable *Summa*, which he entitled:

*Cavillations*, 1 book.
*On the Order of Trials*, 1 book. It begins: “By the prayers and insistence.”

He is said to have written very many other things besides, but they have not come to my knowledge.

Lacantinus, an Italian by nation, a most celebrated cultivator and expounder of both laws, won great glory by teaching and writing at Montpellier. He once held a place among the chief doctors among the professors of this art, because of certain highly commended volumes which he composed; among these, among others, there is said to be extant:

*A Summa of Trials*, 1 book.
Also on the Code, 9 books. It begins: “Since it is dangerous for me.”

Concerning the others I have found nothing. Mantua.

He flourished in the times of Henry VI, in the year of the Lord 1200.

Rofredus, by country a Beneventan, a very distinguished doctor in both laws, subtle in intellect, composed in speech after the scholastic manner, and through long practice sufficiently suitable for resolving doubts and questions of law, wrote in that faculty little works not to be despised by students, by which he consecrated his name and reputation to immortality. Of these, however, we have been able to find only a few, which we have set down below: a very useful volume containing:

*Questions of Civil Law*, 1 book.
*Questions of Canon Law*, 1 book.
*A Summa of Law*, 1 book. It begins: “If I were to consider the intellect.”

On all actions. What more he published has as yet by no means come to my knowledge.

Peter, archbishop of Sens, learned in the divine Scriptures by constant study, rich in the reading of the ancients, and nobly instructed in secular letters, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, wrote little works of no small usefulness, by which he both cultivated his own talent and prepared material for the edification of others. He wrote very subtly, with the opinions of the fathers appended:

On all the epistles of Paul, 14 books.
Also various sermons, 1 book.

He also diligently expounded various books of the Bible, and is said to have written other treatises, varied and useful, which have not come to my knowledge.
