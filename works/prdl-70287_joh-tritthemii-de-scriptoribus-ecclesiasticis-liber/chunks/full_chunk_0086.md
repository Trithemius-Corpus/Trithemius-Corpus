John, monk and priest, surnamed Chrysorrhoas, a Damascene by birth from Syria, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, and no less renowned for his life than for his knowledge, encouraged and stirred by the writings of Pope Gregory III, in which he attacked the error of the emperor Leo, himself also wrote for his Greeks, in the Greek language:

Against the emperor Leo, 2 books.

And certain other things. He flourished under the emperor Leo, in the year 730.

Willibald, first bishop of Eichstatt, a Scot by birth, brother of Saint Walburga, virgin and abbess of Heidenheim, monk and disciple of Saint Boniface the martyr, of the order of Saint Benedict, a learned and most holy man, distinguished in talent and eloquence, wrote for the edification of the faithful, at the request of Saint Lullus, archbishop of Mainz, in a sufficiently distinguished style:

The Life of Saint Boniface, bishop, 1 book. “Therefore the illustrious and truly...”

I do not know whether he published anything further. He flourished under Pippin, father of Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 760.

Pope Zacharias, a Greek by birth, after Gregory III presided over the universal Church for ten years. He was a most learned man, who on account of his erudition and the holiness of his life is rightly judged comparable with the best pontiffs. He translated the four books of dialogues of blessed Pope Gregory the Great from Latin into Greek, and enlightened many ignorant of the Latin tongue by their reading. He also wrote from his own vein, in Latin speech, very elegant and useful:

Letters to various persons, 1 book.

Among these several to Saint Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, are extant. He died under Pippin, two years before the martyrdom of the same Saint Boniface, in the year of the Lord 752, in the fifth indiction, on the Ides of March.

Alcuin, or Albinus, monk and deacon, abbot of the monastery of Saint Martin of Tours, an Englishman by birth, once a hearer of Saint Bede the priest, a man most learned in the divine writings, and in knowledge of secular letters second to none in his time, excelling in verse and prose, summoned from Britain by the emperor Charlemagne, was held in such intimacy by him that he was called the emperor’s beloved teacher; by his instruction the emperor himself strove to be initiated in all the disciplines of the liberal arts. *The Abbot of Sponheim*

The same man, by command of Charlemagne, collected the homilies on the Gospels of the Lord’s days, weekdays, and feasts of the whole year by the most excellent doctors of the catholic Church, and reduced them to order. They are these:

Of Saint Augustine, 48; of Bede, 48; of Gregory, 39;

of Jerome, 6; of Leo, 21; of Severinus or Severianus, 1;

of Ambrose, 10; of Chrysostom, 2; of Haymo, 7;

of Hilary, 1; of Maximus, 17; of Anselm, 14;

of Fulgentius, 2; of Hericus, 37; of Isidore, 15;

of Origen, 4. The sum of all: 254 homilies.

He says that he wrote many other things, which nevertheless have not come to my knowledge. He flourished under the empire of Charlemagne, in the year of the Lord 770.

Stuartus, monk of the order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by birth, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters, ready in talent, distinguished in eloquence, by command of the emperor Charlemagne, after Eusebius, Jerome, and Bede, gathered from the writings of the ancients the deeds and passions of the holy martyrs, confessors, and virgins into one volume of no small size, which we are accustomed to entitle:

Martyrology, 1 book. And certain other things.

He flourished under the same emperor Charles, in the year of the Lord 780.
