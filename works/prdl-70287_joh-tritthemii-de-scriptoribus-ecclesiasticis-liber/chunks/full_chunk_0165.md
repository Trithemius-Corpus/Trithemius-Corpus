Gregory of Rimini, of the Order of the Hermit Friars of St. Augustine, prior general, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and very well instructed in scholastic philosophy, distinguished in talent and eloquence, earned from everyone in the University of Paris great renown for his learning, since he shone forth as very celebrated in life and doctrine. He wrote some little works of no contemptible reading, of which the following are considered the more important:

On the Sentences, 4 books.
On the Epistles of Paul the Apostle, 14 books.
And certain other things.

He flourished in the time of Emperor Charles IV and Pope Clement VI, in the year of the Lord 1350.

Philip of Bergamo, prior of the monastery of Saint Mary of [Avantia?] in Padua, of the Order of our holy father Benedict, a man studious and learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, outstanding in talent and distinguished in eloquence, wrote several little works of holy doctrine, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these I have found only a large volume On the Ethics of Cato, 1 book. He is also said to have written Sermons, To the glory and praise, 1 book.

The other things that he composed have by no means come to the knowledge of my reading.

John de Beka, German by nation, clerk of the church of Utrecht, a man very studious and learned in the holy Scriptures and not ignorant of secular philosophy, published certain little works of his talent not to be despised. Of these I have seen only a work written in a simple and clear style, addressed to lord John, bishop of the church of Utrecht, and William, duke of Holland, on the deeds of the bishops of Utrecht and the counts of Holland; carrying it down from the time of Saint Willibrord, the first bishop, as far as the year of the Lord 1346, he wished it to be entitled Chronicle of the Church of Utrecht, 1 book. Therefore from the year [unclear]. Of the rest I have seen nothing.

He flourished in the times of Louis IV and likewise Charles IV, emperors, in the year of the Lord 1500.

Francis Petrarch, Tuscan by nation, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and, in secular letters, by far the most learned of all his time, a philosopher, rhetorician, and most celebrated poet, who after a long silence called back the letters of humanity, dead, so to speak, from the lower world to the upper, shone forth distinguished no less for holy conduct than for knowledge. For out of love of Christ and of saving philosophy he took up the solitary life, in which, persevering until death in meditation, prayer, and writing, his fame was carried through the whole world. Of his little works I have found:

On the Solitary Life, 1 book. Few men have I known.

On the Remedies of Fortune, 1 book. Monica in tranquil [unclear].
What indeed are we doing now, 1 book.
On the Seven Penitential Psalms, 1 book.
On the Conflict of Cares, 1 book.
The Life of Scipio Africanus, 1 book.
Bucolics, in verse, 1 book.
Letters to Various Persons, 8 books.
Africa, to King Robert, 1 book.
On the History of Griselda, 1 book.
