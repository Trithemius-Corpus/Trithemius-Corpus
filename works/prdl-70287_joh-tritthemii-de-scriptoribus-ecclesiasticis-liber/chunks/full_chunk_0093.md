[He] wrote many little works, both in verse and in prose, of which few have come into our hands. For I have read only the volume which he entitled the *Sequentiarium*, which he wrote while he was still a monk in the monastery of Saint Gall, to Lutuardus, bishop of Vercelli, archchaplain of the emperor Charles the Great. This work begins thus: (Epistle 1) “Summe sanctitatis meri”; “Grates nunc omnes redd[amus?],” 1 book.

The *Sequentiarius* begins thus: “De musica symphonia,” 1 book.

*On the Expositors of Sacred Scripture*: “Cum prudentis sis prud[ens?],” 1 book.

Letters to various persons, 1 book.

Concerning the other things which he composed, nothing has come to the knowledge of our reading. Pope Nicholas I approved this most learned man’s *Sequentiarium* and ordered it to be sung at Masses. Because of the renown of his learning and life, he was first abbot and then, in his old age, was consecrated bishop; tenth in order, he presided over the government of the church of Liège, under the emperor Lothar, son of Louis, in the year of the Lord 850. Under him he died, not without a reputation for holiness.

Christianus, also called Druthmarus, monk and priest of Corbie, of the order of Benedict of holy memory of Monte Cassino, an Aquitanian by birth, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, imbued with Greek and Latin speech, coming from Aquitaine into Gaul, made his name known by writing. Of his little works I have so far deserved to see only one great and excellent work, written in plain speech for the monks of the monasteries of Stavelot and Malmedy, standing under one abbot in the diocese of the church of Liège: *On the Gospel of Matthew*, 1 book. “Hunc titulum non fecit euan[unclear].”

Of the others I have seen nothing.

In the prologue to his exposition of the Gospel according to Matthew he also promised that he would write on the other Evangelists; he is said to have done so, but it is unknown to us.

Wandelbertus, deacon and monk of the monastery of Prum, called [Premensis?], of the order of Saint Benedict, in the diocese of Trier, a German by nation, a man instructed in the divine Scriptures, splendidly learned in secular letters, quick in talent and clear in speech, wrote not a few volumes, both in verse and in prose. Of these I have seen only a certain work addressed to Abbot Marcuardus: *The Life of Saint Goar the Priest*, 1 book.

*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

*The Miracles of the Same*, 1 book. *Martyrology in Verse*, 1 book.

He flourished under the emperor Lothar, in the year of the Lord 850.

*Almannus*, monk of the monastery of Hautvillers, of the order of Saint Benedict, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and excellently taught in secular letters, subtle in talent and clear in speech, wrote various little works, both in verse and in prose, by which he made his name known also to posterity. And because in his time Francia was being ravaged by the Normans, after the example of the prophet Jeremiah he lamented the desolation of that land and of his own monastery, composing it in a fourfold alphabet, 1 book. *The Life of Bishop Vinardus*, 1 book. *The Life of Saint Syndulphus*, 1 book. *The Life of Saint Helena Augusta*, 1 book. *The Translation of the Same*, 1 book.

He is said to have written some other things also, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished under the emperor Charles III, in the year of the Lord 890.

*Milo*, monk of the monastery of Saint-Amand, of the order of the divine Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man most learned in every respect, an orator, a poet, and a distinguished musician, and no less venerable for uprightness of morals than for knowledge of the Scriptures, wrote not a few volumes both in verse and in unbound prose. Of these the following especially are reported, addressed to the emperor Charles the Bald:

*On Sobriety*, in verse, 1 book. *The Life of Saint Amandus*, in verse, 1 book. Letters to various persons, 1 book.

He flourished in the times of the second emperor Charles, surnamed the Bald, in the year of the Lord 870, and composed many excellent things.

*Hugaldus*, monk of the monastery of Elno, of the order of Saint Benedict, a Gaul by nation, a man most learned both in divine Scripture and in secular letters, in music, poetry, philosophy, and the other arts of human culture second to none in his own time, as the many writings composed by him in either style testify. Of these I have so far found only the following:

To the emperor Charles the Bald, a remarkable work of 136 verses, in which all the words begin with the one letter c, as: “Carmina clarisone caluis cantate camoenae”; it is entitled *On the Praise of Bald Men*, 1 book. “Carina clarisone cal[unclear],” 1 book. *The Deeds of Saint Lebuinus*, 1 book. *On the Art of Music*, 1 book. Letters to various persons, 1 book.

He also described the lives of many saints in ornate speech, and composed chants in their honor in sweet and regular melody.
