*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

Expositions of the divine names, 1 book. Formulas of spiritual [unclear], To the sons Solon and Uranus, 1 book. An epitome of Cassian, 4 books. Certain letters to various persons, 1 book. To Bishop Hilary, On the praise of the desert, 1 book. The beginning of the commentaries on Genesis. Instructions, 2 books. He flourished under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 440.

Hilary, bishop of Arles, to whom Eucherius, bishop of Lyon, when he was seeking the wastes of the desert, published a book On the Praise of the Desert, was a man learned in the divine scriptures and sufficiently eloquent in speech. His merits of holiness the catholic Church proclaims. He showed such compassion toward the poor that, after all his possessions had been cheerfully distributed for their use, he was thereafter called, and was, the father of poverty. Among other things he wrote, in quite distinguished style, the Life of his predecessor, St. Honoratus the bishop, 1 book; almost countless homilies; and certain other works. He flourished under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 440. He died, however, under the emperors Valentinian and Marcian.

Zosimus, pope, a Greek by nationality, sat in the pontificate after St. Innocent for one year and eight months. He was a learned, holy man, beloved by God, who by word and example governed the Church entrusted to him [unclear], and for whose edification he elegantly enacted many provisions, as is contained in his acts. He wrote for the ecclesiastical estate to various persons: Letters, 1 book. From the report of our brother [unclear]; On Easter, to Hilary, 1 book; On the priesthood, to Esychius, 1 book: “Your affection demands before...” He died under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 419, in the second indiction, on the seventh day before the Kalends of January.

Boniface, the first pope of that name, succeeding blessed Zosimus, presided over the Church for three years and four months. He was a man of great learning and quite fit for the governance of souls, who enacted many provisions for ecclesiastical utility, which are found scattered in the canons of the holy fathers. He wrote, for the edification of the faithful, as they say, some little works; of these I have found only: Decretal letters, 1 book: “To the Roman Church, which...” He died under the emperors Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 422, in the fifth indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of November.

Proba, a most elegant woman, wife of Adelphius, Roman proconsul, a woman most learned in secular literature and not ignorant of the divine scriptures, burning with marvelous zeal, surpassed her sex by her learning. Perfectly instructed in Greek and Latin letters, and excelling both in meter and prose, since she held Virgil word for word in her mind, she conceived the idea of describing the New and Old Testament from his verses and of showing, by his own poems, that he had thought in this way. And so, courageously undertaking the work laid up in her mind, carefully going through all the books of Virgil, taking now this verse, now that one, sometimes half a verse, and sometimes smaller particles of verses, with marvelous industry, preserving everywhere the measure of the feet, she described the catholic volumes in the poet’s verses. This work, composed in almost seven hundred verses, she entitled with a Greek word, A Virgilian cento, 1 book: “Long since to have profaned...” If she wrote anything further, I do not know.

Nevertheless this work is counted among the apocrypha, since, although a devout woman undertook a pious study, Virgil nevertheless never intended that his little verses be referred to the honor of Christ or to our faith. For the interpretation of this matter is not proper but fictive, assumed by Proba. She flourished, however, as some think, under Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 430.

Eudoxia, wife of Theodosius the Younger, a woman most devout in the Christian religion and most learned both in the divine scriptures and in secular letters, returning from Jerusalem, brought with her the relics of the most holy protomartyr Stephen and placed them honorably in the basilica of the blessed martyr Lawrence. Since she was very learned and excellent in prose and verse, she is said, in imitation of Proba, wife of Adelphius, to have bent Homer’s poems back toward the Christian faith, likewise placing before the little work she published the following title: Homeric centos, 1 book. She also wrote certain other works. Since Saint Jerome, in a certain letter, declares that he had read Homeric centos and Virgilian centos, they must necessarily have been composed before his death. She flourished with her husband Theodosius, who took up the empire in the year of the Lord 423.
