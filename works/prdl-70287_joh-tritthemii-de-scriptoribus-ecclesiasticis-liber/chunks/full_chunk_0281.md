*On ecclesiastical writers, second addition.*

…a very old man, distinguished by knowledge of various things, is said to have made many things Latin from Greek; of these I have as yet seen nothing except Symeon Seth, *On the Powers of Foods*. He is also reported to have written other things, which you have below:

Certain poems.
The Life of Hercules.
A treatise on the Muses.
On years and months, and the other divisions of time: an easy and clear dissertation on this material, difficult and obstructed up to this point.
The Roman Calendar.
On tombs and the various rite of burial, one book.
Some dialogues on poetry and the history of poets.
On nautical matters, one book.
On the vessels that pertain to the sacrifices of the gods and their ceremonies, one book.
He flourished in the year of the Lord 1543.

[On the authority of the printed text “Lilium,” which the OCR places at the head of the following entry:] Aegidius [Lilius?] Libellius of Città di Castello, a disciple of Gregorius Tifernas and teacher of Raphael Volaterranus, endowed with Latinity four volumes of the works of Philo the Jew. Likewise Dio on kingship, and some books of Chrysostom.

Lippus Brandelinus Aurelius, an Augustinian Hermit, published *Christian Paradoxes*.
The same man wrote a dialogue on the condition of human life and on enduring bodily sickness, to Matthias Corvinus, king of Hungary.
Some poems by the same man on the death of Platina are extant.
He flourished under the emperor Frederick III, around the year of the Lord [1480?].

Lucianus Mantuanus, a monk of the order of Saint Benedict, a man learned in Greek and Latin, made Latin the commentaries of Saint John Chrysostom on the Epistle of Paul to the Romans, and wrote a defense of Chrysostom against those who suspect and accuse Chrysostom of having weakened divine grace and exalted freedom of choice beyond measure.

Lucius Ioannes Scoppa, an Italian by nation, a grammarian, published at Naples *Grammatical Collectanea*.
By the same man, a *Spicilegium*, and collectanea on various passages of authors.
He flourished around the year of the Lord 1512.

Ludovico Bigo Pittorio of Ferrara, a man renowned for remarkable learning and excellent dexterity of character, composed an elegant book on heavenly hymns and epitaphs.
Likewise two books of epigrams.

*Against the Abbot of Spanheim, On Writers.*

[Agentinus?], metropolitan or archbishop of Mytilene, a man deeply skilled in sacred scripture and philosophy, published a most learned exposition on Aristotle’s book *On Interpretation*.
Commentaries by the same man on the two books of Aristotle’s *Prior Analytics*.

Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra, flourished under the rulers Constans and Constantine, and wrote many volumes on various subjects, especially against the Arians.
The books of Asterius and Apollinarius are carried about against him, accusing him of the Sabellian heresy.

Marcus Antonius Antimachus, a man powerful in both languages and at this time professor of Greek letters at Ferrara, wrote an oration in praise of Greek letters.
The same man translated two books of Gemistus Pletho on the deeds of the Greeks after the battle at Mantinea.
The precepts of Dionysius of Halicarnassus on panegyric, nuptial, birthday, and epithalamic oratory.
Likewise the precepts of Demetrius of Phalerum on clauses and incises, on periods, on composing letters, on characters of speaking, and the preface of Polyaenus on military matters.
He himself also composed eight books of epigrams, and some things in Greek prose.

Marcus Hieronymus Vida of Cremona, poet and bishop of Alba, a man skilled and learned in composing verse, published certain poetical works, of which the following circulate among the learned:
The *Christiad*, that is, on the life, deeds, and death of Christ, six books.
On the art of poetry, three books.
On the care and use of silkworms, two books.
On the game of chess, one book.
Hymns.
Bucolics.
Odes.
A letter to Giovanni Matteo Giberti.
Likewise some epigrams.

Marcus Marulus, or Marullus, of Split, published an *Evangelistarium* divided into seven books, six books.
And on instruction in living religiously.

Marcus Vigerius, priest of Santa Maria in Trastevere, wrote two books on the Christian decachord to Julius II, supreme pontiff.
