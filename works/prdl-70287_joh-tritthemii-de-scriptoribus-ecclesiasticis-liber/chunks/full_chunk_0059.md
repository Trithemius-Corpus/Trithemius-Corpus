*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

A certain Socrates, Greek by nation, a learned and eloquent man, a distinguished historiographer, renowned for experience of great affairs, wrote, from the time of the first Constantine, the great emperor, down to the reign of Theodosius the Younger, a volume of *Ecclesiastical History*, one book.

He is also said to have written several other things, but they have not come into our hands. And it should be noted that three Greek authors, namely [Theodoret?], bishop of Cyrus, Sozomen, and this Socrates, each wrote histories of ecclesiastical affairs at almost the same time; Epiphanius Scholasticus translated these into Latin as they stood, and from them Cassiodorus Senator, a monk, made one continuous history, which he called the *Tripartite*.

Vigilius, a deacon and monk, a man studious and learned in the holy Scriptures, a guardian and director of monastic discipline, is reported to have fashioned certain little works for the edification of monks, which distance of places and times has prevented from coming to our knowledge. The priest Gennadius says, however, that he wrote a very useful rule:

*On the Conduct of Monks*, one book. He says nothing about the rest. He flourished under the princes Honorius and Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 420.

Osius, bishop of Corduba before Isidore, to whom Chalcidius the grammarian is recorded to have translated Plato’s *Timaeus*, a learned and eloquent man, presiding at the council of Sardica, uttered many excellent opinions, as is contained in the acts of that same synod. In his old age, however, greatly terrified by Constantius, the Arian prince, and fearing lest, as an old and wealthy man, he should suffer either exile or loss of property, he soon consented to Arian impiety. But not long afterward his life, as he deserved, was ended by a cruel death. For after his impious betrayal, when Saint Gregory, bishop of Eliberis, had come to Corduba by imperial decree and refused to communicate with Osius, the latter, moved with anger, asked Clementinus, Constantius’s prefect and vicar, to drive Gregory into exile. The vicar said to him: “I do not dare send a bishop into exile unless you first depose him from the episcopate.” Osius, tolerating no delay, mounted the chair as judge and summoned Gregory by royal edict. When Gregory appeared before the multitude, and Osius, proud in his arrogance, tried to pronounce the sentence of deposition, Gregory appealed with all his strength to Christ, that he should not allow so unjust a presumption of his to go unpunished. And behold, as the impious Osius sat there and wished to pronounce the sentence, his mouth twisted, and at the same time his neck was distorted; falling to the ground, he suddenly expired. Fear then seized everyone; thereafter no one dared touch Gregory. Osius, while still a Catholic, wrote:

*To His Sister, On the Praise of Virginity*, one book.

In the aforesaid council, eighteen opinions, one book.

And several letters. He flourished under Constantius as a very old man, by whom also he was deceived, and he died around the year of the Lord 590.

Ithacius, bishop of the city of [Lemica?] in Spain, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, renowned for his manner of speaking, who, together with Bishop [Ursacius?], because of the killing of Priscillian, whom he had accused at Trier, was separated from communion with the Church and driven into exile, and died there. He wrote works not to be despised, among which these are reported:

*Against Priscillian*, one book.

*Chronicles from the Time of Theodosius*, one book. And certain other things.

He flourished under the venerable emperor Theodosius, in the year of the Lord 400.

Leporius, priest and monk, studious in the divine Scriptures, but presuming too much on the purity of his life, taught as doctrine that he lived religiously by his own judgment and effort, not by the grace of God, defending the Pelagian error. But after being admonished by the Gallic doctors and corrected in Africa by Saint Augustine, he abandoned his error; the heresy which he had formerly preached he afterward vigorously attacked. Therefore, giving thanks for his correction, he wrote:

*A Corrective against Heresy*, one book.

And certain other things.

He flourished under the prince Theodosius the Younger, in the year of the Lord 450.

Sedulius, priest, a Scot by nation, from early youth a disciple of Hildebert, archbishop of the Scots, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures and very learned in secular letters, excellent in verse and prose, leaving Scotland out of love of learning, came into France, then traversed Italy and Asia; finally, departing from the borders of Achaia, he shone forth in the city of Rome, renowned for wondrous learning.
