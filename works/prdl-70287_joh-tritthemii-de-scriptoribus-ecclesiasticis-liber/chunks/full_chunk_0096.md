*On Ecclesiastical Writers*

To the monks of St Gall, one book.

Otfridus of Wissembourg: On the Psalter, three volumes, three books. On the Last Judgment, one book. On the joys of the heavenly kingdom, one book. Poems of various kinds, one book. Letters to various persons, one book.

He composed many other things besides, which, lost through the negligence of earlier men and erased and torn through the ignorance of monks, came to me. He flourished under the emperors Louis, Lothar, and Charles, succeeding one another, in the year of the Lord 870.

Drevaldus, also called Albertus, a monk of the abbey of Fleury, of the Order of St Benedict, a man in every respect distinguished and very learned, made his name known by writing, when for the edification of simple brethren he composed many volumes on the holy Scriptures in a varied style. Among the other small works of his talent he wrote, from the time of the translation of the body of St Benedict from the abbey of Monte Cassino to the monastery of Fleury down to the time of Odo, king of the Franks, a history in lucid language, containing:

The Miracles of St Benedict, one book.

And many other works which I have not yet seen. He flourished in the times of the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 890.

Odo, once the pupil of the monk [unclear] of Auxerre, a man devotedly learned in the holy Scriptures, an outstanding restorer of the fallen order of the divine father Benedict, shone forth no less clearly in holiness than in learning. He was a distinguished musician, archcantor of the church of Tours, who, when he heard the fame of Berno of Gigny, a most holy abbot, entered the order under him; later, appointed abbot of Cluny, he adorned it with great holiness. Since he was an exceptional declaimer of homilies, he composed many sermons for the brethren. Among the other works of his talent he also wrote:

The Life of St Gerard, one book. Praises of St Martin, one book. A concise Chronicle, one book.

He also composed various hymns and chants in honor of the saints, in sweet regular melody. At the request of the brethren, too, he abridged the books of the blessed pope Gregory on Job and brought them together into one volume. He flourished in the times of the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 920.

Rathod, the fourteenth bishop of Utrecht, a German by nation, a man very learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, subtle in intellect, renowned in speech, most holy in life

and conduct, shone with many miracles both living and dead. Nanno, a very learned philosopher, had instructed him in all humane letters; he had been advocate in the palace of King Charles the Fat, and afterward entered a monastery. Finally, having been consecrated bishop, as we have said, he vigorously governed the church of Utrecht for seventeen years during the grave persecution of the Danes. He wrote some excellent little works, of which these are reported:

Praises of St Martin, one book. Praises of St Boniface, bishop, one book. On St Amalberga, one book. Homilies and sermons, one book. Flowers concerning St Willibrord, one book.

He also composed an entire office for the translation of St Martin, and various chants in honor of the saints. He died under Louis, son of the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 917, indiction [unclear], on the Kalends of December, in the eighteenth year of his episcopate, buried at Deventer, where he had previously been accustomed to reside in his bishopric.

Aurelian, a cleric of the church of Reims, very devoted to the holy Scriptures and nobly learned in secular letters, an excellent and distinguished musician in his time, wrote to Bernard, archcantor and later bishop, a distinguished volume on the rules of modulations, which they call tones or tenors, and on their names, whose title is:

A Regular Tonary, one book. He wrote certain other things. He flourished around the times of the emperor Arnulf, in the year of the Lord 900.

Regino, abbot of the monastery of Prüm in the diocese of Trier, of the Order of St Benedict, a German by nation, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and nobly taught in secular letters, easily held the first place among the teachers of Germany and Gaul in his time. He wrote little works of great authority, of which I have found only those set below: to Albero, first bishop of Metz, from the birth of Christ down to the year 908 of the same, containing chiefly the deeds of the Franks and Germans. He wrote:

A History or Chronicle, one book. On Ecclesiastical Discipline, two books. “First one must inquire…”

Of the rest I have not yet seen anything. He wrote the aforesaid work, which is entitled On Ecclesiastical Discipline and the Christian Religion, at the command of Rathbod, archbishop of Trier; he brought it together from the decrees of the holy fathers and general councils into two books, and clearly divided it, according to custom, by rubrics into nearly nine hundred chapters.
