*Abbot of Spanheim.*

Henry of Brussels, a monk of the monastery of Affligem of the order of the blessed father Benedict, a man devoted to divine Scripture and skilled through continual practice, an excellent philosopher and computist, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence, wrote several commendable little works, by which in his own time he became known far and wide. Of these there are reported:

On the Method of Computation, one book. A Calendar on Incensions, one book.

And certain other works, which have not yet at all come to my knowledge.

Alexander of Alexandria, the sixteenth minister general of the order of friars, a man most learned in the divine writings, and second to none in his own time in Aristotelian philosophy, subtle in intellect, scholastic in speech, profound in sense and understanding, composed certain little works in each science, by which, exercising his talent, he brought his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these there are reported:

On the Sentences, four books. Various Questions, one book.

On Aristotle's Metaphysics, twelve books. On the books On the Soul, three books.

He also commented on other books of Aristotle and wrote various works. He flourished under the emperor Henry VII, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Eckard, a German by nation, of the order, as some will have it, of Preachers, a man learned in the divine writings and, in Aristotelian philosophy, the most learned of all in his time, subtle in intellect and distinguished in eloquence; but, while pursuing philosophy too much, he everywhere curiously mixed in novelty of terms contrary to the custom of theologians and introduced various errors, among which there survive seventeen articles condemned by the supreme pontiff and revoked at the end of his life by the same master Eckard. Many of these are contained in his exposition on the Gospel of John. Finally, the masters of the theological faculty of the school of Heidelberg, in the year of the Lord 1430, publicly declared the same errors, with certain others, condemned. Eleven articles, however, were left as suspect, all of which were customarily appended separately to his little works as a precaution. Nevertheless, where he wrote in Catholic fashion, he gave quite profound and useful teachings. Among his books I have found:

On the Sentences, one book.

On Genesis, one book.

On Exodus, one book.

Richard Malumbra, a native of Cremona, formerly a hearer and disciple of Jacobus de Arena, a man not moderately learned in imperial law, sufficiently instructed in secular philosophy, who for many years publicly taught the laws in the school of Bologna, where he acquired no small praise for his learning. Although he was the most learned of all the doctors of his time, nevertheless he is read to have been condemned of heresy. He wrote many volumes in explanation of civil law:

On the Code, nine books. On the Institutes, four books.

On the Digest, fifty books. Various Questions, one book.

And many other works.

He flourished under the emperor Henry VII, in the year of the Lord 1310.

Lampertinus de Ramponibus, a man most learned in both laws and nobly educated in the other arts of humanity, distinguished in intellect and eloquence, and notable for the practice of many virtues, wrote in that faculty many splendid volumes, by which he transmitted his name to the knowledge of posterity with great praise. Of these there are reported:

On the Code, nine books. On the Old Digest, twenty-four books.

On the New Digest, twelve books. On the Infortiatum, fourteen books.

And certain other works.

He flourished under Henry VII, in the year of the Lord 1310.
