Lanfranc, archbishop of the church of Canterbury in England, formerly abbot of the monastery of Caen, of the Order of St Benedict, an Italian by nation, from the city of Pavia, a man most learned in divine Scripture and, in secular letters, especially dialectic, in the opinion of all the most learned man of his time, and no less renowned for holiness than for knowledge, left outstanding monuments of his talent. Peter Damian, formerly a monk of the holy father Benedict, bishop of [Poliziano?], and cardinal of the most holy Roman Church, a man devoted to and learned in divine Scripture, and not ignorant of secular letters, gentle and acute in intellect, calm and orderly in eloquence, and quite capable in persuasion, shone forth no less renowned and distinguished for holiness than for learning. Following the example of the great bishop Gregory, out of love for solitude and quiet he left his bishopric, after he had governed it for some years [unclear], and returned to his monastery; thereafter he spent his life with his brothers in tranquillity of mind. When, however, he was reproved by the supreme pontiff with a charitable rebuke for having, though a useful and suitable man, dismissed the episcopate out of love for his own private salvation, he published apologies addressed to three Roman pontiffs who succeeded one another, Nicholas II, Alexander II, and Gregory VII; in these, humbly excusing his deed by authority, reason, and example, he placated their minds. Bruno, archbishop of the Ruthenians, formerly a monk of the Order of St Benedict, an Italian by nation, a man learned in divine Scripture and an excellent declaimer of homilies to the people, holy indeed and worthy of God, who, preaching the Catholic faith in Prussia, was lawfully crowned with martyrdom by the enemies of Christ. He wrote some distinguished little works of his talent, of which there survive:

Commentaries on Genesis, two books. And certain other works.

He suffered at the hands of the Prussians under St Henry of Bamberg, who was the second king of this name and the first emperor, in the year of the Lord 1008, in the fifth indiction. After they had afflicted him with many tortures and cut off his feet and hands, finally by beheading him they made him a martyr of Christ.

Erhard, a monk of the Order of St Benedict, a man devoted to divine Scripture and learned through continual exercise, subtle in intellect and ready in speech, was of excellent diligence in declaiming homilies to the people. By expounding the divine Scriptures he made his name known, and by frequent examination of them he became an outstanding teacher.
