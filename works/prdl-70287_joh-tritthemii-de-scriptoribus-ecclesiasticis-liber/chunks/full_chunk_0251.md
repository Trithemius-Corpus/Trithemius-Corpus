*In Abbatem Span. Dei Scip.*

...he imparts to the disciplines, consulting for his name and for posterity, as he has already begun, by the many works which he has brought to light. Their titles are set down below:

A Sibylline poem on the Last Judgment.
An eclogue, named Eumorphus.
A poem on the foolish vows of women.
A dialogue in praise of Jacques Lefevre d'Etaples, book 1.
Elegies, book 1.
Epigrams, book 1.
Many various letters to different persons.
Far greater works are expected.

*Johannes Bartholus of Paris*, a doctor of medicine, the most excellent of all men of this age, and for that reason specially endowed by various most devoted and expert lovers of every discipline with the name of Apollo and Aesculapius, is of such authority and learning among all that, whether you seek what belongs to his own profession or what belongs to any other doctrine, you will find absolutely no one to compare with him. Indeed, most accomplished in every kind of discipline, he discusses everything so learnedly, so elegantly, so skillfully and readily, and has so deep a knowledge, that he alone seems to have embraced most eagerly all arts, both divine and human.

Antonius [Sylviolus?] of Paris, a distinguished poet and orator, although he knows many kinds of languages, nevertheless excels in three, namely French, Latin, and the common Italian tongue. By these, among all men, and especially among the most learned and most noble men, and above all among the lords of the house of Amboise, the greatest and most renowned family of France, he has won for himself such fame and favor that no disparagement of the envious can dissolve it, no decay of devouring age can abolish it, since by this threefold gift of language he has already sought immortality for himself, and among our people, for this most pleasing fluency in the French language, he is held in no less esteem than Dante among the Florentines. How great an eloquence he possesses, how much he is endowed with the light of his genius, with what subtlety of invention he stands out, in what skill of arrangement he excels, with what richness of variety he abounds, and finally with what weight of things and sentiments he proceeds: his distinguished works give the fullest testimony. For he wrote many excellent works, first of all several comedies in his own vernacular, in which he showed how varied, how abundant, how eloquent, how elegant, how witty, and finally how urbane he was; in Latin, however, he issued the following:

*Ecclesiastical Writers, Addition I.*

[unclear], book 1. Epigrams, book 1.
Several weighty dialogues.
A Triumph concerning the Venetians.

He wrote, and day by day writes, other far greater works, by which he may show that the French have their own poets and orators, not inferior to the Italians, indeed superior to them, if they devote constant, unwearied labor to these coverings of language, by which every Italian either flatters or catches at favor. He still lives and breathes the ethereal air, the orator of the reverend father in Christ, George of Amboise, archbishop of Rouen.

Wilhelm Cop of Basel, a most learned philosopher, highly skilled in Latin and Greek, in this our age excels at Paris in the art of medicine, of which he is a most eminent doctor and professor. To his house not only Parisians flee, as to the sole remedy of health, but even those who rule over this kingdom of ours, having heard the man’s reputation, send for him most swiftly and summon him, and experience his aid. Hence he alone among all the chief men of this profession has won himself a name, I do not know whether immortal or rather envied. This Wilhelm is subtle in intellect, polished in expression, eloquent in speech, admirable in learning, and to be placed after no one in knowledge of natural things. He lives at Paris under Louis the Twelfth of that name, with great renown and expectation of himself among all. For he is forging things for posterity to read, among which we already have:

The healthful precepts of Paul of Aegina, clothed by him in Latin elegance.
Likewise the *Praenotastica* of Hippocrates.

Jodocus Clichtoveus of Nieuwpoort, a most consummate doctor of theology, a most elegant philosopher, and so distinguished an orator that he brings the savage manners of men, and the whole mortal race, into order by eloquence divine rather than mortal. He was born for these duties: to guide those ignorant of letters with straight reins, to instruct [unclear] with polished eloquence, and to illuminate, with the darkness driven far away, those deprived of the splendor of philosophy with a most brilliant radiance. Mother Nature marked this man with these most noble gifts and dignity of soul, so that whatever he undertakes in this world of sensible things he adapts to the propagation of learning. For he strives with all his might to polish whatever is rough, foul, gaping, and barbarous, to restore to philosophy its own brilliance, and he resolves in mind and soul to devote his zeal for interpretation especially to sacred utterances.
