*On Ecclesiastical [Writers], Addition II.*

Endowed with Greek and Latin eloquence, leaving his homeland, he came to Milan, where he applied himself equally to Latin letters and to theology.

He translated the books of Thomas Aquinas into the Greek language, which are preserved today in the Vatican Library.

He wrote *Against Eunomius, on the Divinity of the Son*. Finally returning, he stopped in Crete, where, having distributed his goods to the poor, he lived most holy in a certain monastery there, though without profession, and there likewise died, about two hundred years ago.

He also wrote *On the Procession of the Holy Spirit*, against Cabasilas, who had drawn his pen against St. Thomas Aquinas.

He rendered from Latin into Greek the book of Richard, a monk of the Order of Preachers, against the Mohammedan sect.

He similarly translated certain works of St. Augustine.

He published a book against a certain Palamas.

*Sermons on Pentecost.*

Also letters and dialogues, all of which survive in Greek in Italy, especially at Rome.

Desiderius Erasmus was born at Rotterdam, which is a town in Holland, a province of Lower Germany, in the first years of the reign of Frederick III, emperor; the restorer of the republic of letters and its foremost illuminator, he spent his life in the most celebrated cities and schools of Germany, France, England, and Italy. After countless labors expended for the sake of students, correcting ancient authors corrupted by the injury of time, translating Greek works, illuminating and explaining the New Testament and certain other things, and finally composing very many new works, he died at Basel in the year 1536.

Whoever wishes to know all his volumes should read his own letter written to Johannes Botzheimus Abstemius.

Dexter, son of Pacianus, famous among the learned and devoted to the faith of Christ, is said to have woven, for me, a comprehensive history, which I have not yet read. Thus St. Jerome.

Diego Hurtado de Mendoza, an illustrious man both by hereditary nobility and by learning and hidden wisdom, ambassador of Caesar’s Majesty among the Venetians, although occupied with the administration of the greatest affairs, nevertheless does not abandon his former studies of letters and philosophy. Especially well taught in philosophical and mathematical matters, he undertook a lucid paraphrase of the whole of Aristotle. He was likewise not only the author of the history of the Tunis expedition, but also, in good part, took charge of it together with his brothers. Many offspring of the most celebrated writers have, by his labor and industry, been restored to their former beauty, among which we have all the works of Flavius Josephus in Greek. He is still living in the year of salvation 1546, intending to restore several Greek copies to students.

Diodorus, bishop of Tarsus, was more renowned while he was a presbyter at Antioch. His commentaries on the Apostle are extant, and many other works, belonging rather to the style of Eusebius of Emesa; although he followed his sense, he could not imitate his eloquence, because of his ignorance of secular letters. He flourished in the time of St. Jerome.

Dominicus Nanus Mirabellius, a citizen of Alba and doctor of arts, wrote the *Polyanthea*, a very useful work, which partly, after the manner of a lexicon, explains not a few words in alphabetical order, and partly brings forward the opinions of every kind of writer on virtues, vices, and other commonplaces.

Donatus Acciaiolus, a citizen of Florence, translated from Plutarch’s *Lives* *Hannibal* and *Demetrius*. Nothing sweeter than his style could be wished for; its ease and ornament display the man’s most agreeable character.

Not only his learning is praised, but also the justice of his life.

He came forth from the school of Argyropylus; he died while on an embassy.

He left complete commentaries on Aristotle’s *Ethics to Nicomachus*, written learnedly and elegantly.

Donatus Bossius, a citizen of Milan, wrote chronicles, or books on the changes of human conditions, from the beginning of the world down to his own times.

Also on the bishops and archbishops of Milan down to the year 1489.

Dorotheus, a man distinguished by rare holiness, composed nineteen sermons in Greek, together with the lives of the holy fathers Antony, Sabbas the Younger, Euthymius, and Athanasius; on the martyrdom of Saints Eustratius, Auxentius, Eugenius, Mardarius, and Orestes. By the same author there is a certain Greek book without a title, and another *On Love*, and twenty-one chapters of teachings.

Also a monastic work. All survive in Greek at Rome in the pontifical library.

A certain Durandellus, a monk of the institute of St. Dominic, wrote against Durandus, of the same order, an opponent of St. Thomas; he began thus: “You thought that I would be wickedly like you; I shall rebuke you, I shall set it before your face.”
