*The Abbot of Sponheim.*

David, of the Order, as they say, of Minors, German by nation, a man practiced in the divine Scriptures, distinguished in life and conduct, was of excellent talent in delivering sermons to the people. He wrote certain little works in a plain style, the reading of which is not to be despised by devout brothers. Of these the following are extant:

To novices.
On the composition of the outer man, one book.
On the composition of the inner man, one book.
Sermons on the season, one book.
Sermons on the Saints, one book.
And certain others.

He flourished not long after Blessed Francis; he is buried at Augsburg.

Humbert, of the Order of Preachers, the fifth master general, from his homeland a man of Vienne, formerly a pupil of Hugh of Barcelona the theologian, a man learned in the divine Scriptures, an outstanding preacher, evangelical in life, pure in conscience, excellent in action, sweet in speech, fit for persuasion, who once drew Hugh, his own teacher of sacred theology, into the order: he wrote certain devout little works, by which his name came to the knowledge of posterity. Among them these are reported:

On the Rule of Saint Augustine, one book.
A Summary for Preachers, one book.
The Deeds of the Friars Preachers, one book.
On the offices of his order, one book.
On the three substantial things of religion, one book.
Letters to various persons, one book.

He flourished while the empire was vacant, in the year of the Lord 1250.

Vincent the Gaul, Burgundian by nation, of the Order of Preachers, called of Beauvais, a man devoted to and practiced in the divine writings, and rich in ancient reading, subtle in intellect, ordered in speech, applied so much zeal to letters that he could never be called away from their cultivation by any labors, vigils, or occupations, since he was always either reading, writing, or preaching. Accordingly he was so great that after himself he necessarily had an equal, if we were to weigh with the others, in an even balance, the arduous little works that he wrote, laboriously collected from many sources. For, at his request and with the king of the Franks supplying the expenses, he brought together with the greatest labors a great and admirable work of Mirrors concerning everything, so to speak, knowable, in many volumes, which he entitled and divided thus:

*On Ecclesiastical Writers.*

Historical Mirror, 32 books. God is a substance in
Natural Mirror, 53 books. Since the multitude in the book
Moral Mirror, 5 books. In all your things remember
Doctrinal Mirror, 4 books. Since the multitude in the book
Also On the Grace of God, 40 books. The book of the generation and [unclear]
On the Praises of Saint Mary, 7 books. If God with prophetic mouth [unclear]
On the Praises of Saint John the Evangelist. Blessed John the evangelist [unclear]
On the Education of Boys, 1 book. You have sons; instruct them [unclear]
A Consolation on the Dead, 1 book. First, therefore, attend
Letters to various persons, 1 book. He also published some other works besides, which have not come to my knowledge.

He flourished under Frederick, the second emperor, in the year of the Lord 1240.

Alexander of Hales, English by nation, of the Order of Minors, teacher of Cardinal Eustathius, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and second to none in his time in secular philosophy, subtle in intellect and clear in eloquence, when he was a doctor of sacred letters in the Parisian school, entered the order of the poor of Christ so that he might become a true disciple of heavenly philosophy; he wonderfully adorned it by his learning and holiness. At the command of Pope Innocent IV he wrote a distinguished work divided into four volumes, usefully embracing many things, which he entitled:

Summa of Theology, 4 books. Since, as Boethius says. To the reverend in Christ [unclear]. The rest of his works I have not seen.

It is said, however, that he annotated both the Old and the New Testament for the most part, and discussed many matters subtly concerning the concord of divine and human law, and composed various other works, which have not come into our hands.

He flourished under Frederick II, in the year of the Lord 1240.

John of La Rochelle, of the Order of the Friars Minor, master of sacred theology at Paris, learned in the divine Scriptures and magnificently taught in Aristotelian philosophy, who, together with Alexander of Hales, subscribed himself in the declaration of the Rule of the Minors to the minister general: he published certain excellent little works, by which he transmitted the fame of his name to the knowledge of posterity. Of these the following are reported:

On the Sentences, 4 books.
A Summary of virtues and vices, 1 book.
Also On the Soul, 1 book.
