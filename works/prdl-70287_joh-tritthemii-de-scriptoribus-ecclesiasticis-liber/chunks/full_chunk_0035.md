He flourished under the great prince Constantine, in the year of the Lord 350.

Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia and afterwards of Tyre, learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular literature, an eminent confessor and defender of the Christian faith and a diligent emulator of evangelical perfection, after the publication of many volumes, was crowned with martyrdom for the faith of Christ in Chalcis of Greece under the last persecution, or, as others affirm, under Decius and Valerian. He wrote not a few volumes in a polished and well-composed style, among which the following are reported:

Against Porphyry, one book. On the Resurrection, one book.
On the Departure of the Soul, one book. On the Song of Songs, one book.
Also a Chronicle, one book. "For it must be known, brothers..."
On the Pythoness, one book. A Symposium on the Ten Virgins, one book.
He flourished under the great prince Constantine, or, as others prefer, under Decius and Valerian, in the year of the Lord 255.

Antony, monk and abbot, Egyptian by nation, a most holy man and distinguished inhabitant of the desert, whose praises the whole world proclaims, furnished many with a rule of religious and blessed life. Although he had not learned letters, yet he was so learned in the divine Scriptures that none of the teachers presumed to prefer himself to him. He had memory in place of books, understanding and sense in place of letters, grace in place of the study of reading. In the Egyptian language he dictated various letters, of apostolic meaning and speech, to diverse monasteries; among these there survives that distinguished one:

To the Arsenoites, one letter. To other monasteries, six letters.
