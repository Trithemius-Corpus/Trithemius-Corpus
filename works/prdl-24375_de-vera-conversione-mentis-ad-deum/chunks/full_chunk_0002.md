I have omitted them, even to the point of ill-will, lest either, for the simpler sort, I should obscure the meaning of necessary admonition by a more polished treatment, or disturb the memory by taking up too many passages. For I have always desired more to move the minds of my hearers to inward compunction and divine love than either to admiration of myself or to love of secular knowledge.

But this is wont to draw me not a little away from my purpose: that in a public assembly of elders, whom both their most holy and reverend character and their learning, richly supplied in every way with the Scriptures, ought deservedly to make terrible to me like the battle-line of armies, I, a sinful and unlearned man, must speak. Not without justice is it commonly imputed to me as a fault by many that I have not [unclear] with [unclear], shining with varied beauty, to whom powers are ascribed by their peers: that novices can wrest the club from most mighty Hercules and can drive [unclear] swifter than the wind, especially concerning those matters which, being far withdrawn from the senses, are accessible only, insofar as they are accessible at all, to those whom that divine ray, when they have been stripped of the evil passions of the flesh, has led, by the ascent of inward love, above understanding into the mystical visions of divine things.

Therefore for the time being I commit the account of my name to you, who have imposed upon me, unequal to it, this burden of speaking. Your authority has always been for me in place of Apollo’s oracles. As much as I can do for you, I remember that I have shown myself wholly yours before now, and I shall promise the same for the future; and if, after the abdication of all things, it is still lawful to speak of my own will, I desire that will to be devoted throughout my whole life to your commands and to those of the supreme maker of all. But now, fathers, as we are about to speak a few words on the conversion of the mind, let us humbly invoke the most excellent goodness of the highest and unchangeable good, which alone by its mercy and kindness can turn us to itself, since it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God, whose grace is ready for those who love him; and let us say: Come, Holy Spirit. If anyone, fathers, thinks that I am going to deliver this oration on the conversion of the mind either from a desire for novelties or moved by empty little glory, he is greatly mistaken, since the very profession of our religion, instituted by most holy men, is nothing other than a certain path of our soul itself, freely, tranquilly, and absolutely desiring, through the exercise of holy love, so long as it lives in the flesh, to turn itself to its principle and to be perpetually united with it. For this reason we have voluntarily left behind the world and all things that are in the world, according to the force of our institution; we have resigned ourselves, all that is ours, and our very own will into the hands of another for the love of God, in the presence of all the saints, for no other cause than this: that our soul, having nothing in this world to which it can cling with delight, may always, tranquil, quiet, free, and truly absolved from all things that are seen on earth, be able to lift itself without impediment from any thing to the love of the highest good by daily and indeed most holy exercises.

For a certain writer says among the holy things: Those at rest can search the law in some measure, if free hearts are empty of the world’s noise. And again, concerning the contemplation of heavenly things, which alone is the wisdom of Christians, he says: Wisdom fills a mind tranquil and free from cares, and will be the guest of a peaceful breast. Since these things are so, among all the matters which the account of our salvation is wont to bring before us, there is nothing concerning which we ought deservedly to be admonished both more frequently and more zealously than this: that, remembering daily as far as we can the brevity of the present life and the terrible necessity of our impending departure, we should always carefully draw our soul away from all earthly things; for it will never be able to be united with the highest principle unless, absolved and free from all other things, it has been united with itself.

But to persuade men of these things no one is fit, no one is sufficient, no one is rightly ordered, except him whom divine love, by the singular gift of its grace, has made experienced; so that whoever confounds his understanding through sensible things, or disturbs his affection by the passions of the flesh, is no more disposed to the contemplation of eternal things than an owl is to the light, or a blind man to the knowledge of colors.
