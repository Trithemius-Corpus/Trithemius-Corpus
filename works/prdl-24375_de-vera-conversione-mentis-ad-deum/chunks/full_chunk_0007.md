For this reason Saint Dionysius, prelate and martyr of Christ, affirms a twofold theology. One is secret and mystical: symbolic and truly perfective, sweetly acting through love purified above mind and understanding. This is accessible to few, friendly to fewer: only to those who, crucifying their flesh with its vices and desires, and trampling underfoot for the love of God all the noise of the tumultuous age, profess that theology not in words but in deeds. The other is better known, manifest, and receptive, physical, transmitted through letters and the writings of men to men. Today we see that it is regarded as quite celebrated among many, although it is less powerful than the former in stirring the soul to divine love. By daily labor a passage must be prepared for us from the second to the first, in which alone the soul is saved.

For one must, by more eager inquiry and by love, pass from the created beauty of the world, which physical theology beholds, to the ineffable beauty of the highest good, which mystical theology considers; there the mind, forgetful of all things that are in the world and freed from all things, carried upward by the fire of purest love, begins sweetly, in affection, to marvel at the beauty of the divine light. This is your theology, O cloistered ones; this singular study of divine love is enjoined upon you above all others, and all your studies must be principally ordered to it. But alas, I am ashamed to recall the studies of cloistered persons, who have turned holy theology into pernicious babbling; who pursue idleness and fables, whose god is their belly, for whom in this life nothing is sweeter than to eat and drink and, contrary to regular discipline, to be fattened on the pleasures of the flesh. The lesser learn these things from the greater, when abbots and the other shepherds of souls, forgetful of God and unmindful of their own salvation, under the cloak of piety most zealously pursue pride and avarice, spending all their time on transitory things and cares. As if by way of recreation after immense labors, they miserably turn themselves to the various pleasures of the body. Woe to you, and again woe, who, immersed in earthly cares or pleasures, do not rise up to the love of the highest good; who, having a heart turned away from the highest principle, are monks and Christians in name alone. I say to you: unless you are converted to repentance, you will have no share with the lovers in the kingdom of God. Wondrous, indeed astounding, is your madness, which has prevailed so far in blinding you that, although you always live rebellious and opposed to God, you nevertheless so boldly expect from him the kingdom of heaven, as though you were just. And how can it happen that a soul joined to the body and subjected to it by excessive sympathy, a soul that does not admit into itself the ray of divine love, should at last, when separated, make its way joyfully to God, whom, on departing, it did not love? O wretched and unhappy ones, how shamefully you deceive yourselves, who seek your consolations anywhere other than from God. It is impossible for a soul departing from the body to be able to reach its own principle, which is God, joyful and blessed, if, while placed in the body, it did not love God himself above all things. For pure love, free from and detached from all else, makes union between God and the soul; and when this has been neglected by a person throughout his whole life, it must be believed that it can never, or only rarely, be grasped at the point of death. Therefore live, mortals, while you live, bound over to love, always attending to the end of life; the less it is known to you, the more it is to be feared.

Come now, soul, search out where he is who created the whole world with such great beauty, who bestowed upon us so excellent a life, who promised bright and eternal dwellings in heaven to those who love him. You see beauty itself flashing in all things and in varied forms. It is beautiful to dwell in it; yet it is more beautiful and more necessary for the mind, already present in the beautiful, to investigate where these things come from and whence they are made beautiful.
