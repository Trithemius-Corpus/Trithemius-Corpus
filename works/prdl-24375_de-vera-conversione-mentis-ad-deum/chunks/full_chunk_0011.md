[Provi]dence governs the world by so beautiful and so manifest an order that we need seek no other more evident proof of his goodness and wisdom than in that which the ordered fabric of the universe itself proclaims to us.

The fourth step is a certain proportional comparison, drawing from the order of inferior works a relation to the divine order. For just as the author of all beauty must be infinite beauty, wholly sufficient in itself, needing nothing, so the ineffable Good, surpassing all things, by whose most peaceful will, ordering it, he clothed the universe in varied beauty, abides always in his majesty in a wondrous and incomprehensible order.

The fifth step is negation, by which you separate everything you conceive from the highest first principle of all things, just as Saint Dionysius taught in the Mystical Theology, and as we spoke in the former part of this discourse of ours. For that highest and incomprehensible majesty, the cause of all things, since it is above every affirmation of the things that we, or anyone else in the world, can know, understand, or speak, rejects every image, likeness, form, or species; and for that reason our mind rises nearer to it by negation than by affirmation.

The sixth step is humble prayer to God, that the father of the intellectual world himself may make you clean, intellectual in act. For this you must remember the benefits and free gifts that the goodness of almighty God has generously granted to you, though undeserving, and especially the benefit of redemption, which alone is sufficient to soften the mind to great compunction, when remembered attentively by use and familiarity.

The seventh and last step of this ladder is this: that, when by the gift of the good itself you have now become an intellectual world, you should then, stirred by love of that good itself, be transformed from the intellectual state into the good that is above intellect; there the quiet seat of holy contemplation unites the holy soul, through its love, to its own principle. The soul indeed remains in its own essence, but, abstracted from all alien things, becomes one by grace with God, not by nature: here in part, after death wholly.

We must therefore ascend, brothers, to that highest good which every soul desires, in which alone it will obtain eternal blessedness, if, turned toward it through love, it departs from the body. Therefore let whoever can enter and advance into the inward places, leaving outside the sight of the eyes, and no longer looking back behind him at the accustomed spectacles of the senses. For one who beholds the highest beauty on the mountain of contemplation must look upon absolutely nothing of bodily beauty thereafter, but, knowing bodily things to be images, traces, and shadows, must flee altogether to that of which these are works and likenesses. For created beauty ought not to hold the mind, but to lead it step by step to the uncreated; otherwise one must cry out with Ulysses: Let us go hence, friends, fleeing to our sweet homeland. But our homeland is there whence we came, as the holy Apostle also says: We have here no lasting city, but we seek the one to come. We must not flee to it on foot, nor again prepare horses for riding, nor ships for sailing. Rather, we must leave all these things behind and not even look forward, but, with the sight of the body closed, take up and awaken another sight in its place, the inward sight of the mind, which indeed all have, but very few use. Yet one must not rashly burst forth toward the unfamiliar light of divine brightness, since that inward eye, suddenly awakened, can hardly look upon very bright things. Therefore the soul must be accustomed and introduced to divine spectacles: first, indeed, to consider beautiful pursuits; then beautiful works, works, I say, not those made by the arts, but those done by upright men, by whose example, provoked to love of virtue, you may become an imitator of the goodness you have observed in them. Examine also what sort your own soul is; recall yourself into the temple and contemplate. But if you do not yet know yourself as beautiful and purified from every foulness of bodily familiarity, you will imitate the sculptor, who, when he desires a beautiful statue, cuts away one part.
