Turn to the mind, and to it as wholly purified from absolutely every iniquity; it will teach you that reason is a certain rational light, and a shining reason illuminated by that invisible light itself which shines in the darkness. But after you seek out, by reasoning, with such eagerness the reason of light as your origin, since this is above all reason, admitting no affirmation at all either of those things which are, or of those which are not, it is necessary that you, leaving behind all your powers, ascend even above the mind, by an affection free and released from all things, purified from every entanglement either of carnal love or of hatred of any kind, to that supreme ray of divine inspiration, so that in the highest truth, which is also itself the highest certainty, and an altogether incomprehensible brightness, you may find the truth of light and a brightness beyond all estimation. Its brightness and truth, being the same, far and incomparably transcend every human and angelic intellect. Its immeasurable abundance of goodness and truth, without detriment to itself, illuminates the minds of those who truly seek it, and powerfully and sweetly drives the soul upward with the invisible fire of its love, when it has found that soul purified from all evil and carnal passions.

But since the body, which is corrupted, weighs down the soul, and an earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things, one must not leap so suddenly to so sublime a contemplation of divine things; rather, the mind warns that one must ascend by degrees, first, namely, with the inner affection purified, and then with suitable exercises observed at intervals, lest it happen to be darkened and wholly blinded by too great a brilliance. Our spirit, however, which I would rightly wish to be called by a more excellent kind of name, is summoned to the beauty above not only through reason, when, namely, it argues from things known to the sense that corporeal beauty, as being set in shapelessness, distance, and mobility, is necessarily imperfect, and therefore proceeds from incorporeal beauty, toward which the spirit itself, as toward the principle of its own beauty, in which alone it knows that it can be made blessed, hastens with eager inquiry, as though desiring to ascend from the beauty which is splendor in many things, as in stars, to the simple light of the good itself, as of the sun. Although in that light there is no form, yet it is itself the beauty of beauty; and since its goodness and beauty are infinite, it will kindle love in those who draw near.

Although, indeed, the supreme and unchangeable good, existing far above every affirmation and negation, is penetrable by no comparison or likeness for any mortal, since there is plainly no proportion of the finite to the infinite, of the creature to the creator, and of man to God, nevertheless in the holy Scriptures it admits many comparisons and likenesses concerning itself, on account of the slowness of our understanding, although none of those things which are said of it by our assumed license belongs to it at all. Not only have our princes and prophets used these things in mystical comparisons and likenesses concerning the highest principle of all things, but also all the wiser philosophers of the gentiles, who were able to understand that there is one God, whom they determined must be worshiped invisibly by no other thing than devotion of mind.

Among these, Empedocles, one of them, wishing by a certain imaginable likeness to persuade men that God is infinite and immeasurable, and wholly incomprehensible, fashioned a sphere whose center is everywhere and circumference nowhere. When he sets the structure of this sphere before a weak mind, he renders heaven and earth narrow, and enclosed by the greatness of that image, and the mind, at once stupefied and shaken with fear, humbly adores the divine power. And another man, when he attempted to demonstrate the same thing by mathematical reasoning, proposed that one should imagine, from a straight line of infinite length, a circle than which neither a greater nor a smaller can be given, while the straightness of that line itself is preserved. Although the mind itself utterly recoils from forming this within itself as an impossible thought, nevertheless it understands that from that line there necessarily arises a circle than which neither a greater nor a smaller can be given, while the line remains by its own nature.
