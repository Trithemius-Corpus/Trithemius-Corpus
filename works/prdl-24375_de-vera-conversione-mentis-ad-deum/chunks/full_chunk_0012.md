So too must you: remove what is superfluous; straighten what is crooked; cleanse and illuminate what is obscure. Do not cease laboring over your statue until the radiance of divine virtue gleams upon you, and you firmly behold it seated in pure majesty and holy temperance. But when you have become clean and pure, and now dwell with yourself, having, that is, no impediment to prevent you from thus being made one, nor again having anything else mixed within you, but being wholly yourself, free, withdrawn, and released from all passions of the flesh and evil affections, then your inner sight itself, now rightly ordered and purified, can in some measure lift the mind to the contemplation of the uncircumscribable light. But if by chance the eye of the mind, which is affection itself, either infected with filth and not yet cleansed, or weak through sloth, is directed toward that most luminous spectacle, it will at once grow dark and discern nothing. For one eye of the mind is called intellect, the other affection; unless both have been duly cleansed, they will render the man approaching the contemplation of divine things blind rather, and mad, than a sharer in the divine light. For whoever, still given over to the passions of the flesh, with them not yet truly mortified in his soul according to the monastic rule, rashly approaches the contemplation of uncircumscribable beauty, will at once, struck back by the immeasurable light, go away blind and full of great errors. For the holy prophet David says: If I have looked upon iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me. And the wise man, concluding to the point, says: Wisdom will not enter into a malicious soul, nor dwell in a body subject to sins. Now attend to yourselves and to your profession, O cloistered men, who have been called to a share in the contemplative life and for that reason released from all cares, and who eat the alms of the faithful, so that no occasion might be given you for interrupting study if you had concern for the necessities of the body. What are you doing? Where is the sweetest tranquillity of contemplation [granted?] to you? Alas, alas, we have all been called to the contemplative life, and almost all of us are ignorant of the sweetness of contemplation. Very rare today is the monk, and much rarer the abbot, who devotes himself to the contemplative life after the example of the holy fathers. Indeed, if it is right to say so, few in this time know what contemplation is. Therefore, O monks, since you are not what according to your institution you ought to be, you are bad. For everyone who is not what he ought to be is in a bad state; but the recompense of the bad is hell. It is rightly said that the soul’s good and beauty consist in this: that it be like God by a certain beauty of purity and love, withdrawn from all things that are accustomed to make it ugly, since union can take place only among like things. Whoever has come to that purity of soul so that he begins to taste the things we are saying will thereafter easily despise any violent passions, and carnal or earthly loves, and will cast far away all things that previously seemed beautiful. Here is set before us the greatest and final struggle, for the sake of which we must labor completely, lest perhaps we be left without this best contemplation; whoever attains it in the present life passes to the future blessed by a happy vision, but conversely he who does not attain it is unhappy. For he is not wretched who lacks riches, money, vessels of gold and silver, and honors; nor he who does not obtain splendid possessions, beautiful horses, a noble wife, freeborn children, and beautiful bodies; nor he who falls from power, an abbacy, a prelacy, a principality, or a kingdom; nor he who is afflicted in this world by diseases, poverty, or disgrace, all of which will shortly pass away; but he who lacks possession of the highest good, for which certainly all the kingdoms and empires of the whole earth, sea, and heaven must be cast aside, if, after all these things have been abandoned and scorned, entrance to the contemplation of so great a good is granted to us.

*End of the oration of Johannes Trithemius, abbot, delivered in the annual chapter at Erfurt at Saint Peter’s, in the year of the Lord 1500, on the penultimate day of the month of August, by the same.*
