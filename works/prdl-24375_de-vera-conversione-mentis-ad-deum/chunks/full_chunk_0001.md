*An oration of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the true conversion of the mind to God.*

Gernandus Naso

Whatever Abbot Trithemius has drawn forth from your inner sanctuaries, learned Greece, both pleases and is read. Only preserve so great a man, because he adorns the doctrine of salvation and refers to Christ, who is its origin.

Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to Johannes Engellender, jurisconsult and most eloquent orator, greetings.

I received your most delightful letter, displaying the ancient majesty and grace of eloquence, when our Mutianus delivered it; by it you affected me both with incredible pleasure and with a certain honorable reproach. For what greater pleasure could have come to me than to be loved by you, a man by far most learned and most upright in every respect, whose learning, wisdom, and eloquence kings and princes admire, cultivate, and respect, to such a degree that all the more learned proclaim that most reverend prelate of Cologne Agrippina fortunate, because he has obtained you, a man most worthy, as prefect of those who are his secretaries? Or what reproach ought to lie closer to my heart than to know myself far beneath your praises, and by no means to be the man whom, deceived by love of me, you proclaimed Trithemius to be?

But I give undying thanks for your charity and goodwill toward me, since you deigned to anticipate me, when I wished indeed, though I did not dare, to knock at the doors of your friendship, a man who has hitherto deserved nothing at all with you. And so that I should not allow these little letters to go off to you empty, I have added an oration on the conversion of the mind to God, which I recently delivered at Erfurt in a gathering of the fathers, to be corrected by the file of your wisdom; and this same oration, cleansed of faults, I dedicate, give, and inscribe to you. And from now on, when I have been made more certain that these very small things have not displeased you, having been received under your patronage, I promise in return that I shall attempt greater things in literary work.

Mutianus Rufus himself, through whose mediation your love for me became known to me, a most celebrated interpreter of nature and of antiquity, who is now wrestling, as I read before him, with Homer and the other authors of Greece, of whom there is an abundance with me, and whose Attic monuments you will see in the near future, sends you many greetings and commends himself to you.

Farewell. From Sponheim. In the year 1500, in the month of November, on the twentieth day.

*An oration of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the true conversion of the mind to its principle.*

I would have greatly wished, reverend fathers, and would exceedingly have desired, that my mind had attained that splendor of the divine majesty by which I might make manifest, in a fitting, proper, and suitable oration, the one goal of our institution: the true, free conversion of the soul, released from the lowest things, to the principle of its essence, God, the best and greatest. For I believe that nothing can be said by me in this holy synod, at this most important time, when, shameful to say, nearly all of us, blinded by evil affections, wander disgracefully from the knowledge and love of the supreme unchangeable principle, either more honorably or more usefully than concerning the conversion of the mind to God, since all that we, voluntarily bound for God by the most sacred laws and the institutions of our elders, do in this mortal life ought to persuade us of nothing other than the return and passage of the soul to the center of its blessedness, which is God.

We know, moreover, that the soul, sweetly and freely turned through love toward its principle, when it is separated from the body, turns joyfully toward God as toward the immovable center of its rest; remaining happy in him and blessed without end, it is thereafter pressed by no disturbances. Therefore, fathers, nothing sweeter can ever befall those who love God in this life than continually either to speak or to hear about the true conversion of the mind to its principle, by which it is always stirred up in greater measure both to contempt of the lowest things and to love of the things above.
