Cyril the Greek, bishop of Alexandria, William of Sanvico, Sibertus de Beka, Philip Ribot, John Baconthorpe, men most learned and most upright: what else do they write about this order, except that it took its beginning from Elijah? If you pretend not to give credence to us in the things we write about this order, have recourse to the writings of the aforesaid fathers; and you will not be able to accuse us of falsehood.

The name of the place therefore agrees, so that Elijah is the founder of the Carmelite order. Hence the brothers of this sacred religion are not to be blamed because they affirm Elijah and the sons of the prophets to be the founders and princes of their order. As regards the matter of the habit, the order of Carmel has agreed with Elijah in a beautiful transfiguration. We read that he was clothed with a mantle; when it touched the Jordan, the river stood amazed and gave itself to be trodden underfoot for passage. So Elisha, so the sons of the prophets, became imitators of Elijah, who remained like him not only in place but also in habit. But if you think to bring an accusation against us concerning the color, although it is a poor argument that disputes about unseen colors, we call you to the histories of the ancients, in which the Carmelite brothers in the primitive Church are read to have been clothed, like Elijah, in striped garments. Nor let the change of color offend you, since it is not of the substance of the order. For the habit can be changed while the integrity of the order remains. Thus also the Cistercians, changing their gray habit, assumed a black one, while the former religion nevertheless continued.

With respect to manner of life, however, Elijah was the founder of the order of Carmel, for he preceded them on Mount Carmel in holy conduct. What did Elijah do that the Carmelite brothers, as far as they are able, do not imitate? He inhabited the wilderness; he loved the vast places of solitude; he was free for God alone and for himself. But the Carmelite brothers from the beginning of their institution led a solitary life on Mount Carmel, choosing to be free for God alone through the secrecy of contemplation, until at last, in almost the most recent times, driven from the wilderness by the persecution of the Saracens, they were placed in cities by command of the Apostolic See. Hence also in antiquity they were called hermits of Mount Carmel. Moreover, just as Elijah announced the will of God, reproved tyrants, and slew the impious, so the Carmelite brothers, as far as pertains to them, preach the word of God to all, reprove tyrants, and, through admonitions of salvation, kill by the power of God the impious movements of carnal desires in themselves and in others.

Elijah, a virgin in mind and body, is found to have been defiled by no stain of bodily intercourse, but shone forth with such purity before God and men that he has deserved to defer death now for 2,430 years. The Carmelite brothers, instructed by the example of their holy prince, bind themselves by the vow of religion to perpetual chastity; they neither marry nor are given in marriage; they build up the Church of God by life and doctrine; with vices driven out as far as human frailty permits, they plant virtues in themselves and in others. And although it is not possible for them to imitate Elijah in all things, nevertheless, if equality of manner of life is considered with a just balance, Elijah is proved to have been the first and best Carmelite.

What, now, will wicked rivalry object against us, that you should deny that Elijah was a Carmelite? If you say that Elijah was not prince and founder of the order of Carmel for this reason, because he did not profess their rule, we do not consider it very important. For what if he did not have a rule, since he existed before the rule? Good, you say. If he existed before the rule, how was he the founder of the order of Carmel? For he could not be founder of the order if he did not give the rule. But the conclusion is not true, and the contrary custom of many invalidates it. Blessed Dominic instituted the Order of Preachers, and yet he did not give the Rule of Augustine, but accepted it. So the Cistercian order, so the Carthusian, so almost innumerable others, had one set of men as the princes of their institution and another set as the givers of the rule. But lest you plead that Elijah preceded the rule of the Carmelites and therefore could not have been the founder of the order, know that in the Church of God there were formerly many orders without a fixed rule, which in the course of time took up a rule.
