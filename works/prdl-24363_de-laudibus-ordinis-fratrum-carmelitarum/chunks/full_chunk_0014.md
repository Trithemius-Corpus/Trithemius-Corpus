*On the first advance of the order of the friars in Europe, and on the multiplication of convents.*

*Chapter 10.*

In the Gospel the Lord speaks: “Unless a grain of wheat, falling into the earth, has died, it remains alone. But if it has died, it will bring forth much fruit.” This saying fits the Carmelite friars beautifully, for it shows them to be the grain and seed of God. For indeed this grain of Carmelite stock fell into the earth when it was expelled from Judaea. It became dead, however, when, worn down by many tribulations, it lamented the hardship of its migration. But it brought forth much fruit when, breaking forth through the [uprightness?] of examples, it was multiplied among various convents in Europe. Truly the religion of the Carmelites bears much fruit: it devotes itself wholly to the service of God; it devoutly embraces the paths of the virtues; by its examples many are converted to righteousness.

Truly the Carmelite order is a dead grain: falling in Judaea, it perished; but multiplied in Europe, it rose again. The Lord enlarged it by hammer and anvil, because, while it is expelled from Palestine fewer in number, it is enlarged as it grows in Europe.

Nor do we believe that this was done without the ordering of divine providence, since the few friars expelled from Carmel and afflicted for a time in the land of pilgrimage produced, in the course of time, a very great number of friars in the order. This is the change wrought by the right hand of the Most High; this is the providence of the highest governor, who knows how to draw good things out of evils and to change the calamity of a few into the greatest joy. For just as iron is stretched by the hammer, so the order of the Carmelite friars was stretched and multiplied by the iron of tribulation.

If it had been able to remain in its first little nest, it would not today have been multiplied so greatly in the church of the faithful. For after their happy arrival in Europe, and after their impious persecutors had been overcome by apostolic authority, they were gradually multiplied very greatly. For seeing that the way of life of the friars was most holy, nobles and the faithful bestowed many benefactions on them and erected a great many monasteries for them at their own expense. Hence it came about that the Carmelite friars, who had lost a few monasteries in the Holy Land, received many more in Europe. And just as the apostles, expelled from Judaea while preaching the faith of Christ, converted a multitude of nations, so the Carmelite friars, having lost the bounds of one province, received all Europe as a place of dwelling. Therefore the desolation of one mountain was the building up of many regions. Carmel, then, shall by no means hereafter bewail itself as deserted, since it is now known to have been spread through the whole world. And when the number of friars had been multiplied throughout Europe and many convents had been erected in different places, the Roman pontiffs, perceiving that their life was praiseworthy, raised their order by many privileges; and, exempting it entirely from the jurisdiction of ordinaries, they joined it to the other mendicant orders. And although it is older than all the orders, nevertheless in Europe it seemed to have been renewed as though through its own mortification. But now it has recovered from all its tribulation; made strong in war, it has overcome and confounded all its adversaries. There is nothing that may harm it any further; there is no one who may presume henceforth to touch with his teeth what has been strengthened by the very great power of God. Rejoice, happy one, triumphing over the enticements of the world, taking hold of the highest rewards of the kingdom, you who preserve the name of your origin and imitate your founders.
