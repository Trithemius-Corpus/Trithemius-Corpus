Brocardus, prior of Mount Carmel, obtained a rule from Albert, patriarch of Jerusalem, which he commended to his posterity to be observed. Nor are the Carmelite brothers to be thought to have lived irregularly up to that time, since from holy conduct they changed custom into a rule. For a rule is not necessary for the perfect, but for the weak.

*On the Confirmation and Approval of the Order of the Carmelites, Chapter 5.*

But the perverse man does not cease to tear good men with an envious tooth, since, by otherwise giving proof of himself, he cannot acquire fame unless he lies in wait for another’s reputation. For the more the pursuits of good men increase, the more also does the envy of the wicked increase, destined to rage. Indeed, although antiquity itself approved the sacred order of the Carmelites, although from the beginning it was exalted by the holiness of many brothers, nevertheless the persecution of the wicked against it by no means ceased. Therefore the unjust detractors said to the holy brothers: “Your order is not established in the Church of God, since the holy apostolic see has not approved it.” From this scandals arose; from this seditions arose among the people, while perverse men strove utterly to destroy the holy order. O foolish abuse among men, that what the Gospel of Christ approves they should think to need human confirmation. Tell me, detractor of good men: have you read bulls approving the ancient orders? What pope confirmed the old orders of Pachomius, Basil, Augustine, and Benedict? Nowhere will you find these orders confirmed by letters from the beginning. Indeed, those whom the holiness of many approves, whom long antiquity confirms, who but a mindless and stupid man would infringe upon them? If holy antiquity confirms the aforesaid orders, the order of the Carmelite brothers, which is most ancient, has need of no one’s confirmation. For it long preceded the Roman pontiffs, since it took its beginning from Elijah the prophet. I have said these things not because the order of the Carmelites lacks apostolic protection, but so that I might show that its rivals are moved by hatred, not by any principle of integrity.

For after the brothers of this religious way of life came into Europe, driven out of Palestine, many supreme pontiffs confirmed that order; among them Alexander, the third pope of that name, was one. After him Innocent did the same, and Honorius III, following in the footsteps of both, with many privileges added, approved anew both the order and the rule. But enough has been said about these matters, since, although the privileges of the Carmelite order are not entirely unknown to us, nevertheless it is not our purpose to bring them to light. I would wish this to suffice for its rivals: that this order has always been approved from the beginning, and will henceforth remain stable.

*On the Change of the Brothers’ Outer Habit.*

*Chapter 6.*

As we have already said above, the Carmelite brothers imitate their founders not only in morals, but also, according to their ability, in habit. Sacred history shows that Elijah was clothed in a mantle: with it, when the Lord passed by, he covered his face, and, striking the Jordan with it, made it passable on foot. Finally, when he called Elisha, he is read to have cast his mantle over him; with it he also endowed the same man when he himself was carried through the air. The mantle, however, if faith is to be given to the writings of the ancients, was round, covering the whole body from the neck down to the middle of the shins, narrow above and broad below, and open in the front. The color of that habit is said to have been white, by which the inner cleanliness and purity of the brothers is signified. They hand down that Elijah was clothed in such a mantle, that Elisha and the sons of the prophets went about in such a habit. But if anyone should contend that this description of Elijah’s habit is apocryphal, he will do us no injury by this, since we write not our own opinion, but that of the preceding fathers. For a cloak does not make an order, but the observance of piety.
