For the fathers of the gracious order will indeed defend you,

Carmel, the famous mountain, gave to these fathers
an apt name by its religion.
O holy mountain, now you duly behold your followers,
and the sacred order endures steadfast continually.
Whoever there shall be [unclear] who venerates you with honor,

If anyone looks upon you with a bitter, harsh face,

The press calls you, easily to be printed,
so that, sweet one, you may be able to be useful to men.
Yet observe that you are not changed, nor ever

As my reward I ask your fathers for prayers
poured out to the Lord, that he may purge every sin.

*Of Johannes Empolidis, canon of Saint Peter of Dumentum, on the book of lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, On the Praise of the Order of Carmelites.*

In the present volume are praised the lights of the sacred
order, which the gracious mother holds and cherishes.
Carmel the mountain gave these fathers their name; thence
flows all the glory of the Carmelites.
Famous antiquity of this order, and holy life,
brought distinguished titles to the brothers.
The Trithemian pen adorns them, brothers, with worthy praises,
and does not allow them to be in gloomy places.
For the increase of the praise of so bright a religion,
with wondrous talent he gathers all things ancient,
and urges the new to advance along the path of the old.
Nor does he spare the venerable ancient customs of the fathers,
but strikes such men sharply with the sword of the word.
Thus he approves the noble by their merits or gracious deeds,
so that by his writings he may call the frail to better things.

Thus is the custom of experienced teachers of the young:
that the rest of the company may learn by the praise of one.
With this ready spirit, reader, receive my poem,
and read the approved beginnings of the famous volume.

*Poem of Rutgerus Venrai, a Sicambrian regular in Heyna, on the book of lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot, On the Praise of the Order of Carmelites.*

The poets of old were accustomed to praise the gods
in verses, and with the lyre resounding and sounding again.
But what thing not blameworthy, not vain, not wicked,
did they, anxious in their talents, not compose?
Turn over the books, reader, and the great volumes
of the ancient bards: you will find many frivolous things sung.
Look at Virgil: you will often see there
warlike, spirited goddesses, Juno and Venus.
The famous book of Homer relates many invented things;
nothing is sincere, nothing weighty, and nothing pious.
The excellent Plautus, and also sharp Terence,
approve decorous things with unchaste games.
Let Pamphilus fall silent; let Maximianus yield;
and let Naso cease to play with unequal meters.
These [unclear] the fire [unclear] halls with modulated songs,
and do not allow young men to be of chaste morals.
Our Trithemius, not following vain trifles,
strives to raise men with excellent praise:
not just any men, but men distinguished by gracious morals,
by various arts, and by weighty uprightness,
and deservedly called fellow-brothers of the gracious Virgin,
who stood out in their life and merits.
For their ancient religion the fathers deserved
surpassing praise before all [unclear].
Now bright knowledge has joined itself
with ancient morals, and wages war with uprightness.
Not that it overcomes it; rather it is overcome by it:
what was ancient remains unviolated.

For one may see not a few of this order
no less skilled in art than upright in goodness.
Seeing these things, lord Trithemius, the renowned abbot,
has, behold, published two distinguished books.
He touched upon the excellent fathers, the praises of the gracious order,
and the old and new customs.
Carmelite, now give thanks to your poet;
for he has made you shine forth from the shadows.

*A Sapphic Poem by the Same on the Same Subject.*

Behold, those to whom Carmel is shown to have given
the name, and the mother of the Lord,
in this book receive the praises of an ancient
religious order.

A distinguished man, the abbot of Sponheim,
Johannes, published it with a fair pen,
skilled in Latin, Hebrew,
and Greek letters.

The brothers and masters of the duly consecrated
order, bright in morals, fair in uprightness,
shining with the light of doctrine, he approves
as a glittering choir.

Therefore the venerable abbot commends
the brothers of Mary with great praises,
so that, mindful of their ancient honor,
they may avoid shameful things.

He has resolved that the frail should always
be rebuked more sparingly, the modest
more strongly and justly approved,
as this little book bears witness.

I judge that the fathers of this sacred order
will readily be grateful to him,
now shining out from black decay
and blind shadows.
