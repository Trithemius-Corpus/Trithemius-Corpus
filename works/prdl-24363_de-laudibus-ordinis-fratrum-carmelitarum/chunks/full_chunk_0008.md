For many years the order of Saint Jerome flourished in the Church of Christ; yet it did not receive the rule of Jerome until Brother Lupus of Oliveto, a Spaniard, general of the same order, at last under Pope Martin V compiled a new rule from various sayings of that same father. Finally, we have known many orders to exist under the titles of certain saints, concerning whose rule we have found nothing. But enough about these matters.

The greatest proof of the institution of Elijah is the testimony of antiquity, celebrated in many writings of the ancients. For the Carmelite brothers existed before the rule, because, by the example of Elijah and the prophets, they were themselves a rule of living for themselves. Read the life of Elijah and of the sons of the prophets, and you will find that before the rule they lived regularly. Nor does it matter if you say that other religious also do those things which we have said the Carmelites do by the example of Elijah; for although the others follow Elijah in their conduct as far as they can, nevertheless the Carmelites alone imitate him not only in conduct, but also in habit and in the name of the place. We read in the histories that Sabacha, the father of Elijah, before Elijah was yet born, saw in dreams men clothed in white greeting him; by them we rightly understand the Carmelite brothers, whom we know to be white in habit and way of life. These greet Sabacha, the father of Elijah, because, adhering to his examples, they show themselves as though his brothers, while by their white conduct they preserve Sabacha, which is interpreted “little net.” But what else is understood by Sabacha, the father of Elijah, than the little net of the holy Carmelite religion, in which many who are to be saved are caught? Indeed, from the time of Elijah and Elisha, prophets of God, down to the birth of Christ, many inhabited Carmel out of love of solitude, as is clearly shown from the histories of the ancients. For after the passion of the Lord, when the apostles were preaching the faith, among the many who were then converted, the hermits of Mount Carmel too were converted; and thereafter, as the number of the faithful increased, they built on the slope of Mount Carmel a [unclear] in honor of the mother of God and virgin Mary.

Nor should you wonder if I have said that there were monks in Israel even before the birth of Christ, who inhabited Mount Carmel and the deserts of solitude, since indeed even among the gentiles, in our Belgian Gaul, monastic life was celebrated; its professors antiquity called Druids. Some of these dwelt in cities and little villages; some inhabited mountains and forests; all philosophized concerning knowledge of natural things. They were in the greatest authority among the people, at whose nod all things were done. Their life was withdrawn and most continent, so much so that no one presumed to say anything adverse about them. They had the power of excommunicating the rebellious and again absolving the obedient. They also enjoyed every kind of immunity. But, that I may not speak at length about what the way of life of these men was, Caesar set it out in the Commentaries. For apart from the faith and the knowledge of the one God, in their conduct they showed themselves to be monks. If the Druids, gentiles before the birth of Christ, lived a solitary and monastic life, what wonder is it if there were faithful hermits of Carmel from Judea? Finally, if you desire to know deeper things about the monks who flourished in Judea before the birth of Christ, read the histories of Philo of Alexandria and Josephus of Jerusalem, Jews, and you will find what sort they were at that time.

*Chapter 3.*

*On the way of life of the sons of the prophets and of other hermits before the birth [of Christ]. Chapter 3.* [Eginus?], in the histories of the ancients, says that Samuel was the first to establish a company of prophets, which they also called a troop. Then, after Elijah had been carried through the air, his double spirit rested in Elisha, who himself also, with the sons of the prophets, inhabited Mount Carmel at fixed times, and took the greatest care to live according to the standard of Elijah.

Of these, indeed, many are listed by name by the fathers; concerning them we not imprudently suspend our own judgment, content with the testimony of the same men. Hence Jerome says concerning the institution of monks: “Our prince is Elijah; our leader, Elisha and the sons of the prophets, who, dwelling in the fields and in solitude, built dwellings for themselves beside the streams of the Jordan.” *Note: On the institution of monks [unclear]*
