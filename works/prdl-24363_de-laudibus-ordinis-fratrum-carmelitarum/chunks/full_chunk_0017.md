*Here begins the second book of Lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim: On the Illustrious Men of the Order of Carmelites.*

After, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have completed the first small work of our intention, in praise of the order of the Carmelite brothers, it is pleasing now to begin the second, in which, with the Virgin Mary favoring us, we shall have discourse concerning the illustrious men who at various times shone in it. A twofold cause has impelled us to undertake that province, one of which concerns the brothers, the other the detractors. For we believe that it contributes not a little to the edification of the younger men if we take care to recall to their knowledge the men distinguished for learning who preceded them in the order. For by this means the most learned men receive among posterity the worthy immortal memory due to their labors, and the lesser brothers of the same order are stirred to love of the Scriptures. The second cause of this catalogue is the rivalry of detractors, who despise the Carmelite order as unfruitful, since they believe that few ecclesiastical writers and doctors flourished in it. But we, grieving that injury is done through the ignorance of slanderers to so holy and most sacred an order, have arranged, with what brevity we could, a catalogue of the learned men who wrote various things in it, in which we have set out all the writers, as many as came to our knowledge. And although many most learned men of this order, flourishing in Palestine before the transmigration, wrote many things, nevertheless, since their works are unknown to us, we have marked down a few out of many. Therefore, passing over those who shone in Judea from the beginning, we have resolved to mention only those whom we know to have given in Europe the clear splendor of their learning. Yet we could not mention all those who flourished after the transmigration, because we are ignorant of their works and times. We leave that province to the cultivators of the same order, so that where we have failed through weakness, they themselves may strive to amend our defect. Now therefore let us take the beginning of the proposed work.

Cyril, a monk of the Carmelite order, afterward bishop of Alexandria, Greek by nation, originally from Constantinople, a holy and most learned man, a distinguished philosopher and an unsurpassable rhetorician, shone with countless miracles and was illuminated with the spirit of prophecy. In the time of the emperor Theodosius and Pope Celestine I, he composed many things against Nestorius and, presiding at the Council of Ephesus, refuted him. He wrote in Greek many excellent volumes, of which few are found among the Latins. He first mingled Aristotelian philosophy with the Holy Scriptures and shattered all the errors of heretics with very strong arguments, as is found in the acts of the third synod of Ephesus. Among other things he wrote against Julian the Apostate, two books. Likewise against Nestorius, one book. On the incarnation of the Lord, several homilies. Many letters to various persons, one book. Several sentences in the synod of Ephesus. All these we saw among the Greeks and read in part, but nowhere among the Latins. He flourished under the great emperor Theodosius and Pope Celestine I, in the year of the Lord 430.

John, the forty-fourth patriarch of the church of Jerusalem after blessed James, comes to be placed first in the order of our narrative. For he was a man of upright and most holy conduct, who is said to have shone, living and dead, with countless miracles. Pope Adrian I, on account of his exceptional learning and the excellence of his life, took him from the monastery of Mount Carmel and raised him to be patriarch of the aforesaid city. Since he was very skilled in the divine Scriptures, he produced certain excellent works, by which he revealed his talent to posterity. Of these there survives a distinguished volume, *On the Beginning and Progress of the Carmelite Order*, one book. He also wrote certain hortatory letters to the brothers. And certain other things.
