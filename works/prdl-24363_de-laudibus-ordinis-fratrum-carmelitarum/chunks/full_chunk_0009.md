For they themselves observed the manner of life of the Carmelite order in the time of the Law; from them our Carmelites, under grace, received both name and order. In all these things nothing differs except the time; deservedly it is called one order of Carmelites and hermits under the Law and under grace, since it is joined together by faith in and worship of the one God. Nor, however, does the title of Elias belong to the other orders; although their manner of life may accord with it, nevertheless the name of the place does not agree. Therefore the Carmelite order is the most ancient of all orders in the Church of God; from it the other orders received the beginning and form of their institution. Indeed, as far as concerns the authority of the ancient writings, Elias and his disciples were the founders of the monastic profession; leaving the cities, they built for themselves little huts in the desert around the streams of the Jordan. Under grace, John the Baptist was the first of this profession, for from his tender years he inhabited the solitude of the desert. And do not think that the holy prophets of the Lord were ignorant of the greatness of the good of solitude, since he was not the least of them who said: “It is good for a man when the yoke of the Lord is borne from his youth; he will sit solitary and keep silence, because he will raise himself above himself.” Those most holy men, enlightened by the divine spirit, knew the usefulness of the solitary life; and therefore before the giving of the Law they devoted themselves in works to that which the Carmelites, later established under a rule, imitate as far as they can.

And although Elias did not give the Carmelites a written rule, nevertheless by the example of his holy manner of life he was for them a norm of living; to them after himself he granted both place and habit.

*When and in what manner the rule was given to the brothers dwelling on Mount Carmel. Chapter 4.*

The holy fathers of old who dwelt on Mount Carmel near the spring of Elias before the birth of Christ are not read to have had a written rule; rather they themselves had been a norm and rule for themselves, living harmoniously according to the example of Elias. Then, after the Passion of the Lord, when the Christian faith had multiplied throughout Judea, those who on Mount Carmel were leading the eremitic life in imitation of Elias began to live according to the Gospel of Christ, as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles: “They were persevering unanimously in prayer, and they had one heart and one soul; nor did anyone among them have anything of his own, but all things were common to them.” This is the apostolic life, which the Carmelite brothers in the primitive Church observed with the greatest zeal, having the evangelical rule concerning these things written in their hearts. But in the course of time, when men gradually began to withdraw from the apostolic life and each to possess something of his own, the zealous inhabitants of Mount Carmel, fearing that their own holy manner of life too might fail through the passage of time, began to think about a remedy for the future. They therefore sought a rule not for themselves, but for posterity, knowing that their order would henceforth not be sufficiently secure unless it were confirmed by a rule. And truly, if today the Carmelites did not have an approved rule, I do not know whether they would survive. For the rule alone is what commends the order of those who have gone before to posterity. In time of peace the expenses of war must be prepared, lest adversity find men unprepared. For if the brothers of Mount Carmel had not received a rule in time of peace, where could they have remained when at last they were driven from Judea by the Saracens?
