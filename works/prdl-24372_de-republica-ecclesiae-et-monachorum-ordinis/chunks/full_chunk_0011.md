The walls are raised high; the pinnacles of the roof gleam with gold. Shame does not allow me to speak of the other plagues of certain abbots: lust, the eating of meat, the abuse of baths, games, and hunts. Would that those might be cut off who by their pestilential morals confound our observance. Behold, reverend fathers, we have briefly heard what the state of our commonwealth is; it remains now for us to think of its remedy. For, descending from Jerusalem to Jericho, it has fallen away from its former beauty; it has fallen among robbers, because from the time of Henry IV it has lost more than five thousand monasteries from the order. I do not speak apocryphal things, nor do I report uncertainties, but things manifest which I have found from the writings of the ancients. Therefore the robbers who have despoiled our commonwealth are, first, demons, who kill the souls of monks with the wounds of vices; second, greedy men, who plunder for themselves the monasteries of our order. Therefore our commonwealth lies naked and torn, since it is seen to be despoiled within by its own, without by strangers. The priest and the Levite, seeing its desolation, passed by, because, despising our affairs, all seek the things that are their own. But our Samaritan, the Lord Jesus Christ, seeing the desolation of our order, moved by mercy, bound up its wounds with hope, when at various times he renewed it to regular observance. On the next day, bringing forth two denarii, namely love of God and of neighbor, or certainly the increase of spiritual and temporal goods, or understanding and action, he gave them to the faithful innkeeper, saying: Take care of him, etc.

The faithful innkeeper was Charles the Great, emperor, who was the first to reform our fallen order in Germany. An innkeeper was Berno, abbot of Cluny, under whose reform there were fifteen hundred monasteries. Innkeepers were Richard of Fulda, William of Hirsau, [of Augia?], and another certain abbot of Hirsau, who each at different times reformed the order in part in Germany. Innkeepers were the authors of the Italian reform of Saint Justina, which, persevering down to the present day, has been honored with many privileges by the supreme pontiffs. An innkeeper was Emperor Sigismund with his helpers, who began the Melk reform in Austria. Faithful innkeepers also were those who furnished the beginnings of the Kastl reform. Finally, most faithful innkeepers were Johannes Rode of Saint Matthias of Trier, and Johannes Hagen of Bursfelde, most illustrious abbots, who laid the foundations of our Bursfelde reform.

Innkeepers were the other pontiffs, princes, and many abbots, whose names we omit for the sake of brevity, who in different provinces, places, and times reformed our order according to their ability. And you also are innkeepers, O reverend fathers, who have each assembled for the preservation of our commonwealth, who cultivate regular observance, who love the union of the Bursfelders. You, I say, are innkeepers; see to it that you are found faithful. To you the true Samaritan has entrusted the sick order; it is required of you that you pour into its wounds the wine of the holy Scriptures and the oil of devotion. For, as we said above, by these two things our order once shone forth in glory. Therefore, if you desire to reform the commonwealth, or even to preserve it in reform, devote yourselves to the study of letters and to holiness of life. Let none of you say: What need is there for me to range through many things by study, when a few things can suffice for living well? Believe me, fathers, for an abbot to live well it is not enough to know only a few things. For if a few things sufficed, the holy fathers would have written so many volumes for the formation of morals in vain. For an abbot must be learned in divine law; and if the study of letters conferred nothing else, nevertheless it is useful for the extirpation of vices, as blessed Jerome says: Love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh. Nothing so contemns the world, nothing so tames the flesh and lust, nothing so kindles the love of God, as ardent study and love of the Scriptures. Therefore let no one excuse himself from holy study; let no one call the lack of teachers a fault. Love conquers all things; charity can do all things; humility suffers all things. If a teacher is lacking, O brothers, read the holy fathers, instructing you in their volumes. Nor do I wish any monk to run off to the schools, since in the monastery he will be able very well to learn thoroughly the things he is commanded to know.
