But where now is such study among monks? Where is the abbots’ love for the divine Scriptures? Shame! Now a man is called great and useful in the order not because he cultivates and loves knowledge of the Scriptures, but because he grows foul in gathering perishable riches. O fathers, O brothers, our commonwealth once flourished not so; our order did not grow in ancient times in this way. But abbots given over to the world, monks who are friends of vanity, are accustomed to reply and excuse themselves from study: “We gladly wish to remain in our simplicity, because we see even learned men go through the precipices of vices into perdition. Profound knowledge will not be required of us at the Last Judgment, but a good life.” O foolish and senseless men, you do not hide your sloth, but, to your greater confusion, you openly show yourselves enemies of the divine Scriptures. It is well said in the proverb that knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man. You lovers of the world, you followers of vanity, who foolishly cast away from yourselves the study of the Scriptures, why do you not love it in others? Or do you not know that every Scripture inspired by God is useful for teaching, for arguing, for rebuking? What will you teach your monks, you who are ignorant of the Scriptures? How will you be able usefully to argue against offenders, since you do not know how to bring forward the testimonies of the Scriptures? Remain, therefore, in your simplicity, which is not right; remain in asinine humility; do not understand, so that you may act well; do not attend to the Rule of Saint Benedict; do not withdraw from your folly. Do you even know what our lawgiver says in the Rule? I know that you know. “The abbot,” he says, “ought to be learned in divine law, so that he may know, and there may be something from which he may bring forth discourse.” An unlearned prelate is a mute dog. A soul that does not love the Scriptures of God is an empty vessel. O you idiot abbots and enemies of saving knowledge, who spend the day in obscene gossip, who drink wine to satiety, who pursue earthly profits, who stupidly play at the gaming-board: what will you answer to almighty God and to your lawgiver Benedict,

when with care they require from you their sheep? For you are the ones who, lying in the deformity of your former life, confound our commonwealth, spurn discipline, and scatter the order. O holy religion, O venerable mother, O commonwealth of monks once honored, to what misery have you come! Where now is the adornment of your former beauty? Where is holiness of life? Where the saving study of the Scriptures? Where are learned and holy abbots? Where are monks devout in regular observance? Behold, you hold more than ten thousand monasteries under the order; of these we believe scarcely a thousand have been preserved in any observance of the Rule. For your abbots, with regular discipline despised and care of souls neglected, are given over to the vanities of the world, intent on earthly riches, submerged in the delights of the flesh. For as a certain outstanding versifier, still surviving, says: “Our abbots and prelates of churches, the worship of the heavenly ones neglected and the command of the Thunderer despised, indulge Bacchus and serve Venus. They carry sacred things [unclear]; they build altars with coins. With gold they pour libations of wine; with gold the ceilings gleam. They cover whores with gems; they set feasts before dogs. Adorned with the spoils of the poor, and rich from plunder, [unclear], they laugh at the heavenly ones and Acheron alike.” There is in them no care for the salvation of souls, no love of God, no reverence for the saints, no exercise of virtues. Would that our observance did not have such abbots; would that our commonwealth knew no such rulers. But I fear, rather, if it is right to say it, I know, that among our own also such men are found, who are reformed in name but in truth deformed. There are, I say, abbots glorying in the name of reform, ambitious, vain, proud, given over to the pleasures of the flesh, who, impatient of seclusion, consider every occasion suitable by which they may migrate hither and thither at the greatest expense. Behold, that man, despising regular discipline, sits the whole day as a curious guest at frivolous [unclear]; this one, making little of the meekness of the saints, involves himself in the cases of litigants; that one remains playing at the gaming-board; this stupid one dances to the drum. What shall I say of buildings, both curious and superfluous, which are liable to censure no less than to expense?
