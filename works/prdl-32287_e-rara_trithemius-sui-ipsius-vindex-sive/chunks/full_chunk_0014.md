For he who has never known a word of the Latin language may be taught in two hours to write, read, and understand Latin sufficiently elegantly and fluently, as much as he wishes, so that all who see his letters will praise his words and understand his Latin compositions.

The fourth book contains many astonishing experiments, but purely natural ones. That is, I can indicate my mind to one who knows my art while eating or sitting with others, without words, without nods, as extensively as I wish, even while speaking, preaching, playing the organ, or singing, without hindrance to any other action whatever; so that, while preaching good and holy things, I may indicate, not by words, not by signs, not by nods, to anyone who knows the art, as fully as I wish, whatever I wish, even with my eyes closed. And the fourth book contains many other secrets which must not be brought before the public.

All who hear these things marvel at them; among them are many noble and most learned men, who think these things either impossible or wholly supernatural. To them and to you I answer what I have answered to many: that many things are naturally possible which, to those ignorant of the powers of nature, seem impossible or even supernatural. And behold, I speak before my God, who knows all things: the wonders I have mentioned are far more excellent, deeper, and greater than I can write or you can believe; and yet they are all purely natural, without any deception, without superstition, without magical art, without invocation or service of any spirits whatever.

I have said this so that, if perhaps some rumor should ever reach you that I either know or can do marvelous things, you should not therefore think me a magician, but a philosopher. For what happened to Albert the Great, that most profound investigator of natural things, namely that because of the marvels he performed by the hidden power of nature he was regarded by the common people as a magician, I am certain could happen likewise to me. Behold, before God, for I do not lie: I have not yet taught these things to anyone, although many, promising great things, have often begged me, except one prince to whom I am writing, whom I have made certain of the possibility of the art by clear proof. Nor is it fitting that any but princes should know these things; otherwise many evils would be done by means of them: betrayals, deceptions, fornications, and whatever else wicked men might wish. But those using the art well could do much good for the commonwealth by it.

Moreover, I can teach all these things in every language of the whole world, even one I have never heard. I wanted to make these things known to you, Bostius, for this reason: so that you would not think me idle, and so forth.

But, Trithemius continues, before this letter of mine had reached Ghent, Bostius departed this life to Christ during the Easter days. The Father Prior of that place received the letter, opened it, read it, and, astonished, I suppose, by the novelty of the promises, shared it with many others who asked to read and copy it. Hence it happened that this letter of mine, in a short time published throughout almost all Germany and Gaul and repeatedly copied out by many people, brought many men, even very learned ones, into amazement and wonder, to such an extent that they hesitated as to what they ought truthfully to think about me.

Among these were some who firmly asserted that my promises in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, and had been shamelessly invented by me for the sake of empty glory. Others, however, said: “This abbot Trithemius promises great and astonishing things; if he can do them, he can do them only through the service of demons, since he plainly seems to go far beyond the limits of nature. But if he cannot do them, who will doubt that he is a liar and deservedly to be refuted by all good and learned men?” But may both charges be far from me, since I promised natural things.

Of all those who rashly judged my unknown matters, the most rash and most unjust appraiser of things at last came forth openly: Carolus Bovillus, a Picard by nation. After he had traveled through the upper parts of Alsace and had come to me at Sponheim for the sake of lodging, on his way back to Gaul through Trier, I treated him with all the courtesy I could, and with every kindness of hospitality for as long as he was with me.
