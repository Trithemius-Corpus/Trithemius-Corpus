For indeed he shows himself a rash judge who gives judgment on a matter before he has learned the truth of the case. First learn this art, and then judge; but if you cannot understand it, condemn not the art itself, for it is good, but your own dull wits. For I know and am certain that no one of sound mind can censure this work of ours unless he happens to be wholly ignorant of it. As for those to whom it is more familiar to despise wisdom than to have learned it, I neither desire nor would wish them to penetrate these mysteries of ours. Thus Trithemius in his own defense.

You have heard, I believe, whoever you are who accuse Trithemius of magic or superstition, either silently or openly, this solemn protestation of his, plainly worthy of a Catholic man; you have heard his defense; you have heard how much he begs and how much he insists that you not believe him a magus or necromancer, or one who made use of any superstitions. Certainly he would not urge this so often and so anxiously if he had ever been devoted to wicked arts of that sort. Therefore let no one wrong the dead man, who, while alive, wronged no one. Let everyone consider that all and each of the things handed down by Trithemius in a more obscure manner were handed down for certain reasons, often named; and let him beware lest, with Bouillus and others who did not know this art and long ago condemned it rashly, he deserve Trithemius’s just censure. And let it suffice to have brought forward these things which Trithemius himself, as his own defender, had to say in his defense. From our cloistered home, in the year 1615.

To the Reader.

The very reverend and most learned Father Johannes Busaeus, theologian of the Society of Jesus, laments, dear reader, in the dedicatory letter of his Trithemian volume to the most reverend and most illustrious prince, the Archbishop of Mainz, Archchancellor of the Holy Roman Empire through Germany and Prince-Elector, etc., that although, with his own help and that of others, he searched through many libraries, both near and remote, he nevertheless could not obtain all the works of Trithemius, which either Trithemius himself carefully listed in the Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers, the Chronicle of Sponheim, and his Letters, or his disciple Johannes Duraclusius listed one year before Trithemius’s death in a letter sent to Nicolaus Hamerius. And certainly it is greatly to be lamented that so many and so excellent writings of Trithemius have, in scarcely a single century, been so destroyed and lie so buried that almost no trace of them any longer seems to survive.

Yet not very long ago I came upon a certain Trithemian fragment, which I call a fragment for this reason, that through the injury of time the whole does not survive: namely, the Answers to the Eight Questions of Emperor Maximilian, which Johannes Duraclusius, Trithemius’s disciple, also recorded in his catalogue of Trithemian books. Since this fragment is of rare and pleasant subject matter, and since neither Father Busaeus, who gathered Trithemius’s spiritual works in a single volume, nor before him Marquard Freher, Palatine councillor, who comprised Trithemius’s historical works in two volumes, added it to their volumes, I have decided to place it here for you, learned reader, to read and examine, so that I might rescue it from destruction and at the same time affect you with pleasant reading.

Farewell and enjoy it.
