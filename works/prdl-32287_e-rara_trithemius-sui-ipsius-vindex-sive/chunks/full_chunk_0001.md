Johannes Trithemius, first Abbot of Sponheim, and afterward Abbot of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg. Very learned in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew letters. Poet, orator, philosopher, theologian; most accomplished master of natural magic, as, among his other writings, the Steganography and the Polygraphy especially teach.

He was born in the village of Trittenheim on the banks of the river Moselle, in the diocese of Trier, in the year 1462. He died at last in the year 1516, before completing the fifty-fourth year of his age, on the very day sacred to St. Lucy, not without the grief and lamentation of nobles, princes, and all learned men.

Trithemius, His Own Defender, or an apologetic defense of the Steganography of the most reverend, most learned, and most ingenious man Johannes Trithemius, first Abbot of Sponheim and afterward most worthy Abbot of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg.

Collected almost entirely from Trithemius himself and made public.

By the most reverend and most religious father, most eminent prelate and lord, Lord Sigismund, Abbot of the celebrated monastery of Seeon in Bavaria, of the Order of St. Benedict.

At the end has been added a Fragment of Questions by the same Trithemius.

With the permission of the superiors.

Ingolstadt, from the Ederian Press, at the house of Elisabeth Angermaria, widow, in the year 1616.

On Trithemius, His Own Defender After Death.

Is this how we refuse to let the buried shades find rest,
and do not allow the pious ashes to lie softly?
Trithemius is said to have practiced the ill-omened arts of Erebus,
and to have worshiped gods whom it is not lawful to worship.
Yet mighty Trithemius stands with noble weapons,
and himself vindicates himself from his own reputation.
Surely uprightness has no need of foreign arms,
for it holds strong weapons enough within itself.
Most beautiful virtue has no need of raised camps;
it is enough for it not to have lain too deeply hidden.
Yet scarcely, Trithemius, do you wash away the arts falsely charged against you,
nor by this art do you escape the stain of a dreadful name.
For he whose ashes, whose urn, we have known,
who now lies in his own tomb, an ancient shade,
will he again go, as if alive, against hostile enemies,
and wage posthumous wars with unconquered hand?
Will he again stand, a bony Mars, in noble arms?
Who would not say that this man was a great magus?

To the Reader.

It may perhaps seem strange to someone that I, entangled in domestic affairs, having long since drawn my mind away from the most beautiful studies of the liberal arts, should now suddenly set aside the throngs of business and undertake to defend the cause of Johannes Trithemius, a man long ago removed from human life, more than ninety-nine years ago. But if he weighs more deeply the reason for my plan, he will say not only that I am doing nothing foreign to my purpose, but also that for very just reasons I have descended into this apologetic writing.

In the year 1499 Johannes Trithemius composed, and then some years afterward dedicated to Philip, Count Palatine, Elector Prince and Duke of Bavaria, at whose urging and encouragement he had begun this work, or, as Johannes Duraclusius says, to Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, Elector Prince, that wonderful and laborious work, the Steganography. In that book he hands down a most certain art of safely and securely revealing the intention of one’s mind to absent persons through hidden writing.

And lest so great and important a secret should come to the ears of the unskilled common people or of wicked men, he assigns the offices, ranks, distinctions, properties, prayers, and various operations of spirits of every kind, through whose intelligences all the secrets of this science are closed and opened.

Some men were offended by such operations and names of spirits; among them there were also very grave, very learned, and very religious men, who, taking the words as they sound, conceived some evil and unfavorable suspicion of Trithemius, silently thinking that the said books had been written against the principles of natural philosophy, were utterly full of superstition, were packed with forbidden magic, and for that very reason should be reckoned among the books prohibited by the Church.
