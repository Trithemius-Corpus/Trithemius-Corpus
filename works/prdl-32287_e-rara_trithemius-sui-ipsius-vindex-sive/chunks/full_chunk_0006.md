For just as a good man and lover of honor can, for private or common good, secretly, safely, and without any suspicion from any mortal, whenever and as often as he wishes, make known the secret of his will to another who knows this art, perfectly, copiously, and completely, through letters visible to all, open or closed, so that no one, however learned or curious, can suspect anything of the sender’s secret, nor even, if he should suspect it, discover it, and can communicate and express it at any distance of places; so too any perverse, slippery, or malicious person, even one previously altogether ignorant of the Latin tongue, as soon as he has acquired this art, which he will be able to acquire from a master teaching him in two days at the very longest, will thereafter write letters in Latin, with suitable composition, open, beautiful, and sufficiently adorned in any narrative whatever, to a friend skilled in this art, in which he will wrap the secret of his will, accessible to that man alone, in such great mysteries that they cannot at all be penetrated by anyone, however studious or learned, without the aid of this art.

Thereafter the learned and the unlearned, man and woman, boy and old man, good and bad, chaste and rather wanton, will also write, in Latin or in whatever language is known to them, throughout all regions of the world, Latin, Greek, or barbarian letters free from all suspicion, in which he would show one thing openly to those who do not know the art, and another thing secretly to those who do. Nor, once this knowledge had been made public among the reprobate, would the fidelity contracted by sacrament between married persons henceforth remain secure, since a wife, though until then ignorant of the Latin tongue, now sufficiently instructed through chaste, honorable, and most holy words of any language or idiom, could very fully understand the evil and shameless mind and intention of an adulterous lover or fornicator, even while her husband carried the letters and praised them as excellent, and could in the same way most securely send back to him her own desire, as broadly and copiously as she wished, in the same or other letters, in a beautiful and quite sufficiently adorned sequence.

Indeed, although this knowledge is in itself excellent and quite useful to the commonwealth, nevertheless, if it were to come to the knowledge of the wicked, which God forbid, the whole order of the commonwealth could in the course of time be disturbed not a little, and public trust endangered; all letters, instruments, writings, and finally the very speech of men would be turned into the gravest suspicion. No one would any longer believe letters, however holy and honorable, without fear, but only rarely would anyone give credence to epistles. For however honorable and chaste the words might be, deceit, fraud, and deception would always be thought to lie beneath them, and men would become fearful in all things and, for the most part, no less suspicious toward friends than toward adversaries.

Therefore, to remove all these inconveniences which I have mentioned, and lest this science, in itself a physical, natural, and lawful science, if it came to the knowledge of all, should create such great evils and inconveniences, Trithemius not without reason used numbers, signs, and foreign names, as of spirits and of others, with which, as with mysteries, he wrapped that art, so that it should be easily accessible to none except one who knows that art, that is, to an honorable and pious man, but should remain forever hidden from the perverse and malicious. And we have learned that the most ancient sages, whom in Greek speech we call philosophers, once did this too; and it is the opinion of the most learned that, if they had discovered any secrets either of Nature or of Art, they hid them by various methods and figures, lest they come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews also confirm that Moses, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in the description of the creation of Heaven and Earth covered the ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words. Hence in many places of sacred Scripture, besides the literal sense, the allegorical, tropological, and anagogical senses have also been received by theologians. Saint Jerome, one of the most learned among our own, affirms that there are almost as many mysteries hidden in the Apocalypse of John as there are words.
