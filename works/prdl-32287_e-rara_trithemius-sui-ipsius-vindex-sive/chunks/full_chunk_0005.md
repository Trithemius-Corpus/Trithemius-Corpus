An eighteenth on Saint Andrew the Apostle.

A nineteenth on Saint Benedict, Abbot.

Apologetical Defense.

A twentieth, on all saints.

And for each of the named commemorations and saints he composed individual new offices of the Mass, conformable to the divine scriptures.

He wrote besides a little work of meditations and prayers on the Lord’s incarnation, nativity, life, passion, and death, suitable for devotion, in two books.

The Mirror of Heavenly Doctrine, of no little service to compunction of mind, one book, which begins thus: Salutis humanae principium, etc.

He also composed many most devout meditations and prayers on our Lord Jesus Christ, on his most holy Mother the Virgin Mary, and on various saints, with beautiful variety, together with very many other things, which all devout posterity will see and admire.

These, my dearest Nicholas, are the volumes of our Abbot Trithemius, which for nearly thirty-two years he worked over by continual study, besides those which, not yet finished, are still in hand and on the anvil, unknown to me. Farewell from my house at Duraclusia, on the fifth of October, in the year of our salvation one thousand five hundred and fifteen.

Thus far he. For my part, weighing the matter more deeply, I cannot sufficiently grieve that so great a man, who deserved so well of letters and learned men, and of the Catholic Church itself, should have fallen under such suspicion, as though he had written and published something contrary to the holy and orthodox faith, harmful to the Christian commonwealth, and unworthy of a religious conscience; since even from the very title prefixed to the Steganographia, as from the end which alone the Author set before himself, it is clear that nothing else is contained in that book than a definite art of revealing the will of one’s mind to the absent through hidden writing. For its use there is surely no need of magical and diabolical art, but only of physical and natural art, as is easily seen in the Key prefixed to this work.

And lest these secrets, or this art or science, should come to the knowledge of the commonwealth or the ears of wicked men, Trithemius thought it no small part of his duty to wrap it in mysteries, since it teaches mysteries to one who does not know how to understand them, so that there would be no one among the unskilled, no one except the most studious, who could by his own power fully penetrate the secret of this science, perfectly and according to the Author’s intention, unless through reception from a teacher. Nor would the knowledge of this most secret art, if made public to the wicked and reprobate, harm the commonwealth less, says Trithemius, than it would benefit the good. For just as good men and those devoted to virtues use all discoveries for good and for the common benefit, so the wicked and reprobate hunt out occasions not only from evil things, but even from good and most holy institutions, by which they may become worse.
