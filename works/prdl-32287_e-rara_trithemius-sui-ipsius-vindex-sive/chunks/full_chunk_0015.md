And since, as often happens among friends, especially those devoted to the study of letters, I had displayed all my things for my most welcome guest to see, among the rest there was also offered to him for remembrance the work of the Steganographia, not yet completed at that time. Bovillus saw it and read it cursorily, his mind occupied elsewhere; he said that he admired the invention, praised it, and took no care to understand in what manner it was to be understood. Hence, since he did not ask for the key to its understanding, he deserved to hear or perceive nothing of what was contained in that lucubration of ours.

After returning to France, he repaid us evil for good, and shamefully violated the bond of Christian friendship. For when he was asked by Lord Germain de Ganay, our common friend, afterwards Bishop of Orléans, what he had found and seen with me at Sponheim, he repeatedly wrote back falsehoods for truths, lies for benefits, condemning and rashly judging things that he was by no means able to comprehend with his intellect. For since he believed that the palm of learning would be conferred on him through envy and falsehood, he wrote back to that most learned man a letter full of falsity, lies, many injuries, and insults, in which, making mention of the Steganographia that he had not understood, he falsely, mendaciously, and with exceedingly injurious rash presumption proclaimed me devoted to wicked arts, a magician, and a necromancer.

To his most lying injuries and blasphemies, God having mercy, I shall shortly reply in such a way that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bovillus, in this matter, is an impious, cruel, and rash liar. I steadfastly affirm, I truly say, and confidently swear upon my soul that I have never had any dealings whatsoever with demons or with wicked and pernicious magical or necromantic arts, but that each and every thing that I have either written or promised to write is pure, sound, natural, and in no respect at all contrary to the Christian faith. Therefore I do not fear to submit my promises to examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as need shall arise, and I in no way intend to resist their determination.

There was a reasonable cause why I inserted this account of Bovillus’s rashness into this preface of mine: so that all may understand that it was done by mature counsel that the work of the Steganographia has hitherto remained buried in darkness, not because I fear Bovillus’s rash judgments, but lest I should seem to give many similar people cause to suspect similar things about me. For indeed, as far as I am concerned, I would rather appease my rivals by silence than irritate them with letters, or provoke them to madness by writings. For I judged it better to condemn all these wondrous inventions to perpetual silence than to incur the mark of harmful magical or necromantic superstition, even by the false estimation of men. Let the Steganographia, therefore, lie hidden in darkness, and let it not become common to the lying company of Bovillus, which is accustomed to judge matters unknown to it and to ruin the reputation of a good man from the mere lust of depravity.

Nevertheless, at the urging of friends, so that our rivals may understand, together with Bovillus, how rash and false an injury he has done us, while the Steganographia remains within its own bounds, we consent to publish the following work, which we call the Polygraphia, divided into six books. Let those who wish read it, and let friends read this volume without ill will; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves judge without envy whether these things are natural or superstitious. For I know and am certain that all things in it are sincere, pure, natural, and removed from every pursuit of evil superstition. And whenever the discourse is more obscure, this is done for the reason that a madman may not abuse the sword of defense to destruction, which a man sound in spirit and mind carries for his own protection. Yet nothing evil is contained in it on that account, since perverse men abuse even good things for evil. But whoever presumes to condemn this edition of ours is himself conscious and witness that he has never truly understood it.
