And so, if it were permitted, without prejudice to the judgment of others, to give my own opinion about this book, I would indeed say, supported by the preceding causes and reasons, that this whole third book contains in itself a method of revealing the secret of one’s mind to a friend, not indeed by open and visible letters, but either by arithmetical numbers, of which sort Trithemius has many numerical schemes in place of letters in his book on Polygraphy, or, as I would more readily believe, by those invisible methods of which he speaks in his Polygraphy when discussing arthrology and Bede’s dactylology, in these words:

“And who could set forth all the ancient methods of writing secretly? For some had owls as familiars in the service of the art set before them; some called them Philophoti, others Mysophoti, others both, in the practice of the art. Again, others transposed the known letters of the common alphabet; others devised characters of their own under the form of barbarian letters. Again, others used the service of visible letters, writing with minium or common encaustic; but others formed invisible characters at will according to their pleasure. The methods of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many and altogether innumerable; to make mention of them all belongs neither to our will nor to our ability. But of invisible characters there are three kinds more commonly used among mortals today. The first is called Dermatic, by which writing is made on the back of messengers by a certain artificial method, visible only through the washing with a certain liquid; no one can penetrate it who does not know the secret of the art. The second is called Hyphasmatic, by which something is written on cloth with certain substances; once dried it cannot be seen at all, until it is perceived when moistened in cold water. The third kind is called Aleotic, which is manifold and varied, both in form and in material, and has almost innumerable methods of writing secretly. Its writing is done on paper or without paper, with nemilua and cala, with raphocam lapolce, and with various other things. Letters are likewise written and hidden in various ways and places: under race tabulata, under rofiri lacubior, in leopi, in necoflas, in ceocali, locubat, in ratera, in lispilia, and in other similar things almost infinite in number,” etc.

See, reader, in how many ways you can report the conception of your mind to an absent friend, even without letters, and without the knowledge of the very person who performs the office of messenger. This perhaps is what Trithemius meant when in this book he says that letters can be sent without a messenger, as we also noted above. And hence Trithemius, in the said third book, on the varied motion of the planets and the interpretation of the tables, almost at the end, wishes the operator to use encaustic, not the common kind, such as he mentioned before when speaking of visible letters, but, as he says, “prepared for this purpose in our manner.” Calepinus reports from Apuleius and Pliny that encaustum or encaustice is a kind of painting done by the application of fire, that is, with burnt colors and wax dissolved by fire. Hence Trithemius says in his letter to Bostius, to whom he writes what steganography teaches, in these words; we shall reproduce the whole letter below:

Note well. Read attentively, you who desire to read these things with profit.

“The second book,” and I plainly think this is the third, for he began eight books on this subject, which now, changing his plan, he reduces to four, “contains many more wonderful things. By fire, namely, see, by fire, in this art I can make known the conception of my mind to one who knows my art, at whatever distance, at a hundred miles or more, securely, without words, without writings, without signs, through any messenger whatsoever; and if he is seized on the road, even if he is questioned under the harshest tortures, he can confess nothing about my message, because he knows absolutely nothing about it. Whatever happens, my message will always remain hidden, nor, if all the men of the whole world were gathered together, could they trace it out by natural power,” etc. See below in the letter to Bostius. And let this be the judgment concerning the third Steganographic book.
