For Trithemius himself writes thus, among other things, to Rogerius Sicambrus, who had asked for the titles of Trithemius’s works:

“After this I began to write the great and exceedingly difficult Steganographic work, of marvelous invention, to be divided into eight books. I brought the first and second books through to the end; but the third I left incomplete, and all the rest I left wholly buried down to this day, persuaded by many considerations, since the labor is great and perhaps the recompense small. Therefore let it remain imperfect as it lies, until some other usefulness persuades otherwise.”

He says almost the same in his letter to Nicolaus Phorcelius, and elsewhere throughout his letters to friends. And indeed, how wavering he was, and how much persuaded by many considerations not easily to allow the Steganographic work to come forth into public, is clear not only below from the Polygraphic preface to Emperor Maximilian, but especially from the letter to Johannes Capellarius, the Parisian mathematician, to whom he writes thus:

“As for my Steganography, whose first two books you saw at Cologne, I do not know whether I shall ever publish it, for three considerations. The first reason that draws me back from its publication is consideration of the future, and a plausible fear of evil that might occur through the abuse of wicked men, since bad men would use my inventions for evil no less than good men for good. The second is the prospect of very great labor and a small return of reward. For, as you saw, the work is difficult and of the sharpest speculation, and it cannot be completed without very great labor. And what recompense is there for so much labor, unless perhaps some modest weight of gold, which is transitory, and therefore rather to be despised by a Christian monk? It was never my intention to labor for a temporal reward, since I see that I have been called into the vineyard of the eternal King. The third cause is the judgment of the unlearned common people, who ascribe to evil arts everything they do not understand. I have written nothing yet at which anyone could deservedly marvel; I have done nothing astonishing; and yet I suffer the opinion of the crowd, while very many think me a magician, asserting that I have raised the dead, summoned demons from the underworld, foretold the future, recovered thieves by charms, and bound robbers. All these things are contrived and fabricated; I have never done them, nor even thought of them.”

And a little later:

“What do you think the foolish rashness of unlearned men will imagine about me in the future, when I have brought the Steganography to light, since it has devised such lies about me when it has as yet seen nothing secret? Although, content with the testimony of my conscience, I do not make much of these chatterings of the ignorant, nevertheless I do not intend, so far as possible, to give occasion for evil suspicion.”

Behold how conscious his mind was of rectitude, and how much good Trithemius feared for himself, lest by this work of his he offend anyone, especially the unlearned and inexperienced, who dare to pass an unfavorable judgment on such good and holy studies, though they have by no means grasped them with understanding. But now let us pass to the uses of these books.

Indeed, what uses and advantages can arise from this kind of writing when rightly employed, Trithemius himself teaches very beautifully, though in part obscurely, in his preface placed before the Polygraphia and addressed to Emperor Maximilian. And although he refers most of those uses to the Polygraphia, they can nevertheless also be referred, not unsuitably, to the Steganography.

“The first usefulness,” says Trithemius, “is that the completed understanding of this work gives an easy method to one in need, for making known the secret of your mind safely, secretly, and without any suspicion on the part of any observers, to one skilled in the industry of the art, however widely it may be necessary, across any distance of places, by regulated speech. What lies hidden in these things, the work of Steganography brings forth: it requires neither letters nor a messenger to carry secret matters openly. Do not take the matter otherwise than it is. The second usefulness is that it threatens [unclear].”
