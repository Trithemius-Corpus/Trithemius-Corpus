The Steganography of Trithemius.

This must be said beforehand: there is nothing frivolous in this art of ours, nothing contrary to the evangelical tradition or to the Catholic faith, nothing at all handed down in a superstitious way. For all the things which we have either said in the preceding volume or shall say in the following ones rest upon natural, lawful, and honorable principles; and the mystery, veiled only by foreign usages, and the words wrapped in the names of spirits, require a learned reader. For we use the ministry of a spirit to veil a secret which, if made public, would harm the reprobate, and so forth.

You speak very well, Trithemius; you plead your cause very well; you defend yourself very well not only from the biting Bouillus, but also from every malicious tongue, whether present or future. But let us hear what you finally say in the preface to the first book of the Steganography.

“And lest anyone,” says Trithemius, “who is to be the reader of this work, when in its course he often meets with names, offices, orders, distinctions, properties, prayers, and any other operations of spirits, through whose intelligences all these secrets of this science are closed and opened, should believe or suppose me to be a necromancer and magician, or to have entered into a pact with demons, or to have used or be using any other superstition whatsoever, I have thought it necessary and fitting, by solemn protestation in this prologue, to preserve my reputation and name from so great a disgrace, injury, fault, and stain, vindicating myself with the truth.

“I say therefore, and before almighty God, from whom absolutely nothing can be hidden, and before Jesus Christ, his only-begotten Son, who will judge the living and the dead, I say, swear, and protest in truth: all and every one of the things which I have said or shall say in this work, all the things by which the knowledge or properties of the art, its methods, form, operations, traditions, receptions, formations, discoveries, institutions, changes, alterations, and everything that pertains, in part or in whole, to its contemplation, discovery, making, operation, and practice, and all things contained in this our volume, rest upon true, Catholic, and natural principles.

“All and every one of them are done with God, with a good conscience, without injury to the Christian faith, with the integrity of ecclesiastical tradition, without any superstition, without idolatry, without any pact of evil spirits, explicit or implicit; without fumigation, adoration, veneration, worship, sacrifice, or offering to demons, and without any fault or sin, whether venial or mortal. All and every one of them are done with truth, uprightness, sincerity, and purity, so that the knowledge and practice of this invention, when put to good use, may not be unworthy of a wise man, a good and faithful Christian.

“For I too am a Christian, voluntarily devoted to the monastic way of life, and I desire to live and conduct myself in no other way than befits a true Christian and a monk professed under the rule of the blessed father Benedict. This rule and the Catholic faith, according to the tradition of the holy Roman Church, I received from the cradle, having been baptized in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. This faith I hold and believe with that same Church and with the universal Church of Christians; and as long as I live, with God’s help, I shall hold it firmly, always preserving it inviolate in heart, mouth, and deed, nor do I ever intend to depart from it on any occasion.

“Far be it from me, therefore, either to learn or to teach anything that is contrary to the Christian faith and purity, harmful to holy morals, or in any way opposed to my regular profession. I fear God, and I have sworn myself to his worship; from him, neither living nor, as I trust, dead, shall I be separated. I have placed this protestation of mine before this whole work of ours not without cause, because I know that there will be many who, when they cannot understand what we have written, will turn to insults and attribute our good and holy studies to evil arts or superstitious inventions.

“Since such men will exist, we earnestly beg them, and all who shall read these our tokens, that if they understand this secret tradition of ours, they keep it forever hidden and not pour such wonderful mysteries out into public view. But if they do not understand it...”
