Added to this is that for this apologetic defense I have taken nothing, or very little, of my own, but almost everything from Trithemius himself, so that so great and learned a man, Trithemius, might seem not to need my help, being sufficiently strong with his own sword and destined himself to be the avenger of his own innocence.

And so, that it may more readily appear who Trithemius was and how far removed he was from all superstition, I shall first bring forward the letter of Johannes Duraclusius, his disciple, to Nicolaus Hamerius of Emmerich, setting forth the life and writings of Trithemius; then a few words on behalf of the author of the Steganographia and its third book; finally, those things which Trithemius himself, as though not unaware of what was to come, published here and there throughout his books in defense of his Steganographia.

From all these things it will shine forth clearer than light that Johannes Trithemius was not a magician, but a most obedient, most submissive, and most humble son of the Church, and that all the things he wrote in the Steganographia are supported by physical and natural principles, and therefore are by no means to be condemned under the name of forbidden magic. Read, friendly reader; weigh, and judge. Farewell.

Apologetic Defense of the Steganographia of the Very Reverend, Very Learned, and Likewise Most Ingenious Man, Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim.

Johannes Trithemius, abbot, was born in the village of Trittenheim, in the diocese of Trier, on the banks of the river Moselle, on the first day of February, at eleven-thirty in the afternoon, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1462, in the tenth Roman indiction; his father was Johannes de Heidenberg, and his mother was called Elisabeth of Long Village, namely in the year in which the city of Mainz, captured in the defense of two pontiffs, lost its liberty and found servitude, with many of its citizens driven into exile; in the fourth year of Pope Pius, the second of that name, and the twenty-second of Emperor Frederick III.

But in the year of the Lord 1482, in the monastery of Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, on the first day of February, the very day on which he had once been born, he laid aside the secular habit and took up the monastic habit under the rule of our holy Father Benedict, according to the observance newly introduced there. In the following year, 1483, after Johannes de Colenhusen, the abbot, had been transferred by canonical postulation to Seligenstadt, Johannes Trithemius, by the common election of the brethren, was promoted and adorned as abbot of the said monastery of Sponheim on the fourth day before the Kalends of July, and presided for twenty-three years, one month, and nineteen days. When these had passed, not without many labors, provoked by the rashness of certain rivals, he accepted the abbey of St. James at Würzburg, which was being offered to him, and of his own free will resigned Sponheim, which was full of useless cares.

In both places he wrote many little works, of which he recently gave me the following list in his own hand on a sheet. Since you, as a friend, ask me for their titles, I gladly agree to your wishes, so that from considering such great labors of his you may hold more closely the memory of our common friend and teacher.

And first I shall record the titles of those writings which he laboriously composed while he was abbot at Sponheim.

First he wrote:

The Constitutions of His Order, Which Were Made in the Provincial Chapters by Command of the Presidents, one book.

On the Visitation of Monks, one book.

On the Manner of Celebrating a Provincial Chapter, one book.

On the Rule of St. Benedict, two books.

Homilies and Exhortatory Sermons to His Monks, two books, which he dedicated to his predecessor.

On the Temptations of Cloistered Men, and Their Remedies, two books.

To Nicolaus, Priest of Mernica, on the Priestly Life, one book.

On the Vanity and Misery of This Life, one book.

A penthic, that is, mournful, work On the Ruin of His Order, one book.
