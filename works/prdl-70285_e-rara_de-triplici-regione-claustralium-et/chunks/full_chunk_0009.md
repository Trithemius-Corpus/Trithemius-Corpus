Second, the citizens of this dark region are disorderly toward their prelate, when they disparage his office, though they are not ignorant that he has been ordained by God to preside in the monastery. On an easy occasion they murmur against him; they do not observe his commands; and those who had long ago, for love of God, subjected themselves to the arms of holy obedience now, after the manner of Sarabaites, with obedience despised, usurp for themselves the will of their own desires as a pernicious lord. They desire to have an abbot suited to their own depraved habits; they reject one who is holy and useful. If they find a lax and sluggish pastor, they intoxicate him with many poisons by flattering him, calling him the best and most pious father; and they subject themselves to him not humbly but willingly, since from him they fear no correction of their wickedness.

Third, carnal monks show themselves disorderly toward their own pastors when they withhold from them the affection of filial love and scorn to obey according to the Rule. Yet since a monk is bound to love his abbot with sincere affection as the vicar of Christ, he will not be able to hate him without very grave crime. Indeed, he who does not love his pastor for the love of Christ is not a true lover of God. For Christ, who wishes even enemies to be loved, how would he permit his own vicars to be hated? Therefore consider him who wished an abbot to be set over you in his own stead, and henceforth there will be no cause of displeasure against him.

Fourth, the disorderly citizens of the shadowy valley derogate from reverence for their pastor, when they not only disdain to render him the honor due by the Rule, but, what is worse, do not even fear to inflict insult upon him. For the Rule declares that the abbot holds the place of Christ in the monastery, and all monks ought humbly to obey him as Christ himself, since the obedience that is rendered to superiors is shown to Christ. But perverse cloisterers, always ungrateful for the precedence of their prelates, do not fear contumaciously to oppose the divine ordinance; they disdain to obey the commands of the holy Rule; and they dishonor their abbot, to whom they ought to show all reverence, tearing him with hatred and detraction. Whatever he does, whatever he ordains, whatever he has said, it displeases them as badly done, since at all times they detract from his way of life; despising his office, these diseased sheep seek nothing more than the confusion of their pastor. Some of these judge and despise their abbot in secret; others contradict him to his face; and while they neglect to show reverence to their pastor, they throw all observance of discipline into confusion. Pride is always familiar to them, and shamefulness friendly to the flesh, since those who voluntarily withdraw themselves from the affection of their pastor fall into abominable temptations and the worst crimes. For whoever despises the vicar of Christ in the monastery hands over to the devil the power of raging against himself, and confounds the unity of the mystical body.

Fifth, carnal monks, disorderly toward their pastor, disparage his authority when they think that nothing is permitted to him in the cenobium unless they themselves have consented. With haughty neck they despise the virtue of humility; they spurn regular subjection; they disdain to yield to their betters. They wish to thrust themselves into all business and cases, and to usurp for themselves the primacy of counsel, so that the abbot may dare to do or arrange nothing unless the matter first proceeds by their consent. But what does the blessed legislator Benedict say in the Rule? “The abbot, having heard the counsel of the brothers, should consider it with himself and do what he judges more useful.” And again: “Let the brothers give counsel with all the subjection of humility, so that they do not presume insolently to defend what has seemed good to them; rather, let it depend on the abbot’s judgment, so that all may obey what he has judged to be more salutary.” And again, concerning the ordaining of a prior, he says: “We have provided that, for the sake of preserving charity and peace, the ordering of his monastery should depend on the abbot’s decision.” This ordinance certainly concerns the appointment and removal of all officials of the monastery; it requires not the consent of the brothers but the counsel of those who fear God. Finally, the Rule commands that everything be done according to the will of the abbot, and that absolutely nothing be presumed that has not been enjoined by him.
