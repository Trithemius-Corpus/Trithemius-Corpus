And again the Lord says of the prelates of the faithful: “He who despises you despises me.” What will you say to these things, rebellious monks? Will you cite laws to me? How long will you corrupt the truth? But I dare to say what I wish you were ashamed to hear. In the strict judgment of God, rebellious monk, presumption of law will not save you, who now proudly scorn the obedience of subjection. Law commands obedience; it requires humble subjection within order.

Consequently rebellious monks fall into the eleventh degree of pride, which is the liberty of sinning: they scorn not only the corrections and exhortations of the brethren, but also those of the prelates. For truly, when rebellion is taken up against the teacher, boldness in sinning is produced, since he who despises the authority of correction already commits all crimes as though lawfully. Love of God voluntarily restrains good cenobites from sins, and fear holds back the weak. But where neither love of God nor fear of correction penetrates the monk, then the mind, through rebellion and the habit of sinning, draws near to hell. For Scripture says: “When the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.” What does he despise? He despises God; he scorns men; in despair, he does not know how to seek the salvation of his soul.

They fall, therefore, into the twelfth degree of pride who through boldness in sinning run into evil habit. This is the nearest descent to hell; this is the path of hell; this is the swift way to the region covered with the darkness of death. Whoever has once fallen into this degree of the habit of sinning rises again only by the strong hand of God’s compassion, because when habit is not resisted, a necessity of sinning is produced. For wickedness always advances toward the worse, and, always falling, falls more deeply.

Behold, these are the twelve degrees of pride by which one descends to hell; whoever desires to escape them should always strive to keep himself in the observance of humility. In these twelve evils every unworthiness of vices darkens the cenobite, makes him disordered toward himself, and in morals changes the man into a very savage beast. The end of these things is destruction; and unless before the death of the flesh the remedy of penitence intervenes, the progress of the evil degrees damns the monk who will burn without [unclear].

Whoever, being disordered toward himself, has once fallen first into the degree of pride, if he does not strive to rise as quickly as possible through penitence, soon casts himself headlong into the second and consequently into all the rest in order. But the disorder of carnal monks toward themselves is expressed in sum in these degrees of elation, by which they are shown to be citizens of the lowest region. What, however, is said of disordered and proud monks, we must also understand of prelates of the lowest region.

*In what way the citizen of the lowest region [unclear]. Article VI*

Whoever, established in a monastery, neglects to order his inner affections toward the purity of holy [unclear], frequently also transgresses enormously in outward matters. Monks disordered toward God are consequently proved to be disordered toward all things. For just as the good cenobite, united by love to the divine will, considers all secondary things in the right order of reason with a simple eye, so one turned away from God through disordered affections sins even in the goods that he uses badly. Therefore carnal monks, cultivators of the lowest region, are badly disordered not only toward the soul but also toward the temporal substance of the monastery. For an outward disturbance of the body follows the inward confusion of the mind, and the one demonstrates the other.

First, indeed, the citizen of the lowest region is badly ordered toward the goods of the monastery when they demand that these be granted to them in an improper order, or in any way usurp more than the use of the regular institution permits. For the Rule commands that all necessary things be given to the monks; it utterly forbids superfluities.

Second, those use the goods of the monastery badly who do not fear to give and receive contrary to the master’s prohibition, or who faithlessly commit deceit and fraud in things entrusted to them. For a monk who, according to his own whim, without reasonable cause, contrary to the Rule, in any way without permission squanders the goods of the monastery.
