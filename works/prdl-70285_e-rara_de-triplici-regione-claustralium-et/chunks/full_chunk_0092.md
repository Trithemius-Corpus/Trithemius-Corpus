Whoever, therefore, despises the vicar of any king has undoubtedly shown contempt for the king himself. But tell me, good brother, why do you withhold due reverence from your prelate, whom you hear to be the vicar of Christ? Perhaps you consider him wicked and wholly unworthy of such an office, and for that reason you think it lawful for you to despise him? Not so, brother, not so; but consider whose place it is in which the vicar of Christ presides, and know that you honor the one who sends, not the one sent. If you wish to be Christ’s disciple, consider always for what purpose you have come, and you will never deny reverence to an unworthy prelate. For what purpose have you come? Certainly that, according to the Gospel and the Rule, you may deny yourself for God’s sake; that under another’s command you may serve Christ as a soldier; that for these things you may patiently await the eternal reward. Therefore, brother, do not, do not lose the crown of eternal happiness prepared for you in heaven because of displeasure with one man, a crown which you ought not to exchange for the whole world. Remember that you have nothing unless from your own [unclear]; you must humbly obey him as vicar of him to whom you have subjected yourself, and you must not examine either his life or his command, since it is not lawful for you to judge your shepherd. For in your profession you swore that you ought not to carry out your own will, but that you wished to obey the abbot in all things according to the Rule. The abbot, therefore, is lord of your will, not by his own assumption, but by the ordinance of God, whose vicar he also is. But the handling of another’s property against its lord’s will is theft, and you know that thieves will have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God. Thus a monk who has made profession according to the Rule to God and to his abbot no longer thereafter has lordship over his own will; and if afterward he usurps that same will of his for himself, he commits theft and sacrilege. Nor will permission obtained save the presumptuous man, if it was obtained by threats and insults. Your abbot, O brother, is the vicar of God; to him you must render the obedience promised, not as to a man but as to God, and show humble reverence in God’s place. For whatever is rendered by subjects to their ruler is undoubtedly referred to God, whose vicar he is. Since, therefore, you have subjected yourself to your prelate for God alone and for no other cause, you should strive at all times to show yourself such toward him that he may freely be able to command you to do or omit whatever seems expedient to him, and may have no fear at all of any insolences or disturbances of your mind. For if your abbot is compelled to fear you, his monk, the order of equity is violated; and where the disciple strives to be above the master, sin is committed against the Gospel. Beware, therefore, lest you withhold reverence and honor from prelates; beware lest by saddening them you offend the Lord. Rather take care to meet them reverently, whatever kind of men they may be, and to obey their commands in all things according to the Gospel and the Rule. Believe me, brother, that the fault which is committed by a subject against a prelate is punished very severely, either in the present life or in the life to come. For it is written: Do not touch my anointed ones. Whatever your prelate commands you, you ought to receive it with such confidence that you believe God has inspired it in him.

*Let the monk’s conduct toward the brothers be most charitable.*

Secondly, the monk’s conduct toward his fellow brothers is commanded to have charity, through which alone peace and concord are maintained in the monastery. Let our faithful cenobite always think on, and turn over in his heart, the common joy of souls in eternal happiness; and let him continually direct the eye of his mind toward the equal condition of souls, so that his thought may not withdraw from love of future fellowship. If a brother offends you in any way by word or deed, if he has been troublesome or hostile to you in anything, do not weigh the movement of disordered sensuality arising from it; do not consider his present weakness, but lift up the eyes of your mind to the nobility of his soul, and consider what sort of fellowship in eternal happiness it will have with you.
