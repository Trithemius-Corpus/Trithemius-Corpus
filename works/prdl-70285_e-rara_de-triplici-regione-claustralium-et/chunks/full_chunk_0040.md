The third member necessary for the fourth degree of good will is called the total extension of the will toward God; through it the soul, admitted into divine love and sweetly burning, is in a certain way transformed into that same love, and is often admitted into the third region.

Finally, this transformation into divine love, according to Bernard's opinion, is love, loving affection, adherence, conjunction, charity, or fruition; no one can attain it unless he has learned to mortify all the affections of his lower spirit. For the spirit of a man who has his heart raised upward into God is called perfect will, and is united with God by a most sweet bond. In these degrees of good will, therefore, the faithful cenobite must direct his effort upward without interruption, and always examine with prudent zeal how much he is advancing or falling short. For Saint Bernard says here: Just as he who remains in a vicious will is condemned, so he is blameworthy who does not strive to advance in other things. For where the effort to advance is laid aside, the danger of falling back is incurred.

*Concerning the exemplary and experiential [aspect] of all the aforesaid. Article VII.*

Although a man does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless each person, by diligently examining his conscience, can recognize what he ought to think about the condition of his state. What sort each person is inwardly, hidden within himself, the testimony of his own conscience makes manifest. Therefore, so that the cenobite may clearly discern the condition of his state, he must observe three things in his conscience, according to John Castellensis on the Rule; without these no one becomes a good Christian, no one is judged a good man or monk.

The first thing, therefore, required for a good man is the testimony of a good conscience, namely that no mortal sin lie hidden in the conscience. For the mind that is polluted by the guilt of mortal sin testifies that the state of the conscience is evil.

Therefore each monk must diligently examine himself and carefully cleanse himself of the faults he has committed, according to the ordinance of the Church. But he who, on examining his conscience, finds it clean from mortal sin should offer humble praises to God and not neglect to add the examination of the following things.

The second thing necessary for a good man, accordingly, is to intend God principally in all works, and to order all things, as far as he can, to his praise and honor. But if, because the multitude and variety of works impede him, a man cannot always actually have God before his eyes, let him at least habitually conform his intention of living according to the most pleasing will of God to this desire.

The third is that in all things he obey God and holy mother Church and the prelates set over him, and not depart from the path of right and enlightened reason; for whoever despises this threefold obedience has denied the faith. It is necessary, therefore, to show equal obedience to these three in right order, according to the ordinance of reason, so that the life of the monk may remain, without fear, irreproachable in its actions.

These three things, possessed with humble sincerity, make a good man and show the state of a conscience that hopes well in the Lord. But a man to whom one of these is lacking is not perfectly good, even if he seems to be good. But if one who repents of his sins has resolved henceforth to fulfill these three things, he is now made good and pleasing to God, however evil he had been before. Moreover, so that from a good man a good religious or spiritual man may be born, three other things are required, according to the aforesaid Castellensis.

The first thing required for making a good religious is that purity of heart be cleansed, through zeal for discipline, from every emulation or incitement of the flesh. This purity is never better acquired than if the world, with all things, is despised.
