*At the first [signal]*

let the monk go to the secrecy of his inner chamber; let him diligently examine his conscience, cleansing it, and compose himself in the following three things.

First, preparing his zeal for prayer, he should strive to recall his mind from outward things, vain and perishable and contrary to the spirit, to inward things, and to think absolutely nothing of secular or temporal affairs. Just as Abraham is read to have stood firm and driven the birds away from the sacrifice, so it seems necessary for us to act against worldly thoughts that fly importunately around us during divine services. The monk can bring his mind more easily to this forgetfulness of passing things if he will often recall to memory the sixth root, set above in the second region, concerning the vanity of present things.

Second, let him direct the eyes of his mind with humility to knowledge of himself, knowing that by his own strength he cannot persist in the holy fervor of the spirit unless divine compassion deigns to help him. Finally, let the humble monk understand that he is not worthy to praise his Creator, and from this let him seek help from the Lord and the merit of worthy praise. But if he desires to know more about knowledge of himself, let the devout monk read the fourth root.

Third, for one wishing to stand devoutly before the Lord God in prayers, it is necessary to raise the mind with fervor of spirit to heavenly things. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: God is spirit, and those who adore him must adore him in spirit and in truth. The most devout suppliant of God, Bernard, also says: The young soldier of Christ must be taught in his prayer to lift his heart upward, to pray spiritually, to withdraw from images of bodily things, and to think nothing bodily.

But because it is not granted to all to be able to raise the mind above understanding to the invisible God, provision must also be made for the weak by an easier manner of elevation. Hence he also says: For the weak person meditating or praying, the image of the Lord’s nativity, humanity, passion, or resurrection is more safely set before him, so that the weak soul, which knows nothing except body, may be moved toward divine things according to the measure of its capacity. For while through faith in the form of the mediator it does not divide God from man, it sometimes reaches God in man. For our Savior, the mediator of God and men, Jesus Christ, is God and man.

Therefore let the devout monk learn often to turn over the first root of divine knowledge, so that he may be able to raise himself higher through love. For the more perfectly God is known, the more ardently he is loved; and the more he is loved, the better he is praised. For praise is very near to love.

But so that you may always be able freely to raise your heart in prayer to the Lord God, learn to love nothing in this world in a singular way, because a mind that is affected by absolutely nothing is more rarely distracted. For nothing so unburdens the heart and renders it free; nothing makes it converse before the Lord so generously and without constraint; and nothing so quickly and lightly gathers a person back to inward things after various outward disturbances, as to be affected in all things and circumstances by no private attachment to anything, but to live humbly in all things, resigned to God and to one’s superiors. Moreover, whatever has been enjoined upon one so disposed for the glory of God and the preservation of regular discipline, the monk accepts all this with humble obedience, whether it is convenient or troublesome.
