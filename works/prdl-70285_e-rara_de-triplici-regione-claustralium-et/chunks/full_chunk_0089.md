*On the Labor of the Hands. Consideration 10.*

Since our most holy father Benedict, in the Rule, commending as true monks those who live by the labors of their own hands, declares them blessed, it seemed fitting to us that after the end of [unclear] we should say something about this most holy exercise. Indeed, we read that this work was commended in many ways by the holy fathers, in which we know that they advanced before God and men in no small measure. For the holy Apostle says: He who does not labor, let him not eat. And what the most learned doctor Augustine thought about this manual exercise, he shows most clearly in the book *On the Work of Monks*. We also read that those ancient, most holy fathers dwelling in solitude sweated not lukewarmly over the work of their hands, so that they might not incur the danger of idleness, and, living from their own labors after the example of the most blessed doctor of the Gentiles, might burden no one. Would that we too, brothers, might deign to undertake this holy exercise of laboring with the same intention and goodwill as they, so that after the end of our labor we may deserve to rest with them without end in the heavenly homeland.

*Four Things Are to Be Observed by Monks in Labor.*

When summoned to labor, the monk should strive to show himself such in all things that he does not lose the merit of bodily labor through dissolution of mind. For we see many among monks going out to labor in no right way, whom we perceive to seek nothing other than the enticements of the flesh. Of these, some take up useless and less necessary labors so that, in the time of the regular fast, they may have an occasion for eating. For they labor in order to eat; whether it benefits or harms the monastery, they do not care. Others are accustomed to arrange various labors so that, as if by occasion, they can run about here and there without the bridle of discipline. Others procure labors useless to the community so that they may freely indulge in vain conversations and frivolities. Others love outside labors so that, as if through opportunity and without suspicion, they may be able to see and speak with people, not to say those whom they know, secular persons and friends, for the flesh and not for God. Others, because of instability of mind, being unable to be quiet in the monastery, always seem to pant after labors. The intention of all who labor in this way is not sincere, and therefore they do not attain the excellent reward of labor. But you, brother, when you are called to labor, take care to submit your intention to the regular institution, so that you love nothing other than the honor of God, the merit of obedience, and the usefulness of the monastery; intend nothing except the advancement of the soul. And while you are at labor, do not imprudently extinguish the fervor of the spirit; do not omit the exercise of spiritual conversation, but take care diligently to observe everywhere the following four things. In outward conduct be faithful, serviceable, and benevolent; inwardly, humble and devout; in speech, sparing and edifying; in manners, composed and mature. If you keep these four things in labor, you will always return to your cell better.

*Outwardly the Monk Becomes Faithful, Benevolent, and Serviceable.*

The first thing that must be diligently observed by a cenobite in labor is that in laboring he should always show himself faithful to his monastery; in serving and ministering, or even in carrying heavier burdens, he should from charity anticipate the brothers; and in all words and deeds he should show himself benevolent and tractable.
