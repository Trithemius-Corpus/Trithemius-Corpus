The highest truth and unchangeable good is God, who said to Moses: “Man shall not see me and live.” And the disciple of the highest truth says: “No one has ever seen God.” But also the prophet, addressing the Lord, says: “Truly, you are a hidden God.” Although, however, the three aforesaid powers of the soul cannot in this life perfectly comprehend their object, nevertheless none should cease from its duty, lest it incur the danger of failing there where it has abandoned the appointed effort. Therefore the citizens of this middle region mortify evil affections in themselves; they adorn themselves with continual virtues; they direct all their works with right intention; they depart not at all from the office of reason.

The citizens of this region are scholastic, speculative, and active men, devoted to various and indeed honorable arts, who subject the sensual or carnal powers to reason; who do not neglect to cleanse these three powers of the soul from all illicit objects; who always raise their intention toward the end of honorable usefulness; who reform the will, in act or in habit, toward its due end. Therefore the coenobite, a lover of this cleansing, who desires to ascend to the third region of the spirit, while remaining in the middle region, must observe the following principal articles.

*The summary of the truth of this middle region is expressed in seven principal articles.* Whoever desires to make progress in this middle region according to the vow of his profession should diligently take care to observe the following articles; if he neglects to keep them, he shows that he is still a citizen not of the middle but of the lowest region. For, as we said above, no one can remain in this middle region without a continual effort to make progress; in it, not to have progressed is to fail, and not to have ascended is to descend. Therefore the monk who, having overcome the dark region, has at last, with the Lord’s help, deserved to arrive at this middle one should know that he must beware lest he abandon, even for a little, the effort to make progress; but he should always stretch himself toward what lies further, and remember the admonition of the wise man, who says: “Unless you hold yourself constantly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overturned,” that is, your conscience. For it is impossible for anyone to make progress in holy conduct who has neglected daily to renew, through fear of the Lord, the effort to make progress. The effect of this renewal is shown to be ordered when the truth of the articles of the middle region is duly observed. The first article, therefore, is the diligent intention of the coenobite’s end; the second is the due restraint of the sensual powers; the third, the faithful cleansing of the powers; the fourth, the attentive reformation of the works of the lowest region; the fifth, the ordered institution of the true coenobite’s progress; the sixth, the adaptation of the powers for entry into the third region; and the seventh, the exemplary or experiential certainty of all the aforesaid matters.

*On the continual and diligent intention of the end of coenobitic conduct. The first article.*

Since the end is first in the intention of the wise man, and each thing is judged more perfect in its own kind insofar as it more nearly approaches its end, the monk who cultivates this middle region should always consider why he entered religion; and if he finds his intention ordered, he should strive to continue it; but if he finds it corrupt, he should not neglect to reform it to the rectitude of the due end.

But note that in spiritual exercises the end is described as twofold, through which the virtue of monastic conduct is increased in merits. The first is charity, which is called principal and ultimate, to which all monastic exercises are ordered as to their first cause. Whatever good we do must always and principally be ordered to this end, since no work of virtue receives merit unless charity always establishes the due foundation.
