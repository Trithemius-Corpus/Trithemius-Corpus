Therefore the more the monk has taken care to conform himself to him in the way of virtues by imitation, the more like and nearer to him he will undoubtedly be in the glory of eternal blessedness. Whatever you do, always look to him, and consider how he bore himself in all things according to the Gospel and your own judgment. For since he is the best, he bore himself best in all things. Say therefore to yourself: If my Lord Jesus were present, what do you think he would do about this, or what would he now say? Certainly he would both act and speak in the best way, whom I must imitate in all things according to my strength.

*General conclusion of all spiritual exercises. Consideration twelve.*

Wishing at last to put an end to this work, we say that the fulfillment of all spiritual exercises consists in two things. First, in the knowledge and sense of one’s own imperfection, so that the monk may always be conscious of his weakness and remain rooted in humility. Second, in the desire and effort to come to a higher way of life, so that he may always desire to advance in spirit toward better things, and by no means withdraw from this effort. Toward this effort of advancing every cenobite ought to stretch out his mind at every moment; if he has at any time abandoned it, he incurs the transgression of his vow and the gravest danger to his soul. But let the monk beware lest the consideration of his own weakness cast aside the effort of advancing; let him beware lest that very effort despise the consideration of imperfection; but let these two so adhere to each other mutually that they never depart from one another. But if excessive consideration of one’s own imperfection has tempted the mind toward [unclear] despair of God, let him recall to memory what the holy abbot Bernard said: Let us breathe again, brothers, for even if we are nothing in our own hearts, perhaps something in us may lie hidden in the heart of God. These two considerations, of our weakness and of the divine estimation, stir up great devotion in us; thus the psalmist said: I am poor and sorrowful. Behold the first. Your salvation, O God, has taken me up. Behold the second.

Finally, it is necessary for one who wishes to occupy himself profitably in these spiritual exercises to have a joyful and free heart, and one alien to every anxiety or care of the world. For the Holy Spirit is the spirit of freedom and joy; he loves a heart free and joyful in God, and, so to speak, applauds his like. For just as a bird bound to the earth cannot lift itself up to heaven by flying, so a mind occupied with perishable things cannot raise itself to heavenly things. Nor should one wear out the spirit by excessive frequency of [unclear]; otherwise the brain is confounded, and one labors in vain through dry habit. Let the cenobite, therefore, fervent in spirit, learn always to persist in these things with moderation, and constantly to refer the cause of every exercise by intention to God, who is the beginning and end of all goodness. Let him faithfully direct his effort to fear and love of him, and fix his mind in profound humility, since he is not easily abandoned by God who is rooted in love.

*Here ends the spiritual exercise of cloistered persons, by Petrus Fridbergensis, in the noble city of Mainz, on the eighth Ides of August, in the year of salvation 1498.*
