Let him hear more willingly than he is pleased to speak, and let him not say everything that his mind has suggested, [unclear]. For thus Hugh says to the monk: “Do not pour out everything you know, like a vessel without a lid, which, as soon as it is tilted, pours out whatever it contains within itself, and lies open to all dust and uncleanness. Take diligent care that you not seem, directly or indirectly, to seek your own glory, nor reveal imprudently to anyone, from a spirit of boasting or otherwise, whatever commendable thing lies hidden in you. If you are silent about yourself and keep yourself hidden, you will be more pleasing; if you disclose and commend yourself, you will become cheap in the sight of all and will be laughed at. Beware of idle words: when we do not pay attention to such things, we slip little by little into harmful ones; from them are produced either disturbances or dissipations, or serious erosions of knowledge. Be such in your inner way of life that, through love of spiritual meditation, silence always delights you more than talkativeness.”

*The cenobite should be composed and mature in his manners.*

The monk should take care to order his manners both in labor and outside it, in every place, and in all things, both deeds and words, show himself mature before men. Let him be clean and humble in heart; let him be kind and useful in speech; let him be modest and grave in his gait; let him be mature and exemplary in his whole way of life. The whole man is ordered outwardly according to the norm of discipline if the effort to make progress is held the more firmly in the soul in all things. For indeed, while the monk desires daily to advance to better things, he avoids all dissipation and disorderly conduct. For a firm effort to make progress has this as its own property: that it makes the soul of the one striving very circumspect in all things. But he who does not have a firm effort to make progress for the better cannot truly be called composed, although he may seem to men to be mature. For feigned maturity quickly dissolves into open dissipation of mind where the root of discipline, the fear of God, is not present. Therefore, so that our cenobite may always be composed within and without, let him strive truly to be rooted in the fear of the Lord. For he who fears God neglects nothing. And the love of God is not idle.

*What the monk’s conduct toward other men ought to be. Consideration XI.*

Since we have begun to speak about the maturity and composure that must be observed by the monk before men, it seems useful to us now to say briefly how he ought to present himself to each one. For he is instructed to present himself in one way to his prelate, and in another way he is commanded to show himself to his confreres and to secular persons. To his ruler, in the place of Christ, he ought to show reverence; to his confrere, charity; to those who injure him, meekness; to secular persons, modest piety. Therefore let the monk render to each one what is his, and he will be truly composed before God and men. What he owes to God, and what to himself, has been sufficiently said above; now let us pursue the matters proposed to us.

*His conduct toward the prelate, in the place of Christ, should be reverential.*

First, the conduct of the monk ought to be reverential toward the prelate, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “He who hears you hears me, and he who despises you despises me.” For the monk who withholds the obedience and reverence due to his abbot does injury to almighty God.
