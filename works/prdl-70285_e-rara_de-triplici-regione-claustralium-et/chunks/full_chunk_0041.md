In the third way a good man is made spiritually good: when, through the desire of love, the spirit is united with God [unclear]. These two last things make our cenobite spiritual, and often introduce him to the citizens of the third region. Whoever, therefore, wishes rightly to trust in the testimony of his own conscience should strive humbly to exercise himself in the aforesaid things, because one never prudently presumes upon safety when danger is not avoided. And what is so dangerous as always to live in negligence and sins, and to refuse to amend the errors one has committed? The confidence of the just, arising from the testimony of a good conscience, is always best, and is continually subject to God in humble service. But the impious and sinners, disturbed by their own actions, raise themselves against God, and do not trust in the testimony of conscience, but rather presume vainly.

*There follow the seven roots of divine fear, which are laid fundamentally in the first degree of humility.*

Since this second region chiefly instructs the cenobite through the degrees of humility proceeding from the fear of God in the aforesaid order, it is necessary for him to recognize clearly the roots of that fear, if he wishes to arrive, by ascending, at the love of God. Therefore, so that we may be able to construct this spiritual house to perfection, we must place those roots in order in the foundation. We did not, however, set them out in the first degree of humility, lest by excessive prolixity we should confuse the order of the things to be said. There are, then, seven roots of the fear of God, through which the cenobite is brought to compunction and, by advancing in true humility, is more strongly rooted.

The first root of the fear and love of God is the intent meditation upon the incomprehensible divine majesty, which is understood so much the less, the more curiously it is investigated by man. For the prophet says: Truly thou art a hidden God. This meditation generates wonder at divine perfection; wonder causes reverence; from reverence arises fear of majesty; and from fear worship is produced. But meditation on the highest goodness is divided, according to the present purpose, into seven conditions, which are not unsuitably fitted to the seven days of the week, or even to the seven canonical hours. And although that highest and most perfect goodness abundantly suffices for us as matter for meditation, nevertheless, because it belongs to few to be able to rise to invisible things without visible things, we wish to raise the minds of the weak through things known to things unknown. For the holy Apostle says: The invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are beheld, being understood through the things that have been made; his everlasting power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.

First, therefore, the wondrous power of God’s might is praiseworthy in the principal work of creation: for he who was able to bring forth all things out of nothing created, by his will alone, things so great, so solid, so varied, and so noble. From this consideration arises wonder at divine power, through which man is stirred to the fear of God.

Second, the provident wisdom of God is praiseworthy, because, knowing all things, he orders them, and governs by his providence all things that he has created. He knows all things, understands all things; by his counsel he disposes all things; by his command he encloses all things. For, as a certain lover of worldly wisdom said, God is a sphere whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. He is within all things and is not enclosed; he is outside all things and is not shut out; he is beneath all things and is not oppressed; he is above all things and is not borne up. He includes all things, for although he is wholly everywhere, he is confined in no place; great and immense, he is bounded by no quantity. He himself orders all things as he wills; stable in himself and always immovable, he changes the vicissitudes of things; he mercifully prepares eternal rewards for the good without passion of mind; angered without disturbance, he condemns the unjust. Great without magnitude, immense without quantity, he has set a limit to things; governing the world, he disposes all things.
