For it is impossible for one wounded by the enemy to recover health if he neglects to open the wound itself to the physician. Therefore you, faithful cenobite, who desire to save your soul and to be a good soldier of Jesus Christ, do not omit humble confession, because you will never be able to conquer the devil better than if you take care faithfully to disclose to your abbot all the thoughts he suggests to you and your sins. It is certain to me, and proved by the destruction of many, that the cenobite who refuses to keep this degree commits himself to being overcome by many temptations of the enemy.

*The sixth degree of humility arises from true confession:* that the monk should consider himself vile for all things and unworthy. For according to the Rule we believe him to be a true monk whom we see, from humble patience, to be content with every vileness and extremity in food and clothing. But that vileness is placed in customary inconveniences. For the former willingly embraces patience in cheaper garments, foods, and whatever necessities of the body; the latter delights in serving the sick, lepers, and the cast off, in being confounded, degraded, and despised in all things. Therefore the monk who is truly dead to the flesh and the world should be content with every vileness and extremity; he should show himself a faithful worker in all things, and yet, because of the corruption of nature by which he offends in many things, he should believe himself vile and unworthy. For truly whatever good is done in this world, however holily and religiously one lives in this age, it can suffice no one for meriting eternal life unless God’s great mercy deigns to come to his aid. We receive the greatest things for the least; we expect the kingdom of God not as owed to us from our own merit, but from the grace of the promises. For the holy Apostle says: The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that will be revealed in us. For if we receive the justice of God without mercy, we are worthy of hell. We all incur wrath by sinning, but we obtain love from grace. No one is saved except through grace; no one is damned except through justice. Meanwhile, however, in order that we may obtain the grace of God, the zeal of our good will is required; without that grace nothing in us works toward merit. For although no one does good by his own holy powers, nevertheless no one does a good thing well unwillingly. For the grace of God and freedom of choice stand in such relation to one another that one without the other cannot suffice for saving a human being, but both must necessarily come together for the operation of virtue. Since therefore without the grace of God we can do nothing good, we ought to esteem ourselves vile and unworthy for all things, so that, while we submit ourselves to God in humility, we may always obtain greater things. And therefore this sixth degree is the merit of confession and the single foundation of all the preceding degrees. For as long as the cenobite remains in this degree of humility, he will stand firm in the former ones.

*The seventh degree of humility* is not only to believe oneself from the inmost affection of the heart to be viler than all, but also to desire with a simple mind that others believe and say this about him. This condition of holy humility is placed on high, and in this time is loved by few monks. For the first condition of this profound humility seems to consist in this: that the monk ought from the heart to esteem himself viler than all. The second is that he should rejoice to be esteemed vile and abject by all, and should desire this from the inmost affection of the heart without pretense. The third condition is placed in a higher dwelling: namely, that from the same affection the monk should delight in being treated most vilely and trampled upon by all. Rooted in this humility, the cenobite loves reproach and insult; he does not fear to be despised and most vilely cast aside, because, submitting his whole self in the love of God, he humbly places himself, and hastens toward insults as toward feasts with the greatest cheerfulness.
