The fruit of obedience, however, is everlasting, and is bounded by no end whatsoever. Almighty God obeys our prayers only so far as we obey our superiors. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, about persistence in prayer, for he who despises Christ’s vicar in the monastery: Christ does not hear him when he prays. Humility raises up prayer; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, the finder of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, closes the gates of hell, becomes a companion-dweller with the angels, and is regarded as the food of all the saints. But the monk who casts off subjection to obedience runs toward death.

*The fourth degree of humility is patience in adversities,* concerning which our holy father Benedict speaks in the Rule: if a monk, under obedience, embraces patience with a silent conscience amid hard and adverse things, or even amid any injuries inflicted on him. This degree too takes its beginning from the third, for no one is truly to be called obedient if he has been found impatient. For, as that divine Chrysostom says, he cannot be acceptable to the king who has been subject to the enemy; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been enslaved to passionate and impatient flesh. For no one will be able to conquer a fierce enemy unless he has first learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, for no virtue is lawfully perfected if patience is lacking.

Indeed, with blessed Gregory bearing witness, it is of greater merit to receive adversities patiently for God than to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: He who believes that tribulation has come upon him for his sins calls forth God’s mercy toward himself more fully for doing good. Therefore let the cenobite learn to set the virtue of patience against all things that happen to him, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. For he who suffers conquers; he who murmurs is overcome.

Moreover, in three things the virtue of patience is especially necessary, and is rewarded with the highest prizes. In insults of words, the patience of a prudent man and lover of God preserves the purpose of humility; in the removal of goods and temporal advantages it receives increase; and in injury to the body, with silent conscience, it strengthens the practice of virtue. Now consider, O faithful soul, how your most loving Savior Jesus endured these three things most patiently for your redemption; and if you believe that you love him, do not be troubled to bear similar things with patience for love of him.

From the three degrees immediately preceding we constitute one end of the cenobites, which, as it seems to us, we reasonably call threefold in degree. For by three degrees, namely the second, third, and fourth, the end of the cenobitic institution is perfected, and the renunciation of one’s own will is consummated. For where patience, the preserver of the virtues, is joined to the renunciation of one’s own will and to obedience, every pleasure and concern of the body is mortified. Whoever has deserved to ascend this threefold degree has fulfilled the Lord’s commandment, in which he says in the Gospel: He who wishes to come after me, let him deny himself through the second degree of humility, and take up his cross through the third, and follow me through the fourth.

*The fifth degree of humility is true confession of sins,* into which the faithful cenobite is said to ascend if he confesses to his abbot, with humble trust, all evil thoughts coming into his heart, or any evils committed in secret. For indeed, if anything is committed amiss through impatience at the adversary’s suggestion in the preceding threefold degree, in this one it is restored through contrition and humble confession. For no one is found so perfect that he does not contract the stain of earthly conduct, as Scripture says: In many things we all offend. For no one is clean from sin, not even an infant whose life is one day upon the earth. Necessarily, therefore, this degree follows the fourth without any intervening step, and in it all the negligences of the preceding degrees are amended. True confession lifts up the soldier who falls in battle, heals him when wounded by the enemy, and comforts him when weak and fainthearted.
