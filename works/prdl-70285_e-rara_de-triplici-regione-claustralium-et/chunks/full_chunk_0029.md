If, therefore, you desire to subject all your affections to the spirit, if you wish to fight successfully against the vices of the flesh, take care to do prudent violence to yourself, and to arm yourself strongly against everything.

Next, a certain holy severity is set before you to be assumed, through which all the contrary and adverse things of this world are borne as almost nothing. For indeed, whoever has once truly assumed this kindly severity of mind for the love of God and with desire for heaven, will thereafter fear no adversities of the flesh so greatly; rather, he is so inwardly flooded with reflection on the divine reward that he seems to take more delight in adversities that occur to him than in prosperities.

But severity is by no means pleasing to the weaker unless it has been tempered somewhat by the sweetness of kindness. Therefore, consequently, this kindness of [soothing?] is to be assumed, so that when, through the weakness of the flesh, someone is tempted in battle, or even wounded, and at times totters, through penance he may soon rise again, to fight more strongly anew. For if the soldier of Christ has fallen in battle, he must, through contrition and the other remedies of penance, rise again as often as necessary, to fight again, until at last, at some time, by God’s mercy, he is able to overcome the adversary. If the just man falls seven times in a day and rises again, how will the sinner, now penitent, not hope for pardon? Rise, therefore, O soldiers, to fight again as often as you have fallen when the adversary prevails; always renew the warfare more strongly from the beginning, so that the enemy whom for the time being you cannot overcome by strength you may at last overcome by perseverance. Do not despair of victory, O faithful cenobite, because you have often been overcome by temptation and have fallen into the snare of vice. Do not lie prostrate any longer because you feel yourself weak, but rise again at once as often as you have fallen, and trust that you have a merciful Lord, by whose help you will at last be victor over the adversary. For no one suddenly becomes supreme, nor is victory over the enemy granted at once to [unclear] soldiers, lest, destined to perish more grievously, they be lifted up in pride.

The possession of a thing acquired with difficulty is always dearer, and a good that is acquired laboriously is guarded with greater diligence. So long does God, the loving Father, permit us, conquered by the temptations of the adversary, to fall at intervals of change into the pit of sin, until, conscious of our weakness, we learn to hope for nothing in our own powers but only in the grace of him alone. But you, man, who often interrupt your purpose by sinning, never delay to rise again; for even if you cannot perfectly subject the enemy to yourself, nevertheless it will be good for you if the last day is able to find you standing and fighting, even after a fall often repeated. But those to whom the mercy of God has granted strength and grace to defeat the adversary, this counsel does not make lax. Nevertheless, let him who stands see that he does not fall.

*On the attentive reformation of the works of the first region.*

*Article IV.*

Since indeed, as we taught at the end of the first treatise, this middle region is nearest to the first on its lowest side, in which, if the cenobite has been even a little negligent and slack, he immediately falls back, without any middle state, into the former one. And therefore whoever desires to persist in this region by making progress should always, with attentive vigilance, strive to reform the works of the lowest region. And, lest we be lengthy, whoever desires to practice this reformation in himself should assume in this region works contrary to the eight articles of the first. For, as is prescribed by the tradition of physicians, contraries are cured by contraries.

The citizens of this middle region, therefore, faithful monks, are commanded first to look to this: that they strive to order themselves toward God above all things.
