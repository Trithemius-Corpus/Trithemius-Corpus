First, because of the delicate liveliness of his flesh, which in its natural complexion was most excellent, and therefore was more grievously afflicted in the endurance of pains. For delicate flesh suffers more than flesh that is coarse by natural condition. Therefore, since Christ's flesh was subtle and delicate, he poured forth bloody sweat in the garden. Second, because of the character of those who killed him, since Christ was not killed by strangers, but by his own blood-relatives and nearest kin, as earlier he complained through the prophet: "My people, what have I done to you?" For one suffers more tolerably at the hands of strangers and enemies than at the hands of one's own people, since at least friends, by reason of nearness of blood, suffer with one. Third, because of the bitterness of the passion, since he is more afflicted in those places in which pain is felt more strongly because of the nerves. For, raised up on the cross, he is fastened through hands and feet; the Lord of all is given gall and [unclear] to drink. For the psalmist says of him: "They have pierced my hands and my feet; they have counted all my bones." Who could conceive in mind the pain of your most holy heart, good Jesus, when the hardest nails began so cruelly to pierce your hands and feet? Would that my soul might always show itself grateful to you; would that the remembrance of your most holy passion might not depart from my memory. I shall easily be able to obtain from you the effect of my prayers if it should happen that I take delight without interruption in the exercise of your passion. Let each person think what he can; I prefer the memory of your passion to all exercises, through which I trust that in prayers I shall receive whatever is fitting for salvation. Fourth, because of its long duration, the passion of Christ was most bitter, since from the hour of his capture until death he was always in most bitter torment. "All day," he says, "I went about sorrowful, and there is no health in my flesh." Fifth, because of the universality of the passion, since in all the things in which Christ could suffer, he suffered in a singular way. For he suffers in reputation, since, falsely accused, he is blasphemed by the impious. He also suffered in possessions, since he is suspended naked and stripped upon the wood, and his garments are divided by lot among the soldiers. He suffers in soul, since, foreseeing the bitterness of the passion, he was sorrowful even unto death. He suffered in his whole body, since from the sole of the foot to the crown of the head there was no health in him. He suffers in his friends, since he is betrayed by a disciple, and, deserted by all, is even publicly denied by Peter. He is afflicted also in compassion with his mother, whose most holy soul, it is remembered, the sword of sorrow pierced. He suffered from the ingratitude of many, since he foresaw that the death which he endured out of compassion for all would be lost in many reprobates. Finally, he suffered in all the senses, as we are taught from the Gospel. He suffers in the eyes, which, with many tears poured forth, are covered with a little cloth. He suffers in hearing, when, amid many insults and outrages, he is mocked and blasphemed. He suffers in smell, since he is defiled with the foul spittle of the Jews. He suffers in touch, since he is tormented in all the members of his body. He suffers in taste, since, being thirsty, he is given gall and vinegar to drink. Sixth, because of the cause of the passion, which was solely the injury done by the Jews, since he suffered wholly without fault. For indeed whoever suffers unjustly is tormented much more in spirit. But the innocent Son of God dies for our sins and is tormented for our crimes, as is read in Isaiah: "Truly he bore our infirmities." Seventh, because of the lack of all consolation, since Christ found no one consoling him in his passion. For he says in the psalm: "You have put far from me friend and neighbor, and my acquaintances because of misery." Indeed, his own stood far off from him.

Fourth, the passion of Christ was fruitful with regard to its effect, which must be considered principally in three things. For first there was the remission of sins; second, the bestowal of grace; third, the presentation of glory. And these three things are indicated by the title affixed to the cross, which, as the evangelist says, contained the cause of the passion of Jesus: "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews." For "Jesus," saving sinners, pertains to the first; "of Nazareth," conferring the flower of grace, to the second; "King of the Jews," presenting the heavenly kingdom to those who confess him, to the third.
