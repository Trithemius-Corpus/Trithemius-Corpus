Therefore the concupiscence of the flesh draws back the mind of the disordered cenobite, so that he cannot despise the world.

Secondly, they are disordered with respect to despising the world because of the vain concupiscence of the eyes. For the monk who forgets the true honor of faithful souls delights utterly in worldly pomps and overly curious ornaments. Indeed, a person’s outward attire reveals what each one is inwardly, hidden within himself. To wear rings on the fingers, corals on neck-bands, scent-vessels in the hands, and pleats in cowls shows not a monk but a soldier; it presents not a humble cenobite but a proud secular man. This adornment in monks cannot please anyone except the reprobate; indeed, it is often found to have displeased even the proud. Woe to you, disordered cloisterers, who through the double concupiscence of the eyes descend to hell, while you desire to see in others what is not expedient, and present yourselves to be seen by others otherwise than you ought.

What have the jewels of the world to do with you? What have the curiosities of the world? All these beauties, as Saint Bernard bears witness, make a masculine soul effeminate; these public refinements quickly weaken a manly purpose. Such men are like ragged boys who, because of one thin red band fastened to a torn sleeve, think themselves very handsome and comely, and insolently mock others set before them, though very well dressed, as if they were naked. These pompous cloisterers see others walking in humble garb, despising the world in the spirit of poverty, and for that reason pleasing both God and men; but they see themselves despised by the wise and by princes, and sometimes not even admitted to conversation. Seeing these things, the proud, even if they grieve bitterly as though over an injury, nevertheless utterly refuse to lay aside their pride.

Thirdly, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered with respect to contempt of the world because of pride of life, since they are infected not only with vain complacencies of the flesh, but also are proud through swelling of mind. For all who follow the desires of the flesh become [unclear] in mind; they do not know how to endure equals; they do not accept superiors. They rejoice that a great retinue of servants attends them; they strive to sit in council; they always wish to appear glorious to men; they walk pompously in public. Swollen with the spirit of pride, they boast that they possess monasteries notable from their foundation, rich in temporal goods, in jewels, in buildings, and in elaborate privileges. With uplifted heart they despise poor monks or abbots of exiled monasteries; they do not blush to prefer themselves to better men. The more they desire to hunt for glory in outward things, the farther they withdraw from true glory with the foolish virgins.

*How the citizens of this lowest region are disordered with respect to penance.*

*Article VIII.*

Since man, born of woman, living a short time and filled with many miseries, cannot remain long in the same condition, it is necessary that with all zeal he turn himself, by daily reforming himself, toward his proper end, since for as long as anyone is permitted to live in this world, he is held to be changeable in either direction. Therefore whoever, placed in this mutability, desires to come to the joys of immutability should humbly acknowledge his own frailty, should always after the fall of sin flee to the remedies of penance, and should fear to lie in crime even for a moment. For the life of a monk must always consist in saving penance, and he must continually await the coming of the Judge with holy fear. For blessed Jerome says: The monk has the office not of one teaching but of one lamenting, who should mourn either himself or the world, and should fearfully await the coming of the Lord. But the disordered cloisterers of the lowest region despise this saving remedy; they consume the acceptable time in vanity; and, since they do not fear to be badly ordered in all things, they show themselves very disordered even with respect to doing penance.

First, indeed, they are badly ordered with respect to penance through carelessness of mind, while they neglect to understand how greatly sin displeases almighty God, whom they do not fear to contradict by the decision of a depraved will. Just as black night is opposed to day, so the sinner, turned away from divine goodness, is considered hostile and adversarial.
