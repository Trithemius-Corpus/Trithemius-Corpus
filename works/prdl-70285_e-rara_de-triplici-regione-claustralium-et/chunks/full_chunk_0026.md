God, says John, is charity; and he who abides in charity abides in God, and God in him. But everyone who abides in God does not sin. The second end, disposing one toward the first and principal end as though through some suitable means, is called secondary or less principal. The monk must always attend to this end, and through it order his exercises toward the ultimate end. For no one can attain the principal end of charity who neglects to observe the means.

This disposing end toward the ultimate, so far as it pertains to cenobites, according to John Cassian in the Conferences of the Fathers, is to cast away from oneself entirely all cares of the world, to mortify one's own wills, to entrust every disposition and care of oneself to the superior, and to think nothing of tomorrow. For the cenobite who wishes to advance in this middle region must take up the complete denial of himself, and then resign himself wholly into the hands of his superior, so that within, in himself, he may be clean from every dust of self-will, and outwardly, for serving the Lord, free from every secular occupation. Indeed, unless our cenobite has come to this end, he is in no way a true monk, because he still sows corruption in corruptible flesh and by no means observes the virtue of his profession.

This monastic denial of self-will seems chiefly to consist in three things, without which no one is truly to be said to have denied himself. First, the mortification of self-will requires in the monk the eradication of his own judgment, because whoever relies on his own judgment or counsel disdains humbly to be subject to another. For the root from which the defense of self-will arises is called adherence to one's own counsel or judgment. Whoever, established in a monastery, desires to cling to his own judgment, sins gravely against the rule as a usurper of self-will. For it befits the cenobite to live by another's command rather than his own, if toward eternal life through the merit of obedience [unclear]. According to Bernard, [self-will?] is the greatest leprosy of the soul; the more hidden it is, the more pernicious it will be; and the more anyone abounds in it, the sounder he seems to himself to be.

Second, mortification of the will requires the emptying-out of one's own affections, because no one is able to stand rightly ordered amid adversities and advantages, and truly subject to God, who does not strongly restrain his carnal affections. Third, for the mortification of self-will that poverty of spirit is required, lest in things of use, in food, clothing, and the rest, the monk's affections be taken captive; because whoever loves these things too much shows himself, against the rule, to be a lover of self-will. For the true cenobite, a cultivator of this middle region, must fervently devote himself to the doctrine of humility, willingly despise all worldly things for the love of God, and love absolutely nothing except God. If he has anything for use, with the rule permitting it, let him beware of attaching the affection of his heart to it, and let him be ready, at the judgment of the pastor and for the benefit of the brothers, to resign all things with a peaceful heart. Let him always choose the things that are simple and meaner; let him give thanks to God in all things, and, placing his hope and sufficiency in him alone, say cheerfully: In you, Lord Jesus, possessing all things, for your love I willingly despise all things; I shall care nothing at all about transitory things.

Therefore let our devout cenobite always direct the eye of his intention, with faithful consideration, toward this end of the monastic institution; let him never withdraw from the sight of it, because whoever in action forgets the ultimate end wanders like an inexperienced and blind sailor amid the storms of the sea, soon to perish. Let the faithful monk therefore imitate [a sailor?] skilled in [navigation?], so that, just as he, always keeping the harbor before his eyes, strives with attentive solicitude to direct the ship there, so the mind of a good man may always be directed toward God as toward the shore and ultimate end. Archers set up some definite mark for themselves, and, lest their sight be scattered from the fixed target, they close one eye. And just as the arrow of the shooter is directed toward the mark, so the intention of the good monk is always ordered toward God; in it also the eye of lower affection is closed, lest the sharpness of higher consideration be scattered through [unclear].
