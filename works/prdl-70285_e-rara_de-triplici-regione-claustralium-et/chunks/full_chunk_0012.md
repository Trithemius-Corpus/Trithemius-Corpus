Whoever, however, strives to evade the unity of the chapter imitates the devil. Thus the emperor of the Bulgarians orders the Christians subject to him to hold an assembly in church, under [unclear], though he grants them freedom to believe whatever they wish. For that crafty man knew that the sincerity of the Christian faith could not long stand, if the office of public exhortation should happen to be lacking. This adversary of the Christian faith the disorderly and carnal abbot strives as far as he can to imitate, when he resists the founding of so necessary a chapter. For the authority of the chapter alone is what maintains the observance of regular discipline in the order; and if it is despised, no integrity of monastic life will remain.

But the inhabitant of the lowest region, the disorderly abbot, devises countless pretexts against regular observance: he mentions the conditions of his monastery as contrary; he complains that his ancient privileges are [unclear]; he is indignant that his subject monks are [unclear]. But woe to disorderly men, through whom it shall happen that even one monastery is lost from this most holy congregation.

Next, the plague of avarice makes an abbot not only disorderly toward his subjects, but also opposed to himself and wholly turned away from God. For cupidity is the root of all evils; it takes away the servant of Christ and makes him a minister of the devil. Therefore the abbot who has submitted his mind to this vice shows himself to be not a shepherd but a hireling. He always gapes after earthly gains; he cares nothing for the progress of souls. A book of sacred reading is rarely or never seen in his hands; registers of revenues are more often turned over. He joins field to field; always burying gold and silver in his chest, he heaps it up; and while he too much desires his monastery to abound in temporal goods, he prepares prey for robbers. Often the public fisc of the lord [unclear] swallows and devours the monastery’s money, which the excessive greed of the monks of the monastery brings in.

Woe to you, greedy shepherd of cloistered men, who bear greater solicitude for brute animals than for the souls entrusted to you. A horse falls ill and at once you prepare a remedy; a soul perishes and you think no danger threatens. An ox and an ass are sick and you have compassion; a brother is swallowed up by sadness and you do not come to his aid. You, therefore, hireling and not shepherd, who with a stubborn heart despise the infirmities of your brothers, who do not succor those placed in bitterness, do not presume that you can escape the hand of the Lord, whose sheep you allow to perish so wickedly.

Third, a ruler is disorderly toward his subjects when those whom he ought to have guarded carefully within the enclosure of the monastery he places outside, not without danger. For indeed, when a weak monk is exposed to the governance of nuns or to secular churches, the integrity of regular observance is violated, because one who, still remaining in the lowest region, has not learned to subject the flesh to the spirit neither knows how to preside over others without danger to himself. But the disorderly shepherd who, for the sake of the advantage of temporal property, does not fear to expose his monks to so great a danger is guilty of all the souls that perish on such an occasion, and before the inward Judge he is bound to restitution for all. It does not belong to the profession of a monk to rule secular churches; it is not expedient for a weak monk to preside over nuns.

But what then? Ought a monk to set himself against the command of his shepherd if he wishes him to reside with nuns or in the churches of seculars? For the Rule commands that nothing is to be preferred to obedience. I truly do not know what I should answer to this question, so that I may not incur the danger that is seen to threaten on both sides. For if I speak the truth, I shall seem to infringe the obedience of subjection; if I consent to exposing monks to danger, I confess that I sin. Yet I shall say in a few words what I think, although I am not ignorant that truth will bring forth hatred. Just as a monk, by the necessity of his profession, is bound simply to obey his shepherd in all things that are according to the monastic rule, so a prelate cannot, without the monk’s own will, compel a monk to those things that are above the rule, especially when danger to salvation intervenes. But to rule churches, to preside over women, to reside outside the monastery: this is not to have obeyed according to the Rule.
