And then the devout cenobite, as his spirit lifts itself up, will cry out: “My soul refused to be comforted.” And that saying of the Apostle: “I counted all things as dung.” For the soul that truly loves God despises everything except him.

From these two powers every disordered affection arises; and good affections likewise are generated from these by opposition. For joy and hope take their beginning from the concupiscible power, and the irascible power brings forth grief and fear. Concerning these Saint Bernard says thus: “The whole heart of man is perverted by four affections; if we bring these back to God, the whole heart of man is converted.”

*On the Faithful Cleansing of the Powers.*

But we are by no means able to restrain the sensual powers unless we also faithfully cleanse the intellectual powers. For this cleansing great and continual diligence is needed, since the cenobite who is slackened by the torpor of negligence is never cleansed from vicious affections. For he brings back corruption from his errors. Therefore the cenobite, the cultivator of this middle region, must correct his inner powers from every disorder, if he wishes to remain constantly in the pursuit of making progress. These intellectual powers, then, are to be cleansed in this way.

First, indeed, the intellect must be purged of every imagining of vain likenesses, so that, freeing itself from all material things, it may raise itself upward, without any imagining of likeness, to its own principle, and, prudently intent upon its object, may turn itself wholly toward the [ray?] of supreme truth.

Then our faithful cenobite must not neglect to cleanse his memory from every [unclear] and illicit occupation; he must admit no [unclear] base or less useful images for [unclear]; and he must study to repel phantasms of irrational passions and [unclear] meditations, and, so far as can be done, to think nothing foolish.

The will also must be purged from every disordered affection, so that it may will nothing except what right reason [unclear] to be willed, and reject nothing except what reason, according to the judgment of truth, forbids it.

This restriction of the powers and cleansing of the powers is thus spoken of: it grants absolutely nothing to anyone [unclear], except to one who is strong in the struggle by great, discreet, and continual diligence, knowing that the kingdom of God suffers violence, and that no one can subdue the enemy to himself in any way, unless he has resolved to fight against him fiercely and perseveringly. For, as the blessed pontiff Augustine bears witness, we know that a man does not die well unless he has first died to vices and concupiscences. But no one among men truly dies to these except the one who resists his own wills through continual labor, sowing in tears. The mind, therefore, must be armed with saving teachings; strength of patience must be taken up in all things. For just as someone unarmed and impatient of labor cannot throw down an armed enemy, so a monk without spiritual arms and a certain continual force of fighting cannot cleanse his intellectual powers. Although the arms of spiritual warfare are very many in their own usefulness, nevertheless three in particular must be taken up by the monk.
