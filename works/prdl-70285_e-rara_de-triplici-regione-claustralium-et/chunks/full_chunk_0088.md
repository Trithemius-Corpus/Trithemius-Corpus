Whoever, from sincerity of heart and without falsity in mouth or mind, shall have uttered these three truths to the Lord, however much he may previously have sinned, is now secure, if he should die in such a state even without confession, in the absence of a priest; yet in such a way that in purgatory he makes satisfaction for what he did not complete by doing penance. But he who, because of an opposing will to sin in which he actually takes delight, or because of a wicked purpose by which he scorns to avoid occasions of sins, cannot truthfully say these same truths from a pure mind, is in a state of damnation; him not even the pope can absolve.

*The Appointment of Future Caution.*

The second thing which is prescribed for the monk to observe in his recollection is called the appointment of future caution. Let him consider what have been for him the occasions of past transgression, and he will more easily guard himself from future danger. For never is the pit of sin better stopped up than when the occasion for sin is avoided. And Saint Bernard says: For the night as it comes on, an appointment must be made, distributing one’s exercises according to the rule of the common institution. In these every debt is duly paid, if the spirit is subjected to God and the flesh to the spirit; and if anything has been omitted, neglected, or imperfect, let it not remain unrecompensed in due manner, time, and place.

*The Request for Suffrages.*

The third thing in the recollection of a good monk is the devout request for suffrages: since he well understands his own weakness, he asks help from those stronger than himself. Therefore, as soon as he has completed his recollection, he turns to the holy and undivided Trinity to ask for mercy; then he invokes the most blessed mother of God; afterward he implores the prayers and aid of all the saints. And here he remembers not only himself, but pours out prayers for the whole order and the Church.

*The Blessing for Resting.*

The fourth thing in the recollection of the monk is the blessing for resting, by which he fortifies himself with prayers and entrusts himself to the divine goodness to be guarded through the night. Therefore, at the sound of the signal for sleeping, or at the appointed time, going to the place of rest, let him fortify himself with the sign of the holy cross, and say with devotion:

*The Blessing of Rest.*

May the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, the merit and integrity of blessed Mary ever virgin, the sign of the holy cross, the power of the Lord’s passion, and the blessing, guardianship, and merit of all the saints, angels, and elect of God be between me and all my enemies, visible and invisible, here and forever. Amen.

*Prayer.*

I commend myself this night and at all times into your wounds, most loving Lord Jesus; and I beseech you, through the merit of your most holy passion, have mercy on me and guard me from all snares and illusions of the enemy, so that with a pure mind and clean body I may be able to rise at the hours appointed for me and praise you with fervor of spirit.

*Discretion for Having Rest.*

The fifth and last thing to be commemorated in recollection is discretion for having rest: just as the monk should never wholly omit this evening recollection, so too he should not wish to prolong it too indiscreetly to the destruction of his head. Therefore, when all things have been completed with discretion, let him raise the desire of his fervor toward God with tears, if he can; let him not confuse his mind. But as he climbs into bed, let him carry with him in memory something of the Lord’s passion, or of the love of Jesus, or certainly of the glory of the heavenly kingdom, in which, as he sleeps, he may rest sweetly. For Bernard says: When you are going to sleep, take something with you in memory or thought, in which you may fall asleep peacefully, which sometimes may also help you to dream, which may also receive you when you wake, and restore you to the state of yesterday’s intention. Thus the night will shine like day for you, and night will be your illumination in its delights, so that you may fall asleep peacefully, rest in peace, and easily wake; and when you rise, you will be ready for redemption in that from which you had not wholly departed. Nevertheless, discretion must always be applied in all these things, lest either excessive meditation, hindering sleep, destroy the brain, or immoderate sleep, taken beforehand, cause meditation to be wholly interrupted.
