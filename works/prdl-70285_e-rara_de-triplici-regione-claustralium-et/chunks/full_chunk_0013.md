Yet truly, I beg you, do not expose your monks to chastity’s peril; do not violently compel anyone to the government of nuns and parish churches, since you can find many who ambitiously seek these offices. Obedience is indeed good, but it is by no means to be preferred to truth and the salvation of the soul. For when the danger of salvation is at issue, it is better humbly to set obedience aside by making excuse than to suffer shipwreck of chastity. Woe to the prelates of monks who incorporate churches of secular clergy into their monasteries, who loosen the bosom of desire with the torches of avarice, because, while they think they are benefiting posterity, they damn many by the occasion.

Fourth, the ruler of monks is made disorderly toward his subjects if he is found turbulent and anxious. In a pastor of souls, pious and prudent discretion is very necessary, lest either zeal for discipline be excessive or the measure of compassion be relaxed by too much laxity. But a disorderly ruler sometimes, through indiscreet fury, thinks himself the tyrant of monks; he does not know himself as father and companion of the humble. What in government is more savage, what more wicked in power, where neither God, nor reason, nor understanding, but a beast, holds rule? Truly, where God and understanding govern authority, the judgments of those presiding do not depart from the rectitude of the law of nature. But a disorderly and anxious ruler is infected by various passions of beasts; he always suspects evil of the monks, and while he wrongly directs the manner of rule, he is always anxious and fears, with Herod, to lose his kingdom.

Fifth, the pastor of cloistered men is called disorderly toward the monks if, in distributing the goods of the monastery, he is regarded as either excessive or obstinate. He is to be called excessive if, in distributing, he has been prodigal, or too diffuse in indiscreet largesse. But he is called obstinate if he has not given at the proper time the things that ought to be given, or if he grudgingly expends necessary things on his subjects. He is also to be called obstinate who casts aside the fear of the Lord, who trusts too much in his own judgment, who admits no counsels of the wise in any matters to be done. And indeed among pastors of souls there are very many disorderly through this sickness of mind, who either distribute the goods of the monastery too improvidently or preserve them with too much tenacity. They prepare most lavish banquets for their flatterers; but to others, toward whom they are averse, they refuse even the least things. In the house of guests or in their abbey, pastors, depraved by disorderly conduct and even to the point of gluttony, sometimes eat; they always drink of the best wine; but to poor monks, who by watching and singing bear the burden of the day and the heat, they scarcely grant scant necessities.

Sixth, the prelate of monks is shown to be very disorderly if, bold against God, by making little of his judgments, he is lifted up into pride. For truly, since a pastor cannot be corrected by his subjects, he is referred by the holy father Benedict to the judgment of God, as is contained in many places of the Rule. “Let the abbot know,” he says, “that concerning all his judgments he must render account to God.” And again: “But the ordained abbot should always consider what a burden he has undertaken, and to whom he is going to render account of his stewardship.” And again he says: “As soon as vices begin to arise, let him cut them off with charity, not with tyranny.” And in another place he warns, saying: “Yet the abbot himself should do all things with the fear of God and observance of the holy Rule, knowing beyond doubt that concerning all his judgments he will render account to God, the most just judge.” But the disorderly ruler of monks, like an asp with stopped ears, scorns to hear this warning of the most holy lawgiver; he despises the patience of God’s long-suffering; he thinks nothing of the future examination, until at last the angry judge says to his face: “You did these things, and I kept silence. Now, therefore, render account of your stewardship; resign to me the sheep of my inheritance, and without delay restore to me the souls once entrusted to you in the body; replace their number whole.
