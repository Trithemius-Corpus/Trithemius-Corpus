For, as we have already said above, that highest good, God, is one; our mind cannot approach it unless, having stripped off every multiplicity that pertains to reason, imagination, and intelligence, it strives to present itself wholly united to itself. For the mind itself naturally desires to attain the highest good; but the more it is divided in its operation through multiplicity, the less fit it is found for uniting itself to God. For multiplicity is opposed to unity, and the more the mind has directed itself toward many things, the more it withdraws from unity. One, two, three, four: they are ten. You withdraw from the one, because you are multiplied in number. Return to the one, if you desire to have the principle. For the one is the principle of all things; if you add anything to it in your mind, you at once withdraw from it. When you say “one,” you say “principle.” If you say “two,” you have already abandoned the principle. Therefore, so that you may always be able to contemplate your principle, take care at all times, having stripped off multiplicity, to render yourself one. For since all good things are from the first good, and every love borne toward any good whatsoever is naturally kindled from that same good, our love cannot rest in any goods as in its final end, unless, naked and pure, it strives finally to return to its own principle. For union is made by the power of that one alone, since we say that things more alike are more easily united to one another for no other reason than that we see them to be one in quality. Therefore the mind is believed at last to be able to be united with the one supreme principle of things when, stripped of every multiplicity and united to itself, it is lifted up to God by most fervent love. Just as no union occurs between things diverse in quality, so there can be no adhesion between God and a mind divided in itself. For the union of one thing with one thing occurs by likeness of quality, and there will be adhesion with God for the mind simplified and stripped of all things through a loving affection. Thus the highest region of the spirit is founded upon simplicity; in it the mind of the purified man is raised up to God by the most fervent desire of love, and through grace is united to its own unchangeable principle. Intellect and affection are the two eyes of the soul. The first of these, intellect, is indeed raised up to know God, but it is not sufficient to come to the mountain of divine darkness, since it advances burdened with phantasms of sensible things. Therefore affection, the simpler eye of the soul, enters the dark mountain without any images whatever; it contemplates God through love, and, separated from all creatures, thinks nothing sensible; ascending in mystical silence, it rests naked. This is the simple eye by which the whole body is made luminous; this is the eye of which the bridegroom boasts in the Canticles: “You have wounded my heart with one of your eyes.” For affection alone, through the fervor of love, enters the secret place of divine contemplation without the guidance of the intellect; and therefore the bridegroom is wounded in one eye, and not in both eyes.

But whoever, through the exercise of devotion and purity, has deserved, by Christ’s granting, to arrive at this region of union and simplification, should learn at all times to remain prudently in profound humility, lest through pride he lose the gift that he rejoices to possess. For Bernard, that profound and most cautious contemplator of divine things, says: “One who strives toward higher things must deepen humility and think humbly of himself, lest, while he is vainly lifted up above himself, he fall from the height to which he seemed to have ascended.” Therefore one standing in this region should prudently return at times to the steps of humility described in the second [section]; the mind should learn to temper itself to remain in profound resignation of self, because as soon as the mind has despised self-denial, it rushes down from the highest mountain into an immense precipice.

For many, trusting in the jubilation of their sensual devotion, have been deceived: they ascended this highest mountain without the foundation of humility. Others, with heads injured and senses destroyed, became foolish, because, not yet mortified in their passions, they presumed to enter the darkness of mystical silence.
