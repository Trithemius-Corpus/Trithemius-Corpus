But when the seniors, citizens of this lowest region, withdraw paternal affection from the younger men, the lesser ones are inflamed with grave hatred; and when the young consider that they are unjustly despised by them, they daily accuse their corrupt morals. Therefore weak young men learn the seniors' morals, which are not upright; and, as it is said in the proverb, just as they see the elders sing, so too the younger ones dance.

Thirdly, monks are badly ordered toward one another when they cast aside good zeal and mutual obedience, and assume the evil kind of zeal that is forbidden by the Rule. By words and deeds they commit various wrongs against one another; each, in his eagerness to rival the other, strives to watch him for evil; one is provoked by another to inflict insult. They mutually reproach one another with their infirmities; they always pant for revenge. Zeal for regular discipline is despised; the passion of indignation and bitterness rules. They meet one another with averted faces, and by open signs they reveal how much hatred lies hidden in the breast. Not so the wicked, not so; for whoever persecutes his brother with hatred is proved a murderer and unworthy of the kingdom of God. O ever-wretched condition of carnal monks, which is as near to hell as it is estranged from the fear of God. For they imitate not angels but demons, all those who, through evil zeal, delight to be discordant in the school of peace. Indeed, just as a brute animal does not abandon the savagery of its nature, so a beastly monk does not withdraw from the worst zeal.

*In What Manner the Monks, Citizens of the Lowest Region, Are Ordered Toward Themselves. Article V.*

The carnal monks, citizens of the lowest region, are disordered in nothing more than toward themselves. For they cast aside every consideration of themselves; they do not attend to the end for which they were created; for while each one pollutes himself with the filth of vices, his wicked mind departs from the original rectitude of his condition. Nor is it any wonder if the cultivator of the lowest region becomes outwardly disordered toward others, when through various passions of the flesh he is inwardly confounded and dissipated from himself. For Scripture says: He who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good? The right order of the human condition established that the flesh should be subject to the spirit; the spirit is commanded to be subject to him from whom it received the form of being. But among carnal minds the order of reason is perverted, when the flesh rules over the spirit, and reason, which according to the power of understanding implanted in it ought to have governed all things, being suffocated by the pleasures of the flesh, does not hasten under command to the rectitude of judgment.

Therefore carnal monks are disordered toward themselves through the vice of curiosity, because by inquiring into another's life they judge curiously, and do not amend their own life, defiled by every vice. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, yet they proudly rebuke others who offend in very small matters. They carefully consider the speck in their brother's eye, and excuse the beam clinging to their own eyes as if it were nothing. They subject all their affections to sensuality, and, after despising the judgment of right reason, they live carnally like beasts; passion frequently stirs them to anger; the frenzy of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With an anxious mind they always demand vengeance against their brothers; often, blinded by excessive fury, they obtain it not without harm to themselves. But disordered citizens cannot long lie hidden in secret; for the more they remain unknown to themselves through the elation of the mind, the more, through blind judgment, they become manifest to others. For every proud and disordered man is said first to be ignorant of himself, because if he truly knew himself, he would by no means be proud. But since the disordering of oneself takes its origin from pride, we cannot describe this plague better than if we take care to lay open its roots, descending by degrees to the depths below. For just as the summit of the spiritual life is contained in the twelve degrees of humility, so the source of all monastic disorder is contained in the twice-six degrees of pride.

Thus the first degree of pride is called curiosity. By it monks badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and morals, and continually inquire into either the deeds or the words of others. At all times they accuse the negligences of the brothers; they rebuke the life of the pastor; and the more curiously they desire to know outwardly what belongs to others, the farther they withdraw from themselves.
