For if a blind man offers guidance to a blind man, he must fall into the pit together with him. But the pastor who does not show himself ordered toward his subjects according to the Rule is to be called not a pastor but a hireling, and he will not remain forever in the house of the Lord. There are, moreover, many disorders of carnal prelates, which do the more harm the more rarely they are corrected.

And first, indeed, a ruler is disordered toward his subjects if he is a hireling: if, neglecting the sheep, he seeks his own advantage of flesh or soul; if, so that he may be able to achieve his own will, he refuses nothing to those who ask things contrary to the Rule. For indeed one who is a hireling does not truly love the sheep: he seeks his own glory; he desires to be over others; he does not care to benefit them according to the Rule; he rejoices to be called abbot; he does not know how to show himself a pastor. Therefore he receives the vain reward of his vanity, who seeks not God's glory but his own; who would sooner allow the souls of his subjects to perish than resign the abbacy to a better man. He is a hireling, and the sheep do not belong to him. He is not a true monk, he is not a good pastor, he is not a sincere lover of God, who, while presiding over monks, is unwilling to yield if by the counsel of those who fear God it has seemed expedient. The care of governing souls rejects a proud man; it requires one humble and resigned to God in all things. Therefore, if you wish to be found a good pastor and a true monk, remember that your intention must at all times be formed in such a way that you are prepared, out of love of God, humbly to feed according to your powers the souls committed to you; and that you are prepared also to resign the pastoral office, at the judgment of God and of good men, from integrity of heart. If you always preserve this intention abiding in your mind, you will be a good pastor both to yourself and to others. But if in the pastoral care you love your own honor; if you prefer your advantage to the benefit of your subjects, you still dwell with the beasts in the lowest region, and you make manifest that you are of a disordered heart.

Secondly, a pastor of souls becomes disordered toward his subjects if his mind is occupied too greedily with temporal affairs; if he bears greater anxiety for secular business than for the souls committed to him. This excessive care for temporal things the holy father Benedict forbids in the Rule, and he rightly judges rational souls to be preferred to all the things of the world. "Before all things," he says, "let the abbot not, by dissembling or slighting the salvation of the souls committed to him, bear more anxiety for transitory, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always consider that he has undertaken to rule souls, for which he is to render an account." Thereupon the loving father gives assurance concerning bodily necessities, if one who loves God worships him with a pure mind. "And lest perhaps," he says, "he complain of smaller resources, let him remember what is written: First seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things will be added to you. And again: Nothing is lacking to those who fear God."

But the disordered pastor of monks is seduced by greed; he is blinded in mind as though without feeling; having cast away from himself the fear of the Lord, sold under sin with Ahab, he runs to the aid of wickedness; he [sets aside?] confidence in the promises made through the holy father about obtaining necessities; he is occupied with external things more than befits a monk. Driven thereafter by the goads of greed, he despises the commandments of God and of holy mother Church; he does not keep feast days; he is wholly immersed in earthly affairs; he meditates on nothing other than temporal things. Like Saul, the fool transgresses the commandment of God; in his folly he thinks that he is expiated by sacrifices against obedience. Finally, grown fat and enlarged either by secular affairs or by secular cares, he very often falls into every wantonness and dissolution; he detests the observance of regular discipline; and while he fears being restrained from evil pursuits by the authority of the chapter, he seeks the dissolution of the holy union. Then, having become reprobate with Saul, he invokes the counsel of the pythoness; he declares obedience to reform damnable for his monastery; he desires to be absolved as quickly as possible from submission to the holy confederation; to this end he relates many things falsely and mendaciously.
