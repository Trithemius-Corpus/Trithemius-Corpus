And just as the eagle is said to look upon the sun with eyes unstruck by its glare, so the monk, God’s servant, contemplates without interruption the end of the monastic institution. Just as a prudent traveler, as he proceeds with pleasure, considers the lodging toward which he is heading, so a good coenobite, in everything he does, always pants chiefly with fervent desire toward his own end, which is God. But a foolish traveler is one who, when placed on the road, forgets the lodging toward which he is heading, and, straying, knowingly becomes entangled in the various impediments of others. And the monk who does not studiously consider the end of his destination assuredly wanders blind on the road.

Therefore the good monk and lover of his own salvation does not neglect his good endeavor in spiritual exercises, but always raises his affection toward higher things by exercising himself in holy works. A good endeavor is to omit nothing of those things which God has commanded; humbly to obey the shepherd in all things for love of God; always to lift up an affection estranged from earthly things to the memory of heavenly things; and, through continual exercise of spiritual conduct, daily to renew the holy purpose. Whatever good the devout coenobite does, let him always offer it to almighty God with faithful attention; let him despise all earthly things with a strong spirit; let him desire to please God alone.

Let the intention of the coenobitic life always be faithful, so that it wishes to desire nothing other than God’s praise and honor; let its attention be diligent, so that it knows how to summon all the senses of the body and the affections of the soul to God’s service, in order that it may serve its Lord faithfully and seek his glory, not its own.

*On the Due Restraint of the Sensual Powers. Article 2.*

How useful and how necessary it is for us to restrain the powers of the sensual passions, I think no one is ignorant, especially among monks. Indeed, whoever in this region desires to reach the due end of regular life which we have described must tame his disordered natural habits and movements with prudent constancy, because no one can truly resign himself to another’s command who does not strongly restrain the powers of the sensual passions. For one who desires to make progress must leave behind both the movements and the prudence of the flesh with a steadfast spirit, and show himself a son of divine grace, not of flesh and blood. For Scripture says: If you live according to the command of the flesh, you will die. Indeed, those who sow in the flesh will also reap corruption from the flesh. But flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God. But what does the Savior say to us? “A man’s enemies,” he says, “are those of his household.” And who are these household members, if not the sensual powers that oppose the spirit? For concupiscibility and irascibility, our enemies, stir up everything in us against the spirit, and continually disturb the house of conscience with various passions. For so long as these sensual powers carry out their natural movements and courses in a person, they do not permit him to ascend to the freedom of the spirit. Concupiscibility delights in the pleasing things of the sense of the flesh, and the contrary power, irascibility, detests the body’s pains. Meanwhile, as long as this war of the flesh is waged in a person, the monk is considered a restless, carnal cultivator of the lowest region. Therefore whoever desires to come to eternal life must strongly restrain in all things these disordered powers of the senses.

Therefore let the faithful coenobite strive to direct the concupiscible power toward pains that are acceptable to God, and to reform all his affections according to God’s good pleasure.
