Of these Bernard says: “He who thinks that he surpasses others, how should he not presume more concerning himself than concerning others? He sits first in the assembly; in councils he answers first; uncalled, he comes forward; unbidden, he thrusts himself in; when a matter has been arranged, he rejects what has been done by others. Whatever he himself has not done or arranged, he thinks has not been rightly done nor finely ordered. He judges those who judge; he prejudges those who are to judge. For the man for whom God is not sufficient in his heart seeks comfort in external things in vain.”

*The eighth degree of pride is called the defense of sins,* which indeed is always born of presumption. For a presumptuous monk always thinks himself better than everyone; if he has committed anything, he either stubbornly denies it, or, if he has been convicted, excuses it in many ways. For how will he be able humbly to confess his fault, who does not recognize himself as guilty? If you try to chastise such a man for his offenses, you always make him worse. Therefore whoever, when corrected for any matter, slips into words of malice; whoever opposes excuses for sins to the one reproving him, falls into this descent of death, opposed to the fifth degree of humility. But the presumptuous man strives to excuse his sin in various ways, for he is found to have fallen into this degree of pride. For either he says, “I did not do it”; or, “I did it indeed, but I did well”; or, if badly, “not very badly”; or, if very badly, “not with evil intention.” And if he has been convicted of malice, immediately, in excuse of himself, he does not fear to bring forward another’s counsel to the abbot, and never according to the Rule will he reveal hidden things to the abbot. But when he comes to confession, he will cloak the truth with a feigned mind, because, so that he himself may be able to escape confusion, he strives to bring others’ negligences into the open. Inwardly disordered, proud, and presumptuous, in confession he cloaks his own faults and confesses those of others. Always restless within himself, with one sentence he condemns the deeds and examinations of others; he complains that no one lives rightly in the monastery; he thinks that there is no charity among the brothers. But if his vain presumption has been detected through the skill of the confessor, he immediately seeks another confessor; and if the abbot should begin gently to wish to oppose him, the monk, lifted up into the ninth degree of pride, falls through restlessness of mind.

*The ninth degree of pride is described as murmuring or impatience,* which for the most part dominates carnal monks. Indeed, the man whose confession is feigned, when touched by one light insult or penance, is immediately raised up into open pride; the deceit of simulated humility is uncovered, nor can the feigned mind hide itself any longer, but it is compelled to bring out in public whatever lies hidden within. Thereafter, having become no longer in control of himself, the proud monk murmurs at everything; he obstinately accuses the shepherd’s judgment, however just; he gnashes his teeth; when stirred, he grows angry; without reason, like a madman, he is troubled by whatever he wishes. But the proud monk, given over to murmuring, having despised the fourth degree of humility, falls into the tenth descent of pride, through which he runs toward hell.

*The tenth degree of pride follows murmuring,* and is called rebellion. Proud and carnal monks fall into this, who, still dwelling in the lowest region, devote themselves to murmurings. Hence Bernard says: “Unless divine compassion looks upon the murmurer, so that at last, converted, he silently acquiesces in others’ judgments, which is very difficult for such men, he will soon, having become brazen and shameless, fall all the worse into this degree, inasmuch as the desperate temper of his mind does not [unclear]. He always advances toward worse things; and he who before had arrogantly despised his brothers in secret now also, what is more serious, openly shows that he despises his master.” But the censure of divine judgment does not allow this degree of pride to pass long unpunished, since monks rebellious against their shepherds frequently apostatize from the order; others, on account of serious crimes, foul prisons; some, expelled from monasteries, wander in exile, homeless and wretched; many, horribly struck by the Lord and despairing, hasten to the lower regions. For it is written: “He who resists authority opposes God’s ordinance.”
