There is also another mode of self-knowledge, which is taken up with respect to God, whenever the incomparable comparison between God and man is contemplated. Concerning this mode Bernard, illustrious in contemplation, says: Never does the measure of human imperfection better detect itself than in the light of God’s countenance and the mirror of divine vision. For there, he who is more in the day, and sees more what is lacking in himself, day by day amends by likeness whatever he has done wrong by unlikeness, drawing near through likeness to him from whom he was made far off through unlikeness. Therefore let us raise the gaze of the mind to the super-bright mirror of divine perfection; let us submit our affection to that unchangeable good, so that in God’s wisdom we may be able to detect our own foolishness, and in his power consider our own weakness.

*On Keeping the Memory of One’s Sins. Root V.*

The fifth root of the fear of God is the memory of sins, and the very foulness of sin often pondered. For indeed, he who truly understands the shamefulness of mortal sin always greatly fears the pit of this evil. But so that you may in some way recognize the gravity of sin, you must consider the seven things that follow.

First, then, consider the beauty of God’s justice, to which sin is wholly contrary, and therefore to be fled above all. For through sin the soul is entirely turned away from the highest good, and the love that is naturally in it toward its own beginning is changed into hatred.

For just as all good things are from the first good, so every love, toward whatever good it may be directed, is naturally kindled by the same good. For since our love cannot rest in any goods as in its end, it has as its end, where it is to rest, the same good that it had also as its beginning. Therefore, whenever sin is committed against the beauty of God’s justice, the mind is separated from its own beginning. For since God is charity, he demands love in the soul; peace requires peace; like loves like; unlike hates unlike.

Second, consider the enormity of sin, and carefully weigh how much its foulness displeases God. Behold, because of pride, the just God cast the most noble angel Lucifer out of heaven, and condemned him with his accomplices to the burning of eternal fire. He also compelled our first parents, Adam and Eve, to leave paradise because of disobedience, and by just judgment condemned the whole human race, in their crime, to the sentence of a twofold death. Finally, let us turn over the whole history of the Old and New Testament, and we shall find that what was clearly committed rashly against God’s justice was often gravely punished by that justice. Therefore the gravity of the crime is easily recognized, if the severity of the vengeance that followed is considered.

Third, consider how grave a scandal arises from sin, especially when one sins before others. For he who by his own evil example provokes someone to sin shows himself to be an adversary of God. For God, by his own will and out of mercy, disposed to save souls redeemed by the blood of his Only-Begotten; this man, through evil example and out of aversion from God, desires to destroy them. But the sin of scandal, with respect to guilt, is for this reason called very grave: because, as we have said, it is opposed to God. But if the one who gives occasion for harm is to be thought to have caused the harm, the sinner subjects himself to be condemned by as many sentences of death as he has made men fall into mortal crime by his own evil example.
