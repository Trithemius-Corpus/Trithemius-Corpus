Second, one must consider that this good disposition of the heart, and of the will ordered toward God, is a sign of the love of God and of the approval of good works. For thus, when the Lord was born in the flesh, the angels sang: Peace on earth to men of good will.

Third, it should be noted that, just as a good disposition of the heart always orders and prompts the mind through holy exercises toward the fear of God and the practice of the virtues, so the negligence of an evil disposition especially inclines it toward vices and acedia. Consideration of this difference gives rise to conjecture.

Fourth, one must consider that an evil disposition of the heart hinders and makes void the grace of God and merits in a person, since it makes the soul vile, ignoble, and degenerate, and always disposes it toward graver and worst things. Indeed, the root of evils of tongue and deed comes from an evil heart, as is clear in Saul and in many similar cases.

The fifth consideration is that evil in deed and tongue is never well examined unless the evil of the heart is first truly known. But each person can examine his own heart better than another can, and consider what sort of person lies hidden within himself. Therefore for the cenobite to whom care of another has not been entrusted, it is enough to inspect his own heart diligently, and in no way to occupy himself with scrutiny of another’s heart. But the shepherd of monks, to whom care of souls is entrusted, is also commanded to know the face of his flock.

Sixth, it should be noted from these things that it is very rash to wish to examine another’s heart and to judge the inward parts of brothers from words and deeds. The Lord knows who are his; nor is it granted to a man, without reason of office, to become a rash judge of another’s heart.

Seventh, it should be noted that a small evil of one’s own heart does more harm to the one who knows it in himself than a great evil suspected doubtfully concerning another’s heart. For the former is to be lamented, because it is certain and known; the latter is to be piously excused, because it is unknown.

Eighth, one must consider that since each person sees many imperfections in his own heart which he cannot recognize in others, on account of these he ought to judge himself inferior, insofar as they are obstacles to grace. But insofar as they defile the soul, he should despise himself as [more vile than all others?], even if he knows their public crimes. For since the root of their heart is utterly unknown to him, he cannot know with certainty how divine election has been ordained from eternity concerning him. Who would have believed that the thief crucified with the Lord was chosen from eternity, [if] he judged him according to works? All things turn to good for the elect, since where sin is seen to have abounded, often, by the will of divine election, greater grace is found. For divine love regards and embraces its elect from eternity, and looks upon them, as elect, not according to the misery in which they now are, but according to future glory. Therefore the cenobite is careful not to detract from others; he is careful not to judge others, if he desires to escape eternal death. For who are you, that you judge another’s servant? He stands to be judged by his own lord alone.

But the disposition of this seventh degree, because of that clause, “from the inmost disposition of the heart,” has a threefold root of its origin, namely truth, hope, and charity. For indeed, when the faithful cenobite sees that he does not have his being from himself, he truly thinks himself, insofar as he is from himself, to be nothing; and consequently, in the same respect, he considers his works, insofar as they come from himself who is nothing, to be nothing, since he is not unaware that nothing is made from nothing. Therefore the monk [rejoices with?] truth, who in this respect judges himself and his works to be nothing, because he fears incurring the vice of falsehood if he were to believe himself to be something great. And therefore, withdrawing from himself, he abandons himself, and places his being, living, and ability in God alone. Second, this disposition arises from hope of acquiring greater grace, because when a person, through humility, abandons himself, he approaches nearer to the grace of God. Third, the humility of this disposition takes its beginning from love of God, and continually receives increase in it. For whoever perfectly loves God seeks nothing other than his honor in all things, nor does he know how to exalt himself in anything, and for God’s sake he desires to be subject to all.
