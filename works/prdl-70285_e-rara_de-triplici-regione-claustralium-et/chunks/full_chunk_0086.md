*After Compline.*

When Compline has begun [unclear], return at once to your cell; and, after the customary prayers have been said, take care to prepare yourself most diligently for the following recollection of mind.

*On recollection after Compline. Consideration IX.*

Since we cannot remain wholly unmoved in one holy state of mind, it is necessary for us often to renew our holy purpose, so that as often as during the day, by wandering through the byways of sins, we withdraw from the one first principle of our soul, the most high God, so often by contrition and compunction we may return to him, with amendment firmly purposed.

Hence our holy fathers established for us that we should attend to recollection after Compline, and examine ourselves concerning the negligences committed during the day, so that the purpose of holiness, which we know to be quite weak and infirm, we may renew to fervor of spirit through the continual exercise of recollection. Therefore our devout monk, wishing to recollect himself fruitfully and with useful continuation, ought principally to examine himself in order in five matters.

*The first thing to be observed in recollection. The rendering of account.*

The rendering of account is the rendering of account by which the monk is bidden to bring back before the eyes of his mind, for chastisement, whatever he remembers himself to have committed during the day by negligence, or badly, or less well, by transgressing. Therefore, turned toward the Lord, with hands clasped, let him lie before his bed and say in this or a similar manner:

I give thanks to you, almighty and merciful God, for all your benefits, which you have deigned to show to me, an unworthy sinner and one deserving utterly nothing, from my youth up to this hour; for though I have deserved no good, I have received many and innumerable gifts of your goodness. Would that I could worthily give thanks to you, the highest and unchangeable good; would that, useful in your holy service, I could praise you with a pure heart and a clean conscience. But alas, with weakness, concupiscence, and evil habit prevailing, I, who ought to have clung with a pure heart and conscience to your most holy love, have offended you again and again with many negligences and faults; and as often as, according to the inward inspirations of your goodness, I have tried to rise to fervor of spirit, alas, so often I grieve that I have fallen again.

Behold, good Jesus, into how many faults, how many negligences, how many guilty acts have I fallen today alone; how many vain, how many useless, how many harmful thoughts have I admitted in so few hours; how many useless and harmful words have I uttered; how many negligences and offenses have I perpetrated in my actions this day.

Woe to me, wretched man, who could not pass even one very short day in your most holy service without impurity of heart and negligence; who today have committed so many faults and various imperfections. I confess to your goodness, O most gentle God, all my sins into which I have fallen from my earliest age; before your feet, O sweetest Jesus, I place all the negligences that I have committed today. I seek pardon, which I am not worthy to receive; I ask for grace, which I confess that I have so often received in vain. O most loving mediator of God and men, Jesus Christ, do not despise me, a wretched sinner, whom you willed to purchase with your own blood. I confess to you all the negligences into which today in any way I have fallen, and the faults that I have committed.

Here the monk, prostrate in mind before the Lord, should recall the more serious negligences to memory, and, humbly asking pardon, should lament his imperfection. Above all, however, let him reconsider whether he has offended anyone by word or deed; whether he has said anything against a brother; whether he has committed anything against charity and reverence; or knowingly saddened anyone badly.
