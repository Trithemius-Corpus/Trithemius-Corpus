*Epistle of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to the little work On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons.*

To the reverend fathers in Christ, the lord presidents, definitors, and the other abbots of the annual chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict, of the Bursfeld union and observance: brother Johannes, called Trithemius, abbot in Sponheim, of the same order and congregation, in the diocese of Mainz, sends reverence and charity.

I have obeyed your commands, most venerable fathers, and, as you instructed me, have undertaken to correct the little work On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons, together with the spiritual exercise of monks, lately edited by the venerable father lord Johannes, abbot of Bursfeld. And truly, had your authority not compelled me, I should never have dared to lay a hand on the distinguished work of so great a father, who, it is clear, being far more learned and holier than I, wrote excellently. For what could I correct, where everything is found most approved? A good and devout man, he wrote no otherwise than as he lives and [teaches?].

Nevertheless, in order to satisfy both your command and the requests of the author himself, I have rendered the whole little work in briefer, though not fewer, sentences, and, attending to accents, have brought it as far as I could into my own manner and style of writing. I kept the order and material of the earlier volume, but completely changed the form and customary mode of expression. I added many things that seemed useful to me in almost every place; I took away a few; in individual cases I altered the words, not the sense. For your command moved me to dare this, and the insistent request of the aforesaid father persuaded me. For you wished me to make a new work out of old material, to add whatever seemed suitable to me, to cut away superfluities, and to alter what had to be changed. I have done what I could; I have obeyed your commands; by rewriting it I have completed, according to the strength of my ability, which is slight, the work enjoined upon me.

Receive now, therefore, most beloved fathers, a work not mine but yours, not composed by me but rather rewritten. If it pleases you, praise the talent of the aforesaid father; but if, because of its meanness, it displeases you, correct my imperfection, since I, who had from another's resources the best material for a spiritual edifice, have through my inexperience constructed an unsuitable structure. If I have become foolish, you compelled me. Therefore, whether the work pleases or displeases, it was easier for me, by writing ineptly, to accept your command obediently than, against obedience, to keep silence. Yet I trust not in my own powers but in another's merits, since a work that charity has produced will displease no one who loves God.

I desire that your paternities may always remain happy in Christ. From Sponheim, the fourteenth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord's incarnation 1497.

*Here begins the book On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons, and on their spiritual exercise.*

When I call to mind the state of the monastic life in this time, I grieve deeply over its calamity, because the fervor of spiritual exercise, which once produced most abundant fruits in the order of our holy father Benedict, has now for the most part failed among monks. For we see cloistered persons returning in their morals to the world; we see the institution of regular observance despised by many; and religion, which from the beginning had been instituted for contempt of the world, is again being recalled to the vanities of the world by wicked monks. The crown has fallen from our head; the integrity of regular discipline has been corrupted; and the former fervor of holy conduct has perished. We see many monasteries of our order lying buried in the worst deformity; but we see very few reformed to the purity of the Rule.

The cause of this destruction of regular institution is negligence in spiritual exercises; for as soon as the spirit of fervor grew dull in the minds of cloistered persons, the flesh, previously dead through self-denial, revived, and the world, which before had appeared dry and contemptible to devout hearts, began to flourish again in carnal minds. Indeed, the thought of the human heart, prone to evil from youth, unless it has been stirred up to fervor of spirit by the daily bridle of spiritual exercise, soon falls back into its former negligence and grows lukewarm from its holy purpose.
