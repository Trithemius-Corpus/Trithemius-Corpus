*On the devout fitting of the powers for entrance into the third region. Article VI.*

When, by the degrees of humility, the innermost powers of a person have especially been purified, founded, and reformed, it is now said to be very necessary in monks that they be fitted more devoutly for entrance into the third region. This diligence in striving toward higher things is, as we have said, necessary for the cenobite, because wherever the effort to advance is laid aside, acquired virtue is utterly dissipated. No one is permitted to stand still in this second region; rather, we are all commanded to ascend to the third through continual effort. For truly this continual movement casts all the slothful and lukewarm back into the first region of the flesh, but gently and without violence always impels the humble and those fervent in desire toward the third. Therefore whoever has deserved to obtain from the Lord, through humility, the reformation of his powers is already near to the third region of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it powerfully, it is necessary that, by most diligently fitting the powers of his soul, he order them to their several operations. The intellect is then said to be rightly fitted for entrance into the third region when it is illuminated to know the highest truth according to its capacity.

For the same purpose the memory is ordered when it is fully absorbed in retaining and cherishing the highest truth. But if the will is disposed to loving the Highest, which is God, it is well fitted for the entrance of the spirit.

Now, in order that the cenobite may be able to attain the purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, dying to the world, the mind, pacified from every inward and outward tumult, should rest, so that, buried within the bosom of divine love, it may remain there and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union. To this rest of the mind [unclear]. *The first degree of good will lays the beginnings of the virtues*, which is called right will.

For the soul which, consenting to the law of God, is subjected to God through right will, even if it still seems weak, is directed to the path of virtue. For virtue is perfected while the flesh resists, and how right the will is is proved by the force of the spirit. Yet often a will is called right and good even when it is found to be less strong. The good that it loves it cannot always accomplish; the evil that it hates it grieves that it has committed through weakness. Through the weakness of the flesh it is very close to the lowest region, but through rightness of intention and the effort to advance it is continually kindled toward the liberty of the spirit. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the mind that thinks many things. O who could describe this battle between spirit and flesh, where the spirit itself desires to subject the flesh to itself through right will, and the weak flesh always resists the spirit through contrary desires?

The second degree is a will strong in rectitude, so that it both bravely flees the evil that it hates against the impulses of the flesh, and strongly grasps by action the good that it loves, although with labor.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in all riches. For truly the will does not long remain strong if it has not been devout in the commandments of God.

The fourth degree is a full will, so that a man does not divide his powers at all, but continually stretches himself wholly toward his God through the denial of all things. Three things are necessary for this degree, so that good will may be made full in us.

The first is complete denial of oneself, because the will is shown still to be weak if it is not mortified through every kind of self-denial.

The second thing required for a full good will is perfect resignation [unclear]; if anyone desires to apprehend it, let him fervently ascend the third degree of humility. F ii
