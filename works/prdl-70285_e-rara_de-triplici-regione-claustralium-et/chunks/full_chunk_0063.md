Contemplation, however, is sometimes accomplished by an enlargement of the mind, because when the soul, by its own effort, stretches itself more intensely in holy meditation, meditation is changed into contemplation. And this mode of contemplation is accustomed to rise up by three degrees: by art, through which we learn what must be done by us; by exercise, through which we perform what has been learned; by attention, through which we fulfill our duty.

Sometimes contemplation is taken up into God by an uplifting of the mind, when the mind, divinely irradiated above human things, passes as though by [unclear] into the contemplation of divine things. This mode also is raised into the height of contemplation by three degrees: for sometimes the understanding of a person, divinely inspired, receives something that wholly exceeds the measure of its own knowledge. The second degree raises the mind above effort, when, divinely enlightened, it perceives something to which no human effort reaches. In the third degree the mind is raised on high, when above the bounds of human nature and effort it is divinely illuminated in contemplation.

At times, too, contemplation directs itself by alienation of the mind toward the mountain of divine darkness, when, the memory of present things lulled to sleep, the soul passes by the illumination of divine operation into a state unknown to human knowledge. And this mode belongs especially to this supreme region of the spirit, in which the mind of the true lover of God is seen kindled with so great a fervor of love that it is often proved to be alienated from itself. For sometimes, by the greatness of devotion, it is carried outside itself; sometimes by exceeding wonder it is lifted above itself; at times, on account of immeasurable exultation, it leaves itself behind and, by the ray of love, passes into the supreme elevation of contemplation. But the delight of this highest and supreme region is not understood by a mortal man who has not ascended to it, purified through the experience of love.

*Preface to the second principal part of the spiritual exercise of the cloistered. Happily begins.*

Since, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have completed the little work on the threefold region of the cloistered, recently enjoined upon us by the annual chapter, it remains now that, undertaking according to our strength the principal matter of the second part of the work, we describe the manner and form of daily spiritual exercise.

For even among the cloistered there are many weak and feeble persons, of whom some perhaps would gladly ascend to the contemplation of divine love if they had a method suitable for beginners. For all who are established in holy religion must take the greatest care that the mind not grow sluggish in idleness, since whoever has fallen away from the continuous labor of spiritual exercise gradually falls into the depth of torpor. For just as this visible fire of ours cannot continue in its operation without fuel suited to it, so the love of inward compunction cannot remain in a person’s mind without the continual exercise of devotion. For as soon as the labor of spiritual study has been interrupted through negligence, all compunction of devotion gradually vanishes. Indeed, no one can remain stable and firm in a holy purpose who does not strive daily to renew the fervor of the mind through some exercise. For as soon as the fuel of divine love is withdrawn, devotion is changed into torpor. For Scripture says: “Every idle man is in desires.” Therefore, lest the mind gradually slip away through sloth from the holy fervor of the spirit, let it learn to renew itself daily through some spiritual exercise, so that, while fuel is supplied to love day by day, the desire of making further progress may be more greatly kindled.
