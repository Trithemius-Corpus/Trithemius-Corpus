The first degree of humility, therefore, is described by Saint Benedict in the Rule as the fear of God, which is called the beginning of wisdom. The fear of the Lord is handed down under a threefold distinction: namely servile, initial, and filial, which is also called chaste. From this threefold distinction three roots are said to proceed, according to which the beginning cenobite ought to order his meditations. For each root has its own proper meditations, and when one is rightly disposed, another, better one is always generated.

For from servile fear, taken up not merely in a servile manner but prudently and virtuously, there proceed meditations on horror and punishments, which, restraining the soul from sin, gradually introduce filial fear. For indeed, when by consideration of punishments the mind is turned to horror, the boldness of sinning is restrained, because the servant who fears blows keeps the commandments of the Lord. But this servile fear alone is not sufficient for meriting the fatherly inheritance; nevertheless, as an initial effect, it disposes one toward filial fear, which is sufficient.

For initial fear is placed midway between the two, and is easily changed from servile into filial. This initial fear receives meditations concerning the last things, and fears both to incur punishments and to lose the sweet homeland. And here [unclear] is placed in the Rule, where its roots are also described.

Filial fear, however, embraces its meditations in love, since it fears nothing else except that the beloved father should be offended. Of this fear the wise man says: The fear of the Lord is glory and boasting, joy and a crown of exultation. The fear of the Lord will delight the heart and will give joy and gladness unto length of days. And again: It will be well with him who fears God at the last, and on the day of his death he will be blessed. This fear is also called chaste, because it detests evils not from fear of punishments but from love alone.

From this threefold fear we see three breadths of spiritual exercises arising around the degrees of humility, and individual exercises corresponding to individual fears. For servile fear performs its exercises in one way, initial fear applies itself to virtues in another way, and filial fear proceeds in its works in a much different way. For just as the affections of hearts arise in various ways, so the exercises of one fear are done in different ways. In one way the publican, mindful of his crimes, feared the sentence of the judge; in another way Jerome said that he heard the voice of the trumpet calling the dead to judgment. For the holy Gospel says of the former: Standing afar off, the publican would not lift up his eyes to heaven, but striking his breast he said, God, be merciful to me, a sinner. But that blessed Jerome says: Whether I drink or eat, I always think I hear that trumpet sounding in my ears: Rise, dead, come to judgment. The compunction of each arises from fear, but both are not generated from the desire of one affection. For the publican, terrified by the guilt of his sins, feared the sentence of the just judge, and therefore, being compunct, he said: God, be merciful to me, a sinner. Jerome, however, is not shaken by fear from the guilt of [unclear], but, founded in charity and reckoning himself a useless servant, reveres the abyss of divine justice.

*The second degree is to relinquish one’s own will for the love of God, and in no respect to love one’s own willing against the Rule.*

This degree arises from the first ascent of humility and is raised into the third. For indeed, he who fears God, and truly fears to be separated from him and to undergo punishments, hates nothing more than his own will. For the faithful cenobite knows that every sin arises from one’s own will alone, and therefore he detests it as the gate of hell. For that blessed man truly said: Take away one’s own will, and now there will be no hell. For nothing burns in hell except one’s own will; nothing loses the grace of God except voluntary fault. Therefore whoever, truly fearing God, fears to lose his grace, greatly fears his own will as the betrayer of his soul. Consideration of the danger of one’s own will makes the cenobite despise his own willing and deny himself to himself. And this denial of one’s own will and of oneself is accomplished in three ways, as will be said in what follows.
