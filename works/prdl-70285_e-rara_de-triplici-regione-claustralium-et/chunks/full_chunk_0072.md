Therefore he who tries to accuse another should beware lest he be moved against his brother by the zeal of bitterness; for if the charity of the accuser does not intervene, rancor does not amend the accused. The admonition of the accuser never bears fruit in the offender when the love of God and brotherly charity do not commend it. For indeed, although almighty God is supremely good, he will not give to your accusation the purpose of compunction in the heart of the brother, when he finds that you have been moved to accuse not out of charity but out of displeasure. For where he finds the root of his charity, he will not deny good fruit. But if you perceive that you have been moved against your brother by some indignation, it is better to postpone the accusation for a time than to wound yourself so cruelly with another’s wound.

*The third chapter is that of private humiliation.*

The third chapter of private humiliation is prescribed whenever a brother is rebuked by a senior for his negligences. For our most holy father Benedict commands, in chapter 72 of the Rule, that a monk, in whatever place or time he has been accused, as soon as he has perceived his senior’s mind to be even slightly moved, should immediately cast himself down at his feet, and there lie humbly until his command. Here he is in no way permitted to excuse himself or to place the fault on another; rather, the monk is ordered to be silent and humbly acknowledge himself. For he who is a true monk neither murmurs when justly corrected nor excuses himself when unjustly accused. Likewise in the divine offices and in the other conventual places, as soon as a devout cenobite has offended in anything through negligence, humbling himself according to the custom there, he makes satisfaction, and chooses rather to perform a greater penance in doubtful cases than to wound his conscience with a lesser one, or to cause scandal to others. But he who still understands this humiliation, to be undergone at every time and place, as burdensome and difficult, does not walk in the spirit but still lives carnally; unless, by subjugating this Jebusite, he has made it tributary to himself, he will never be a true and devout cenobite.

*The fourth chapter is that of discussions and counsels.*

We call the fourth chapter deliberative, when we call the brothers to counsel for some difficult matter. How this chapter ought to be held, Saint Benedict makes clear in the Rule concerning the brothers to be admitted to counsel. Therefore the humble and true cenobite, when he has been called to counsel, approaches humbly with reverence and modesty; when asked what he thinks, he speaks few words; he is by no means offended if his counsel is disregarded and another’s approved. For he states humbly what he thinks, but always preferring the counsels of others to his own; and with true subjection he commits both himself and his opinion to be examined by the one presiding. For he so annihilates himself in deep humility that he always believes more in others than in himself, and trusts that good counsel is divinely inspired in those better than himself; and in his mind he is accustomed to place his prelate before all as the vicar of Christ. Indeed, the monk especially advances toward the mortification of his own will who, in giving counsels, refers his own opinion to the abbot with cheerful humility, [unclear] from true humility, and recognizes that the occasion for pride has been taken away from him.
