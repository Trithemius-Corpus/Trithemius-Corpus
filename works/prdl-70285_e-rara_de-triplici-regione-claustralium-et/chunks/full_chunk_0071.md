*On the chapter of faults*

Since nothing in human actions is perfect in every respect, let the devout cenobite rejoice that he has found a remedy for his imperfection, because whatever is committed in well-reformed monasteries through human frailty is corrected daily. For the chapter of faults, continued each day, washes away public offenses; sacramental confession, repeated daily through the grace of contrition, remits hidden negligences. We discern four chapters in the monastery, of which we shall speak in order.

*The first chapter is passive, when a brother proclaims himself or is proclaimed by another.*

The first chapter is called passive, in which our devout cenobite proclaims himself for his negligences, or is proclaimed by another. In this chapter the lover of God, who has denied his very self to himself, humbly both accuses his negligences and suffers himself to be accused of them by others, lest he be judged more harshly by the Lord after this life. For the holy apostle of God says: If we judged ourselves, we would certainly not be judged. And the prophet Nahum says: Affliction shall not rise up a second time. For indeed, he who here, for his sins and for love of God, is now voluntarily afflicted, is worthy, as one well purged, to be freed by the mercy of the Creator from future affliction.

Would that all cloistered persons knew how greatly capitular proclamations, voluntarily endured by them, would profit toward the merit of eternal life: they would endure all accusations and injuries for love of God with inestimable joy. For we merit more when we patiently suffer injuries and adversities for love of God and brotherly charity than if we devote ourselves to good works.

Therefore, let the devout monk, standing in judgment, fear no proclamations whatever, but desire to be accused by others in memory of the Lord’s passion, knowing that before him the height of glory has been set forth, and is to be conferred in heaven upon humble patience. O brother, why would you not endure the proclamations of the brethren, you who expect the fellowship of angels? Let all indignation be far from you; let murmuring be far away; let pride withdraw far off. For you have no cause for impatience. For you are proclaimed either justly or unjustly. If justly, why would you not endure it? It is better to be purged here in small matters than after this life in many. And he who sins publicly is to be rebuked before all. But if you are proclaimed unjustly, remember that you are a monk, and you will find that you are bound to injuries. For when you are proclaimed justly, know that you are to be purged through humble patience; when unjustly, you show yourself a true monk and merit more. Therefore, when you are proclaimed, recall to memory the fourth and sixth degrees of humility; and if you are a true monk, pray even for those who accuse you. For the true lover of God loves the one who proclaims him all the more, and always on the same day bestows upon him either some prayer or some special service.

If you are good and wise, proclamation will not be able to displease you; but if you are evil and foolish, you are worthy to be proclaimed at all times. For Scripture says concerning the wise man: Rebuke the wise man, and he will love you. But concerning the evil and foolish the prophet says: They hated the one who rebuked in the gate, and they abhorred the one who spoke perfectly.

*The second chapter is active, in which one proclaims another.*

The second chapter we call active, in which we no longer proclaim ourselves, but another. For the act of this proclamation charity is supremely necessary; compassion also, no less than discretion, must be applied. Therefore, when you wish to proclaim your brother by reason of charity, you should always apply the fear of God; and, mindful of the fellowship of eternal life, at the beginning you should say: Our beloved brother committed this or that; I ask that he be willing to amend himself for the Lord’s sake. Whatever must be said, let him put it forward in few words and with compassion; let him state the deed simply; let him not increase the fault of the one proclaimed by aggravating it; let him silently entrust to the presiding officer the judgment whether what he did was good or bad.
