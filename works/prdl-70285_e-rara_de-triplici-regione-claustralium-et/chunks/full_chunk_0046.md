We are therefore commanded first of all to do everything for the praise of God, and in everything that we do to raise our intention chiefly to his honor. For only a good intention toward God distinguishes works; love alone makes them fruitful. Behold, the Lord has conferred on us the art of nature for praising him; moreover he has given the teachings of salvation through the law; he requires from us praise and love above all things. Let us therefore exercise, for the honor of God, the art conferred on us, and through the teaching of the law the eye of intention will become pure.

Fourth, the merciful Father has conferred on us the world with its necessary elements; for these he requires from us a sincere contemplation of himself as our due. Whatever we see in this world ought to bring us to the remembrance of God, and continually stir us to love its Creator above all things. Hence Bernard, practiced in sacred matters, says: The whole and true lover of God, wherever he turns, has a familiar reminder of his Creator, and uses things as a mirror, and by thinking thus is lifted up from creatures to the Creator. For concerning each thing he says: If those things seem good, beautiful, and sweet which, created with time, are changed and pass away, what goodness, beauty, and sweetness does their author, God, have, remaining forever, who has reserved for himself things incomparably better than these? By this consideration of things the devout person looks upon all human beings with the simple eye of the mind as a likeness of the image of God, and does not curiously attend to the pleasing or displeasing form of the body in them. For in a human being he considers not what he sees outwardly, but the image of the Creator, which he knows to be a sharer in eternal happiness.

Fifth, the Son of God offered himself for us in torments, and redeemed us, lost as we were, from the destruction of perpetual death by his blood. We are commanded to offer a willing imitation to this love of our Savior, if we desire to come to his kingdom. For the prince of the apostles says to the faithful: Christ suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow in his footsteps. What shall I repay to you, O good Jesus, for your benefits toward me, so great and so immeasurable? I shall suffer with you in heart, I shall suffer with you in deed; I shall imitate you in the affections of the mind according to my ability.

Sixth, the Creator has conferred on us the time for meriting eternal life; he [gave?] the necessary nourishment of the body. For here we are debtors to ourselves, since in this time we owe ourselves the acquisition [of merit?]. Why then do we spend the days of salvation so negligently and without the fruit of merit, when we have nothing more precious than time? Behold, within a brief time now we can merit the joys of eternal happiness, and all of us neglect to do what is good. Woe to us, foolish and insane, who have received nothing in this world more precious than time, and yet are accustomed to spend nothing more cheaply. For now an acceptable time is granted to us; now the day of salvation is bestowed, in which we ought to devote ourselves to holy works with the greatest fervor, and always to [provide for?] the hour of death.

Seventh, he set before us all heaven, filled with joys, and promised himself, together with the things contained in it, as rewards. For this munificence of our Savior we ought to devote ourselves to the holy works of mercy and, according to our strength, show ourselves imitators of God. To this he exhorts us in his sacred Gospels: Be merciful, as your Father God also is merciful. At the end also, when the day of judgment has come, the merciful, as the Lord himself promises, will go into eternal life, since whoever has done mercy will obtain mercy. And lest someone from among the monks say to me, “I can show mercy to no one, since the religious life permits me to possess absolutely nothing,” let us know that among the necessary works of mercy the spiritual are the more powerful. If you, monk, wish to be merciful, pray to God for the wretched; bestow saving teaching upon the unlearned.

Therefore the sum of meditation on the benefits of God rests upon three exercises, in which it is principally contained whole.
