What then will you answer to the supreme shepherd, useless ruler, when not only the blood of those perishing is required of you, but also an account of those making less progress is demanded? Behold, now in the worst security, disregarding God’s judgment, you walk about; you think that everything that pleases you in the body is permitted to you, and you do not ever consider to whom you will have to render account for all things. Soon the Lord of all will come, to whom you do not fear to show yourself an unfaithful servant, whose honor you do not fear to arrogate to yourself. Unless meanwhile you do penance, unless you amend your life for the better, you will burn with demons in hell without end.

Seventh, it happens that a shepherd is disordered toward his subjects if, neglecting the common good, he seeks his own advantage with a vicious intention; if, presiding in a monastery, he serves not God but his own interests, while he rejoices that he is prelate in order to obtain the pernicious liberties of the flesh, and fraudulently provides for himself for the future from the goods of the monastery. This disorder, however, is especially seen in those who resign their office on whatever pretext; to them, as the reward of their stewardship, as if they had been bishops, an annual pension is assigned from the goods of the monastery. This is not the manner of those seeking the Lord; this is not the way of the old monks. When you resign the pastoral office for God’s sake, resign it simply; do not demand anything beyond food and clothing, if you do not wish to sin with proprietors. You are a monk; you were a monk before you became abbot; you will remain a monk after the office. Why then should you demand a pension, when you know that you are a monk? Just as in buying prelacies the vice of simony is committed, so by demanding a pension for the resignation of an abbacy one sins against the rule. What then? Do we condemn those who, resigning the office of pastoral care without fault, have received annual provisions from the goods of the monastery? Far from it. We do not forbid necessities; we deny superfluities. Yet he who is a true monk and lover of God is content with regular provision even after an abbacy.

*In what ways the monks, citizens of the lowest region, are disordered toward one another.*

*Article Four.*

The citizens of the lowest region, monks and carnal men, are disordered toward one another in many and various ways; for since, stirred up by animal passions like beasts, they do not direct themselves according to the right judgment of reason, it is no wonder if they pervert the order of brotherly life. For a troubled conscience is always savage and presumptuous, and an evil mind never has the joys of peace, but is continually shaken by vice and sorrow; it is inwardly tormented by the everlasting fires of crimes. And so monks, citizens of the dark region, blinded by evil and disordered affections, do not consider the end of the monastic profession; they love nothing but the advantage of the present life; they neglect their own salvation; they do not rise up to the love of eternity.

First, therefore, they are greatly disordered toward one another when they cast aside the intellectual regard that ought to be had among brothers; when they do not notice the image of God in man; when they do not think of the communion of grace and glory infused into men and to be granted in the future; nor do they care to show one another the love of brotherly fellowship. They live with one another like horse and mule, concordant in bodily appearance, because whatever is pleasing to sensuality they love, and whatever accords with reason they do not admit. One is loved by another not because he is a man, but because he is like his lover in the affection of sensuality. For the carnal man loves in man not virtues, not religion, not, finally, nature itself, but flesh, blood, bodily quality. Since, therefore, the love of carnal men is carnal, it is easily changeable by occasion. For the citizens of this lowest region venerate similar ways of life, and monks always embrace monks like themselves with affection. The one whom God loved from eternity the disordered cenobite persecutes; the one whom God, because of his demerits, despises, this man venerates as a saint.

Second, cloistered men are disordered toward one another by reason of dissimilarity, when either the seniors, stirred up by harshness and pride, despise their juniors, or the juniors contumaciously set themselves against their seniors.
