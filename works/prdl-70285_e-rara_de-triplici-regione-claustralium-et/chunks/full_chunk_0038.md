It is clear from what has been said that the perfect charity which is promised to those ascending the steps of humility is shown in this seventh step. For the other five steps that follow in the Rule add nothing to the perfection of the inner man who is established in the seventh, but rather order the outer man, lest he disturb the inner. Finally, from this seventh step we can erect certain new ascents of humility, and in them raise all the other steps higher. For filial fear resides more fervently in him who, by the breadth of love, can make greater progress.

Now that the ascents of the heart up to the step of charity have been learned, let the cenobite consider with what zeal, with what effort, with what perseverance he ought to exercise himself in these things, so that he may reach this seventh step of love. But he who does not always strive to advance in these steps of humility should know that he sins against the Rule; and unless by a holier effort he comes to his senses in repentance, he incurs the sentence of damnation. For thus the Rule says: “But if at any time he has acted otherwise, let him know that he is to be condemned by him whom he mocks.” But if anyone making too little progress in religion is deemed guilty by divine judgment, what else does one who abandons the effort of advancing deserve than to receive the sentence of damnation? For where, according to the Rule, one is held guilty for lesser progress even among those who are striving to advance, monks who despise every effort of advancing are without doubt condemned by the sentence of the Judge. Therefore, if you do not wish to incur the danger of damnation, strive continually to preserve the effort of advancing under the Rule.

Since we have said that the other five steps of humility pertain to the custody of the inner man, let us also expound them briefly. For the monk who neglects to order his outward conduct according to the Rule will not remain ordered for long in inward things. For just as a body stripped of clothing is corrupted, so humility without conduct is [unclear].

The eighth step of humility, then, is set down in the Rule thus: if a monk does nothing except what the common rule of the cenobium, or the examples of the elders, encourage. In this step, singularity of outward conduct contrary to the Rule is forbidden to monks; but inward singularity of greater charity and humility is forbidden to no one. Therefore let no one prefer his own inventions to the holy Rule; let no one place his own customs before the examples of the elders, because to stand in the seventh step is not enough for one who does not keep the rule of the community.

The ninth step of humility follows from the Rule, through which excessive talkativeness is forbidden. For it befits a monk to keep silence, especially while he sits in the midst of his seniors. Indeed, he who speaks without being questioned is deservedly regarded as a fool.

The tenth step of humility forbids readiness of laughter and levity, because whoever takes delight in laughter is shown to be foolish and estranged from the fear of God.

The eleventh step of humility enjoins modesty and maturity upon the monk, so that when the opportunity and permission to speak are granted, he may speak with humble gravity, without laughter and anger. Let the monk’s speech be rare, brief, and reasonable, because it does not befit one who wishes to please the Most High to say useless things.

The twelfth step of humility teaches the monk to order the outer man, in appearance and bearing, from a humble heart, and to demonstrate also by outward conduct how devout he is inwardly. And although this step is last in the order of all, nevertheless in the practice of those beginning to advance it is first. And therefore Bernard, in describing these twelve steps of humility, begins from this one, and by ascending to the first, as I might put it, descends through the order. For no one easily advances inwardly to compunction of heart and fear of God who does not order his conduct outwardly.
