Let him recollect himself for a little while, and kindle himself more fully toward the holy purpose of perseverance. Then, turning to the Lord, let him say:

*Prayer.*

I beseech you, most sweet Lord Jesus, through the virtue of your most holy body and blood which I have received, though unworthy: grant me the sweetness of your love, the sincerity of brotherly affection, strength in temptation, purity in heart, and discipline in conduct, so that, by your bounty, I may satisfy my profession and continually advance in your most holy love. Then, until the first signal for choir, let him read something good, meditate, or pray, or certainly write; and let him fervently apply himself to the work enjoined upon him by obedience.

*At the first signal for choir before High Mass.*

When the first signal has sounded for the hour which customarily precedes High Mass, the monk, immediately putting aside everything he has had in his hands, should read the preparatory prayers for choir, as they have been set out above before Matins. Afterwards let him go to choir with reverence and maturity, bowing and praying according to the rite set forth above.

*At High Mass.*

*Consideration IV.*

When the bells are rung for High Mass, you will be able to read the proper hour of the Holy Cross, and afterwards, so far as time permits, to recall to mind something from the proem set before private masses. For the sum of our salvation is placed in the merit of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; nor is any good work believed to be meritorious for us which is not offered in its power. The memory of this passion is enacted in the celebration of masses, where the innocent Lamb, slain for us, is offered to God the Father in an ineffable mystery. Therefore we truly hope for salvation from our Savior alone, the Lord Jesus Christ, whom we believe to have suffered for us. And so whatever the Church asks in the collects, she humbly prays that it be given to her by God the Father through him. Therefore let our devout monk keep in his heart this regard for the merit of the Lord's passion; upon it the whole office of the Mass may sweetly run, as upon a lower millstone. For every Mass, of whatever kind it may be, that is said in the holy Church of God, is at all times referred to the merit of the Lord's passion.

*On the Introit of the Mass.*

We do not intend, however, to explain the office of the Mass point by point, since each of our men can find many works by others; but we wish to give devout brothers occasion for meditation, whom we know to be inflamed by inward desires. Therefore you can, by meditation, refer the first part of the introit and the verse to the incarnation of the Lord, and refer the repetition with the Gloria to his nativity. Nevertheless you ought carefully to examine what the introit contains according to the literal sense, so that you may be able to find a suitable spiritual understanding for yourself. For nothing is done in vain in the divine office, but all things are filled with mysteries and sacred actions.

*On Kyrie eleison.*

Kyrie eleison is Greek, and is composed of a noun and a verb; and among them these are two words. For the first is from kyrios, which is “lord”; kyrie is said in the vocative. The second is from eleeo, which is “I have mercy”; eleeson, “have mercy,” is uttered in the imperative. Hence kyrie eleison is formed, that is, “Lord, have mercy,” by elision for kyrie eleison, since a vowel before a vowel can be elided for a better sound.

Therefore Kyrie eleison is said three times to the Father, so that he who, out of exceeding charity, willed that his Son be incarnate from the Virgin and die for us, may will to come to our aid in our necessities by his mercy. To the Son also we say Christe eleison three times, in which we pray to him that he may come to our aid. We also utter Kyrie eleison three times to the Holy Spirit, in which we humbly call upon him, through his ineffable goodness, to be our help.
