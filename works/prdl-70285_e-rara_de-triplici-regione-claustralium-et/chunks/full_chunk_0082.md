There are three places in which a devout monk and lover of God ought frequently to be found: namely, in the church, in his cell, and, at the proper time, in the refectory. But he must not allow himself to wander about the monastery, because it is written of the wicked, the reprobate, and the gyrovagues: “The ungodly walk in a circuit.” But of the just man Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he shall sit solitary and be silent, because he has raised himself above himself.” The works of the cell, however, are few. The first is to attend to commands; the second is to read profitably; the third is to devote oneself at fitting times to meditation; the fourth is to pray frequently; the fifth is to rest becomingly. Let us speak briefly of these in order.

*First, in the cell you must attend to commands:*

First, then, while you are in the dwelling of the cell, learn to attend to commands, and not to prefer your [unclear] devotions to obedience. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than sacrifices, and to hearken is better than to offer fat.” Therefore, if something has been assigned to you to do in the cell, do not transgress the paths of obedience on any pretext, but strive diligently to perform the work imposed on you for the love of our Lord Jesus Christ and the merit of holy obedience. Hence Hugh, lover of quiet, says: “Be gladly in the cell, and always do something in it that may edify you and has been enjoined by a superior, considering that you have no right to leave it without permission, except for the necessity of nature.” You have also on this matter the prohibition of our most holy father Benedict in the Rule, who forbids you exit and wandering without permission.

*Second, in the cell one ought to read profitably.*

It especially befits a monk, while he is in the cell, always to read something good and to occupy himself profitably in all things. For sacred reading overcomes the temptations of the devil, calms the fires of the flesh, makes one despise the world, and lifts the mind to heavenly things. For Jerome, lover of divine Scripture, truly said: “Love knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh.” Not all things are to be read by all monks, since he who is still weak in spirit must be imbued only with simple teachings. And Hugh says to the monk who loves simplicity: “Do not read in order to be considered more learned, nor read curious things that do not edify, since vain reading begets vain thoughts and extinguishes devotion of mind.” Therefore simple things, not lofty ones, are to be read by the simpler monks; devout things, not curious ones, so that those who, because of slowness of understanding, are not learned in higher matters may not become dissipated in vain ones. But for those who are stronger in spirit we do not forbid studies suited to them, since all things are clean to the clean, and nothing read harms the truly devout. Nevertheless, let no one claim for himself a strength of spirit that he does not have; rather, it is placed in the abbot’s discretion to judge who seems fit for freedom of reading.

*Third, in the cell you must devote yourself at fitting times to meditations.*

As a flower springs from the root, so meditation is generated from reading. If the reading has been vain, the meditation too will be fruitless and useless. For meditation is the fruit of reading, and prayer is the offspring of meditation. Take care, therefore, to devote yourself at fitting times to holy meditations, so that you may sometimes also build up the mind and lift it, through the fervor of divine love, into contemplation. Finally, meditation itself lifts the mind.
