It is a very true sign of weakness to examine the deeds of others and, beyond the rule of one’s office, to be occupied with matters belonging to others [unclear]. For the monk who, according to the definition of his name, is commanded to care for himself alone, scrutinizes the deeds of others not without manifest danger to his own salvation. But whoever subject themselves to the vice of curiosity soon descend to the second degree of pride. For one always disposes toward another, and no single sin alone rules over proud monks.

The second degree of pride, therefore, is lightness of mind and the unstable occupation of a wandering heart. Carnal monks fall into this degree of disordered conduct through various affections, which they have sensually contracted from the vice of curiosity. But the true cenobite must remain steadfast in his holy purpose, because when lightness of mind is admitted, the constancy of virtue is dissipated in the heart. It is the judgment of a disordered mind to run about through various images of things and always to produce new forms of sensual affections.

The third degree of pride is called empty joy, which is produced from the lightness of a disordered man’s mind. Carnal monks compose for themselves various images of affections; when these meet agreeably with sensuality they rejoice, but when otherwise happens, contracting grave indignation, they slip into various temptations. But that is not true joy which [unclear], nor does the gladness long continue which does not have its source in the love of God. And therefore disordered men, tossed by vain joy into boastful vaunting, fall, when they falsely ascribe great things to themselves.

The fourth degree of pride, then, as we have said, arises from empty joy; it is called foolish boasting. Carnal monks fall into this when, magnifying themselves before others, they exalt themselves. If they have done any good, they boldly bring it forward in public, often mendaciously inventing even things they have not done. They desire to seem holy, learned, prudent, and noble, and they devise many things by the craftiness of their wit, so that they may appear glorious outwardly to men, though inwardly they know they are empty of the merit of virtue. If you begin to praise anyone in their presence, they at once rise up to the contrary; they say many vicious things against him, because, while they desire to show themselves alone praiseworthy, they do not permit anyone else to be praised in their presence.

The fifth degree of pride is understood as singularity, which is generated from the swelling of vaunting. Into this degree proud cloistered men fall, who through boasting have presumed that they surpass others either in merit or in knowledge. Hence, lest they appear like the rest in their manner of life, out of self-pride alone they show themselves singular in all things. But this singularity is reprehended in the monastery, where all are commanded to live in common according to the rule. Proud monks, as blessed Bernard testifies, while they prefer themselves to others in virtues and wisdom, fall into such arrogance of rashness that they flatter themselves more over one fast while the rest are eating than if they had fasted seven other days. In short, one little prayer said singularly seems more advantageous to them than the whole psalmody of one night. During the meal they often carry their eyes around the table, and if they see someone eating less they groan and begin to grieve that they themselves have been beaten, cruelly withholding from themselves what they had provided for necessary nourishment. These proud cloistered men are energetic for their own singular practices, sluggish for common ones. In bed they keep watch; in choir and at the regular reading they drowse.

The sixth degree of pride takes its beginning from the fifth; it is called the arrogance of the proud. Indeed, when disordered cloistered men begin, through zeal for singularity, to appear outwardly to others, they burn into a greater madness of arrogance, and whatever is said by men in their praise they foolishly judge to be true. O pitiable folly of the proud: although in all other matters they believe no one more than themselves, about themselves alone they believe themselves less than another. Whatever praise of themselves they hear from the unskilled, they ascribe not to the ignorance or good will of the one praising, but to their own merits. But whoever is delightfully fed by human praises is held before the Lord to be empty of every virtue and full of arrogance.
