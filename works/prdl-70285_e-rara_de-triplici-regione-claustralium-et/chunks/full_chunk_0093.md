For whoever loves God with his whole heart does not admit displeasure against his faithful brother. But a monk ought to be established in so great an elevation of mind that, whatever a brother has committed against him, by considering the nature of that man’s soul he cannot be moved.

*Let the monk’s conduct toward those who wrong him be gentle.* Third, the monk’s conduct toward those who wrong him ought to be gentle; and this is done well only if regard is always had for their common future happiness and fellowship. To attain this gentleness of spirit, let our cenobite learn rather often to recall to mind the ways of life of the holy fathers, and faithfully consider with how much joy they received injuries. Let him strive above all, as far as possible, to imitate our Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who not only endured the injuries and insults of the impious with a most gentle heart, but also entreated the Father for his persecutors, that they might not perish. The holy fathers, imitating this example of the Savior, were accustomed to receive injuries patiently and gently for love of God, and, what is more, to love the very persons who wronged them. To these things let the cenobite recall to mind the degrees of gentleness, which are contained briefly in these verses: Fit. Comfort. Gentle, bear contrary things. Preserve. Restrain. Rest wholly within yourself. Correct prudently. Be merciful. Give wisely. Pray, putting yourself forward, desiring martyrdom. For if humility has possessed the mind of the cenobite, it makes him love injuries. The degrees of humility are contained in these words.

*The twelve degrees of humility.* The first of the degrees of the righteous fears the Almighty. The second puts reins upon the allurements of the flesh. The third submits himself reverently to the abbot.

The fourth, when insults are disregarded, patiently obeys.
The fifth discloses to the abbot the hidden things of the heart.
The sixth stands as vile and unworthy in his own judgment.
The seventh believes that he is lower than all.
The eighth does nothing that the rule of the cell does not allow.
The ninth does not speak while another does not ask.
The tenth banishes laughter and condemns loud guffawing.
The eleventh speaks a few words of reason.
The twelfth always looks at the ground with head bent.

*On the same matters, otherwise.*
Behold God, and do not do your own will; let another rule you.
Endure hardships; hide nothing in your heart, but seek also the lowest things.
Be refuse itself; do not wander idly; obstruct the tongue.
Let laughter be sparing, speech grave, gestures humble.

Note: Peripsema ought to be read and written, not peripsima, as the ignorant read in the Epistle to the Romans; and it means filings and the cleansing of all things.

*Let the monk’s conduct toward lay people be modest.* Fourth, the monk’s conduct toward lay people ought to be modest, so that neither familiarity with them is excessive nor withdrawal indiscreet. Hence Hugh says: Concerning people, as far as lies in you, beware of everything that can be no good example to them, just as it befits a faithful servant of God to promote the honor of his Lord in all things and to guard against dishonor to him. Nor should you be familiar with any woman, even a holy one. For if they are discreet, by this you will please them more; but if they have been troublesome, they are the more to be avoided. Do not be suspicious. Be ready to serve the sick in humble services; be very truthful in words; let your words be as an oath, and you will edify others. Love poverty. Be chaste in all things, and everywhere God-fearing.
