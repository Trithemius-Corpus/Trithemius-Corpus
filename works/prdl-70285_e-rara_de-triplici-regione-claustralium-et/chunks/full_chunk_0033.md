And first, indeed, he denies himself to himself who strives to live according to the teaching of eternal Wisdom and not according to his own impulses, and to obey Christ in all things when he counsels him to humility. Therefore let the Savior say to the cenobite: If you wish to deny yourself to yourself and follow me, turn away your eyes, lest they see harmful things; your ears, lest they hear vain things; and in place of the sweet things of this world take bitter things. Learn to despise the pleasures of your body, to love tranquillity and peace of heart, to receive all tribulations for love of me patiently and joyfully, and desire to be seen by all as base and contemptible. Consequently learn to break your own wills in all things, to mortify the desires of the flesh, to despise all earthly things, and, as far as can be done, to conform your life with all your strength to the way of life of your Savior. For he who says that he remains in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. Whoever has done these things has well denied himself.

Second, the denial of one’s own will is then rightly perfected when the advantages of others are put before one’s own. For the holy apostle says: Not seeking the things that are their own, but the things that are others’. For according to the judgment of Bernard, the soul is laid down for the brethren as often as their advantages are preferred to one’s own interests for the love of God.

Third, the resignation of one’s own will receives the fullness of perfection when the cenobite, contrite and wholly bound over to God, desires out of love to serve him freely, without regard for reward. And this most holy resignation of one’s own will makes a man very near to God and always rests in his love.

Just as this resignation of oneself takes its foundation from the second degree of humility, so also it necessarily requires an inward resignation, in every way, to the will of God in all things. For the Lord says in the Gospel: I have not come to do my own will, but that of the Father who sent me. Perfect resignation, therefore, is that by which the human will is wholly subjected to the divine will and conformed to it out of entire love. For thus the Lord himself taught us to pray: Thy will be done, as in heaven and on earth. And that saying of the psalm-writer: Into thy hands I commend my spirit. Never in this world is one lived more securely than if the human will is perfectly subjected to God.

Indeed, the perfect resignation of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his will to God, shows the straight way to the highest good without any intermediary. Here there is the greatest tranquillity of mind, the utmost security is in the heart, because the soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will no longer be able to fear any contrary thing of this world except sin alone. Whatever adversity should rush upon it, with the Lord’s help it has learned to overcome; whatever advantage it desires, it understands that it possesses in the Lord alone. It despises all adverse things for love of him; it has placed all good things in his love. For that devout man spoke prudently: Let go of all things, and you will find all things. Leave behind covetousness, and you will find true peace.

Finally, in order that this resignation of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it implores, so to speak, the support of a threefold aid.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance, so that a will once denied is never taken back. For that holy Bernard says to the contemners of this aid: Why do you again meddle with yourselves, you who once, by mature deliberation, handed over your will to us? For the monk who has once handed over his will to God into the hands of the abbot does not take it back without the crime of sacrilege.

The second thing required for perfect resignation of the will is that it never be relaxed. Hence the Lord says to the lukewarm soul in the Apocalypse: I have a few things against you, because you have left your first charity, that is, your fervor.
