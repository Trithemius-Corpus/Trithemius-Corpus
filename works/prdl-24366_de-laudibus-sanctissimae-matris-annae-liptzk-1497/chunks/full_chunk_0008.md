Therefore I warn you, learned men, distinguished in education and knowledge of letters, not to despise the devotion of the simple, not to condemn the worship of the most holy mother Anne as though it were new, but rather, as far as you can, to imitate it. For I have not been persuaded in vain to say these things. For there are among you, if I may say this without offense to good men, some swollen with the worst kind of pride, who despise all the devotional practices of the simple, scorn the miracles and examples of the saints as though they were ravings, and think that nothing holy should be admitted unless they prove it by the arguments of the philosophers. They reckon all revelations shown by God to devout people to be lies, or the dreams of women; they call the legends of the saints fables; and while they rashly proclaim their own learning, they shamelessly attack the works of the great God. Treatises of the holy fathers and of devout people, because they do not display Ciceronian eloquence, they cast aside as lacking learning; and they reject the words of heavenly instruction because of their simple style.

O fools and corrupters, why do you deceive yourselves? Why, though you are Christians, do you despise ecclesiastical simplicity? God’s grace is not known in eloquence, but is acquired in holy simplicity. At the last judgment of God, Quintilian eloquence will not be required of you, but a Christian life. God rewards not ornate speech but humble devotion. There the arguments of sophists will cease, where the secrets of hearts will be manifest. Then the simple and devout will appear in glory, and the learned proud in punishment. See, the unlearned and simple rise up and receive the kingdom of God in their devout simplicity, while you, with your doctrines, descend in pride and arrogance to hell. You believe the fame of your learning is enough for you, so that, after the example of the ancients, you may gain immortality of name among posterity. But be it so. Let what you seek come to pass: let your name and fame stand in the world and be proclaimed with praise. What will it profit you? Truly foolish consolation, if you are praised where you will not be, and are tormented where you will remain forever without end. Therefore I warn and counsel you: lay aside your haughtiness, drive away madness, cast off swelling pride, so that you may be able to escape the torments of hell. For God resists the proud; he looks not to learning but to humble devotion; he rewards not words but deeds, not knowledge but life, not doctrine but conscience. Rather, then, change your position, and do not despise the devotion of the simple, but imitate it. No one understands the gift of God except the one who receives it. What, then, you do not understand, with what rashness do you tear it apart? The gift of God is not enclosed under the reasoning of philosophy. Devotion is rare among the learned, because while they put confidence in the profundity of the doctrine they have acquired, they withdraw far from simple devotion.

For the grace of God is not well received when it is examined by human curiosity. While flavor is sought in speech, inward sweetness is lost. The Holy Spirit will despise an exalted dwelling. Therefore block rashness, restrain ill-spoken tongues, and do not speak falsehood against God; do not tear apart the devotion of the simple, lest it be counted to you as a grave scandal. Let no one think me, in saying these things, an enemy of the good arts; let no one believe me a slanderer of the learned, since I have always cultivated and loved letters, have always been accustomed to honor learned men, and have sometimes defended them even to the point of hatred and persecution by the unskilled. But those who seem learned in their own eyes should beware lest, by despising the devotion of the simple, they be thought to have to be called back from reverence for Saint Anne. If there will be any such men, I would believe no truly learned person to be among their number, since true learning should help devotion, not condemn it. Many very learned men also, still living today, have splendidly exercised their talents in verse and prose, dedicating them to Anne’s merits.
