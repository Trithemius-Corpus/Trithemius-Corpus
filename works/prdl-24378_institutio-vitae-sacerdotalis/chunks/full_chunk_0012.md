*On the Remedy against Lust*

*Chapter 3*

Physicians have been accustomed first to show the sick the seriousness of the disease by subtle discussion, and only then to apply the help of the remedy. For once the seriousness of the sickness is known, the bitterness of the potion causes less fear. This I am going to do with you. First, indeed, I disclosed to you, by many kinds of demonstration, the danger of cohabitation with women, and, as far as I could, I declared what is the end of this disease. It remains, therefore, now for us to consider the remedy. For the flesh always lusts against the spirit, and unless it is restrained by wholesome exercise, it casts both itself and him down into the depth of damnation.

Hence the poet gives good warning, saying: “If you take away idleness, the bows of Cupid have perished; scorned, his torches lie there without light.” Holy Scripture also declares that every idle man is among desires. Therefore he who wishes to extinguish the vice of lust must continually exercise himself in some holy work. For idleness is the enemy of the soul. Hence blessed [unclear] says: “See to it that the devil finds you occupied.” For the best remedy against evil thoughts is occupation in some holy work. Indeed, while the mind is delightfully intent upon some good exercise, it does not think of other things. No access will lie open to the devil if the mind has been occupied in some exercise.

Take the example in holy David: while occupied with matters of war he subdued lust; while idle, remaining at home, he committed adultery. When the hand is free from work, the mind grows weak in thought. If, therefore, you wish to overcome lust, flee idleness. But in what exercises you ought to spend your time, receive in a few words, if you please. In the morning you should accustom yourself to rise from sleep before dawn, and to devote some time to prayers, both canonical and voluntary. And when by devout prayer you have completed the appointed measure of your service, you will next devote yourself to sacred readings, until you understand that you have been called by duty to celebrate Mass.

After Mass one must again devote oneself to prayers, and apply oneself to sacred meditations most devoutly. Then one must work with the hands; the affairs of the house must be cared for, and whatever things must be arranged because of necessity should be disposed with the fear of God. The manual work of a priest, however, is to write; to bind books copied by himself or by others, or printed; and to apply himself to other honorable labors according to his condition or necessity. Finally, after food has been taken, praises must be rendered to God, who feeds us here on earth; and one must pray again that after this life he may feed us without end in heaven.

When these things have been duly completed, one must not go at once to chatter, but again devote oneself to sacred readings. Then one must work with the hands until Vespers, which should not be extended beyond the fourth hour in manual labor. But when the day is now declining and one must sleep, one should first have recourse to most devout prayers. When these have been completed, you should set in order the account of your actions and of the day, and, crying out that you are guilty before God for your negligent way of life, you should demand pardon and indulgence. For if you have judged yourself, you will be absolved by God. And unless you have constantly kept yourself in the fear of the Lord, the house of your conscience will soon be overthrown.

But if you have been overtaken in some fault, immediately have recourse to the lamentation of penance; and, if it can be done, choose such a confessor as both knowledge of the Scriptures proves to be suitable and manner of life commends. For he rarely opens the way of salvation in truth to those confessing, who lives with a reprobate manner of life. But if, at the enemy’s suggestion, you begin to feel temptations of the flesh in your mind, do not strengthen them by delay, but drive them out as quickly as possible through prayers and holy exercises. The serpent of lust is slippery, and unless it is crushed in the head it is not easily destroyed. While the adversary is still weak, he is easily overcome. And if you have once conquered yourself, do not think yourself secure, for the victory is not complete where the battle is waged daily. Therefore, as long as you can be subjected, do not believe that you have conquered. Do not trust in your own strength; do not claim the victory for yourself, but know that your hope consists in the aid of God alone. For no man is chaste by his own powers.
