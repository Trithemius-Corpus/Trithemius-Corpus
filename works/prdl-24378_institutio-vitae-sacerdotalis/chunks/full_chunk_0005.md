Nowhere among mortals is one’s life more secure than when danger is always feared. Hence it is written: Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil. Indeed, he who fears danger takes care; he who neglects it falls into it. The minister of Christ, the priest of God, must be a man, not a child, so that he may know and understand what a burden he has taken up, for the administration of which he will render account in the strict judgment of God. Therefore let him be prudent, sober, chaste, and quiet; let him be modest, kindly, gentle, so that he who is appointed instructor of an untaught people may suffer no contempt on account of his conduct. It belongs to a priest, when questioned, to answer concerning the law, constantly to rebuke the vices of his subjects by the words of holy preaching, and to fear no adversities for love of the truth. He who performs the priestly office among the people of God must be a man learned in the divine law, so that he may know how to bring forth in due season things new and old, lest among dumb dogs unable to bark he lose the dignity of his office. Now it is your part, O Nicholas, to rebuke the vices of others by diligent admonition, and to show yourself free from the vices of others, lest, if you are vicious and rebuke crimes, you render yourself worthy of condemnation. For no one can confidently censure in another what he remembers, with conscience as witness, that he himself has committed. Therefore he must be irreproachable who sits in judgment on the sins of others, for he who commits in himself what he rebukes in others does not correct, but provokes. But today many among priests are found like hypocrites, who are angry at brothers who offend in lighter matters, and do not wish anyone at all to be angry at themselves. Wretched condition of human frailty: a sinner condemns a sinner, and in the same crime delivers an unequal sentence. I would be lying if I said that it has not very often been an adulterer who rebuked his neighbor for fornication; a judge vicious in many things condemns a few vices in another, and, so that he may be able to conceal his own depravity, he makes public the offenses of all. Against him it is said by the Lord: Hypocrite, first cast out the beam which you carry in your own eye, and then you may see the speck in another’s. Why does the sacrilegious man censure the thief? Why does the wicked man rebuke the vicious? Entirely fruitless will be every correction which an equal foulness of life stains. Therefore, that you may lack the reproach of hypocrites, take care, as corrector of another’s heart, not to offend in a similar sin. Correction will never become useful among subjects while he who presides bows his neck to public sins. Indeed the right of correction is lost when the corrector’s conduct is defiled by the vices of his subjects. The authority of the priesthood grows cheap if public iniquity has corrupted the uprightness of conduct. Therefore let every correction take its beginning in its own author, and only then proceed safely into public. Let him whom his own conscience bites in a similar crime suspend for a time the sentence of reproof. The condition of believers is now for the most part corrupted, and as the people are, so too is the priest; all are devoted to greed, from the greatest to the least. Woe to the sinful nation, which always slips toward graver things, while it beholds its own judges guilty of an equal offense. Ecclesiastical authority has now come to such misery that we are ordered to embrace the words of priests, not their deeds. For the unhappy priests say concerning themselves, bearing witness to their own defilement: O Christian faithful, attend to the words of our instruction; beware of our examples. O words most worthy of everlasting confusion, which, as much as they ought to have instructed the people of God by teaching, so much do they scandalize them by perverse works. The unlearned and ignorant now come to the priesthood without discrimination of merit, and by their very wicked morals they unhappily kill the sheep of Christ. No holiness of life is now required in clerics to be ordained; no learning in letters is demanded; purity of conscience is not considered. It is enough among them if they can construe the current lot, if they know how to answer what part of speech “lord” is, if they understand yes and no. Woe to the bishops of our time, who, intent upon secular business, do not consider the ordinations of clerics, but entrust the care of examination to the unskilled; what a strict sentence they are going to undergo, who show themselves to be the cause of all evil in the Church. Indeed, since a priest of the faithful ought to be learned and holy, with what rashness do the unskilled come to the priesthood, who can instruct neither themselves nor others? For neither does a good life without doctrine suffice in a cleric, nor does doctrine without life instruct the people wholesomely.
