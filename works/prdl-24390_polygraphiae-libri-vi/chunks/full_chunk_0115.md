But one must take care that at one time, all at once, no more than one word be taken from one alphabet, lest the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, pass over some alphabet and confuse the order of the narration. By “day” understand the open narration, but by “night” the hidden, concealed one, which is not made too long but brief. The paradigms that follow in the text, concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen, pertain to both narrations, the hidden and the manifest. “As often as a leaf is moved by the wind,” and the rest: we advise that you count the secret narration in relation to the open one in such a way that both end together at the close of each period. But if anything remains over from the secret matter, let it be extended with such moderation that both narrations end at one point, lest confusion of the eclipse be produced. The other things that follow in the first lesson of the explanation of the Polygraphy are all implicitly referred to what we have said.

It should be noted, however, that under the order of the alphabets no word is empty, but each represents the letter set before it at the head. So that the reader may not lack perfect knowledge of this matter, let us explain by an example. Let us suppose the case that a wicked man seeks through you to be advanced with some friend of yours; but you, in order to cut off his importunate entreaties and yet not advance an unworthy man, decide to write more secretly to your friend through the man who is asking, so that he may not understand: “Beware of this man, because he is a thief, worthless, most wicked.” Go to the first alphabet; write the word that signifies C. In the second, “clemens,” which gives A; in the third, “discernens,” which represents V; in the fourth, “mundana,” signifying E. Now in the hidden narration you have: “Beware.” Proceed successively also through the other alphabets, and a discourse like this will emerge:

“Conditor clemens discernens omnibus sanctis suis in caelis, amen. Mansuetudo gloriosissimi principis a sanctis ubique reverenter rememoranda, optimis praeceptis nos abiectissimos dormientes hortatur praesentia propter venerationem directoris bonorum derelinquere, gloriam perpetuae quietis omni affectu [unclear] expectare. Eruditione igitur [unclear] derisores accipite, [unclear] ad felicissima tripudia archangelorum remuneratoris orthodoxorum valeatis ingredi felicissime decori. Refugite igitur a spurcissimis honoribus huius peregrinationis, cum sitis ad iucunditates benigni salvatoris saeculorum beatissimas sanctissime ingressuri.”

In this public narration written above there is contained in secret this hidden sentence, namely: “Beware of this man, because he is a thief and worthless, most wicked; but be silent, I pray.” One must proceed in the same order in all other cases whatsoever, and begin from the head as often as necessary, until you have enclosed the hidden narration of your mystery in the manifest one. But the words that are placed outside the alphabetical order designate no letter, nor do they serve the hidden narration; they have been inserted because of a lack of synonyms, so that the sequence of the Latin discourse might be completed. For we could not always have so many words of the same meaning, especially among pronouns, conjunctions, prepositions, and interjections, that we could have arranged an alphabet in place of the twenty-four roaming letters, as is clearly evident after the eighth alphabet, where we placed two words outside the order, lest we leave the discourse defective. For thus we put: “Cum omnibus”; and thus: “Cum universis.” Of these the writer may take the two he wishes, so that the Latin discourse may be completed. Let him do likewise in other cases, whenever he finds similar ones. We have adopted the double w because of our native speech, since in our German language we often need it. For whoever is ignorant of the Latin tongue may write his hidden secret in German under a Latin discourse without any difficulty.
