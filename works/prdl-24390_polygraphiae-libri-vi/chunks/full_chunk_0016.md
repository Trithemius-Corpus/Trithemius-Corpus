What did the wonderful invention of the nautical art profit the distinguished [artificer?] Bartholo under Pope Innocent VIII, which in a certain little book he promised for sale for one hundred thousand ducats, when neither the supreme pontiff himself, nor the Venetians, nor the Genoese, to whom he proposed that his device be bought, deigned even for a small sum to come to terms with him? For he promised that within thirty months, by his art, he could preserve a ship unharmed in the middle of the sea, repair it when broken, and recover without danger whatever had been cast into the deep. But no one cared. Nor does it escape me that many have perished, with Naso, through their own talent. It is therefore better to set a limit to knowledge than, without profit, to expose oneself to the hatred and detraction of many, for whom it is more familiar to despise any knowledge whatever than to have learned it.

*Here is the end of the preface, on the seventh day of April, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1508.*

*The First Book of the Polygraphia, addressed to Caesar Maximilian, by Johannes Trithemius, abbot of [Paeapolis?], formerly of Sponheim.*

Twenty-four are the letters, or characters, of the signs of the Latin language; when set in order before just as many significant words, they serve most abundantly in all respects, according to the wish of each person working in this art, for secrets. These paraphrases are arranged artificially in such a way that they make a wholly Latin discourse, if only one is taken from each order. And it should be noted that, by reason of our German speech, the twenty-fourth letter, the letter w, is taken at the end of the alphabet, since all the rest perform service for both languages. We have placed the co-signifying words with such industry in order that, whichever one you take from each line, once the total reckoning has been made, agreement will remain. Yet circumspection is needed, so that from any line of syntax you take no more than one, and no unit do injury to the binary. Provision must also be made that, as often as you approach the cabalistic operation, you first count the minutes of the night, each and every one, and join them in due proportion to the degrees of the following day. But if the minutes, by the secret institution, do not suffice to the end for the degrees of the day, the windy runner must by prudent counsel be led back to the arch-measurer [unclear].

Nor does it matter if by chance night falls back upon the head of the day, since a prudent operator in the art can withdraw by voluntary addition from the front by as much as he sees the night exceed the light of the day. And this is to be understood throughout the whole work of the Polygraphia concerning the individual chapters. Yet we say that this consideration must be assigned to the unit, by which the binary is certified by the known little sign of return, lest it happen to go astray. From the monad, according to what has been established, the binary will stand, and the monad is produced. Nothing is put here in vain, nothing alleged without cause or idly. Therefore we necessarily warn everyone who wishes to transcribe this work of our Polygraphia, either in whole or in part, that he first take diligent care not to depart in anything from our archetype, nor skip, neglect, or transpose any word whatsoever. Indeed, if he has omitted one letter or word in substantial matters, if he has changed a word contrary to its nature, he has made the course of the whole work useless. Therefore let him either transcribe it exactly, or not transcribe it.
