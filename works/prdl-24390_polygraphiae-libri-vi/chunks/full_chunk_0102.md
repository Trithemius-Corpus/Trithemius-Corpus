The Normans, however, together with the Asiatic Dacians, having once come forth from the Scandinavian islands, which are now called Gothic, roamed through Gaul for more than forty years and devastated it most grievously. In order to provide for their secret affairs, as Bede the English monk bears witness, they devised from the letters of the Greeks a new and elegant method of writing of this kind. For they adapted the numbers of the Greeks up to twenty-four, comprised in ten letters, to Latin characters, and prudently hid our letters under a foreign form, namely in the way in which we use numbers in place of letters: that is, when for a we put 1, for b 2, for c 3, for d 4, and so on successively to the end of the alphabet. It was indeed a fine and sufficiently safe invention, which neither a Greek nor a Latin will easily understand. For the whole alphabet is not found in it, but only nine letters and one numerical sign, namely q̄, which among them signifies six; and in this manner of writing it stands for f, the sixth letter of the Latins. Furthermore, paying regard to our German tongue in which we were born, we have placed w, double, at the end as the twenty-fourth letter of our nation, which we have formed according to the Greek number thus: kδ. They represent the Latin letters, and they will be readable neither by a Greek nor by a Latin who has not first acquired knowledge of this device. This method of writing can also be varied in many ways, when the letters are changed by transposition of the number. This can be done in Greek letters in full, according to the chosen will of each industrious writer. Nor will the manner of forming these characters be very difficult, especially for one who has become somewhat practiced. The characters written above also suit the German language sufficiently well, since with them we can denote everything that occurs to us, in whatever speech we may be skilled. Whenever iota is placed by itself, so that it may be known to denote the letter k, the tenth of the alphabet, thus ĩ, it is marked above.

*On the Alphabet and Method of Writing of the Ancient Franks.*

The opinion of very many holds that the Germans once had letters from the Greeks, and they steadfastly affirm that they used the Greek language especially in their sacred rites. For they say that a great correspondence exists between the Greek language and our own, although the long interval of time has utterly extinguished that agreement. That most learned man, once my teacher, Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, most reverend bishop of the church of Worms, had collected several thousand Greek words which signify the same thing in both languages, Greek and German. And Caesar testifies in the Commentaries that the ancient Druids, priests of the sacred rites in Gaul, used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. But when the Romans were laying waste the world, they at length violently imposed the use of their letters, together with their dominion, upon many nations.
