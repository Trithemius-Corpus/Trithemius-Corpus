With these signs or characters we once saw an entire Latin psalter, beautifully written, in the library of the greater church of Strasbourg. Some ignorant judge of such matters had put this title on the outside: “A Psalter in the Armenian language.” But he was mistaken, since it should rather have been written thus: “A Latin psalter written in Tullian characters.” For each character represents one whole Latin word. Nor is my judgment deceived, since I possess the very dictionary of Cicero, corrected by Saint Cyprian, written complete and uncorrupted in an ancient volume, which I have seen nowhere else. So great, indeed, is the abundance of signs or characters, with the words assigned to them, that they are entirely sufficient for writing in the Latin tongue whatever you wish.

*Mystery.* Faramundus, the forty-third from Marcomer, king of the Franks, when in the year of the Lord’s nativity 424 he entered the borders of Gaul [unclear] and powerfully, etc.

*Explanation.* The kings of the Franks, from the time when, in the year 430 before the birth of Christ, they left the Scythian borders with their people and settled at the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is, waged continual war with the Gauls for almost nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject to themselves, as we have written in the first volume of our Annals. Of these kings Faramundus, the aforementioned king, in order to send messengers safely through the kingdom, devised new letters proper to himself, which no one could know without his voluntary instruction. Nor did he use one alphabet only, nor a few, but many new alphabets.

*Mystery.* Clodius Crinitus, son of Faramundus and afterward king of the Franks, who subjected all Gaul to himself after the Romans, whom he found there, had been destroyed, used his father’s inventions in secret writings.

*Explanation.* This king fashioned alphabets of his own, new ones, and handed one to each of his dukes, while he alone retained with himself the knowledge of them all.

*Mystery.* Charles, surnamed the Great, the sixty-third king of the Franks, who was the first of the Franks to wear the Roman diadem of empire, etc.

*Explanation.* This man, the elder son of King Pippin, is read to have been born in Brabant, in the county of Namur, bordering on Liege, although someone asserts that he was born in the village of Ingelheim not far from Mainz. He too discovered various modes of writing, and, with Nanno the grammarian supplying assistance, devised different alphabets, by which he communicated secrets to the individual prefects of the kingdom in a singular fashion.

*Mystery.* The Northmen, who long ago came out from Gothia and its borders, when they were devastating Gaul with a cruel invasion, having been summoned from Greece, prepared a safe hiding place for [unclear] with owls for secrets, etc.

*Explanation.* The Northmen, ravaging Gaul, as Bede the monk in England bears witness, in order that they might safely write their secrets among themselves, set Greek numerical letters under the Latin letters, from a, the first, up to the twenty-fourth point, such as w, or gu, according to the custom of the Gauls. This mode of writing is altogether very beautiful: in it all the letters are Greek, and according to numerical order they designate Latin letters in Pelasgian figures, as a, 1; b, 2; c, 3; d, 4; e, 5; f, 6; g, 7; and so successively up to w, which is 24. Neither a Greek nor a Latin will understand this writing, unless he has first learned the method by diligence and art.

*Mystery.* Our Bede, a monk in England, ignorant of the deeds of the Northmen, described many old inventions similar to these, by which they in turn made a clear mystery obscure. He himself [wrote] *Artrology*, etc.

*Explanation.* This Bede, the monk, in his little book on the inventions of the ancients, reviewed many and various noteworthy methods of writing secretly, by which he safely announced his secrets to friends, and by which those present spoke without voice. He was the first of all to commit artrology and dactylology to letters; by their use one person may very safely reveal to another whatever secret he wishes, which none of those standing by will notice, and much less understand. Bede himself taught that this could be done in many of these and other ways. Since we have written nothing about it in the *Polygraphia*, we rightly decline to say more of it here too. We long ago assigned this very matter, in its own place, to the *Steganographia*, which we do not yet intend to publish publicly. And who could set forth all the methods of secret writing used by the ancients? For some, in the service of the art, had familiar [spirits] set over owls, the Philophoti; others had the Misophoti; others summoned both into practice by art. Again, others, having been made known, transposed the letters of the common alphabet; others, under the guise of barbarians, fashioned their own characters for themselves. Again [unclear].
