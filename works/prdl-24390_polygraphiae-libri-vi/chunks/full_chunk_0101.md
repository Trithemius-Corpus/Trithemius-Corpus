They also began to sink more deeply into the pleasures of the flesh, to take beautiful wives, and, when many children had been begotten from them, they notably fell away from natural simplicity. They invented games and songs, began to play the lyre, and whatever they declare to pertain to the influence of Venus they devised rather eagerly, following too much the impulse of the flesh.

They named the third intelligence, that of Jupiter, Zachariel; taking up dominion over the world after Anael, it made men restless with desire for ruling, to engage in hunts, to make tents, and to adorn them with various coverings. While he held sway there came about, in accordance with the quality of Jupiter, who deprived his father of the kingdom, a great division between the good and the evil: the former calling upon God, the latter given over to the flesh and the world. Under that rule Adam, the first man, died, leaving to all posterity the testament of dying once.

The fourth mover, or angel of Mercury, Raphael, after Zachariel, pouring influence upon worldly affairs, began in the year of the world 1055 at the end of February, and lasted until the year 1410; under him the use of letters is said first to have been invented. At that time, however, the letters were, as they report, different from those which the world has today, devised in the form and likeness of trees, plants, and animals; yet afterward, with time, they were changed in many ways, and took on an easier manner of forming and a more cultivated ornament. For the fictions of the poets said that Mercury was the presiding deity of all literature, promising that great secrets of things lay hidden beneath fables.

After this, when the human race had multiplied and had spread everywhere over all the earth, the unity of letters did not long remain; rather, the division of bodies and minds far from one another found diverse and various modes of writing, concerning which there is a long narrative for us. Plato the philosopher, in the Phaedrus, relates that a certain philosopher named Theuth first invented letters and the use of writing, together with the reckoning of weights, numbers, and the game of dice, and taught Thamus, king of the Egyptians. But concerning the other ancient men who are said to have handed down special letters to almost each nation and people, letters which remain in use among them down to the present day, it is not my purpose on this occasion to set down or indicate the narrative.

For all our teachers know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said first to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: α, β, γ, δ, ε, η, ι, λ, μ, ν, ο, π, ρ, σ, τ, ϖ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is reported to have added four: ξ, θ, φ, χ. And Pythagoras afterward added the letter v, so that there should be twenty-one. ζ, κ, and ω were subsequently invented by others. Jordanes, bishop of the Goths, gave letters proper to that people. When the unity of language had been confounded, the simplicity of characters too was varied in many ways.

Hence it came about that the use of letters, in the course of time, usurped such license for itself that not only individual peoples, but also individual men, fashioning new characters for letters at their own discretion, prepared hiding places for their mysteries. Yet every writing in an unusual character will not be free from suspicion among those who see it. Notwithstanding this, many kings, tyrants, and princes of old once devised for themselves a familiar mode of writing, separate from the common one, caring little for the suspicion of others, provided only that what they wanted to remain secret was not perceived. Moved by imitation of them, and desiring to bring this work of Polygraphy, as far as can be done, to a perfect conclusion, in this sixth book, which is also the last, I have resolved to call back from the dead certain characters of the ancients, which have long since passed out of mortal use, lest they perish altogether. And first indeed we shall set down the ancient mode of writing of the Northmen.
