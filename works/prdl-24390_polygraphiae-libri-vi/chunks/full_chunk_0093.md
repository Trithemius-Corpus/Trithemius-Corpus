*Of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James the Apostle [unclear], Book Five of the Polygraphia: beginning of the chapter.*

Those things which, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have set forth, as best we could, in the four preceding books for the completion of our Polygraphia, reasonably require that in the fifth book we briefly treat the remaining precepts and methods of this instruction that are left over. We shall therefore give in it a sufficiently concise method by metathesis, that is, by the transposition of letters, by which a friend may write securely, safely, and most secretly to a friend whatever he wishes. We read that Caesar Augustus used this manner of writing in secret matters. This method of sending messages, although in many ways secure, is nevertheless always regarded as suspect by those who look upon it, because as soon as a transposition of letters is seen, anyone curious immediately suspects that something lies hidden beneath them. But the methods of this invention are almost infinite; from them we have marked out the following for the reader as a necessary introduction, reserving the rest to be searched out by his own ingenuity. First, therefore, we have put before him the standard table of this practice, to which we have added a second, reversed one. In these two tables the root and foundation of the aforementioned transposition are wholly laid down, upon which the structure of the whole edifice is placed. For once these two tables have been properly known and understood, no transposition of letters will be able to escape one who works diligently. To these we have joined the order of twenty-four alphabets. The use of these, as one wishes, consists in each individually, or, if you prefer, even in all of them. If you have secret dealings with many people, assign individual alphabets to each of your confidants, while you commit to memory what you have given to each one. Let the first alphabet be entrusted to the first person, the second to the second, the third to the third, and so on with the rest. But if, because of a very great number of affairs or of associates, the alphabets which we have marked out should not suffice for all things, or if some of them seem too open and obvious, we shall be able to introduce various new transpositions, whose number may be very great, while the method of the secret always remains perpetually hidden. Let us give an example of this matter: let B be a, f be b, l be c, p be d, t be e, z be f, c be g, m be h; and so let it be done consequently through the whole customary alphabet, so that between any chosen letter three lie idle and transposed. Afterwards four, then five, more or fewer, yet in such a way that the whole alphabet, as though revolved in a circle, comes together, and no letter at all is omitted, but each one is placed in its own order in the same sequence for another. In whatever way you write, that very method must be firmly entrusted either to writing or to memory, lest, having slipped through forgetfulness, you can no longer read what you yourself had written. For the transposition of letters can be varied in so many ways that, according to the estimate of human understanding, which, enclosed by temporal operation in a mortal body, does not properly grasp infinities, there will arise for a mortal an innumerable number. Hence whoever wishes to have more methods of writing by metathesis, apart from our instruction, if he is even a little ingenious and diligent, will easily achieve the effect in his work. For the mind of man can do very great things if it is aided by study; but art that despises the smallest things never reaches the greater. I know that there will be very many who will mock these studies of ours as vain and childish; to them we give this answer: that we shall be entirely content provided they please those at whose request they were composed by us. The straight table follows.
