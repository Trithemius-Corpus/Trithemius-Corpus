...the completion through the quaternary in the twice-perfect binary, establishing the root, grants us an infinite number under the minutiae of things and of the paraphrases of polygraphy. For even if among us all our things are terminable, nevertheless many things very often occur which, because of the slowness of our understanding, seem infinite to us. Who will be able to count the drops of the sea? And who will penetrate the inscrutable heart of man? Just as a finite and circumscribable number can be led by progression into infinity, so the instruction of this art is enlarged immeasurably by varied transposition. Whatever is seen anywhere in the whole world is in all respects conformed to our instruction; nor is there anything known to us that is free from mystery, if the method is understood from the master. But we, persuaded by reason, remain under the service of the binary, and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor is occasion given us to raise our undertaking to weightier matters; if it is offered to us as acceptable, we shall not refuse to make them manifest to those who desire it. For it is written: Where there is no hearing, never pour out speech. Therefore, adding the second book of Polygraphy to the first, we keep its form and method, placing three hundred and eight paraphrases in the light of day by simple progression, as an honorable memorial to the most chaste Mother of God. If a suitable time had been at hand, we could have given things both more elegant and, with sufficient broad spreading, more extensive. But studious posterity will easily provide both: that it may add polish to the material, and sufficient progress to the form. The first inventor of any art passes on greater things to posterity, because, since the beginnings of an art are hard and unfamiliar, no subtle invention is found perfected from its origin.
