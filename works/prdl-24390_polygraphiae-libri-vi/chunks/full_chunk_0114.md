Others used the service of visible letters, writing with red lead or with common ink; but others formed invisible characters at their own discretion and command. The methods of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many and altogether innumerable, and to make mention of them all is neither our wish nor within our power. But of invisible characters there are three kinds more commonly used among mortals today. The first is called dermatic, by which writing is made on the back of messengers, visible only with liquids; no one can penetrate it who does not know the secret of the art. The second is called hyphasmatic, by which something is written on cloth with certain substances; when dry it cannot be seen at all until, moistened in cold water, it is perceived. The third kind is called alleotic; it is manifold and varied, both in form and in material, and has almost innumerable methods of writing secretly. But because none of these things serves our present purpose on this occasion, we must therefore hasten to the matters that reason requires. There is also another kind, dactylethron, not indeed of writing, but by signs, by which in most subtle ways one can communicate to another the conception of one’s mind. This too is varied by so many distinctions that there is no one in the world who could enumerate all the forms of so great a mystery. By the principal method, and by many others deriving from it, the practitioner, sitting at table or in any other place whatever, will be able safely, most securely, and without anyone’s suspicion, to communicate whatever you wish to one who knows the art. Finally, how you may by letters declare the secret of your mind to an absent person, even without the knowledge of the very man who performs the office of messenger, remains assigned to the discoveries of steganography. For indeed, since kings and princes encounter many affairs of which they do not at times trust that knowledge can be given with sufficient safety by ordinary letters to their deputies far away, it will be necessary for each of them to have certain special methods of writing and sending messages, by which they may most safely make known their secrets to friends, especially in these times, in which, as license to sin prevails, no safe trust is found in the world.

Now the messengers of princes are seized everywhere by savage robbers, despoiled, beaten, and very often killed; nor do wicked plunderers spare even the letters of kings, sealed and closed with rings. Therefore it would be necessary for your commonwealth, O most glorious Caesar Maximilian, that you put on the skin of Hercules and brandish the club in your hands, and purge the world for yourself, by God, of these savage monsters. He who disturbs the peace of the kingdom shows himself to be the enemy of the king. These are the robbers who beset the roads of travelers; they seize what belongs to others. The earth is full of them today, and nowhere is the road safe for those who walk.

*Brief Explanation of Those Things Which Seem Rather Obscure in the First Book of the Polygraphy*

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphy, nevertheless, lest its composition perhaps seem profound to the simpler, we shall briefly explain the things that are thought more difficult. We have distributed the words of the Latin language in order, more than nine thousand of them, under almost four hundred alphabets, in such a serial way that if from each alphabet only one after another has been taken from the beginning to the end, they will cohere with one another and will make a fitting Latin sentence. For it will be a suitable method of writing most secretly whatever we wish, since each word represents the letter placed before it at the head. And let it not trouble you, reader, that the multitude of words in the open contains a brief sense in secret, since there lies hidden in this childish arrangement of ours another, higher method of writing whatever you wish more broadly and much more secretly, which public reason has forbidden us to disclose to all. But whoever is endowed with intelligence and applies the necessary powers and resources to the inquiry will find subtler things among the hidden teachings in these matters, and will understand things wonderfully unknown before and plainly more wonderful. Nevertheless, this suitable method of writing will suffice for all things, since you can repeat it as often as you wish, and by frequent repetition the narrative is varied.
