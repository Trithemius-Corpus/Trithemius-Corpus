*Six Books of Polygraphy, by Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James of Würzburg, formerly of Sponheim, addressed to Emperor Maximilian.*

With grace and privilege of L. D.

Johannes Trithemius.

*To the divine Emperor Maximilian: the letter of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James of Würzburg, on the Polygraphy.*

I was wavering in mind, most unconquered Emperor Maximilian, and for some little while I doubted within myself whether I would be doing something worth the effort if I dedicated the volume of my Polygraphy to your imperial majesty. For among the great you are the greatest, among the more powerful the most powerful, and among all the wise by far the wisest; for that reason nothing should be inscribed to you unless it is great, nothing dedicated unless it has been elaborated with the highest learning. But I, having attained no, or little, literary learning, hesitated, not without reason, whether I ought to present my trifles to so great a majesty, since I am neither fit by talent to compose anything, nor adorned with the necessary charm of expression. For I know, and cannot deny, how scanty is my equipment, how untrained my talent, and how I have no vein of eloquence in speaking; for which reason, after due consideration, I paused for some time and feared to appear before your glorious majesty as an inept writer. For since after God you are by right lord of the world, it is no wonder if I, an unlearned man and the least of monks, fear the judgment of so great a majesty, and dread to offer those writings of mine which I know are not worthy of your hands. And while I was turning these matters over in my mind, a messenger arrived with letters sent by your highness, in which you summoned me to Cologne, the necessary expenses for the journey having already been sent through another. But when I had read again the letters of your serenity, confidence at once overcame shame, and consideration of your inborn gentleness drove fear away. For I know your disposition to be most mild: since you are the most modest of all, and are accustomed both to grow gentle toward the humble and to resist the proud. You do not know how to be vainly puffed up, nor, after the manner of tyrants, to despise the humble who are devoted to your highness; for just as on earth you have no way to become greater, so no envy will penetrate your mind. You are the greatest; you have also accomplished many and great things, no less bravely in peace than in war, by which you have both obtained an eternal name and are celebrated, with the reverence of highest honor, by the affection and favor of all your subjects. These are the riches of princes: the love and favor of the people, and doing justice to each person according to the commandment of the love of God. Since, therefore, all bear your name to the stars with praises, and all proclaim you most mild and most humane, there was no reason thereafter for me to suspect that you would be especially severe toward me, particularly when by a letter you deign to entice me to write.
