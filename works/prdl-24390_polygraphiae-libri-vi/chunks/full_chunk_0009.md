After this he returned to France and repaid us evil for good, and shamefully violated the bond of Christian friendship. For when he was asked by Lord Germain de Ganay, a mutual friend and later bishop of Orléans, what he had found in my possession and seen at Sponheim, he wrote back falsehoods instead of truths and lies instead of favors, condemning and rashly judging things that he was quite unable to comprehend with his understanding. For since, through envy and falsehood, he believed that the palm of learning would be conferred upon him, he wrote back to the aforementioned most learned man a letter full of falsity, lies, wrongs, and many insults; in it, making mention of steganography, which he had not understood, he proclaims me, falsely, mendaciously, and with too injurious a rash presumption, to be devoted to depraved arts, a magician and necromancer. To his most mendacious wrongs and blasphemies, God having mercy, I shall reply briefly, so that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bouillus is impious, cruel, rash, and in this matter a liar. I steadfastly affirm, I truly say, and I confidently swear upon my soul that I have never had any commerce with demons, nor with depraved or pernicious, magical or necromantic arts; but that each and every thing that I have either written or promised that I would write is pure, sound, natural, and in no respect at all contrary to the Christian faith. Hence I do not fear to submit my promises for examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as there shall be need; I in no way intend to resist their determination. There was a reasonable cause why I inserted this history of Bouillus’s rashness into this preface of mine: that all may understand that it was done with mature deliberation that the work of steganography has thus far remained buried in darkness, not because I fear Bouillus’s rash judgments, but lest I seem to give cause to more men of the same sort to suspect similar things about me. Indeed, as far as I am concerned, I would rather appease my rivals by silence than irritate them with letters or provoke them to madness by writings. For I judged it better to condemn all marvelously discovered things to perpetual silence than to incur, even by the false estimation of men, the mark of magical or necromantic pernicious superstition. Nevertheless, at the urging of friends, so that our rivals, together with Bouillus, may understand how rash and fabricated an injury he has done us, while steganography remains within its own limits, we consent to bring into the light the work placed below, which we call the Polygraphia, divided into six books. Let those who wish read it, and let friends read this volume without resentment; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves be judges, without envy, whether they are natural or superstitious.
