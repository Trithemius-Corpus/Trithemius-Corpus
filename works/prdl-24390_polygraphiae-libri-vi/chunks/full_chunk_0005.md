*Preface of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James of Würzburg, in the Polygraphia, to Caesar Maximilian.*

We have read that very many wise men of the ancients, philosophers, kings, and princes, once devised various and manifold methods by which they might safely entrust to messengers sent to more remote places the secrets of counsel, and whatever mystery of secrecy should occur to be confided; so that they might be fit in carrying them and safe from every assault of the wicked, lest either the simplicity of the cabalists should lay bare the mystery, or the innate curiosity of the bacucei should bring harm to apostolic affairs.

Great among these, Augustus Caesar of the Romans, desiring in difficult matters to use the service of messengers in a darkened way, changed the breathings of the conceived voice from clear things into obscure ones; their barbarousness, resisting his wishes, he made, by the dance of metathesis and by a beautiful invention, to agree at his nod. And that receptive and, as it were, perpetual order, nevertheless subject to will and to the account of time, although it preserves the messengers safe on the road from the assault of the bacucei, yet leaves them anxious for their own affair.

Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the chief mathematician, took a white bird suited to his instructions, bare at head and feet, and fastened it around to a staff cut into the form of a tetragon; and, the attendants having been arranged in due proportion, he entrusted the work to the winged instrument, until the image of the white bird appeared changed into a magpie. When this had been duly performed, he loosened what had been fastened with his own hand; and when he had allowed it to fly away, there suddenly occurred among the familiars of the inner chambers a great separation of appearances, and soon there arose the previously too-wandering monster of a twisted monastic image; at the sight of it no one was able to think what it was. Nor did the disagreement of the bubones come to rest before the image of the master had skillfully bound the magpie again with a similar tetragon.

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator, using talent and art, changed the small details of phrase into signs, and set the careful working, or character, of his will in place of the customary phrase. Indeed he used such abundance in this very novelty that his invention could suffice for almost all things in the world. To these heralds of mysteries, moreover, the institution of the art gave a marvelous agility, so that, beyond the nature of all things, they use solid steps of the feet, and not by small points or moments, but by whole degrees they observe the good will of the master. Their service a king can always use very safely, although not without great labor and the manifest suspicion of the bucachei.
