*II. The second precaution* instructs you, as has been said, not to take from any alphabet more than one word at a time in one narrative of public Latin discourse; otherwise you would confuse the open meaning and would entirely draw away from the understanding of the mystery the friend to whom you had written.

*III. Third,* one must take the utmost care not to skip through negligence any alphabet from which you do not take one word suited to the secret. Likewise one must provide that, whenever one or more words occur outside the series of the alphabet, you take the whole upper line, or the lower one, not both.

*IV. Fourth,* care must be taken that whenever the speech of the open discourse has been brought to a complete sentence, the next sentence should take its beginning from a larger, thickened letter, according to the form of the preceding example, in which, according to the sense of the Latin speech, we have taken large letters at the beginning and small letters in the rest, in the common manner. When the secret of your mind is lengthy, so that it exceeds the narrative of the open discourse, if much remains you should begin again from the beginning; if little remains, let the discourse be brought back at will from any place. For no one even slightly skilled in this art will be unable to know where you have begun again. Nor is there need to fear the harmony of the discourse, since the manifest narrative will always be varied with the hidden one; as often as one begins from the beginning, a new appearance of the speech will follow. The continuation can also be made from the end of the first book through the second.

*V. Fifth,* we advise that whenever you wish to write on a difficult matter by this paper, prepare for yourself a small booklet in which you write your secret mystery in manifest speech. Whoever sees or reads this booklet will think that no secret lies hidden, nor, even if he has suspicion, will he be able to discover any of the things that you write.

*VI. Sixth,* it should be noted: if, throughout the whole *Polygraphia*, you transpose the order of the words in each alphabet by changing it through metathesis, and write two volumes according to it, one for yourself and one for any friend, you will write between yourselves with the greatest security whatever you wish, and there lives no one on earth who can penetrate your secrets. This transposition, however, can be made in infinite ways.

*VII. Seventh,* we warn you beforehand that one must write polygraphically in one way to a man ignorant of Latin speech, and in another way to one skilled in it. For the former, all words must be written whole, without abbreviation, distinctly, completely, beautifully, and entirely legibly, without partition, without division, and without confusion, lest, being deceived, he think one to be two, or suppose two to be one. The things that are set down must be attended to.

*VIII. Eighth,* one must provide that the person to whom you write mystical things by this art should have a book exactly like yours in every respect; otherwise you would write vainly and fruitlessly to one who does not understand.

*IX. Ninth,* if you wish this art to remain forever secret and hidden, rewrite the *Polygraphia* anew, but do not set the letters of the alphabet before the words, and no one will easily suspect the method of the secret. But so that no difficulty may occur for the writer and the reader of the mystery, let the letters of the alphabet be written on a small sheet in the order corresponding to the words, and let a movable [unclear] be fitted to their headings. The letters can also be engraved on a copper plate, so that they may be moved from one order to another.

*X. Tenth,* it must be known that although we do not use the Pythagorean y in German and Latin words, nevertheless we have admitted it on account of the Greek language, so that we may have a doubled twelvefold set. Likewise we have adopted the doubled w as the twenty-fourth letter, which is altogether necessary for us in the German language.

*XI. Eleventh,* let the person who receives letters written from the *Polygraphia* to be read know that understanding is needed in the reading. And therefore, whenever he receives a paper or booklet written polygraphically, let him go to a secret place and, with the [book?] of the *Polygraphia* opened, consider the written words, and very diligently note with chalk on the table, or with ink on paper, what letter each one represents. And when he has gone through the whole letter in that way, let him compose the letters into words, and he will easily understand what has lain hidden in secret.
