A most powerful king of the Franks in Germany, when, in the four hundred and twenty-fourth year of the Lord’s nativity, he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, in order that everything might be done safely, bound foreign minutiae to his secrets.

Clodius, also called Crinitus, the elder son of Pharamund, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks, and after he had violently subjected all Gaul to his rule, ordered all the Gauls, as conquered slaves, to be shorn and shaved as to the beard; but the Franks, the victorious lords of the Gauls, he commanded to let the hair and beard grow. Hence he was called Crinitus. In imitation of his father, he wrapped the mysteries of his counsel in signs of his own and foreign ones.

Charles the Great, surnamed [unclear], the sixtieth king of the Franks after Marcomer, who first of the Franks ruled over the Romans, with Nanno the grammarian as his teacher, desiring to adorn the German language, which was his native tongue, with grammatical rules after the pattern of Latin, began an arduous work, which nevertheless, being entangled in many affairs and overtaken by death, he did not complete. He bound [bubones] with various, numerous minutiae; greater than him Brabant, or Namur in Germany, had not produced. To the individual prefects of the kingdom he assigned individual [bubones] by art.

The Northmen, having gone out from the borders of Gothia, when once they were cruelly devastating Gaul, summoned [bubones] from Greece and prepared a safe hiding-place for their secrets, which today is known to few. Our Bede, a monk in England, not ignorant of the deed of the Northmen, described many inventions of the ancients similar to these, by which they made bright the path that was dark with mysteries. He first commended artrology to the memory of posterity; he invented dactylology. There were those who distinguished minutiae by visible accidents, by whose prudence night was turned into day; without the service of [bubones], light shone in darkness. But concerning these matters of our steganography, when the illustrious count palatine, prince elector, was once solicitous, he received from us a manifest proof of a tested document.

Others, multiplying minutiae by regulated division, after the manner of barbarians, overturned the figures of nature; in darkness, with the shadow of things coming into being keeping the secret dances hidden, where the secrecy of counsel required it, they supplied a cabala to the absent through [diapyrata]. In these things there is great labor and a long exercise of the mind over many years, in which no one suddenly becomes skilled for use. Necessity bears all things. There were also those whom the heavenly harmony taught to compose foreign sounds; for those who understood there was a twofold use of these, while one suspends the sense, the other [raises it?] into the ethereal mind. God can do all things. Many tried to seek light in shadow without light; nevertheless they could not, because the fire lies buried in ashes.
