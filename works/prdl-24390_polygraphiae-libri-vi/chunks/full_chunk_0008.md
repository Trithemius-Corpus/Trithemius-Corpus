At the urging of the aforementioned prince and lord elector Philip, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, I, Johannes Trithemius, now abbot of the monastery of Saint James at Würzburg, but then of Saint Martin the bishop at Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, began to write eight books of mysteries in steganography. But while I was already continuing my night-work on the third book of the same work, an incident occurred, which I shall relate briefly if I can.

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 1499, in the second Roman indiction, when Arnold Bostius, a Carmelite of the town of Ghent, asked me by letter what new thing I was putting forth, or with what studies I was occupied, I wrote back to him as to a friend, through merchants, on the day after Palm Sunday: that I had under the hammer a new and wonderful work of steganography; and I set out in order what was contained in each of the books. But before my letter had reached Ghent, Bostius passed from this life to Christ during the days of Easter. The prior of that place received the letter, opened it, and read it; and, astonished, as I think, by the novelty of the promises, at the request of many others he shared it to be read and copied out. Hence it came about that this very letter of mine, published in a short time through almost all Germany and Gaul, and repeatedly copied out by many people, turned many very learned men also to astonishment and wonder, to such a degree that they hesitated over what they ought, with truth, to think about me. Among these there were some who steadfastly asserted that the promises I had made in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, and had been shamelessly devised by me for the sake of empty glory. Others, however, said: “This abbot Trithemius promises great wonders; if he can perform them, he can do so only by the ministry of demons, since they seem far to exceed the limits of nature. But if he cannot, who will doubt that he is a liar and deservedly to be rejected by all good and learned men?” But let both be far from me, since I promised natural things. Of all those who rashly judged matters unknown to them, the most rash, and at last the most unjust appraiser of things, came forward in public: Charles de Bovelles, a Picard by nation. When, after travelling through the upper parts of Alsace, he had come to me for hospitality at Sponheim, intending to return to Gaul through Trier, I treated him with such courtesy as I could, and showed him all the kindnesses of hospitality, so long as he was with me, with a cheerful face. And since, as is usual among friends, especially those devoted to the study of letters, I showed all my things to my most welcome guest to be seen, among the others the aforementioned work of steganography, not yet completed at that time, also presented itself. Bovelles saw it and read it cursorily, his mind occupied elsewhere; he said he admired the invention, and praised it, but did not take care to understand the manner in which it was to be understood. Hence, when he sought the key of understanding, he did not deserve to hear or perceive anything of what was contained in that night-work of ours.
