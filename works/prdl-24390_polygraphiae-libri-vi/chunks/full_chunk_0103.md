Thereafter, waging war almost continuously with the Romans and Gauls for nine hundred and fifty years, as Hunibald is the authority, they finally brought almost all Europe under their empire or kingdom. Yet they did not abolish the letters of the Romans once they had received them, but rather, mingled among the Germans and Gauls, they learned their language, and preserved their letters and form of writing. Wasthald, a writer of the histories of this people, wrote in his native tongue books of the deeds of kings, dukes, and princes over almost seven hundred and fifty-eight years, with the following characters appended, as Hunibald says; he mentions them in his volume, which he published in eighteen books on the origin of the Franks.

Unless the fault of the scribe has deceived me, I have represented Wasthald’s alphabet suitably, as I think. In it some characters differ entirely from the Greek, while some also seem partly to resemble them. Yet I do not know what to judge: whoever their inventor was, although he did not keep the form of the Greek letters, he nevertheless observed the order of the letters. The manuscript, crumbling from excessive age, could scarcely be read; hence I fear that I have rather been deceived by some interpolator than found Hunibald’s true and certain meaning.

But let us pass on to what remains, since if Hunibald’s statement is true, it will have been well written by us; but if it is someone’s fiction, it will neither harm us nor do injury to the reader. There follows another fictitious alphabet found in Hunibald’s manuscript, to which this was inscribed:

*The Alphabet of the Franks, which Doracus invented.*

A Π F M ρ ζ q T φ
Π b D h P n C r Δ w

Τ d E k A p η t X x

Whoever wishes to use the characters written above should know that they must be written below the lines in the manner of the Greeks, not above as the Latins do. Likewise it must be observed that, of double letters, the first are written at the beginning and in the middle of words, but the second always at the end.

*From the grammars of Otfrid, monk of Wissembourg.*

We read that Charles, king of the Franks, surnamed the Great, taking pity on the barbarous condition of his nation, with Nanno, Theobald, Albinus, and Berenger as helpers, attempted to give rule to the German language. And although, as Turpin bears witness, he had begun so arduous a work, he did not complete it, being hindered by frequent incursions of wars; but, forestalled by death, he left it unfinished, nor did another arise after him who would recall care for this matter to mind. Otfrid, however, the already mentioned monk, formerly hearer and disciple of Rabanus of Fulda, under Louis the Pious, son of that Charles, having mastered that grammar, however imperfectly, wrote many things in our German language, which seem to sound forth the admirable gravity of a regular instruction. For he worked out many poems according to that same rule, in heroic and also elegiac meter; moreover he composed some things also in prose to Louis, king of Germany, son of Louis the Pious and brother of Emperor Lothar; also to Otgar, archbishop of Mainz, to the monks of Saint Gall, and to others. Having obtained fragments of this grammar, I too have extracted the present characters from many, lest they should perish entirely. It is established, with Otfrid as witness, that Charles devised several alphabets of his own, which his very extensive kingdom might use securely in secrets with individual prefects.
