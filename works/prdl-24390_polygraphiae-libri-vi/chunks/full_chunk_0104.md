And so that this institution might remain perpetually unshaken, he gave the same men the power of substituting also others, fit men under certain conditions, who, enjoying the aforesaid faculty, might with impunity exercise the office of inquiry and of death against the guilty. Finally he prescribed for them secret laws and hidden signs, together with a form of oath, by which they might proceed justly in judging and punishing, and, known mutually to themselves, might remain hidden from others, and might preserve perpetually in Saxon land the necessary judgment in greater secrecy. They also used certain alphabets among themselves for a time; these, however, at length fell entirely out of use. Yet the office of this sort of inquisition remains down to the present time, whose ministers are commonly called [felmeri?].

Although these characters do not appear sufficiently beautiful and elegant, nevertheless it has pleased me to join them with my aforesaid new inventions, for the honor and reverence of antiquity, since there is no one, even of slight industry, who cannot devise prettier ones for himself at his own discretion. Yet continued practice in writing will be able, by an easy correction, to amend this misshapen crookedness, and by frequent use to make it pleasing by habit. For no one, however learned or educated he may be, is a good writer in any language unless by long habit he has transformed the practice of writing into an art. Another also follows.

If anyone at his own discretion wishes to fashion a new alphabet for himself, let him know that two things especially must be observed, which nevertheless I do not always find preserved in the preceding examples. The first is that he form characters of easy drawing; and the simpler they are, the better they will be. The second is that he arrange the individual letters so that, when joined together into words, they present a beautiful and elegant appearance. I add a third, not promised: if you wish the writing to remain secret, make some letters of the alphabet double, after the manner of the Hebrews.

This Hichus the Frank came with Marcomer from the borders of Scythia to the mouths of the Rhine in Germany, and, as a supplement to the German language, is said to have invented these characters, which seem to agree sufficiently with it in many respects. Pharamund also, king of the Franks in Germany, the forty-third after Marcomer, who had brought in the nation, died in the four hundred twenty-sixth year of the Lord’s Nativity; when he had sent his scouts through Gaul for the purpose of invading it, he ordered them to write in such characters as these.
