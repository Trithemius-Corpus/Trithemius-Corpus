*On the signs and wonderful, but excessively laborious, method of writing of Marcus Tullius Cicero, and afterwards of Saint Cyprian, bishop and martyr.*

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, wrote a book of signs of no small size. Saint Cyprian, bishop of Carthage and martyr, enlarged it with many signs and words, adding terms necessary for Christian uses, so that the work itself became useful not only to pagans, but much more also to the faithful.

The manuscript is rare; I have found it only once, and bought it at a cheap price. For when, in the year of the Lord's nativity 1496, I was examining many libraries out of love for books, I found the aforesaid manuscript in a certain monastery of our order, neglected because of its extreme age, cast away under dust and contempt. I asked the abbot, a doctor of law, at what price he valued it. He answered: he would prefer to it the little works of Saint Anselm, recently printed. I went to the booksellers, since the matter happened in a metropolitan city, and bought the requested little works of Anselm for one sixth of a florin; I gave them to the abbot, with the monks rejoicing, and freed the manuscript, which had already nearly been brought to destruction. For they had resolved, out of love for the parchment, that it should immediately be scraped.

Almost two years after this, I rode to Strasbourg on business of my order, and, being admitted by Johannes Keisersberg, the distinguished preacher of the place, into the library of the cathedral church, I found a psalter written wholly in the same signs of Tullius and Cyprian, and most handsomely written with golden capitals. But an inscription had been placed on the outside by someone ignorant of the mystery, as follows: “Psalter in the Armenian language.” I brought in a scholar, showed the falsity, and advised that it should be rewritten thus: “Psalter written in Ciceronian signs.” Whether he did so or not I am uncertain, because I did not afterwards return to that library. So that you may see, reader, the form of Cicero's notation, we have decided to append a few signs.

This was the alphabet of Iaimiel, great king of the Artici, surnamed Megalopis, a very wise man, which he used in secret matters; it too can be altered in many ways. To it we also, out of love for our prince, have appended this one, in which writing, when more frequently used over time, will become easier.
