Hence it must be considered that one series of writing can be led through both tables, as we have already done in the demonstration of the first example, or, if you prefer, through one alone, as we have sufficiently shown in the reversed table. Nor will it cause difficulty for the reader to whom you send the hidden message; he will easily understand everything if he has had even a little practice in the art. But the strongest lesson, which increases knowledge, is frequent use in any art.

After the expansion of the tables there follow certain *orchemata*, in which there is not only a transposition of all the letters, but also an ordered leaping of one over another. For *orchema* is called *transilitio*, “leaping across,” in Latin. And first we marked the leap of the letters in expanded form; afterward we reduced it into an *orchema*. In the operation of this *orchema* there is a remarkable leap. There are twenty-four alphabets in it. In the first no letter intervenes; in the second, one; in the third, two; in the fifth, three; in the sixth, five; in the seventh, six; in the eighth, seven; in the ninth, eight; in the tenth, nine; finally in the eleventh, ten, and so on successively as far as the twenty-fourth alphabet, in which twenty-three letters intervene. Afterward there is a return by likeness to the beginning. The method in the *orchemata* for the mystery of writing is similar to that which is observed [unclear] of the *orchema*: from any black alphabet take only one letter, which has been shown written in red at the head. For understanding this, let us set down an example: Prdb. ifyqyn. bdyen. zbkt. sxfol. inq. Its sentence is this: Beware this man, because he is evil. When, in writing, you have reached the end of the *orchemata* and the secret begun in your heart has not yet been completed, you should know that you must return to the beginning as often as necessary, until the mystery has been completed through all things. And know that from one *orchema* of letters as many alphabets are composed as the diligent investigator may wish to have. Finally, this method of writing by *orchemata*, that is, by transilitions and leaps, is the most secure of all, because the transposition of letters arises not in one uniform way but varied in many ways. And it should be noted that true and perfect knowledge of the tables and *orchemata*, if you are industrious and studious, will give you understanding of all writings that are written in any way by transposition of letters. And it is a great secret, known to few, that all meanings hidden in any transposition of letters can be found.

*Brief Explanation of the Sixth and Last Book of the Polygraphy*

In the sixth and last book of the *Polygraphy* we have brought together certain characters of the ancients which were used in place of letters, so that, if anyone wishes to appropriate his own alphabet from it, he may be able to do so, although anyone moderately skilled, if he wishes, can devise for himself characters more beautiful than these. All the systems of this book are sufficiently open and clear, and therefore they need no explanation, even a brief one. Yet those which have been newly invented by us require a definite declaration, lest perhaps they seem to have been set down without reason. First of all, we have made all the letters of the Latins numerical, after the manner of the Hebrews and Greeks, thinking it shameful for us if we should be found inferior to them in this respect. For what is it that we have doubled certain letters so that they might suffice for an infinite number, when the Jews long ago did the very same thing, compelled by the same necessity? And the Greeks, because of the lack of letters, admitted certain marks into their number, since they were unable to double the letters. But we, because we are pressed by an equal lack of letters, have admitted two marks, namely this one and the one which our writers put for *et*; nor does it matter if it is written thus, *et*, provided that it represents six in number.
