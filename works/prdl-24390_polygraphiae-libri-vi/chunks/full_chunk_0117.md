XIII. Thirteenth, it should be noted that, in order to stop the mouths of those who speak unjust things, we show that the truth of this new invention of ours is possible, placing the words in the alphabets of the first book so that they cohere with one another in such a way that, in the manner of an exhortation of supplication offered to God, they render a fitting and Latin prayer. Beneath the narrative of this prayer we can conceal whatever secrets occur to us, and write them in such a way to a friend skilled in the art that, if all the men of the whole world came together as one, they could never discover the secret lying hidden beneath the open discourse, provided it has been rightly arranged.

XIIII. Fourteenth, it must be known that in this very invention of our polygraphy there still lie hidden certain things far better than those prescribed above, namely how you will be able to instruct someone ignorant of the Latin language in a few days, not to say hours, so that he may know how to write, read, speak, and understand Latin, not indeed all things universally, but, according to a certain mode, whatever necessary matter may occur to him in his affairs on any subject. Likewise, how you may make known to your friend far away, who knows the method of the art, all the conceptions of your mind, securely, safely, and most secretly, without written messages and without the messenger knowing them, and sometimes even without a messenger, naturally and without any superstition, danger, or fear. But reason has moved us to let these things remain buried in their hiding-places, together with the rest, because it neither pleases me nor is at all advantageous to cast precious stones, once found, away, or to accomplish difficult things without the fruit of gratitude. Finally, there lie hidden beneath these things no small mysteries of subtlety concerning medicines, herbs, plants, precious stones, rivers, the members of the human body, brute animals, saints, nations, cosmography, astronomy, the seven liberal arts, and also all things that are held in human use; concerning the universal world, both of visible and of invisible things. And, lest we be more prolix than is proper, all things that are in the world, shown beforehand through the enlargement of likeness, serve the mystical teaching of the art of polygraphy.

XV. Fifteenth, note that all these inventions which we have handed down in the Polygraphy are certain small introductions in comparison with those which still remain to be discovered and written in this same faculty; concerning these we pass over in silence, lest we seem to boast vainly. For we have given to ingenious men cause for discovering greater things than we have either written or perhaps been able to discover. Let him who can add, for the completion of this invention, the things which he knows to be useful; but let him who cannot do so not rashly judge what he does not understand.

*Explanation of those things which seemed more obscure in the second book of the Polygraphy.*

In the second book of the Polygraphy, apart from the order and sequence of the words, nothing new is handed down from the first; and therefore one and the same method of working will apply in both. All the things which we said must be observed in the first, we diligently warn must also be observed in the second. In the narrative of the prayer alone a manifest difference is seen to have arisen, since the former is composed concerning our Lord, the Son of God, but the latter is raised to his mother, the ever-untouched virgin. In the second book, however, there are three hundred and eight alphabets set before the words in order, though under different subjects. For from the beginning the narrative of the discourse is directed to that lady of ours; afterward the Lord’s Prayer is expanded under twenty-four alphabets; then the angelic salutation is appended; consequently the Salve Regina; and last, a brief prayer concerning holy Anna, mother of the Virgin Mary, mother of God. In all the books of the Polygraphy one must beware that a single word not be doubled in any alphabetical order, that is, that it not be placed twice, nor that any be omitted, because it would create great confusion for the reader. Therefore the greatest care must be taken that the copies be corrected in every respect and emended exactly, lest you, in writing, fall into error. But if, through the negligence of the scribe or the carelessness of the printer, you find some word duplicated under one alphabet, erase the last and put in its place another suitable one, which has not previously occurred in the same alphabet.
