Behold, you now have here this alphabet made from Roman numeral letters in imitation of the [Normans?], with which you will be able to write and report very safely to an absent friend whatever you wish; and no one lying in wait will be able to penetrate your secret except him who knows the hidden principle of the art. For all who see the writing will think that the letters have been transposed, and they will labor in vain in reckoning them up. The hidden meaning of this writing is as follows: Beware the bearer of the present letters, because he is a very wicked man, a thief, a robber, a liar, and worthless. From the demonstration of this example it is easy to understand how safe and secure this method of writing is universally, in every language known to the writer, and in every matter or business, however difficult, secret, great, or lengthy. Nor do I think that there lives in the world a man who, by his own wit and without a teacher, could read this writing itself. For all who see it will think it is nothing other than transposed letters; and when they have been unable to obtain an understanding of them, at last worn out by despair, they will cease from investigation, and whatever you have written will remain hidden. For no one, as I think, will find out this craft of writing secretly by his own powers, as it is in itself, unless he either has the Polygraphy, or this key to it, at hand, or has been taught by another. From these numeral letters also many other alphabets can be made according to the order of numbering, when the beginning for writing alphabetically is taken not from unity but from any other following number. But in that progression you should know that care must be taken that no more than two numerable signs are taken to represent one letter. Hence, when you have come to ww, that is, ten times one hundred thousand, stop your step and do not go further, lest the multitude of signs, by deforming it, confuse the beauty of the writing. For, after unity has been set aside, ten times one hundred thousand signs included are sufficient for almost thirty individual alphabets. Yet the method of the first alphabet, beginning with unity, is the more elegant for enveloping written secrets, inasmuch as it is shorter and simpler. Finally, it makes the writing less tortuous. This method of writing too is tortuous; it is subject to no regular rule, since it can be varied and changed at the pleasure of the writer by a chance transposition as much as anyone wishes. Next it should be noted that at the end of the sixth book of the Polygraphy we have handed down another method of writing by means of numeral signs, which people commonly call ciphers; under its covering you will safely write to an absent friend whatever you wish, and no one ignorant of the art will be able either to discover or to suspect that a secret lies hidden there, especially if the rules that we have given on these matters are observed with diligent caution. This is that the sheet should be formed in the manner of an astronomical table, as though it contained something pertaining to the reckoning of the stars, namely so that characters of the signs or planets are inserted, so that someone into whose hands it may perhaps fall by chance, led on by the likeness of a table, may think that no secret lies hidden in it, but may believe that everything pertains to some astronomical calculation. We have arranged twelve alphabets according to the number of the twelve signs of the zodiac, assigning to each one the number twenty-four in place of letters; the first of these, of Aries, begins from unity and ends at twenty-four.
