I have taken confidence from your gentleness. For indeed, since all draw confidence from the fountain of your piety, why should I alone not approach trustfully? You, who are always merciful, mild, and pious to all, certainly cannot be cruel to me alone. Therefore, with that humility which is fitting, and which I both ought and am able to show, prostrate on the ground I approach the throne of your most excellent majesty; I devotedly offer the aforesaid work of Polygraphy; and I humbly commend my littleness to your serenity, as a servant to his lord. Now therefore, most merciful Caesar, receive the very small little gift which I bring; in it may you deign to weigh my mind, most subject to you, not its value. In the signs set beneath our Polygraphy, however, there are in certain places some mysteries wrapped in enigmas, lest the deeper meaning of the art which lies hidden should become manifest to the understanding of all. Just as not all things befit all people, so also not everything always suits everyone indiscriminately. There are therefore certain mysteries shut up here, which in my judgment can be more useful and more necessary to none than to princely men. For the more numerous the matters to which they attend, the greater caution of secrecy must be applied to their counsels. For this book teaches princes to command secrecy over their hidden affairs. But because your Caesarean majesty, occupied with many things, does not have time to examine those matters which lie wrapped in mysteries, lest you be deprived of the fruit of my labor, I have appended a little explanatory book of the whole work, which I have entitled the Key of Polygraphy; with it as guide you will more easily understand everything obstructed here by figures. May God preserve you unharmed for our salvation. I wrote this on the twenty-sixth day of the month of April, in the year of Christ 1508.

*Register or Index of the Works of Johannes Trithemius, first abbot of Sponheim, and afterwards of St. James at Würzburg.*

Johannes Trithemius the abbot was born in the village of Trittenheim, in the diocese of Trier, on the banks of the river Moselle, on the first day of the month of February, at the eleventh hour and the thirtieth minute after noon, in the year of the Lord's nativity 1462, in the tenth indiction of the Romans; his father was Johannes von Heidenberg, and his mother was called Elisabeth von Longuich. This was, namely, the year in which the city of Mainz, in the dissension of two pontiffs, was captured, lost its liberty, and found servitude, many of its citizens having been driven into exile; it was the fourth current year of Pope Pius, the second of that name, and the twenty-second of Emperor Frederick III. In the year of the Lord 1482, however, in the monastery of Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, on the first day of February, that is, on the day on which he had once been born, he put off the secular habit and there assumed the monastic habit under the rule of the divine father Benedict, according to the observance newly introduced. In the following year, which was the eighty-third above the four-hundredth and the thousandth, after Abbot Johannes von Colenhusen had been transferred by canonical postulation to Seligenstadt, Johannes Trithemius, by the common election of the brothers, was promoted and ordained abbot of the aforesaid monastery of Sponheim on the fourth day before the Kalends of July, and presided for twenty-three years, one month, and nineteen days. When these had elapsed, not without many labors, provoked by the rashness of certain rivals, he accepted the abbey of St. James at Würzburg, which was being offered to him, and of his own free will resigned Sponheim, full of useless cares. In both places he wrote several little works; of these, he recently recorded for me in his own hand on a sheet the works written below. When you have their titles from me, for the memory of our friend and teacher, you may hold it more firmly from consideration of such great labors of his. And first, indeed, I shall record the titles of those works which he laboriously composed while he was abbot at Sponheim. First of all he wrote the constitutions of his order, which were made in provincial chapters by order of the presidents, one book. On the Visitation of Monks, one book. On the Rule of Saint Benedict, two books. Homilies and Exhortatory Sermons to His Monks, two books, which he dedicated to his predecessor.
