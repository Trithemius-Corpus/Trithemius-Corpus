With the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, savior of faithful souls, who lives in sacred trinal unity with the Father and the Holy Spirit, threefold in persons and one God in substance, incomprehensible, almighty, and everlasting, from whom, in whom, and through whom everything good, beautiful, and true proceeds, we have brought this book of Polygraphy to its end. To him be praise, honor, virtue, and glory for this, who through the innocence of his death freed our race from the destruction of eternal perdition, and by perpetual mercy opened again the entrance to eternal life for all who believe in him with a pure conscience. Line by line we have set down three hundred and eight paraphrases, avoiding, by [unclear] counsel, a more extended wandering digression. For at the beginning of this little work it seemed enough to us if we gave those who wished it cause to seek fuller things, since both time and reason failed us for them. Farewell, friendly reader. From Peapoli, on the penultimate day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord 1508.

*The Third Book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapoli, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

What confounded the unity of language from the beginning of human creation? Was it not the division of bodies and the variety of minds? For as Truth declares in the Gospel: from the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For the tongue is the instrument for bringing outward those conceptions which the mind has thought within itself in its inner parts. Yet of this ministry of the mind's secrets to sense there are many modes, whose properties vary by practice and by art. Practice, introduced at pleasure, is common to many; art, however, to few. Finally, no one will quickly estimate how much the very practice of speaking, unregulated, has varied among the Teutons alone in six hundred years, unless he has often reread Otfrid, monk of Weissenburg, in his poems in the German tongue. The sea flows and flows back; men, once they have died, return no more to the present life. Customs succeed customs; the vigor of mortal minds always falls toward softer things. Who today, among the Germans, the ancient rivals of the Greeks, possesses knowledge of the old idioms? In chronicles and old histories of the Franks, Hunibald, Doracius, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed many things, in old [unclear], said in the prose of the Teutonic language, into their compositions, which today are understood by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own idiom among the Eastern Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhinelanders, Mosellans, Cologne people, Saxons, Thuringians, undergoes notable variation every day?
