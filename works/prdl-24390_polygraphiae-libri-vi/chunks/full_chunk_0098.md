In the present orchema a manifold alphabet is handed down, by transposition and leap, both according to the rule of order and according to the position of the letters. All these things are sufficient as an example for those who cannot be content with fixed ones, so that they may have a method and form for discovering the rest, since they can be varied without number.

ii

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot [unclear], to the reader, greeting.*

Since all the letters of the Greeks and Hebrews are numerical, I have often wondered that the inquisitive Latinity of the ancients did not also make its own letters serve the reckoning of numbers. The Hebrews, by doubling certain letters, established twenty-eight signs sufficient for every number. The Greeks, however, with like foresight relieved this lack of letters by the addition of four characters. Since we therefore have twenty-four letters, why do we not also make our own numerical, seeing that we can similarly find four doubled letters? Let us give four as an example, and all will be sufficient for number: final m and n; likewise closed v and open u. When we add to these the twenty-four usual letters, we have characters sufficient for all twenty-eight numbers. And it will be no matter of very great labor to commit this number to memory.

For this invention of ours will be able to be useful to many, since in number it comprehends many thousands in few letters, all of which at first glance, without any calculation, can be discerned and understood more clearly than light. Many who are less experienced in the art of calculation think that this cannot be done so easily by the figures of algorism, especially in large numbers. For as soon as you have looked with your eye upon any number, however great, you will be able both to understand it fully and to express it without circumlocution or division of speech. Since this is done so easily, it is fitting that we mark the letters themselves reduced into number. Written 16 [unclear] 1508.
