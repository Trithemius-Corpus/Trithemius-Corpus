But in order that you may be able to avoid this difficulty, distinguish the great revolutions of our whole sphere by arithmetical degrees; where the day is repeated in place of the night, set the mark of Archimedes upon the flying thing, lest the untimely ternary, on its return to unity, be shut off by the binary. For on this account we would relieve [unclear] the paraphrases of counting by single minutiae. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in these things which we have handed down as the principles of a new art, believe me, you will find greater things hidden there than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these things, know one thing, not the least: that by the prudent operation of metathesis, whenever you wish, you will make even one most skilled in this art ignorant, the seeing man blind, the learned man unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, namely that there are eighteen thousand worlds for each individual human being, this invention of ours would give to each one individual ways of safely and secretly writing and very widely communicating whatever secrets they wished in Latin.

*The end of the first book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James the Apostle at Wurzburg, is here, on the twelfth day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord's nativity one thousand five hundred and eight.*

*Here begins the second book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of [Peapolitan?], of the Order of Saint Benedict.*

The reckoning-method of mortals, by art, tries to imitate nature, which admits no calculation of the monad. Hence, although the arrangement of the preceding book of the Polygraphy, through the extension and metathesis of particular things, may suffice as it were for the universals of this art in the infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a certain consideration has moved us to join another to it. The condition of light is different in each; but night preserves inviolate in both the order of its course. The prudent reader, from these small inventions of ours, not sufficiently polished to the rule, will easily be able, if he is strong in excellent talent, to discover greater and more polished things. Whatever visible thing is set within the whole circle of the universe supplies ready support for the service of this new art of ours. Air and fire, water and earth, springs and rivers, trees and fruits, flowers and grasses, words and names, accident and substance, stones and sand, dung and excrements, things and terms, honorable and modest things, wood and metals, hard and liquid things, things existing and non-existing, history and fable, men and beasts of burden, particular and universal things, whatever speech can express or the soul can think, provide a very broad field for our Polygraphy, and promise that the art, so far as it can be established for mortals, will be enlarged to infinity.
