*Homily XVIII.*

*Folio XLII.*

...someone passing along the public road, and, as though he had presented himself as a traveler because of the rain, threw him into the mud; and by treating the man with injury he made him a pitiable spectacle of his own shamelessness. No one was present to restrain the insolent men; yet the monk’s patience overcame the raging men, and, full of the love of Christ, while he was being mistreated he answered nothing other than “Thanks be to God.” Thus the more he showed himself tranquil in mind and spirit, the more the scoundrels dragged the innocent man about, thinking that he was heated with wine, whom the love of Christ had made joyful and glad.

He left to all monks a great lesson in patience: when he could have avenged himself, he did not wish to do so though wronged. Would that, my brothers, for love of God and for our sins, we were as patient in the smallest things as that good man was in the greatest. There are among you certain men, I say it unwillingly, too insolent and impatient, who are moved to anger by a word, even one not insulting; and while they are justly corrected for their negligences, they unjustly murmur, adding sin to sin through impatience.

But to what end do I set before them the patience of the man just mentioned, when they do not heed Jesus Christ, the most patient of all men? Indeed, whoever does not embrace patience and humility after the example of the King of heaven is brought back to gentleness by the imitation of no man. For the Son of God is the highest and supreme example of patience and humility; whoever is not bent to endurance by the recollection of his passion is rightly judged hardened and a companion of demons.

No good Christian doubts that the words of our Lord Jesus Christ are true. But Christ says in the holy Gospel: Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the earth. If the meek are to be called blessed, what prevents the rancorous, wrathful, and unmeek from being named accursed? Certainly nothing. And if, as the Lord himself and our Savior Jesus Christ bears witness, we possess our souls in patience, there is no doubt that we lose them through impatience.

It is therefore especially fitting that monks be meek, gentle, and patient, who have solemnly devoted themselves to the imitation of Christ and the apostles. But Christ, the meekest of all, commands his disciples and imitators in the Gospel, saying: Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls. Hence the holy bishop Augustine says: If you wish to reign with Christ in heaven, first learn from him to be meek, patient, humble, and gentle; for unless you have lived in this world after the imitation of his life, you will by no means reign with him forever.

The divine Ambrose also says: When I have laid aside every sin, and stripped off every malice, and, content with simplicity, have been poor in evils, it remains that I temper my morals. For what does it profit me to be free of crimes, unless I have been meek and gentle? But he is meek who yields to wickedness and does not resist in evil, but overcomes evil with good. And he is gentle, as Chrysostom bears witness, who, when he has been provoked by injuries, neither thinks of doing evil nor does it.

Cassiodorus, however, says: We call the gentle “accustomed to the hand,” that is, patient and meek, who tolerate the iniquities of others and do not presume to burden anyone. Of such persons it is said in the psalm: My soul shall be praised in the Lord; let the gentle hear and rejoice. He did not say, Let those skilled in the law rejoice, nor those who fast, nor those who sing psalms, but the gentle, who, together with charity, have been accustomed above all to have moderation in all things.

But those are truly meek, gentle, and patient who harm no one, injure no one, offend absolutely no one, but, standing firm in their simple humility, always bear all injuries, insults, and mockeries evenly for the love of Christ, and are moved by no pain of spirit against those who harm them, just as we have already said that monk was, whom at Bingen the raging boys treated with exceedingly insulting behavior.

But it is easy to be meek, patient, and gentle so long as nothing is done except according to the will and judgment of the one who is patient. Yet it will become clear how far he is from true gentleness if a slight occasion arises. It is a great virtue if you do not harm the one by whom you have been harmed; great strength of soul if, for the love of God, you forgive injury to the one who inflicts it; great glory if you observe the commandment of Christ and pour out prayers for those who injure you. No one has known the powers of his own soul whom a notable insult has not yet burned; let no one boast himself meek or patient who has not been sated with reproaches.
