But since now you faithfully believe what he also commands, and have placed the foundation of the spiritual building upon a firm rock, it is now necessary that without delay you also do the remaining things that are needed for perfection. Therefore love peace and truth, so that in the faith of Christ, as true soldiers, you may be able at all times to remain stable and most steadfast. For, as Scripture bears witness, truth is great and stronger than all things, nor is there anything unjust with it. Whoever loves truth is a true disciple of Christ, and loves Christ himself, who says in the Gospel: “I am the way, the truth, and the life.” All the ways of the Lord likewise are mercy and truth; from this Satan fell through pride, because he did not remain in everlasting truth.

Therefore the holy Christ-loving Paul commands the soldiers of the Lord to gird themselves in truth; in this he wished to show the state of perfection toward which cloistered persons especially ought to strive. For what else is it to love truth than to be a human being as one is commanded to be, and so to live according to the norm of justice, true and solid, such as holy perfection requires of nature through grace? Hence Firmianus Lactantius says: “Let those who devote themselves to truth cast away the pleasures hostile to it and troublesome, which bind the soul to themselves and do not allow it to be free. But let eternal things always be preferred to false, useful things to pleasant. Let nothing be pleasing to the sight except what can be done justly, piously, and religiously; nothing sweet to the hearing except what makes the soul better.”

Therefore soldiers have their loins girded in truth when, having despised the enticements of the flesh and the vanities of the world for the love of God, they strive with all their strength so to conduct themselves and live that they may be able in all things to please the highest Truth, which is God. After this the soldier of Christ is commanded to put on the breastplate of justice, without which no one can rightly and fruitfully serve the Lord as a soldier. Justice, moreover, is to preserve and distribute to each person what is owed to him according to law or nature. And this is the most excellent of all virtues; without it there can be no piety, no religion, no holiness, and no discernment of good and evil. For it is light and a great splendor, from which the force and dominion of every good rises up among mortals. Justice is the mother of innocence, the nurse of concord, the parent of friendship, the begetter of piety, and the preserver of religion. Justice, as Scripture bears witness, is perpetual and immortal; injustice, however, is the acquisition of death.

In what ways the Christian soldier ought to put on this breastplate of justice, our Savior himself declares in the holy Gospel, saying: “All things whatsoever you wish men to do to you, do you also likewise to them.” Therefore, my brothers, keep this manner of living in the monastery: that each one should do for his fellow soldier in all things just as, according to the right judgment of reason, he desires at all times to have done to himself by others. For this is the first duty of justice, that one harm no one; then, that one use common goods without any harm to another; and, what our warfare further requires, that one do for a brother what, by just reason, conduces to salvation as to one’s own.

O how great a peace mortals would enjoy if they followed the exhortations of the apostle, by which each is commanded to bear the burdens of the other. “Everyone,” says Scripture, “who does justice is born of God.” This is the breastplate [unclear]: to show worship and piety to God, holiness to ourselves, and brotherly charity to our neighbor. We all serve Christ as soldiers under the trophy of justice; if anyone is unjust, he shows that he belongs not to the soldiery of Christ, but rather to the company of demons. For justice is the greatest virtue, which distributes to each what is his own; if you lack it, you lose the name of soldier. Therefore render worship and piety to God; to your superior [unclear]. For this indeed, brothers, is the true justice of monks: that each one should conduct himself without offense according to the prescribed norm of the law, assigning to each his proper dignity: reverence to his superior, concord to his equal, discipline to his inferior, worship and obedience to God.
