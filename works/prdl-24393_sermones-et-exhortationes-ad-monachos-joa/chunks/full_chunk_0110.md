*Homily 24.*

*Fol. LVI.*

... works the righteousness of God; he is not uselessly sorrowful. A good conscience always advances in the love of the Lord, and he who lives innocently will not fear danger. A good conscience has peace and concord with all in God, and in humble simplicity cares for its own salvation. A good conscience strengthens the soul; but he who is constant in evils will fall into despair. A good conscience confidently rebukes the foolish man; but he who is evil keeps a bitter silence. A good conscience gladdens the soul; but he who is conscious of his own evils continually suffers desolation of mind. A good conscience safely rebukes the impious man; but he who has lost the testimony of uprightness always fears reproof. A good conscience is the most beautiful adornment of the soul; and he who does not care for wisdom in this life will die in want.

A good conscience is the dwelling-place of the virtues and the true mansion of heavenly mysteries. A good conscience is the consummation of all philosophy and the fullness of all sciences. A good conscience is the best teacher of the virtues, and the perfected discipline of all studies. A good conscience bears the best fruit of holiness, and, standing founded in the fear of the Lord, admits no sin. A good conscience is more precious than all the riches of the world, and much sweeter than all desirable things that can be had in this life. A good conscience is the merit of Christian perfection and the humbly presumable beginning of future happiness. A good conscience is a fertile field of virtues, and the sweet and pleasant fragrance of the paradise of God. A good conscience is a very sweet companion to the good man, and a glorious mediator between God and man. A good conscience is very fearful to demons, but a pleasing friend to the holy angels. A good conscience makes the dying man secure against the snares of demons, and he merits having the escort of angels. A good conscience does not fear to appear in the sight of judgment, nor does it dread the judgment of an accusing synteresis. A good conscience has hope in God and, without the unrest of hesitation, the best confidence, because he who remembers having committed no evil comes confidently to the examination of judgment. A good conscience will be richly rewarded by the Lord God, and is changed into everlasting joy.

A good conscience is not one that perpetrates no evil, but one that, preserving purity for the love of God from every sin, also adorns itself most fittingly by the exercise of all good works. For a good conscience, according to the prophet’s admonition, wholly declines from evil, and with most zealous intention always and continually does good. A good conscience especially adorns the monk, and makes him far removed from secular actions. A good conscience follows the Lord Jesus in simple purity, and is stained by no spots of errors. For a conscience that is good, and so long as it is good, cannot be erroneous, but always remains founded in the truth of the Holy Spirit. But a conscience that has been erroneous is not good. For error both opposes truth and in no way shares in goodness. But when conscience is in error, danger is close at hand, and the descent to hell very steep.

There are many among cloistered persons who are weak and exceedingly feeble in mind, who are not always directed by the right judgment of their consciences, being preoccupied by various causes. And often indeed a small or great error of conscience arises from ignorance, whenever, that is, one less skilled in spiritual exercise doubts whether what he is considering in his mind is good or evil. For who, walking rightly in his simplicity, understands everything? In doubtful matters the counsels of elders are to be sought, and what they have decided the monk must embrace without doubt as the truth of the opinion. Sometimes conscience errs from negligence, when the monk neglects to examine himself by the scrutiny of reason and does not labor in doubtful matters to investigate the truth. Hence a punishable error arises in the conscience, through which reason is gradually clouded. Frequently also pride of conscience arises from singularity, to which presumption is always joined [unclear]. And this plague often [unclear] among monks and those who profess holiness without the necessary foundation of humility. For the sheep that walks singular paths is more quickly caught and strangled by wolves. Sometimes also an excessively disordered affection casts the conscience down into error, namely when a man loves his own will more than is expedient. For he who is led by the affection of his own sensuality is frequently blinded in the judgment of truth.
