Father Benedict, detesting the idleness of monks, says in the Rule which he handed down to us: “Idleness is the enemy of the soul; and therefore at fixed times the monks ought to be occupied in manual labor, and again at fixed hours in divine reading. For then they are truly monks, if they live by the labor of their own hands, as our fathers and the apostles also did.” Hence also the holy Christ-loving Paul says in the epistle: “He who does not work shall not eat.” In the beginning of the monastic state this was the custom of monks, that they admitted no one into their fellowship without labor, not so much on account of the necessities of food as on account of the salvation of the soul, lest through idleness an opportunity be given to pernicious thoughts.

At that time monks had no assessments, no tithes, no revenues, no annual proceeds of any kind; but all, living together in common, procured food and clothing for themselves with their hands, burdening no one with the exaction of what was owed. The holy apostles also of our Savior, although they had the right to live from the preaching of the gospel, preferred to labor with their own hands, lest they should seem to have burdened any of the faithful, and so that they might have not only the means by which they themselves should live, but also something with which they might furnish necessities to the needy.

But after monks began to have assessments and revenues, the former pursuits of simplicity soon failed; and little by little, together with riches in temporal things, pride grew, to such an extent that in time monks refused all manual labors. As long as the monks were poor and humble in birth, they worshiped God in truth; but after they began to abound in riches, duplicity put simplicity to flight, and the exaltation of the nobles extinguished the humility of the monks. Once the integrity of regular observance had been taken away, soon also in many monasteries the wealth of temporal things failed by the just judgment of God.

Of their number was the present monastery of Sponheim, brothers of mine, neither the last nor the first, which for many years in both states remained miserably desolate, to such a degree that the five monks only who were living in it a dissolute life did not have the means from which they might take the necessities of life for themselves. Hence, by the mercy of God at last providing for it, Frederick, duke [unclear], having invoked the counsel of the fathers of the reformed order, in the year of the Christians 1469 expelled the monks deformed in life, deposed the abbot, named Otto of Cologne, and placed in this monastery four others, called from the observance of Bursfelde, from the monastery of Mont Speciosus. Over them he set as abbot John, from the noble family of Budisbach, which they commonly call “of the Carbonaria house,” a man such as you have seen and heard, worthy of eternal memory; he found this place in extreme poverty, and, presiding over it for almost fourteen years in great patience, brought it back to the state in which we now live.

Therefore, since scarcity of temporal things still presses heavily upon us, there would be need of continual labor, by which we might nourish ourselves with necessary food and free the house of God in which we dwell from debt. But what are your labors, or how much can they contribute to us for such great matters? We are few in number, and unfit for rustic labors; nor does the institution of the priestly order permit us to spend the whole day in the field turning stones with our hands, and to interrupt the divine service.

We are wretched, weighed down by excessive poverty and lack of all necessities; and if we have labored much, we gain little from it; and if we have worked little, surely we fail still more in spirit. For man, as Scripture bears witness, is born to labor just as a bird is born to fly. Therefore, lest you eat your bread idle, you will always have to be assigned to some honest and useful labor, by which both the mind may be relieved from weariness of regular observance, and our grave poverty may be repaired, at least in some small measure.

In harvest time for hay, and on the days of autumn for the vintage, we have been accustomed to occupy you outside the monastery only when, evidently, the labor is very necessary and the dearness of hired workers increases the price and wages of servants. For the rest of the time we have decreed to keep you in the monastery, because your labors, in comparison with the expenses, contribute little to the monastery’s uses, and, as it seems to us, very few of you willingly present yourselves as volunteers for rustic pursuits.
