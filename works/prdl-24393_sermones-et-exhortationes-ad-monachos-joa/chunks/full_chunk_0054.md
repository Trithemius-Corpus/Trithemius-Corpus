Humbly cast your eyes down to the earth, lest they look up at what it is not fitting for a servant of God to desire. Do not take part in the conversations of carnal men; if you have sought to avoid their presence, take care not to lend them your mind. Even in the midst of ribald men, do not transgress your rule, and do not abandon your accustomed maturity. Do not fear insults and the reproaches of [unclear], and let your heart not be troubled by the words of dark men. The preservation of monastic discipline is righteousness; and those who despise the simple pursue the innocent with laughter.

Keep your holy purpose in all things, and the Most High will avenge the injury. Do not say in your heart, “I suffer violence,” nor be ashamed at the words of those who deride you. The glory of a monk is to endure insult, and the crown of beauty is to rejoice in reproaches. He who is patient under injuries is stronger than Atlas, and he who is not saddened when mocked is greater than all. If you fix your desire in heaven, nothing on earth will disturb you; and if you are a true monk, your heart will exult at injuries. There is a judge in heaven who knows all things, and there is the Most High God, who cares for all things. He is the just repayer of merits, and he will remember his poor. The more, now in this life, you cast your soul down into the depth of humility for love of him, the more in future glory you will be exalted above the rest. For by patience heaven is climbed, and by humility the kingdom is reached. For Christ himself also had to suffer, and so to enter into his glory.

A self-indulgent soldier is one who seeks delights while his emperor endures insults and mockeries, and while he hangs on the gibbet of the cross for his army, he himself follows pleasures. A soldier who goes out naked to war without weapons, and a monk who disdains to embrace patience and humility amid insults: the destruction of both is swift. The monk who is a sincere lover of Christ is not angered at injuries, does not threaten those who bring insult, stands fixed in holy purpose, and, however wicked they may be, is not moved.

Just as timely rain increases the hay in the meadows, so merit grows for the monk from the humble endurance of injuries. Humility is the sinew of all virtues, and constant patience under injuries for the love of God is the mine of good works. Therefore, when you are about to go out on the road from the monastery, prepare your heart for the endurance of injuries, and strengthen your mind against the insolences of those who mock you. When scoundrels have shouted after your back, repeating the name of monk, remember Christ mocked and spat upon before the governor, who did not answer a word, but in silence preserved patience. Imitate him as your leader, O monk; always listen to him as your teacher, who was meek and humble of heart. Do not answer a word to those who mock you; do not turn your face backward, but say with the prophet: “Those who sought evils for me spoke vanities, and all day long they meditated deceits. But I, like a deaf man, did not hear; and like a mute man not opening his mouth. And I became like a man who does not hear, and who has no reproofs in his mouth.”

Christ did this; Jesus taught this; the king of all commanded this. If you wish to be his disciple, you must always be ready for every injury. When set upon a journey, do not neglect to say your canonical prayers with what devotion you can, and always turn over something good with yourself in your mind, either by praying or by meditating. [The Lord’s eye?] goes with you, and his holy angels accompany you as you walk. And therefore preserve a clean heart in all things, lest you offend the majesty of such great companions. The Lord is wholly eye and ear; from his knowledge nothing is hidden. Apply constant guard to your words, and let deliberation of the heart precede every word of the mouth, so that you speak nothing at all for which you could repent. The mouth of a monk will speak both few and reasonable things, and his lips never exceed in speech. His whole conduct is set toward the edification of his neighbors; and he who teaches very many by his morals, even when silent, speaks useful things. He who is a true lover of God walks most humbly on the road, and he who fears the Most High restrains his mouth from loud laughter. In your whole life, think of yourself as mortal, and live as one dying, as one who is already breathing out his last breath.
