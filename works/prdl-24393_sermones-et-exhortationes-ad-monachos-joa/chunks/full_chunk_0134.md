*Sermon V.*
*Folio LXVIII.*

What shall we wretches say on that day, or where shall we stand before the strict Judge, if, as Saint Peter the apostle says, scarcely shall the righteous man be saved? If Jerome, a righteous and most holy man, feared the judgment to come, O good God, where will Trithemius, an unhappy sinner, appear? If the righteous man fears, where will the sinner stand? What, then, shall we say, or what shall we answer, when grievously accused on every side? All the elements will stand against us in testimony; earth and heaven together will accuse us. But even if all things were silent, each person’s own conscience will be a grave accuser. He who knows all things is ignorant of nothing, for there all things will be manifest to the Judge. There will be present the evil deeds we have done, grievously accusing us; there will stand round about us, as testimony against us, innumerable demons. We shall see opened before us the dreadful chaos beneath us; above us we shall behold the Judge in wrath; and from every side the utmost danger will threaten us.

These dangers, my brothers, of the examination to come, keep always turning over in the memory of your heart, and diligently consider how dreadful it will be to fall under the future sentence of the Judge. In that terrible hour pure hearts will be of more worth than the clever words of rhetoricians; a clean conscience will profit a man more than the money of the whole world, since he who is to judge all things is not deceived; indeed, he is changed neither by a price nor by prayers. O what incredible horror will invade all the wicked when they are about to hear the fearful sentence: “Go, accursed ones, into eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels.” O worst of all evils, O deepest sorrow of all sorrows: to be separated from the fellowship of the saints and to be tormented with demons forever. Truly the day of judgment is dreadful, fearful for all because it is exceedingly bitter; there the synteresis of conscience dies by the worm that does not die; there one once damned to punishment is never freed.

Therefore the monk who desires to attain eternal salvation should keep himself, as far as he can, always clean from every sin, and that voice, “Go, accursed ones, into eternal fire,” should never depart from his memory.

Hence the third thing which we judge to be very useful for turning away from sin is diligently to consider the bitterness of the penalties of eternal damnation. For there is no temptation of carnal pleasure so vehement that it will not at once be overcome and fail, if the one tempted recalls in careful meditation what the damned suffer in hell. What do riches once possessed now profit the damned? What do past pleasures confer on the lustful placed in hell? What do the vainest honors confer on the swollen with pride? What does power once broad confer on the proud? Think, brothers, what those already damned suffer in the lower regions, and, lest similar things happen to you, persevere continually in good and holy works. If you fear hell, flee sin. For nothing in hell will harm a man if he has remained in the faith of Christ, free from sins; nor will he burn in punishment who previously, here, had not burned in guilt.

Hell is a place to be feared, in which there is no redemption, but whoever, once damned, enters there will never again come out to salvation. The wretched cry out amid torments, placed in anguish and everlasting horror, saying: “What now does our pride profit us: riches, honors, dignities, luxury, gluttony, and all past carnal pleasures? What do they now profit us, damned forever? Behold, all things have passed away like a shadow, and all have vanished like the brief dream of one night. But we have been assigned to these eternal torments, in which we know most certainly that there is no redemption.” In hell, as Augustine testifies, even if there will be the sting of penitence, nevertheless there will be in the damned no correction of the will; in them iniquity will be blamed in such a way that the equity of justice can by no means be loved or desired by them.

There, then, the devouring flame of hell burns those whom carnal delight now pollutes; and each person is burned in torments so much the more as he here in the flesh takes greater delight against God. In the lower regions, as Gregory testifies, there will be intolerable cold, inextinguishable fire, the immortal worm of conscience, intolerable stench, palpable darkness, pains beyond estimation, lashes without interruption, a dreadful sight of demons striking them, confusion over crimes committed, and despair of all good things. But of all the punishments in hell, that one must be considered by far the gravest: that there the damned are forever deprived of the vision of eternal light.
