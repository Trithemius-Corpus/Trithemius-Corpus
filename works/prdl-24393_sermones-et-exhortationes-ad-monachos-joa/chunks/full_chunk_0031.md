I am ashamed to say it, and nevertheless I am compelled to utter it: your labors outside the monastery and the cell bring more destruction than salvation to the purity of the soul. For how do you labor, and what are your pursuits in your labors? Is it not to wander about, run here and there, indulge in [unclear] and cups, and occupy the rest of the time in vain and useless talk?

You do not have in yourselves that fervor of the Holy Spirit of God by which we read that the ancient monks of our institution were once inflamed; and for the common necessity, with such desire, such zeal, such effort, all and each labored with their hands that you would have thought the business of the whole monastery was placed in the prudence of each one. For each one labored as though he alone had had to feed the whole congregation with his own hands. Amid these things they paid the services of divine worship with such eagerness by day and night, as though they were in no way occupied by the labors of the hands. Which brother among you is an imitator of such men, so that he either reckons our common want as his own, or strives, according to his strength, to relieve it by laboring in the sweat of his face? They, laboring with their hands, continually besought God with their hearts; but you, intent on tales, neither satisfy the duty of the hands nor [unclear] as monks.

In the time of the regular fast, too, they either carefully kept the measure of abstinence appointed for them, or, if they could not do so because of the greatness of their labors, they ate for this reason: so that they might be rendered fit and capable for their labors. But you do not eat in order to labor; rather, you labor in order to eat what, according to the Rule, ought to have been fasted from. You take occasion from labor to relax the fasts.

Again, how foully and most basely some of you treat their garments: what zealous lover of regular discipline could bear this with equanimity? [Unclear], my brothers, [unclear] are handled so wastefully. Everything that belongs to the monasteries of the servants of God is the patrimony of Christ, and the whole is said to be dedicated to God; nor is anyone permitted to abuse the things of Christ, or to turn them to another use. Do you not know what our holy lawgiver Benedict says to you in the Rule? “If anyone has treated the things of the monastery filthily or negligently, let him be corrected. If he has not amended, let him be subject to regular discipline.” And in the chapter on the cellarer of the monastery: that he should regard all the substance as if it were the sacred vessels of the altar.

Finally, I cannot and ought not be silent about how filthily and negligently most of you treat everything, as though either you would not be seen by God, or no loss would come to our property from sloth. Know, brothers, that I announce this to you beforehand as a warning: for these things, as also for your other sins, you are to render account to the Lord God.

For this reason I have reduced for you the labors outside the monastery, lest by working badly you should sin more gravely; and manual work is to be enjoined upon you: to write books, to bind them, and these and similar things which you can perform within the enclosure of the monastery with tranquility of mind and body. Would that you at least did these manual works [of yours?] with diligence for the love of God, so that you might not eat bread altogether idle. There is no manual work which, in my judgment, better befits a monk than to write books of sacred reading, and to prepare the necessary things for others who write. For it will generally be permissible to interrupt this holy labor with prayer, and to keep watch for the food of the mind no less than of the body.

Necessity also compels us to apply ourselves diligently to writing books, if we desire to have at hand that with which we may mutually occupy ourselves profitably in spiritual study. For you see that the whole library of this monastery, which once was distinguished and large, was [unclear] by the monks before us, sold and alienated, and that no more than fourteen volumes were found here by us. Indeed, the industry of the printing art, recently invented in our days at Mainz, daily brings many volumes to light; but it is by no means possible for us to buy everything new, since we are still weighed down by the greatest poverty.

Wherefore I advise and exhort you that, since you do not come very gladly to external manual labors, you should at least labor diligently in writing volumes for the honor of God; for just as idleness is the enemy of the soul, so moderate labor is the spiritual preserver of life. A monk, since he is a soldier of the eternal King, ought never to be idle. For idleness begets acedia, and that brings upon the soul the destitution of pernicious death.
