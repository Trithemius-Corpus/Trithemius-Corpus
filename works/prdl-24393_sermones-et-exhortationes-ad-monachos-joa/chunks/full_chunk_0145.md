But some who ascend this sixth degree have hidden within themselves a certain defect of pride, because, while they look back incautiously upon themselves, they do not commit themselves in all things to the divine will as they ought. This defect takes its beginning from a certain very subtle vanity, when the soul, trusting in its own purity, asks that some secret be revealed to it by the Lord. And in this one thing the climbers of the sixth heaven differ from those of the seventh, who, being devoted to God, flee the signs of vanity. For signs have been given not to believers but to unbelievers; hence the monk who asks a sign from the Lord shows that he has not yet perfectly subjected himself to the divine will.

They ascend the seventh degree who most zealously keep all the things prescribed, and, subject to God in all things, live without the stain of vanity. They flee, as far as they can, every sin, whether venial or mortal; they do works of virtue for the love of God alone; in all things they obey their shepherds; they seek no miracles of vanity. Whatever almighty God has ordained concerning them, they receive gratefully; they do not at all depart in anything from the observance of monastic purity. Yet in one thing they are lower than those who follow, because perhaps they suffer some defect of which they are unaware, by whose impulse they sometimes commit something of their own sensuality.

Whoever has deserved to ascend the eighth degree grants nothing further to sensuality. Here is the sweetest rest of the soul; here the perfect tranquillity of the mind. At this degree nothing vicious in beauty is left behind; here nothing is found that can sadden the soul. Here all things are clean, all things pure, all things most sweetly burning with the fervor of charity. O that we might strive to ascend to this degree; O that we might sometime deserve to arrive at this harbor of tranquillity. The more one standing on this holy ladder begins to taste the sweetness of inner delight, the more, hastening toward what lies ahead, he desires further things. O desires of holy love, always to be proclaimed; O exercises of venerable perfection, where the clean conscience of one loving God shines without sins, where the wicked battles of vices have ceased and great tranquillity has succeeded.

They ascend the ninth degree who, already consummated in the eighth, keep all the things prescribed purely and simply for the love of God, and desire nothing other than to praise, bless, and glorify God. Whoever has ascended to this degree of perfection, as though forgetful of himself, resigning himself wholly to God, submits humbly. He reckons himself unworthy of all God’s benefits; he takes no thought at all for himself, because he has already learned to commit himself wholly to God and to be subject humbly in all things to his ordinance. Hence he asks for no miracles and seeks no consolations in this life, but with the holy apostle of Christ he truly cries: To me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain. The world has been crucified to me, and I to the world. For the true monk who loves Christ has offered himself wholly to God out of charity and has despised the world with all its riches, honors, and vanities. Hence the world is said to be crucified to him, in which there is nothing that seems able to penetrate his mind.

Happy are those monks who serve the Lord God with a pure conscience in true charity, who run without offense under the rule of humility and obedience, who ascend this last degree of perfection. These are true monks, who seek nothing other than the honor of God. Indeed, my brothers, although monks are not bound, by necessity for salvation, to be perfect, otherwise very few would be saved, nevertheless we are all bound with good will to strive toward perfection and with all the strength we can to pant after this ninth degree. For it is very contrary to the monastic profession to be unwilling to make progress, and it is a kind of apostasy, after a vow of perfection, to wish to remain in imperfection. For the enemies of our salvation are the vices that attack us, and until they either conquer or are conquered they do not cease to pursue us. Let us imitate the history of the ancient battle, when the children of promise destroyed their enemies not in one day but successively. For it is difficult to conquer all things at once, unless the charity of God suddenly overshadows the mind. Against all the vices that attack us in any way, let us wage war: let us conquer gluttony today; when it has been properly overthrown, let us overcome acedia; and so let us deal with the rest, so that at last we may come forth victors over them all. The Lord will be present with us as we fight, and God will give victory to those who love him. Amen.
