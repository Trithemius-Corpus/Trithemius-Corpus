If you wish to be chaste, be abstinent. It is most shameful for a monk to serve the belly and the throat, who for the love of God has renounced all the pleasures of the world together.

The twelfth thing that makes a monk useful and vigorous in regular observance is that he be most diligent about the service of the divinity. For he who is a true servant of God, which all monks ought to be, is always fervent and diligent in the work of his creator; and because he truly knows God to be present everywhere, he prepares himself with complete devotion for obedient service to him. For it is written: Cursed is the man who does the work of God negligently. Therefore, as you approach the service of the highest and unchangeable God, dearest brothers, chant the psalms sweetly, wisely, fervently, and cheerfully, and with as much devotion and purity of mind as you can, diligently praise your creator and savior. Let all sloth be far from the hearts of those chanting the psalms; let no drowsiness be admitted while praising, for since God is present in the midst of those chanting, the fervor of devout minds ought altogether to be present. In the time of divine service let all things be clean, all things pure, all things most full of the fervor of charity, so that wicked thoughts may have no place and all things may be done fittingly.

When the day has been completed and Compline has been sung according to custom, as soon as the monk has reached the privacy of his cell, let him examine his conscience as to what affairs he has occupied himself with. This is the very good and praiseworthy custom of the brothers of the Bursfeld reform: that no one should presume to place himself upon his bed after Compline unless he has first taken utmost care to examine his conscience concerning the faults or negligences committed during the day. But if, after making examination, you find that during the day you have offended in anything, you know, taught by long custom, what must be done. Hidden faults must at once be erased on the next day by contrition and humble confession; manifest faults, however, must be referred to the chapter of faults, which is held daily. This is the custom of the reformed order, this the holy institution of the reform: that we should never allow the faults of offending brothers to grow, but should take care to cut them off as soon as they have begun to arise. The soul must be cleansed daily; the purpose of holiness must be renewed daily; for just as we cannot remain in the same state without change, so it is necessary for us, by continual exercise, to stir up the mind more frequently to the former fervor of charity.

*What are the signs of true humility: Sermon Three.*

Let none of you, most beloved brothers, deceive himself by his own estimation of himself; let no one mislead his heart by foolish and vain presumption. Not all who desire to seem humble are humble before God, nor are those who keep silent under injuries always truly patient. For the roots of true virtues lie hidden in the heart, and not in the mere outward display of exhibition. We discern certain persons among you who seek rather to be considered humble than truly to be so; and they show many signs of pride, but either none, or certainly very few, of humility. We feel deep compassion, as rightly we ought, for those who err so greatly: they desire to have the name of virtue, but they despise virtue itself. Whom, I ask, do you deceive, wretched men? Do you not destroy yourselves by shamefully misleading yourselves, you who, despising truth, love a lie? But so that you may know how proudly you live, we shall set forth in order the indications and signs of true humility, some of which remain hidden in the heart alone, while others, breaking forth even outwardly, must become manifest to all who see them. Let this be for the monk the true mirror of perfect humility, in which, looking each day, he may examine himself.

The first sign, then, of true humility each person can recognize in himself when he can regard himself as lower and more worthless than all men, not by painted speech, but by the humble judgment of the heart. This is the greatest sign of profound humility: that the monk, always advancing toward a better way of life, should believe himself an unprofitable and unworthy servant of God. True humility belongs to the heart, not to speech; it is the pursuit of the mind, not of bodily display alone.
