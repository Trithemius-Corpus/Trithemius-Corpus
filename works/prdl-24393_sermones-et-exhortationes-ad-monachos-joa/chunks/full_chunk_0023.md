In consideration of this, many of the pagan philosophers used to say that the whole life of a wise man is a kind of continual meditation on death. Hence Saint Jerome says: “Nothing will profit you so much toward temperance in all things as frequent meditation on this brief and uncertain life, which, like a leaf seized by the wind, passes away; and when it is not feared, it falls into death. Therefore keep the memory of your death, which by no counsel can you escape, and you will easily be able to overcome all the movements of the flesh. For he who sometimes thinks that he is going to die despises present things, and, unless he is utterly foolish, will prepare himself for future things.”

For I know, and in part I confess that I have experienced, that the will of the human mind cannot long remain uniform in the holy fervor of spiritual life; and for that reason it has pleased me to recall the memory of death more often before your eyes, so that the mind, struck by fear of future things, may be renewed daily in a holier purpose. The human soul, bound through the exercise of itself to the eternal body [unclear], is very small and wavering and unstable; now it is carried off to one thing, and now it is moved to another. Therefore the wisdom of God says through Solomon in Proverbs: “My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes keep my ways.” As if he were saying: Unless you have bound the whole love of your heart to divine wisdom, you will never be stable and confirmed in keeping the Lord’s commandments. For just as the heart is the first principle of motion in man, so the greater is the watch kept over it for the preservation of life, since if it is struck by the assault of diseases, the whole life of the body is already in danger. Hence it is written: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.” By the name of the heart, however, we understand the affection whenever it inclines toward the love of some thing, clinging to it so much that it entangles the whole will. Hence the psalmist says: “If riches abound, do not set your heart on them,” that is, do not love them with the desire of the will. Nothing is more changeable than the human heart, because it is altered by almost every single thought and can with difficulty remain in the same purpose, unless it restrains itself by a certain forceful intention. Saint David, not ignorant of this, said to the Lord: “But I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty. You turned your face away from me, and I was troubled.”

Therefore, my dearest brothers, if there is any one of you who desires to serve our Lord and Savior in true purity of heart, if anyone wishes to be a monk in deed as well as in name, let him learn first to trust not in himself but in God; and let him take care to renew the good purpose of holy conduct with an ever-new institution, not only every day, but, so far as he is able, even at each hour and in the moments of his actions. The fervor of spiritual conduct, to whose experiential operation the institution of the cloistered life is understood to be wholly ordered, cannot long remain at the same point, because if it does not always advance toward what lies ahead, it necessarily slips back toward lower things, and, what is worse, weaker things. There is no stationary, lingering standing-place in the arena of spiritual exercise, because it is the property of the human heart by its own natural motion always either to advance toward better things or to fall back into that which is evil. Hence, if you do not advance in spiritual conduct, you necessarily fail; if you do not progress toward holier things, you certainly go backward. Since experience has made this clear to us, I do not cease to admonish your charity that you always stretch the desire of your heart in good conduct toward further things, lest there you perilously incur the danger of failing, where you have not found an opening for becoming perfect. The fervor of spiritual purpose must be directed toward the love of God, and cannot otherwise be preserved in him. For the love of God is not absurdly compared to fire: just as fire, when the fuel of wood is withdrawn, is necessarily extinguished, so the love of God, if continuous exercises in good works are lacking, is endangered as it vanishes away. Hence there is that saying of the Savior in the Gospel: “I came to cast fire upon the earth, and what do I desire except that it burn?” O monks, happy is that conscience in whose breast nothing burns except the love of Christ, which loves him above all things and despises all pursuits of vanity. Now consider, brothers, your consciences most diligently, and examine precisely whether you love God above all things.
