*Sermon VII.*

Our temptation is wholly upon the earth; in it, if we do not make progress in good, do we not fail? And there, as the holy pope Leo says, we fall into the danger of failing when we draw back the appetite for being made perfect. Therefore flee sloth, and always keep watch in good works. And consider what happened to the lazy servant, who hid in a napkin his lord’s money, which he had received for trading. Trade, I pray you, while there is time, and as long as you live be always occupied in good pursuits.

Fourth, the grace of devotion is very often withdrawn from those who, placed under obedience, follow their own will. For the monk who loves his own will most justly loses the grace of God. But the man who, for God’s sake, humbly subjects himself in all things to the shepherd of obedience, who despises the will of his own heart, [God] pours into him various gifts of spiritual grace and illumines him with the light of inward fervor. We can offer nothing more precious or more acceptable to God than if, for love of him, we deny our own will. Finally, the very profession of the monastic institution enjoins on us, by necessity of salvation, to despise our own will and humbly to lie subject to him who has been set over us. Therefore the monk who follows the movement of his own will is judged like an unclean vessel and unworthy of spiritual grace. Hence the grace of God is deservedly withdrawn from the monk of his own will, who, living otherwise than he promised the Lord, lies.

Fifth, the grace of devotion is withdrawn from a monk when he thinks that he has it from his own merits. He does not understand that sin dwells in him; if he has received any good, he ascribes it to his own merits. Against this sluggishness of mind the holy prophet said to the Lord: Lord, in thy will thou hast given strength to my beauty, because it is not of him that wills nor of him that runs, but of God who has mercy. Thou turnedst away thy face from me, and I was troubled. For no one is becoming through his own merits, no one holy by his own powers. But let us acknowledge that all the causes of the withdrawal of the grace of God which we have mentioned pertain to our weakness.

But so far as concerns those who already walk in the way of perfection, such a reason is given, so long as they have persevered. For from them the grace of God is never withdrawn, although the sensible fervor of compunction is sometimes taken away for a time. And we shall state in order the causes of this removal.

And first, indeed, almighty God sometimes withdraws for a time from his lovers the grace of sensible devotion, so that, when he pretends not to be present when invoked, the desire of inward love may grow. All who have felt the fire of divine love and who are true emulators of the holy fathers have known this alternation of holy ardor. For whenever the bridegroom withdraws himself from the bride, the desire of the lover grows. While she is present, she seeks him whom she loves; from every desire of love she groans.

Second, this grace is withdrawn from the devout for an hour, lest frailty be raised up into pride. For when we lose the grace of compunction even against our will, by humble examination we judge our weakness, which can do nothing of itself. Therefore, so that devout worshippers of God may remain humble in their exaltation, it is necessary that they recognize their own frailty.

The third cause concerns merit, because to seek inward consolations is not the mark of the strong but of the weak. The Lord must be served freely because he is good; but those who seek sensible sweetness in their devotion are less devoted and seek the Lord’s [unclear] for little consolations. The Lord is to be loved usefully, and all womanish and soft affections are to be driven far from the soul. Therefore, so that the Lord may increase merit for his worshippers in holy compunction, he sometimes denies the grace of sensible devotion to those who ask. But lest, affected by the bitterness of weariness, they grow wholly torpid in the purpose of holiness, the comforter of the deserving does not go far away, but remains nearby. He suddenly returns to his beloved, and the one whom he had pretended for an hour to be sorrowful he consoles with the sweetness of spiritual presence.

Fourth, to these causes we join this: that we are warned to walk more cautiously. For if we fear losing the grace of compunction, let us learn to guard more diligently what we have. A rare and precious treasure, the more its possessor loves it, the more diligently he guards it. Therefore the monk who has deserved to receive from the Lord the grace of devotion ought to watch over it with the utmost zeal, lest he lose it through his own negligence. That you may know how you ought to preserve the granted grace of compunction, lest it perish, the order of what is to be said clearly makes this manifest to you.
