*On the daily renewal of one’s purpose.*

...the more zealously a monk has devoted himself to thoroughly learning the Scriptures, the less he is inflamed by the fires of carnal pleasures. For although, according to the saying of Saint Jerome, the monk’s office seems not to be that of one who teaches, but rather of one who mourns, nevertheless, because he professes the warfare of Christ, it is fitting, indeed necessary, that he should know the well-pleasing will of almighty God, to whose words and arms he has sworn. But just as a soldier cannot know the will of his lord who is far absent except through messengers and letters, so the monk will not understand the precepts of the eternal King except through sacred readings and sermons, the evangelical Scriptures. The reading of the Scriptures opens the gate of salvation to those who do not know it; it leads the diligent reader by the straight path to the knowledge of God; it makes the lover of it despise all worldly things and love nothing other than true wisdom. But nothing is more shameful than for a monk in the spiritual life to be unwilling to make progress, and knowingly to wish to be ignorant of the will of God. Let no one flatter himself on account of his weakness or ignorance, since he who is ignorant will be ignored, and he who hates saving knowledge is deservedly condemned. The [Lord?], about to send the preachers of the Lord into all the earth, warned them beforehand, saying: Be prudent as serpents and simple as doves, lest either you be supplanted by someone through ignorance, or you devise injury against someone through deceit. For there is little difference in vice between being able to deceive and being deceived. Therefore I beg you, dearest brothers, that you be willing to attend to the Rule of our holy father Benedict, which admonishes you in very many places to labor frequently in readings of the divine Scriptures. For in the Scriptures we are taught, instructed for every piety, how we ought to live in this very brief pilgrimage of our sojourning, so that we may be able to please the Lord our God above all things. For it belongs to monks, for the love of God, to despise the world and all pleasures of the present life; manfully to restrain the illicit motions of the flesh; to apply themselves also to prayer, reading, and meditation on the Scriptures; and continually to lift the mind from earthly things to heavenly. *On the Daily Renewal of the Holy Purpose: Fifth Homily.*

We have heard, dearest brothers, the reading recited to us from the book of holy Job, in which he laments the misery of the human race, and the calamity of this life, and at the same time its unstable brevity. “Man,” he says, “born of woman, living for a short time, is filled with many miseries; he comes forth like a flower and is crushed, and flees like a shadow, and never remains in the same state.” “Behold,” he says, “the brief years pass by, and I walk the path by which I shall not return.” “All flesh shall fail together, and man shall return to ashes.” What, brothers, do you notice among these things, or what do you think? Is there any man among us who does not know that he is mortal, who can say with truth, “I shall not be moved forever”? Alas, my brothers, how blind we are while keeping our eyes open; how forgetful and sluggish travelers we are, who know most certainly that we are going to die, and yet place such confidence in the vanity of the present life as if we were not mortal. But “what man is he who will live and not see death, who will deliver his soul from the hand of hell?” Behold, Scripture says, “we all die, and like water we flow away upon the earth.” Therefore remember, my brothers, that death does not delay, and all that has passed will be counted brief; because, as the holy lover of Christ Paul says, “it is appointed for men once [to die].” If, then, with the eyes of the mind we were always to place before us the coming of death, and were so to order our life and [unclear] that we might always be found prepared, how blessed and secure our departure could become.
