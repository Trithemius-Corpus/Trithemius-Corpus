The monk, therefore, who is strong with true virtue neither ventures rashly nor fears imprudently. For fortitude together with constancy is the royal road; from it the rash and obstinate man turns aside to the right, but the fearful, faint-hearted, and timid man to the left. The monk, constant and strong in spirit, never withdraws from the purpose of holiness, but is neither cast down by adversities nor lifted up by prosperities. Hence also the most holy bishop Ambrose says: “The fool is changed like the moon. But the wise man is not broken by fear, not changed by power, not puffed up by prosperities, not overwhelmed by sorrows.” For where wisdom and virtue are, there is constancy and fortitude of spirit. Therefore the wise man is always the same in spirit; he is not diminished or increased by changes of affairs, nor does he waver like a little child so as to be carried about by every wind of doctrine, but he remains perfect in Christ, founded in charity and rooted in the perfection of faith.

Lastly, my dearest brothers, there must be perseverance. Take away perseverance, and neither will virtue have a reward, nor service a recompense, nor a benefit merit grace. For he alone who has persevered to the end will be saved. No virtue has merit if perseverance does not bring it to its increase. The kingdom of eternal blessedness is promised not to those who begin the good, but to those who persevere in the good even unto death, because not in the morning, not at midday, but when evening had come, the denarius was paid to those laboring in the vineyard.

For it is not he who began well who is to be praised, but he who continued well to the end is more honorably regarded. Therefore, my brothers, I am accustomed earnestly to admonish you, and I must never cease, that you should diligently attend to your vocation and walk cautiously in all religion and holiness, as befits sworn soldiers of Christ. For now is the time for doing good, because when the hour of death has arrived it will cut off entirely every capacity for working. And therefore, as long as you live sound and unharmed, do good; always consider the future repayment, and daily grow from spiritual progress. Seek the Lord while he can be found. The life of Christ the Savior should be your imitation; you are bound, from the foundation of your institution, to follow him not by bodily steps, but by your powers, by likeness of affections. Walk cautiously in all things; always place the fear of God before the gaze of your mind in true humility. For great purity is needed by you who have consented, in mind and body, to the holy warfare of Christ. It now belongs to you, O brothers, to despise the world and all things that are in the world for God’s sake with perpetual fortitude; to overcome all motions of fleshly pleasures; to flee sin; to occupy yourselves constantly in good and holy works. Your life also, as far as human weakness allows, ought to be made righteous in angelic purity, and to flee and detest every [unclear]. But the holy angels are never idle, but are always intent, without weariness or tedium, upon the praises of God. The holy angels love God above all things, and do not withdraw from his charity even for a moment. In imitation of them the monk ought to be assiduous in the service of Christ, and always occupied in good works according to the Gospel and the Rule. The monk ought to have most fervent charity for God, and it is proper that he have true and sincere love of neighbor. For burning love toward God perfects the monk and does not allow him to be idle. For the four-times most holy pope Gregory says: “The love of God is never idle. For it works great things if it exists; but if it refuses to work, it was never true love of God.” For if the mad poet spoke truth concerning carnal love, “let him love who does not wish to become idle,” how much more powerfully does the most chaste love of God drive the mind that it possesses toward arduous things. Love God, brothers, with the whole heart, as far as the strength of desire can extend itself, and you will be able most easily to observe all things that have been commanded you. For nothing is difficult to one who loves; but all hard things, all grave and arduous things, burning love makes easy.

*What the ordering of a good monastic life is: Sermon Two.*

We have set forth that the institution of our most holy father Benedict, which by the customary term we call religion or order, shone forth as most excellent at the beginning of its foundation, and was very greatly and manifoldly spread throughout the whole church of the Latins. Hence for very many years in this same order many wise, learned, and most holy men flourished.
