On this occasion at last he instituted an annual chapter for the preservation of the reformation; he transferred the presidency from Saint Matthias to Bursfelde; and he commanded, through the apostolic see, that all things which he had established for the good of the reformation be confirmed.

The first abbot of Bursfelde in the reformation was Johannes, a monk appointed from Reinhausen; the second, Johannes called de Hagen; the third, Theodericus, whom we saw; and the fourth, Johannes, who has now lately been installed in the government. We briefly touch upon the origins of our reformation, dearest brothers, for this reason: that you may know with how much labor it began, and that unto death you may persevere steadfast in the holy observance of the rule. Among all the reformations of our order that preceded it, none was more conformable and agreeable to the one rule of the divine father Benedict, and none more alien from foreign aids. We have read the statutes of nearly all other reformations; but we judge that none cleaves more than ours to the rule, to the purity of Benedict’s intention. This is the Bursfelde reformation, most holy in the order, which teaches true obedience and preserves subjection in humility. In this holy religious life the monks live poor and without private property; they live humble; they live obedient; they live chaste. O venerable union of monks, in which charity reigns doubled, obedience serves, humility complies, prayer keeps watch, perseverance fights, glad chastity triumphs. This is the school of regular discipline, the institute of Christian humility, which directs morals to rectitude and restrains the vices of the flesh. No one in this arena of virtue is permitted by his own will to follow [unclear] against the rule; no one is forbidden to do good. In this holy union one lives according to the purity of the rule; and if anyone begins to stray from the path of rectitude, the diligence of the elders immediately recalls him to the observance of justice. The rule of the most holy father Benedict is read publicly three times in the year, so that no one may try to excuse himself from observance under the pretext of ignorance.

There are two things that promise me great hope for the perseverance of this holy reformation: namely, the annual chapter and the diligent biennial visitation of the monks. But if either of these is neglected, the reformation will soon be in danger. Consider now, dearest brothers, and take the most diligent care you can, that you do not occupy this monastery of the divine bishop Martin uselessly under the title of so venerable a reformation; for unless you live according to the rule and the statutes of the order, purely, holily, and blamelessly, you trust in the name in vain. We shall say to you a few true admonitions for salvation, through which the zealous observance of our Bursfelde reformation will possess its name by merit and dignity.

First of all, before all things, direct the whole power of your striving and your whole desire toward monastic perfection; and as often as you feel it growing lukewarm, renew it by the intent remembrance of the Lord’s passion and most innocent death. For although we are not bound to that perfection by necessity of salvation, nevertheless we are bound to strive for perfection with all our desires. The monk who casts aside the striving to be perfected falls into the pit of failure. The second thing which the sacred custom of our reformation has established for you to keep preserves strongly the striving for perfection in its first desire. Therefore when, awakened, you rise for Prime, do these things in secret. Examine and discuss the course of the coming day; prudently consider what useful thing stands before you to be done. Set in your hearts in what caution you wish to live the whole day, and arm your spirits with patience against all things that might occur as adversities. Whatever you do, perform it in the fervor of charity, and always order all words and deeds to the praise of God. The third thing we judge useful and necessary for you is that, with most devout prayers, you always strive to implore the help of divine aid in all things. And it seems very fitting to us, as we have often said and always admonish, that each of you at least once a day make remembrance of the Lord’s passion, and for the many benefits received from God say an act of thanksgiving one time. That this may be done uniformly by each one of you, listen to this brief form which we have arranged; if you wish, write it out for yourselves.
