What if, as a lover of truth, I speak more boldly: adversities profit us more than prosperous things, if we are learned and not forgetful weighers of our own condition. But the third degree of patience, certainly the highest and most perfect beyond the rest, is rightly granted to those who not only, when there is need, endure contrary things with equanimity for the love of God and the benefit of the brethren, but who also, more fervent and inflamed with the spirit of charity, thrust themselves forward of their own accord and hasten gladly to receive insults and injuries. And this kind of patience is divided in two ways, just as a twofold account of martyrdom is found. For some, though they could have lain hidden in the persecution of believers, with the charity of Christ burning in them, under no compulsion but by their own free will, presented themselves to the tribunals of persecutors; publicly confessing him as redeemer of the world, they received with joy the torments inflicted on them. Others, however, did not thrust themselves forward of their own accord, yet gladly received the martyrdom offered them, always prepared to endure for the faith of Christ whatever the cruelty of tyrants could inflict. To this degree hasten the most shining confessors, by merit very near to them; and if the cruelty of the impious, by persecuting them, did not take them away, nevertheless they in no way lost the glory of martyrdom. For, crucifying their flesh in this world for the name of Christ, they endured both a voluntary and a lasting martyrdom; through abstinence and humility they were made glorious, always remaining steadfast in spirit and most patient, and most ready to receive martyrdom bodily. Therefore take care, O monks, to be imitators of these according to your powers; and since enemies who kill the body for Christ are now lacking, by struggling against the desires of the flesh and by patiently enduring adversities for the love of God, show yourselves martyrs in mind and spirit. The best kind of martyrdom in the peace of the saints is to afflict the flesh for the name of God through abstinence, to desire nothing in this world, always to make progress in good works, and if anything evil should befall, to bear it in all things gladly and patiently with cheerfulness of mind. Henceforth, dearest brethren, live according to this rule of patience, if you desire to reign eternally with Christ. Bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ: let each person gladly bestow upon his brother what he desires to be done for himself.

*On the True Humility of Monks: Nineteenth Homily.*

It is almost the opinion of all the learned, dearest brethren, that the highest and chief virtue of monks is none other than humility, because it is itself the mother and nurse of all virtues. For humility makes the monk; pride destroys and corrupts him. Hence the Lord says through Isaiah: To whom shall I look, if not to the poor little one and the contrite in spirit, and the one who trembles at my words? For God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble, because pride is the beginning of every sin, and without true humility no one can obtain salvation. Since this is so, I think it necessary for me, brethren, to exhort you without ceasing that, fleeing pride, you always preserve profound humility in all your works. Indeed no good work is believed to please the Lord God if it is presented without the seasoning of humility. For humility is the most healthful medicine of faithful souls, which provides for all, because it presses down the swollen ulcers of the spirit, restores what is wasting away, corrects what is depraved, and removes what is overgrown. O humility, mother and parent of all virtues, you strengthen patience, stir up compunction, illuminate charity, adorn chastity, make prayer fruitful, and commend fasting and abstinence. O holy humility, truly beloved of God, you justify the impious through penance; you preserve the just man in the purpose of holiness; you powerfully open heaven to your lovers; you close hell to the penitent. O humility to be venerated, exceedingly pleasing to God, you compelled the Son of the eternal Father to descend from heaven into the womb of Mary, the most chaste ark, the perpetual virgin; you made him appear visible to us, and you laid him, wrapped in poor cloths, in the manger, so that he might adorn us with the ornaments of perpetual blessedness in heaven.
