*How sin is to be fled.*

...does not imitate God but the reward of sin, the devil. Let us love God, and sin will not have dominion in us; let us love Christ, and we shall do nothing contrary to our salvation. There is nothing better, nothing stronger against sin, nothing safer, than to love God, who first loved us and washed us from all sins in the blood of his only-begotten Son. We are bound to that God, and we are obliged to love him with all the powers of the heart for many reasons.

First, indeed, God is supremely to be loved by us for no other reason than for his own sake, who is the highest and sole good; in him alone are rest and the salvation of the soul; without him there is no salvation, no rest, no beatitude, no happiness, either in heaven or on earth. For since God is the cause, maker, and beginning of all things, he alone is the object of the soul’s beatitude; to his everlasting enjoyment one comes by charity alone, and only by purity of mind is one led to love of him. Therefore, since God is the sole and highest good of the soul, he is to be loved by us for his own sake with all our powers. Outside God there is nothing good, nothing holy, nothing by which the soul can be gladdened forever. For God alone is the center of the soul’s beatitude.

Second, we are commanded to love the Lord our God, because he deigns to show himself to us as a most loving father. For thus he taught us to pray: Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Truly God is the father of us all, who created us all, nourishes us, feeds us, and preserves us in being, lest we perish. There has never been any father so indulgent upon earth who loved his only-begotten son with such charity as God almighty, our Father, loves, cherishes, guides, and follows us with loving affection. And therefore we are bound to love him with a pure heart above all things, who gives us both being and living. For he himself is our father; and Adam, who was created the first of all men, did not know us.

Third, we ought to love God above all things because of the likeness by which man was made in the image of God, following his creator in a certain likeness in his intellect. But God is perfect, infinite, and everlasting intellect; by participating in his goodness through grace, our intellect is made blessed in him who created all things. For although there is no comparison between God and man, yet there is, from the nobility of its condition, a likeness of the rational soul itself to its beginning, a likeness of no contemptible dignity. For the prophet says: I have said, you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High. The Lord God also said in the beginning: Let us make man to our image and likeness. Great is the condescension of divine loving-kindness toward our race, which it befits us at all times to remember with pure mind in the observance of charity.

Fourth, we ought to love our God above all things because of his inestimable munificence, whom we acknowledge to have bestowed such great benefits upon us when we did not deserve them. When we did not exist, he first established us in being by his goodness, and ordained us to participation in his glory, which will never end. Thus Scripture says of the nobility of man: Thou hast made him a little lower than the angels; thou hast crowned him with glory and honor, and hast set him over the works of thy hands. Do you wish to know, brothers, in what respect man is less than an angel? Attend carefully to what we say. There are two intellects created from the beginning: angelic and human. The angelic, immediately from the beginning of its creation, was made full and perfect in actuality with respect to all knowable things, and by nature knew and comprehended all that it ought to know; but the human intellect, first indeed, exists in potency with respect to all knowable things, ignorant of all, but in the end, through many actions, it will at last acquire knowledge of all things, as the angelic intellect does. For the perfect consummation of nature, which is present in the angelic intellect from the beginning, will be granted to the human intellect at the end. Hence it is clear that between these two created intellects there is only this difference: that the angelic intellect is what it can be, while the human intellect is to receive the likeness of angelic perfection in the end, that is, after the death of the flesh. For when the angelic intellect was first created, without any action of its own, immovably, substantially, and by nature, it was full and perfect with knowledge of all things. Of these, those who adhered to their beginning, namely God, through love have been confirmed in eternal happiness; but those who, glorying in themselves, withdrew from God in affection are forever wretched and unhappy. But the human intellect, since from the beginning it is created in ignorance and privation of all things, must by action, motion, and art at last arrive at the knowledge of things set before it. As long as we are in the body and are pilgrims away from the Lord, our intellect, because of its excessive commerce with the body, is confounded as if placed in the darkness of ignorance, and is by no means illumined to perfect knowledge of future things. But afterward, when the soul has been separated from the body, then the intellect will enjoy angelic perfection. Therefore we are distant from the angels only to the extent that we participate in bodily impurity.
