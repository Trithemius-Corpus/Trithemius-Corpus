*On the abstinence and fasting of monks.*

He is a man of very keen intellect; although he is wholly ignorant of letters, nevertheless, like another Antony, the hermit of old, he understands the meaning of the Scriptures exceedingly well. We have seen how most eloquently he disputed with a certain master about the sacrament of the altar, a thing at which anyone who has not in the least tasted heavenly things through the pursuit of contemplation will not be able to marvel enough. I have introduced these matters, brothers, so that you may understand that nothing is so difficult that habit, continued over a long time, does not make it easy.

This holy man, as he himself confesses, did not suddenly arrive at the height of this abstinence, but over time, by successive degrees, little by little withdrawing a small amount of food from himself, until at last he was able to abstain altogether. For, as we have said, he has abstained from every corruptible food now for almost the twentieth year. By the example of this man, my brothers, let each of you learn daily to subtract something from his food and drink for the love of God, and let him accustom himself to provide for his body not as much as he can, but as much as is sufficient for the support of nature. For nature, as Boethius bears witness, is content with few and very small things; if you try to force its satisfaction with superfluous foods, what you pour in will become either unpleasant or harmful.

Many dishes beget many illnesses, and gluttony of the belly dulls the mind and torments life. For the wise man says: "Sweet is the sleep of the worker, whether he eats little or much; but the fullness of the rich man does not allow him to sleep." The belly is to be filled without satiety, and one must rise from the table without disgust, so that, clearly, we may eat not as much as we can, but as much as we ought when nature requires it.

For monks, however, variety of foods is especially to be avoided; for anyone who uses various dishes, attempting to put more into the stomach than nature raises up [unclear], feasts not without the vice of gluttony. The strength of our body is to be restored by moderate and simple food, not shamefully oppressed by many and various foods, because the body was not created for food, but food for the body. That variety of foods brings various diseases upon the body we prove from the temperance of the ancients, whom a uniform simplicity of foods preserved for a very long time in this life.

But our most holy founder, Benedict, the most modest hierophant of all the leaders of monks, prescribed for us a rule adorned with the highest discretion; in it, wishing to show monks the measure of foods, he says: "We believe that two cooked dishes are sufficient for the daily meal, whether at the sixth or the ninth hour, for all tables, because of the infirmities of different people, so that one who perhaps cannot eat from one may be refreshed from the other. Therefore let two cooked dishes suffice for all the brothers. And if there are fruits or fresh legumes there, let a third also be added."

If I understand the mind of our lawgiver correctly, he granted monks two cooked dishes where the necessity of infirmity requires it, not for the filling of the appetite of the healthy. For the monk who is healthy is rightly excepted from this indulgence, so that he may be refreshed by one cooked dish only. But in my judgment the infirm man is rightly permitted to be refreshed from both, since excess is feared in no respect.

Yet since not all of us can do all things equally, and one person needs more food, another less, it is impossible to prescribe one form of abstinence for everyone; but it is left to the judgment of each person's discretion, that he should take as much as necessity requires, not as much as the pleasure of appetite commands. This must especially be guarded against: that immoderate satiety of foods should not creep in, for it stains the soul when either disgust follows the meal or heavy sloth follows the mind. Vomiting that proceeds from satiety belongs to dogs rather than to monks.

The Christian religion especially detests a man who is a glutton, because the delights of the servants of God are not food and drink, but piety and justice, by which God is both worshiped in purity of faith, and to each person what is his own is administered justly. Therefore whoever you are who desire to please God with your whole heart, whoever you are who hasten to arrive at the joys of eternal happiness, impose upon yourself a manner of fasting with a measure mixed according to your strength, and apply the greatest care that, while fasting, you be constant in prayer, just in judgments, outstanding in brotherly love, patient in injuries, humble under rebukes, circumspect in speech, clean in conscience, fervent in the love of God, and, with prudence accompanying you, fearful in all your actions.
