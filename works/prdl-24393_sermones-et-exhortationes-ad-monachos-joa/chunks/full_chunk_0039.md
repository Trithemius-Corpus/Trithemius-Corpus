...having exhorted him to true wisdom. Among other things he said: “My son, attend to my wisdom, and incline your ear to my prudence, that you may guard your thoughts, and that your lips may preserve discipline.” For it is no small part of wisdom, dearly beloved brothers, if a monk knows how to discern his thoughts prudently and to guard their movements carefully at all times. It is a great thing to think nothing evil, but it is not a lesser mark of prudence to be able to repel evil thoughts from their very beginning. For the deep reflection of the human heart is ever shifting, varied, manifold, and unstable; to observe its beginnings and restrain its movements is the height of prudence and a great sign of wisdom. If I have called the heart of man a mill, driven by perpetual motion, I have not strayed from the truth, especially if anyone should wish to examine its restlessness most closely. For what else is the human heart than a kind of mill, always in motion, always grinding something? The human heart is never idle, never rests without movement, but is always doing something; and just as an empty mill consumes itself by its own motion, so the heart of man shamefully runs down from itself when thought of the good does not constrain it. It is always moved toward some object with the desire of its love; it is always grinding, always acting, always occupied with something delightful, whether it be good or evil. As the body is not moved without the soul, so the mind of man does not remain fixed in the good unless it has been continually restrained by holy exercises. Therefore the wise man said in Ecclesiasticus: “Joyfulness of heart is the life of man, and the treasure of holiness without failing; and the exultation of a man is length of days. Delight your soul in what is pleasing to God, and hold it in, and gather your heart in his holiness, and drive sorrow far from you.” What is the gathering of the heart, if not the diligent guarding of thought? For he gathers his heart in the holiness of the Lord who continually observes, in the fear of God, the movements of his thoughts. And therefore Scripture says: “With all custody guard your heart, because from it life proceeds.” But thoughts take the beginnings of their movements from those things which are presented by the senses; these wander the more dangerously the less they are examined from the outset by the judgment of reason. Therefore whoever desires to be watchful over his thoughts, whoever wishes to gather his heart in the holiness of the Lord, should first of all guard his outward senses with the greatest diligence, and set the bridle of prudence upon each of them. Hence Scripture commands thus concerning hearing and sight: “Hedge your ears with thorns, and do not listen to a wicked tongue; and make doors for your mouth, and bars for your ears.” “Blessed is the man who stops his ears lest he hear bloodshed, and closes his eyes lest he see evil.” We also read thus in holy Job: “I made a covenant with my eyes, that I would not even think of a virgin.” But against those who have fallen into the pit of sins through the service of their senses, the Lord thus bears witness through the prophet Jeremiah: “Because death has climbed in through your windows; it has entered your houses to destroy the little ones outside, and the young men from the streets.” Death enters our houses through the windows whenever a wicked thought is let into the mind through the service of sight or hearing. For the house is conscience, of which Scripture says: “Unless you hold yourself constantly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown.” [unclear] guards it. Therefore the house of conscience must be fortified by the fear of the Lord; the senses too must be restrained with the greatest diligence, because he does not sufficiently guard his thoughts who does not turn his ears and eyes away from the occasion of evil. And so, if you wish to be a prudent guardian of your thoughts and a true gatherer of the heart in the holiness of the Lord, hedge your ears with the thorns of compunction and of the fear of God, lest they hear evil; and turn away your eyes, lest they introduce sin through sight. What it is not lawful for you to desire, do not look upon with desire; and what it is not expedient to have, do not think upon. Many have perished through the service of the senses, and because they proceed incautiously to see outward things, they have often shamefully lost their inner goods. He who does not fear danger is already surely near destruction; and he who presumes on his own virtue runs hastily toward the ruin of sin. He who flashes his eye into the brilliance of lightning will be blinded for the moment; and he who gazes upon the beauty of women will not be free from danger. I praise the prudence of that monk who is alone in the midst of crowds, and sets a censor over his senses, lest he admit anything evil to his lady.
