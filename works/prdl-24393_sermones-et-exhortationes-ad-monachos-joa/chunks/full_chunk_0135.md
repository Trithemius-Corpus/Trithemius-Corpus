*How sin is to be avoided.*

O voice by far the most terrible of all terrible things; O sentence to be feared and exceedingly dreaded by all mortals: to be hated by Christ for one’s deserts, to be reproached by the judge, to be separated from the fellowship of glory, and to retain no further hope of recovery. Consider now, brothers, the punishments of the impious, most certainly to come, and compare whatever pains of this life, whatever torments of this passing age, and all imaginable tortures of this world, with those everlasting punishments of the damned; and they will be as nothing to something, imagination to truth, a dream to waking. You will easily overcome the temptations of the flesh and the law of sin if you continually keep thoughts of these things in memory. O eternity, what an inconceivable time; an age without end, a world without time. Blessed is he who now, in time, foresees how he may fare well in eternity after this very brief life.

The fourth thing that can draw a person back from sin is to have continual remembrance in the heart of future and everlasting happiness. Truly the sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the glory to come, which will be revealed in the saints, where there will be no discord, no dissonance at all, but the highest peace of all and the everlasting concord of the rejoicing saints. In the heavenly blessedness of the saints there is true joy without sadness, peace without discord, goodness without malice, gladness without end, light without darkness, everlasting life without pain. There is sweetness without bitterness, rest without labor, praise without reproach, love without fault, brightness without cloud, satisfaction without weariness. Eternity remains without tedium; goodness abounds without defect; happiness continues without misery. For where God is all in all, perfect and full joy will be present to everyone.

There the soul, or holy mind, is joined to its first principle through love; the intellect is perfectly illuminated for the knowledge of the highest good; and from these two things the enjoyment of eternal happiness is poured into those who rejoice. To know and love God in the kingdom of eternity is to possess, by enjoyment, the very glory of happiness. For this was man created: that he should understand God his creator to be the highest good; that, understanding him, he should love him above all things; and that, loving him, he should enjoy and possess him forever. For the attainment of this everlasting happiness, dearest brothers, all good things must be done, even with the greatest labor; all adversities must be endured most patiently, since the very brief exercise of virtue brings forth for us an eternal reward. Hear what Saint John the bishop says on this matter: “If every day,” he says, “we had to endure torments; if, finally, we had to bear Gehenna itself for a little time, so that we might be able to see Christ reigning in eternal glory and be joined forever to the number of his saints, nothing sorrowful that could happen to us would, by any comparable reckoning, be worthy, if by it we might deserve to possess the glory of so great a good.”

But one does not arrive at the highest good except through the greatest labors, since, as [unclear] testifies in the holy Gospel, [unclear] vice and loves the good of virtue. Every human being who lives according to the command of reason is a lover of virtue; but he who, filthy in the dung of vices, rolls about, lives like a beast and not like a human being. But for you, my brothers, vices must above all be detested, and virtues loved, since for this reason you have despised the world, that you might profitably live in the monastery a life estranged from sins and adorned with holy virtues. Therefore virtues must be cultivated by you, not images of virtues; I say cultivated not with incense, not with sacrifice, but with love and with the purpose of a most upright will. To cultivate virtues is nothing other than to embrace them in the soul and, for the love of God, to keep them inviolate even unto death. For as Lactantius says, the whole duty of virtue is not to sin; and therefore there is no true virtue except that which, through faith in Christ, tends to that end where the highest good of man is, than which nothing can be better. But he is not virtuous who does not love God, since charity is the sum of all virtues. Therefore, if you love God with your whole heart above all things, you are truly virtuous. Virtue is the way of the good, by which, through faith and love of Jesus Christ, one comes to the kingdom. He who truly loves God does not sin, out of love of virtue. Consider your dignity, my brothers, and flee sin, since everyone who is swollen with pride, who is defiled by lust, who is infected by the other plagues of the soul, does not live as a human being, but is confounded in himself and proceeds into death. And the more anyone slips into sin, the farther he withdraws from God and the nearer he approaches to hell. But since all human beings naturally seek the good, it is most shameful to love sin, since every sinner abandons the good and turns himself toward evil.
