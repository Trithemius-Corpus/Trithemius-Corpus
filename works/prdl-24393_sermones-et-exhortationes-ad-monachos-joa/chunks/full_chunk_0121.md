For there are some who write that from this monastic religion of ours fifteen thousand six hundred, men as well as nuns, have been entered in the catalogue of the saints. The multitude of these saints in the order, brothers, is set before us for imitation, not for vain glorying. What can their multitude contribute to our salvation, if we do not imitate the manner of life in which they went before us? Whenever we desire profitably to praise the most holy men of our order, let us hasten by holy conduct to come into their fellowship. For then we shall glory fruitfully in the multitude of the saints of our order, if, living rightly, we earnestly strive to increase their number. Therefore indeed we set before you so great a multitude of saints, so that, when you see that many before you in this very order conquered the world and the flesh, you may at last rise up from your lukewarm manner of life. The virtue that God’s mercy granted to them he will not deny to you, if you ask in true charity.

They conquered the world because they loved Christ with entire devotion, despising all things for love of him. They despised all things, and the Lord Jesus provided for them whatever seemed necessary to them for the sustenance of life. This fervor of devotion among monks endured after the first institution of the order for many years with great constancy, and converted the souls of many thousands of people to the Lord. But nothing among human things is stable; nothing among perishable things is everlasting. Holy religion brought riches upon the monks, until in time the daughter suffocated the mother. For, as Scripture says, all those who wish to become rich fall into the temptations of the devil. The beloved grew fat and kicked back; made fat and enlarged with earthly possessions, he abandoned the holy pursuits of the first institution, went after vanities and false madnesses, and an abundance of temporal things made him needy and wretched in spiritual riches.

This pitiable deformity of our order began almost two hundred years after the passing of the divine father Benedict, and with time it grew more and more, until at last the harmful poison spread through the whole order. But this deformity occupied the order not suddenly, but through the succession of many years, until it had taken over almost the whole of it, although almighty God never left it wholly without good men, but always reserved for himself some who had by no means bent their knees to Baal. For men of mercy arose, whose righteous deeds will never be handed over to oblivion; pitying the ruin of the order, they put their shoulder under the labor and, the Lord generously granting aid, restored it in many places.

Indeed many varied particular reformations flourished often in the order at different times, but there was never any general one that brought the whole order back to the integrity of regular observance. Finally, we now see that the particular restorations, which we have said were many, have almost all collapsed again. Most recently, however, namely in the year of the Christians one thousand four hundred and twenty, when the general council was being celebrated at Constance, a certain new and, up to now, last particular reformation took its beginning in Germany. It took its name from a certain monastery in the regions of Saxony, then indeed the poorest of all, which is called Bursfelde.

The first author and leader of this most holy reformation was Johannes Rode, abbot of the distinguished monastery of Saint Matthias at Trier. Having been appointed general visitor of the order by the aforesaid Council of Constance, lest, while preaching to others, he himself should be judged reprobate, he first displayed reformation in himself. For first of all he reformed both himself and the monks of the said monastery; he issued constitutions conforming to the Rule; he obtained confirmation of the holy work from the apostolic see. But among all the monasteries of the order, Bursfelde, a humble and rather poor monastery, first received this reformation, which it afterwards extended to Reinhausen and to a few monasteries of Saxony neighboring it.

When at last Johannes Rode, abbot of Saint Matthias and leader of our reformation, had died, since in that place no suitable successor was found for extending the undertaking that had been begun, Johannes de Hagen, abbot of Bursfelde, who had already reformed certain monasteries, a holy man and very zealous for the order, was elected at the urging of Jacob, archbishop of Trier. Content with his poverty, he did not consent to leave Bursfelde, lest he seem to have preferred riches to poverty. He therefore remained in his own monastery, and another was appointed in the place of the first reformer. Since he seemed comparable to his predecessor neither in learning nor in zeal, he was able to accomplish little for the continuation of the undertaking begun, nor to enlarge the reformation among many.
