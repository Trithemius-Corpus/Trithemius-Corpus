*On the daily expiation of sins*

To endure. For we must make satisfaction for our sins; but if we neglect to do this in this life, in the life to come we shall necessarily do it under compulsion. There are among you some who take corrections badly; to them we announce [unclear], since it is necessary that in the future they endure very grievous things, those who here refuse to endure small and light ones. But I, considering their weakness and ignorance, am grievously tormented within myself, because I see their minds wounded and yet I am afraid to apply the necessary medicines. For if I apply remedies suited to their ailments, I fear that, becoming fainthearted, they may fall into despair; or certainly, given over to a reprobate sense, they may withdraw entirely from their holy purpose. Therefore I use as much discretion as I can, as all of you will bear me witness, preferring to send the weak, through patience with charity, to purgatory, rather than through [just?] severity to have them descend in despair to hell.

For who in this mortal life has ever been able to make satisfaction for sins, who has applied the good will of penance? For good will alone commends the works of penitents. But since many are punished unwillingly, they are still far removed from perfect satisfaction. Believe me, my dearest brothers, that satisfaction for sins is commended more by suffering than by doing. For so our Lord and Savior himself, Jesus Christ, expiated the sin of the whole world by suffering on the altar of the cross. Would that you knew what an effective medicine it is for a soul befouled by sins to receive proclamations and corrections with humble patience, and also, for the increase of perfect satisfaction, to bear injuries and insults with equanimity. I am certain that humble patience in adversity far surpasses every kind of satisfaction; and the readier the mind has been to bear insults and injuries for the love of Christ, the more easily and quickly it will obtain from the Lord pardon for all its sins and a fuller indulgence.

For this place in which we now speak is the workshop of purgation and expiation, in which zeal for regular discipline holds chief place, discretion presides over examination, and charity directs and completes all things. Therefore in this very place patience is very necessary for you; without its presence no satisfaction will be consummated. Whatever monk is a zealous lover of his salvation and loves this place and workshop of expiation never goes forth from it without having been made better than himself. For this place is to be feared by demons and is terrible to them, but dear and greatly venerable to the holy angels of God; in it the fault of humble monks is washed away, and whatever is committed openly is also expiated publicly before all. But if any guilt of hidden fault has been committed by anyone, the secret confession of the penitent will likewise purge it away. In both cases pure confession with humility is required, and both here and there patience is declared necessary for you.

Therefore, whoever you are, monk, who desire to be good, prepare your soul in all patience, so that both in adversity and in prosperity you may keep the tranquility of your mind most constant. When you have been proclaimed by some fellow-brother for your negligences, bear it patiently with all humility, faithfully bringing to memory how innocently the Son of God was accused for you. For if that just and innocent one, who committed no sin, suffered insults, why should you not patiently bear rebukes from a senior, you who offend daily in many things? He most patiently endured innumerable injuries for your salvation; and why should you not, for your sins, humbly and patiently endure brief and few admonitions? For if you are justly proclaimed in judgment, and are blamed for fault, you must necessarily be patient, since you yourself have deserved what you suffer; and the humble toleration of rebukes will be imputed to you as satisfaction for sins. But if you did not deserve the proclamation in judgment, nevertheless bear it patiently for the love of Christ; now it will be reckoned to you not only as satisfaction for preceding sins, but much more as justification. Therefore whether a monk is proclaimed justly or unjustly, he has no just cause for defense, because if he has sinned, he suffers according to his deserts; but if he is innocent, he is the more bound to patience.
