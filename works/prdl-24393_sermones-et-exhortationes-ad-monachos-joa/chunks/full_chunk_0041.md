For you have heard the fearful sentence of the Lord, and you are not unaware of the command of our holy lawgiver, who says in the Rule: “Buffooneries, idle words, and words that provoke laughter we condemn to perpetual exclusion in all places, and we do not permit the disciple to open his mouth for such speech.”

Here holy father Benedict forbade three kinds of words to monks, and condemned them to perpetual exclusion in all places: namely, buffoonish, idle, and laughter-provoking words. What buffoonery is, the interpretation of the grammarians has declared to us. For the vice of buffoonery is opposed to refinement and modesty, and is a certain wanton jocularity provoking the laughter of hearers, without shame or honor, immoderate in words and gestures, which do not accord with reason, do not befit a good man, and are not suitable for a wise man. Buffoonery is so called from a buffoon, who follows the houses of the rich, provoking his hearers to laughter by wanton and dishonorable words and gestures for the sake of a reward or a coin.

And this kind of words holy father Benedict forbade to monks and condemned utterly, because those who serve God are rightly commanded to abstain perpetually, in his sight, from foul and dishonorable words. Between buffoonery and much-speaking there seems to be this difference: the former exceeds by reason of dishonor, the latter by reason of excess. The former is foul and dishonorable; the latter is superfluous without virtue.

Foolish talk is rightly joined with these, since it is forbidden not only to monks but also to all Christians. Hence the holy apostle of Christ says: “But fornication and all uncleanness or covetousness, let it not even be named among you, as befits saints; nor filthiness, nor foolish talk, nor buffoonery, which are not to the purpose, but rather thanksgiving.” Idle words are likewise forbidden to all monks, because they are prohibited by the Lord, as you have heard, in the holy Gospel. Words that provoke laughter also do not befit Christian people, and much less are they suitable for cloistered men. For the monk, who is dead to the world, is commanded always to think on his last things; but he is bound everywhere, for love of God, to despise laughter and every insolence of the present life.
