... brothers, and those who have now preceded us in this monastery. Just as there is no memory among us of those who have gone before, so also we shall be in perpetual oblivion among them and among those who follow. Let us ponder these things beforehand, dearest brothers, while we live, lest we fall into death unprepared. Just as we hasten daily toward death, so daily let us prepare ourselves, as far as we are able. He dies securely who in life is adorned with good and holy works. Nor let the hope of a longer life deceive you, for the longer we live in this world, the shorter is the life you will have. Behold, daily we die as we tend toward death; daily one part of life is charged against us; and the longer our life extends, the more the remaining time of life is shortened. All the time that we have lived is wholly gone when it has passed; what we live today we divide moment by moment with death; how much time for living remains to us in the future we do not know. Why, then, do we trust in the vanity of this life, when we are soon to die? Thus let us continually think, brothers, that we shall soon come to that place to which all our elders have gone before us. We are all born under this law: that we should soon die. And let death appear terrible to him whose whole life is polluted in the pleasure of the flesh, and who stores up nothing good in heaven. But we who place all our hope in Christ Jesus our Savior, despising all the pleasures of the world for love of him: why should we fear death? Yet since the mind is most terrible of all terrors [unclear], we shall be able to drive out indiscreet fear of dying in this way, if we prepare ourselves daily as though about to die. It is one thing to have fear of death, another to have trembling. For even the Lord Jesus, the patron over death, felt the trembling of approaching death, to show the two properties of his nature. Therefore continual meditation on death is altogether necessary for us who are weak; indeed, the mind not yet perfected in the love of Christ is roused to the grace of compunction as if by a violent impulse. For the memory of future death, like a nail driven into wood, thus pierces the mind with a useful dread. The memory of death introduces arduous penitence and true sobriety of mind. The memory of death is necessary for beginners, since conversion begins from servile fear, and afterward is gradually raised in charity through filial [fear]. The memory of death and of the strict examination of the future judgment of God is especially necessary for those who, through an evil and long-standing habit, are drawn to sin. And there are among you some who are too lukewarm and negligent in the spiritual warfare; admonishing them, we exhort them as a father that they remember they are mortal, and live henceforth in such a way that they do not fear to die. For it is very dangerous to live in that state in which one would be afraid to die. We must live in such a way that we await death securely; this can finally happen if, preserving ourselves in all purity of mind and body, we continually burn in the exercises of divine charity. Let us live, as far as can be done, innocently, according to the rule of our divine father Benedict, which we have professed, and we shall have good hope that almighty God will have mercy on us, for he does not permit a man of good will to perish. Let us always think of God as present with us everywhere: he sees all things, knows all things, understands all things, rewards all things. And let us live in that purity in which we have promised to serve his most holy majesty.

*The end of the twenty-fifth homily of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to the monks.*

*Here is the end of the first book of exhortations to the monks, written by my own hand, of me, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the day of the month of June, in the year of Christians 1486, in the fourth indiction, in the thirty-third year of my age. I have dedicated this work to my predecessor, now abbot of Seligenstadt, most dear to me.*
