*On Patience and Gentleness*

If you wish to keep the bounds of endurance, first of all there will have to be someone present who inflicts an insult. “If I had lived,” says the lazy and sensual monk, “in the days of the martyrs, I would have feared to undergo no kinds of punishments for the love of Christ; I would voluntarily have exposed my body to the torturers, so that I might carry off the crown of martyrdom.” Great is the rashness of folly. You have never been able to suffer a word patiently for the love of God, and do you promise the steadfastness of the holy martyrs? You fear a light correction, you who promise that you will give your body for Christ.

If martyrdom delights you, hear what the most blessed pope Gregory says. If, with Christ helping us, we strive to preserve the virtue of patience and live in the peace of the Church, we also hold the palm of martyrdom. For we can be martyrs without sword and flame, if we truly keep patience in our soul. To die at the hands of a persecutor is martyrdom in open deed; but to bear insults and love the one who hates you is martyrdom in hidden thought. Patience, however, is to endure another’s evils with equanimity, and not to be gnawed by any grief against the one who brought the evil.

What now will you say, lazy monk, who, incapable of suffering the very light martyrdom of words, boast so insolently of steadfastness in shedding blood? Learn, wretch, to have patience in light matters, and consider that, as Scripture says, if a man is unfaithful in a little, who would entrust to him what is greater? You murmur at everything that happens in the monastery; when rebuked, however gently, for your grave offenses and negligences, you bear it most impatiently. In your eyes no one walks rightly in the monastery; no one except you, in your estimation, conducts himself religiously. You alone, as it seems to you, are holy in the monastery; you, most learned of all, concede that no one knows what you alone do not know. You think yourself, above all others, worthy of all honors, because you not only lack patience, but retain neither true humility nor obedience.

This is not the way by which one goes to the kingdom of heaven: when in the school of humility a monk is proud, and by his conduct a Christian lives contrary to Christ. But a monk, the solemnly sworn servant of Jesus Christ, in order that he may imitate his Lord, ought not to be proud but humble, obedient not rebellious, gentle not cruel, nor insolent but mild. But a monk who is proud, wrathful, contentious, and a lover of his own will, such as, alas, those set over temporal affairs often are, whom among us according to the Rule we call cellarers: their pride has made for many a road to destruction.

Grief compels me to speak, and shame forbids me, although you all know the fall by which the devil lately brought shame upon us in the case of a brother. Who in our whole congregation was prouder than he? Who went about filled with greater insolence? But because he seemed among us to have greater experience in temporal matters, on that account he considered himself superior to all. The Almighty permitted him to fall, so that he who, [unclear], had refused to recognize his own weakness, at last confounded, not without the scandal of many, might understand that God is the adversary of the proud.

I grieve vehemently, and more than can be said; the fault of the man who fell seems to me heavier, because by his fall we are all confounded. May God grant him to do true penance, so that those who had been scandalized by his fall may be edified by his satisfaction before God and men. Pride has always been destructive to monks; and the mind of a man which it has once invaded it sinks into the pit of all vices. Beware pride, my brothers, because it is the beginning of every sin; it is that which through the envy of the devil introduced death into the world. Pride is the death of all virtues, and the source and spring of all vices. Humility, however, the nurse of virtues, most befits monks, because it makes them both mild and gentle. For every proud man is impatient, and every arrogant man an enemy of gentleness. He who is humble of heart has patience in adversities for the love of God; and he who is tranquil in soul in the face of injuries merits a double crown.

A patient man in adversities becomes close to God; but he who is proud and impatient gladdens his enemies. For Scripture says: Better is the patient man than the strong man, and he who rules his own soul than the conqueror of cities. Likewise the holy bishop Augustine says: The just and pious man ought to be ready to endure patiently the malice of those whom he desires to become good, so that rather the number of the good may increase, and not that by equal malice someone should add himself to the number of the evil.
