*Homily XIII. Folio XXXII. Of monks. Homily XIV.*

Although our whole way of life, my dearest brothers, may rightly be called penance, since we seem both dead to ourselves and already buried to the world, yet because we are surrounded by mortal flesh and sojourn in the midst of a sinful nation, it cannot happen that, by the impulse of our own frailty, we do not daily offend in some matter. For the expiation of this, satisfaction must be made by doing penance not only generally but also specifically. For penance is necessary for all mortals, by which they both lament the evils they have committed, with the intention of amendment, and thereafter resolve, so far as can be done, to live without sin.

It is proper to monks to do penance without interruption, and continually to pray both for their own sins and for those of others. For the monk does not have the office of teaching, as Saint Jerome says, but plainly that of mourning: either, if he is weak, he mourns himself, or, if perfect, the world, and in fear awaits the coming of the Lord. If you are a perfect monk, do penance for the sins of others; but if you are weak, it is right that you repent the more for your own. Therefore the monk must always do penance, for he is appointed before God as intercessor not only for his own sins but also for the offenses of others.

But who among you, brothers, is not weighed down by his own offense? Who, I ask, among you does not need penance? I see you offend daily in many things, and I grieve; I perceive how you neglect penance for past things, and I tremble. Do you not recognize that you are mortal? And why do you not make satisfaction for the name of monk, by doing the penance necessary in time? Remember, I beg you, the threatening Scripture, which says: Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For his wrath will come suddenly, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you. For you will not escape death, which is at the door for all of you; and it lies hidden in ambush for the younger also, but when you least expect it the spirit, compelled from the body, will depart.

Therefore, if you recognize that you are sinners, if you desire thus to come to the mercy of God, now, I pray, take up penance at once, and amend your conduct in all things according to the purity of the rule. Let it be enough to have neglected true penance up to this point, to have continued for so long a time in a way of life too lukewarm. For God, who overlooks the sins of men on account of penance, has not promised prolongation of life to those who defer repentance. Consider the long-suffering of God, who has mercifully borne with you sinning daily for so long, and do penance, so that you may find the remission you desire.

For the Lord says through the prophet: I do not desire the death of the wicked, but that he be converted from his evil way and live. Likewise in the holy Gospel he says: Do penance; for the kingdom of heaven will draw near. Penance is a great good for mortals: it cleanses filthy souls and recalls minds from destined destruction. Here blessed Augustine says: Penance heals infirmities and raises the dead; it increases health; it preserves grace. Penance restores movement to the lame, sight to the blind, abundance to the barren, strength to the weak; it drives away vices, adorns virtues, fortifies and strengthens the mind.

But that is true penance, brothers, which its necessary parts truly commend as a whole: where contrition pierces the mind; where the hidden sin is reported in humble and pure confession; where obedience makes satisfaction according to its powers; where the penitent so grieves over past things that he truly takes care at all times against future ones. True penance is that which love of God adorns with groans and tears, where the sinner grieves more over offense against God than he fears the torment of hell.

And who of us, most beloved brothers, would not grieve? Who would not groan that he has sinned against the law of monks of the Most High, from whom we have not only life, but also await the promise of eternal happiness? Woe to us, brothers, if in this life we have neglected to do true penance, which, as monks, we ought always to do. Not pleasures, not delights: our way of life ought to be occupied in the crucified Jesus Christ alone, and we ought to keep ourselves estranged from the consolations of the world. The consolations of the world do not befit you, O monk; not laughter, not guffaws, not any consolation of the flesh, but sorrow and bitterness with tears. If you do not give yourself continually to these, you have lost the name of monk. It is not lawful for you to rejoice with the age, you who are dead to the world; nor ought you to seek any comforts of the present life, you who promised the Lord, according to the monastic rule, to serve in solitude. It is yours, monk,
