Nor does he pray only for his own sins, but also for the sins of the whole world. The greatest remedy against the exaltation of the mind is the remembrance of one’s own offenses.

The third mark of humility is that he desires to keep all the good works he does within himself in secret, and hides his own progress out of zeal for humility. He who is humble does not boast in vain glory, nor seek praise from men, because he hopes for the glory of eternal happiness. Whoever among you considers himself humble and a true monk should not on that account despise this sign of profound submission and abasement for God’s sake.

The fourth indication of true humility is that, voluntarily descending into the abyss of contempt for himself, he considers all his brethren, even those openly sinning, better than himself before God; forgetting the good things of his own, he wisely considers his evils, while passing over the evils of others in silence, and opportunely making known whatever good things they have done. This is the condition of true humility: always to admire and proclaim the good works of others, and prudently to make little of and hide one’s own, for the avoidance of pride.

Having attained the fifth sign of humility, he is quiet in his simplicity and does not speak of the defects of others, unless compelled either by reason of office or by the command of charity. Yet prudent humility preserves moderation in correction, so that the one whom it cannot make better by correcting him more severely, it strives to admonish gently and meekly. It also sometimes wisely dissembles rebukes, and waits for and observes a more opportune time.

The sixth sign of humility is shown when a monk, from love of humility, is found slow and not ready to accuse and rebuke the sins of others. For he who is truly humble always considers his own weakness, and silently weighs in secret how many evils he himself has committed against God’s justice. Hence he greatly fears to rebuke the deeds of others, because he recognizes himself as a sinner and therefore unworthy to judge others. We except the prelates and rulers of monks from this norm of humility, insofar as we know them to be bound to the correction of their subjects.

The seventh sign of true humility is that he gladly receives corrections and amendments for his faults and negligences, and is embittered by no grief against the one who rebukes him. Whenever the humble man is rebuked by someone, he rejoices in spirit with the prophet, saying: Let the just man correct me in mercy and rebuke me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head. Of the proud, however, Scripture says: They hated the one who rebuked in the gate. He who is truly humble loves the one who rebukes him, but flees the flatterer.

The eighth indication of true humility is clearly shown when, rebuked, he does not defend, deny, cloak, or excuse his fault, but confesses it with simple shamefacedness. The truly humble monk, when he is rebuked, does not murmur; he does not protest that he has exceeded the measure of equity, but at once, falling to the ground before the judge, cries out most meekly: I have sinned; I confess the fault; I beg pardon; I promise amendment.

The ninth indication of monastic humility makes one mild, sweet, and modest in speech, and not loud in voice. The monk whom you see to be loud and hasty in speaking you will judge not humble, but proud and rash. The humble man speaks modestly and almost under silence, and is always found readier to listen than to speak before others. It is shameful for a monk to raise his voice too much when he speaks, since, crucified to the world, he is commanded to love silence.

He possesses the tenth indication of perfect humility who is accustomed to interpret everything he has heard about his neighbor in the better part. For our Savior says in the holy Gospel: Do not judge, and you shall not be judged. And again: With what measure you have measured, it shall be measured back to you. The Apostle also says: Do not judge before the time, until the Lord comes, who will both illuminate the hidden things of darkness and make manifest the counsels of hearts. Who are you, who judge another’s servant? To his own lord he stands or falls, and not to you.

The eleventh indication of holy humility is that he despises no man in his heart on account of any excesses whatever, but places all before himself in the merit of sanctity. He also considers all who offend publicly to be better than himself, and therefore condemns no one. He always thinks with himself in his heart what evil he himself has done, but humbly weighs the good deeds of others as outweighing his own. Hence he spurns no one, makes little of no one, despises no one, because he desires all to be saved in the Lord.
