And Saint Augustine warns us how necessary it is to pay to God what has been promised, saying: “There are certain things which, even without vowing them, we owe to the Lord. But after we promise them to the Lord, we are constrained of necessity to pay them.”

But now, my brothers, I ask you carefully to consider what you have vowed to the Lord your God, and diligently to weigh under what necessity of paying what you promised you have been placed. For you vowed to your God, not to man, obedience and the conversion of your morals according to the Rule of our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and perpetual stability in this order of religion. The obedience, however, which you promised to God, you owe to his vicar, a man, whom you have learned from the Rule which you profess to bear the place of Christ in the monastery. What sort the conversion of morals ought to be according to the Rule you see plainly set down in the fourth chapter, on the instruments of good works; in it you are not ignorant of the deed of all monastic perfection [unclear] at the beginning of his profession.

Indeed, all of you have heard what our divine father commanded to be done concerning novices, and you are not ignorant of what was done in the case of each one of you at the beginning of his profession. For thus he says: “But he who is to be received shall promise in the oratory before all concerning his stability, the conversion of his morals, and obedience, before God and his saints, so that if ever he acts otherwise, he may know that he will be condemned by God, whom he mocks.” Consider now therefore, brothers, under how great a necessity of regular observance you have been placed, since unless you fulfill in deed what you promised in receiving the monastic habit, you will have to be judged before the almighty Lord not as servants of God, not as friends, not as monks, but as mockers.

Therefore always be mindful of those things which the Rule commands you, according to which you promised to order your morals; and labor as true soldiers of Jesus Christ, continually keeping watch in the fear of the Lord, since, as Scripture says, unless you hold yourselves steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown. Consider your calling: because the Lord has called you not to idleness, not to the pleasures of this world, nor to pursuing the desires of the flesh, but rather to continual labor, to abstinence from all delights, and to the constant mortification of the flesh under the image of the Crucified, so that you may bear his stigmata in your hearts.

Certainly you have now put your hands to the plough of the holy heavenly agriculture, in which you are commanded to sweat continually in labor, and not only never to look back, but, always advancing from virtue to virtue, to strive toward the summit of supreme perfection. For to live as a monk is nothing other than to shun human company and, in solitude, by the desire of most fervent love, actually to please God alone. Hence Scripture says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he will sit alone and be silent, because he will lift himself above himself.”

Therefore, if you wish to be perfect, you who by regular institution are bound to love perfection, begin from the way of life which Christ taught; and since already for love of him you despise all things, do not neglect humbly to conquer, overcome, and despise even yourselves. But the servant of Christ has nothing except Christ; he loves nothing, cares for nothing, seeks nothing except Christ, so that he may be perfect and may please him whose soldiery he has voluntarily professed. But the monk who has anything besides Christ cannot be perfect, since true love is not divided, nor is the mind truly lived among diverse and contrary things.

For this reason the Lord says: “If you wish to be perfect, go and sell all that you have and give to the poor, and come, follow me.” You indeed, most beloved brothers, have already, as I trust, left the world and whatever you had in the world; and not only all the goods of the world which you possessed and could have had, but you have also laid aside every desire of having. And you have come to this monastery of the divine prelate Martin, in which the poverty of temporal goods is great; and this alone has been given to us in place of riches: not to find causes of avarice.
