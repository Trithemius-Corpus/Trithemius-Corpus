But the human intellect, which our teachers have called the soul, was indeed created at the beginning in complete ignorance, and knows none of those things which the angelic intellect understands by nature. These two intellects differ as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, possession and privation, beginning and end, simple and composite, substance and accident, nature and art, truth and likeness. For, as divine Scripture bears witness, all things that are present in the angelic intellect by nature and from the beginning the human intellect will attain through the arts at the end, as the Lord Jesus says in the holy Gospel: “In the resurrection of the dead they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but will be like the angels of God in heaven.”

For in order that the mind may be exercised toward understanding through many labors, as though through the arts, it has been placed for a brief time in the workshop of the body. If there, turned toward God, it duly completes its craft through love, then at last, departing from here, it becomes equal to an angel. But if, consenting to the impulses of the flesh, it has not kept its order, then disturbed and wretched, like the apostate spirits who fall, it will depart from the body to be tormented forever. For since our mind, or soul, was created in ignorance and privation of all things, it must by continual action, movement, and art come to knowledge of things. The end indeed makes the human intellect equal to the angelic, changing its potency into act, and will make from privation possession, from empty full, from imperfect perfect, from deprived possessing, from dark luminous, from nocturnal diurnal, from lunar solar, and from earthly heavenly. This end, my dearest brothers, is the beginning of eternal salvation; no one can happily come to it who does not strive with all his powers toward the summit of perfection.

But since it is difficult, in the midst of crowds, always to keep unbroken this necessary effort of advancement, where a thousand occasions for sinning are offered, the holy fathers who founded the monastic order taught us by word and example to flee the world, and not only in mind but also in body to avoid frequent intercourse. Of those who were stronger in spirit, many flew to the hermitage, so that they might become nearer to the Lord in mind the farther they withdrew in body from the world. Of these we read what was said by the prophet Isaiah: “The wilderness will rejoice and will blossom like the lily; sprouting it will sprout, and it will exult, rejoicing and praising.” Others, wishing to profit many, established cenobia, in which they placed a multitude of monks, whose manner of life would be separated from the peoples and yet would be social among themselves. For it does not suit all to sit in the hermitage, for whom it is more fitting to serve the Lord in the midst of brothers. For our monasteries too have their own solitude, so that whoever wishes to be solitary can be so in the midst of brothers, provided he maintains the privacy of the cell in which he dwells. For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is called solitary, because in mind and body he is separated from the tumults of this world.

Therefore, as I am about to make today’s sermon to your charities, I desire to admonish you, as much as I desire your progress in spiritual life. I ask you, I say, and exhort you in the bowels of brotherly charity, to love the quiet of the cell as the workshop of your salvation, in which there is great peace for those who love God, and great advantage for perfecting the intellect toward knowledge of the highest truth. For the prophet Isaiah says: “In silence and hope will be your strength.” Whoever does not love the solitude of the cell and the quiet of silence by no means loves purity of mind, but, wandering in body, always draws new vanities into his spirit. Hence blessed Jerome says: “Why do we desire the crowdedness of cities, we who are reckoned from singularity? For to me the town is a prison, solitude a paradise.”

If, brothers, the hermitage and solitude delighted us, this place would afford great advantage to our purpose, where, apart from poor peasants, a guest is rarely met; no one disturbs our quiet, no one troubles us, no one brings in spectacles or rumors of momentary novelty. But I see few among you who love inward quiet, and I grieve vehemently, because I know very well that a monk cannot make progress in spiritual life when it is clear that he does not love the quiet of the cell.
