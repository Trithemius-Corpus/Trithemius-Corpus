*On the daily renewal of holy purpose.*

Let pernicious thoughts find a place where they may be strengthened. Just as rust consumes iron, and a garment is gnawed by worms, so idleness weakens the mind; and virtue of the soul, long neglected, gradually ebbs away and is utterly overthrown. Therefore flee idleness, and overcome sloth. But since we cannot remain firmly and without change of mind in one state of divine love, it is necessary for us, lest we ebb away entirely, to renew our holy purpose daily, so that as often as, through the negligence of human weakness, we begin, however slightly, to grow dull in spiritual fervor, so often, examining ourselves by the memory of things to come and by contemplation of the Lord’s Passion, we may at once recall our conscience to its former purpose of making progress.

But this we must guard against with the utmost care: that we not put off this holy effort to make progress until tomorrow, since it has often done harm to have postponed what could have been [unclear]. Hence the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For suddenly his wrath will come, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you.” Augustine also says: “Why do you put off the good until tomorrow, saying: life will still be long? For who knows whether it will be long? And if it is not good, what profit is there that it will be long?” Without a good life, the conduct of a monk should be called death rather than life.

The remedies of a more amended life, therefore, must not be deferred by any delays, lest the time for conversion perish through tardiness, when already no postponement of repentance is granted. For he who promised pardon to the penitent did not promise tomorrow. No one who fears concerning himself delays being converted to repentance, nor extends today’s salvation into tomorrow. But every man who knows that he is set in tepidity of mind and who defers amendment of life from day to day is deceived by hope of a longer [life], since if he knew that he would die tomorrow, today without doubt he would change his purpose. Hope of a longer life has miserably deceived many, who, while they daily defer the amendment of their conduct, suddenly fall into the depth of death.

Which of you is there, brothers, who does not resolve in his mind to assume the purpose of a more amended life before he dies? For I believe that each one of you intends to exercise still greater devotion toward God before he falls into the danger of death. And why do you wait so long for the day? Why do you put it off from day to day? Behold, you who now live in health and do not give yourselves to the spiritual exercises of divine love when you can, and when the Lord patiently waits: the last day will come for each of you, and now it is hastening, on which, with the angel of death pressing hard, the power whose effect you delayed so long is taken from you. Therefore again and again I ask you, brothers, and with what diligence I can I admonish you: turn yourselves to the Lord our God with your whole heart while you live healthy and unharmed; and do not further defer the will, lest you lose also the power granted to you. Think often and think again that we are not citizens of this world, but strangers and pilgrims; nor do we have here a lasting city, but we seek the one to come; and every day we hasten toward death, not even knowing it.

*On the love of quiet and solitude: Homily Six.*

Two intellects were created from the beginning; Holy Scripture does not plainly disclose [this], but by the teaching of the fathers it is known to lie hidden, brothers, that the angels are the work of God, and it counted them among the works of the six days. But there are some who write that the angels were created long before this visible world; and just as we cannot prove this by the testimony of the divine Scriptures, so neither do we rashly attack it. For there are several grave and holy men who placed the creation of the angels long before the beginning of the world.

But the angelic intellect was made at once from the beginning in the act of all things knowable, and it knew all things that it could know, and thereafter admitted nothing toward its own perfection. For, created by the Lord in its perfection, it did not require an increase by which it might understand more. For indeed, since pure intellect was made joined to God without intervening medium, it was made such by nature, nor did it need practice in order to understand after our fashion, since its being admits no corruption.
