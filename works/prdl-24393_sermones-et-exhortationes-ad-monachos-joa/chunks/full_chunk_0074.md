*All things. XVI. Fol. XXXVIII.*

For sloth is the root of despair, and the mother and nurse of all vices. For just as a garment both produces and nourishes the moth, so the most pernicious plague of acedia both gives birth to and fosters despair. This beast is accustomed to vex no one more than monks, and especially those who lead their life withdrawn in solitude. Therefore, monks, you must beware with the utmost care of this enemy of the salvation of souls, and it must be fought not by fleeing but by resisting bravely; and one must diligently consider that no one has attained the honor of holiness by torpor and sloth, nor has anyone deserved the kingdom of eternal happiness without great and continual labor.

Therefore the mind must always be renewed by daily and fresh increases of virtues, and this perilous journey of living must be measured not by what has passed, but rather by what remains. Let no one promise himself security in this life, which is named wholly a temptation, full of as many dangers as there are affections in the soul; but let each one take care that he who could become better from worse does not become worse from better. And especially in these holy days let us all take care, as much as we can, to become better, adding for ourselves, by the counsel of our divine teacher, something to the usual measure of our service: namely special prayers, abstinence from foods and drink, so that each one, beyond the measure prescribed to him, may offer something to God of his own will with the joy of the Holy Spirit, that is, may subtract from his body something of food, of drink, of sleep, of talkativeness, of ease, and may await holy Easter with the joy of spiritual desire.

For a monk is proved to be little religious at other times if in these holy days he is not found more religious, more fervent in the love of God, and more devout. Hence the divine pope Gregory says in a certain homily: Lent is to be observed with the highest observance, so that the fast in it, on account of the Lord’s days that have been subtracted from abstinence, unless sickness has hindered someone, is by no means relaxed, because these days are the tithes of the year which we are commanded to offer to the Lord. Saint Ambrose also says: He keeps Lent who by fasting and keeping vigil ascends to Easter. For just as at the remaining time of the year to fast brings a reward, so in Lent not to fast is a sin. The former fasts are voluntary, and these are necessary; the former come from choice, but these from law. To the former we are invited; to these we are compelled. And therefore he who passes beyond the appointed number by eating on one day is accused not as the violator of one day, but is charged as the transgressor of the whole of Lent.

In these sacred days all desires of the flesh must be restrained; the body must be chastised through abstinence, so that if perhaps at other times we have lived less temperately, we may in some way make compensation by this Lenten fast. Therefore, dearest brothers, let us follow the admonitions of our most holy father Benedict, and with as much diligence as we can let us exercise ourselves in holy works, subtracting something daily from our body, for love of God, from food and drink, so that we may prove ourselves true servants of God.

Next, however, our holy founder adds, saying: Yet this very thing which each one offers, let him report to his abbot, and let it be done with his prayer and will; because what is done without the permission of the spiritual father will be imputed to presumption and vainglory, not to reward. Therefore all things are to be done with the will of the abbot. The words of our holy lawgiver are plain, brothers, so that no one could have recourse to the covering of ignorance. For if all things are to be done with the will of the abbot, then without his permission absolutely nothing is to be done.

Indeed, the more a good work is adorned with more virtues, the more it must be considered of greater merit before God. Therefore, when a monk, by the license and blessing of his abbot, subtracts something of foods or drink from his body for love of God, he will receive not only from that abstinence itself the reward of eternal satiety promised by the Lord, but also the kingdom for humility, and for holy obedience: he has resigned his will to Christ in the hands of the abbot; and if he thereafter usurps it again by his own decision, he has now shamefully denied Christ. For what else is a monk who usurps his own will, except the unjust possessor of another’s property?
