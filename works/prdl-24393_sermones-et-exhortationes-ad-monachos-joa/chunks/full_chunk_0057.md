*On the Daily Expiation of Sins.*

We are not without sin. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and an earthly dwelling depresses the understanding that thinks on many things. For Scripture says: “There is no righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin.” The blessed evangelist John also says: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” Indeed, through intercourse with the flesh the soul, excessively delighted, sins, and its strength is diminished by [unclear] of the spirit. For the mind is deceived by the presentation of sensible things, and readiness to do wrong is strengthened by long habit. The weakness of the flesh weakens the fervor of the spirit, and the frailty of the will resists the holy purpose of the mind. Hence Solomon, the most wise king, says in the book of Proverbs: “Seven times in a day the righteous man will fall and will rise again; but the wicked will fall into evil.” By the testimony of this saying we are warned not to grow lukewarm in the purpose of holiness, even if it should happen that we have fallen seven times in a day. As often, O monk, as you have fallen through sin from the purpose of holiness, so often rise again to repentance, thereafter never to return. But however often people have fallen, they can always rise again to repentance and obtain by tears and prayers the grace of God which they had lost by sinning. For the heart of man is flexible; it can be moved in either direction, because never can a man in the flesh become so evil, if he makes use of the grace of the Lord. Since these things are so, my brothers, you will have to be warned by my exhortation today to walk carefully, so that he who, with conscience as witness, already walks in the paths of righteousness may persevere in it by advancing daily, and that he who understands himself to be guilty before the Lord may amend his life as quickly as possible. If human frailty daily drives you on, or, to speak more properly, hurls you down and makes you do wrong, let the love of God and of your own salvation also stir you daily humbly to expiate your fault through true contrition. For thus the righteous man falls seven times in a day and rises again, because, even if he slips through human frailty, yet, persevering always in the purpose of holiness, he is strengthened more and more in mind in the love of God. For, keeping his conscience free from grave sins, he is unwillingly polluted by daily negligences; and when he sees that he cannot live without faults, recognizing his weakness humbly, he groans the more deeply. But if he should also have been overtaken by a graver offense, he soon rises again to amendment through repentance. Therefore the ancient governors of monastic discipline, considering the condition of human frailty, established various kinds of expiation, by which both monks of good will might daily advance toward the better, and the wicked might be compelled to the observance of the regular life. For in the Rule the holy father Benedict gives this command, saying: “With all solicitude let the abbot take care concerning delinquent brothers, because it is not the healthy who have need of a physician, but those who are ill.” For this reason the first leaders of regular observance, with provident consideration, established that every day after Prime, in an appointed place, a chapter of faults should be held, in which, with the abbot or prior presiding, the actions of all the brothers are brought into the open with equal discussion. This place, my brothers, bears the figure of the purgatory of souls, since here not only are past sins expiated, but we are also fortified against future ones. For in this place, which we call the chapter room, whatever wrongs you have committed openly are corrected and expiated through salutary repentance. Here daily, as the paraphrast pronounces it, you learn your monastic law, according to whose integrity you have promised to live; here every day you hear the exhortatory word of salvation; here you listen as the victories of Christ’s martyrs and their many sufferings for the orthodox faith are briefly recited. This is the school of monks, necessary for the consideration of regular discipline, in which everything is learned that is commanded to be observed by those in the cloister.
