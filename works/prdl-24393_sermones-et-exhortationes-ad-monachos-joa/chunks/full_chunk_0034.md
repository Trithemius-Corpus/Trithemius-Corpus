*That one must pray without interruption.*

...no usefulness at all. Hence Saint Jerome says: God is to be prayed to not in words only, but with the heart. Therefore the chanting of five psalms with purity of heart, serenity, and spiritual cheerfulness is better than the singing of the whole psalter with anxiety and sadness of heart. And the holy pope Gregory says: True petition is not in the sounds of the mouth, but in the voices of the heart, that is, in holy thoughts. For it is not words but desires that make the stronger voices in the ears of God.

Except, therefore, for those prayers which we are bound to say either by decree of the Church or by the institution of the monastic order, [prolixity?] in the rest and multiplication of words contribute nothing, or little, to true and inward devotion of the mind, since where there is no fervent desire in the love of God, an excessive extension of speech is introduced in vain. For sacred Augustine says: Let much speaking in prayer be absent, but let fervent attention persevere; for to speak much in praying is to handle a necessary matter with superfluous words. For prayer embraces many things in few words, so that the simplicity of faith might quickly learn the sufficiency of its salvation, and the prudence of the ingenious might stand amazed at the depth of the mysteries.

But for those in whom, because of hardness of heart, the compunction of inward fervor has been lacking, when they prostrate themselves in prayer there is need of more extended speech, shouting, and tears, so that by long exercise of the mind they may at last strike forth within themselves the burning fire of divine love, until they rise in the affection of holy prayer in the Spirit of God. But afterward, when the fervor of divine love and compunction has penetrated the mind, there is thereafter no need for the exercise of words, but the continuation of spiritual nourishment is required, lest the fervor of the spiritual fire be extinguished. To this end our most holy father wished prayer in the assembly to be shortened, so that if anyone wished to pray apart in the spirit, he could do so.

There are, however, some who are so hard of heart that while praying they find little or nothing of spiritual compunction; for them freedom brings ruin, and solitude often brings despair. Attend, I pray you, dearest brothers, to what I say to you, and consider how greatly you are bound to pray without interruption. It is not enough for a monk, striving toward perfection in the school of Christ the eternal king, to discharge the canonical prayers; nor is it sufficient for a man solemnly dedicated to God by vow to fulfill the common prayers of the order in the convent, since one who is commanded always to pray without interruption is bound beyond verbal prayers also to spiritual prayers. For prayers instituted by the Church or by the order fall under the merit of obedience, and under the law of necessity by reason of a precept; but that manner of praying which is called spiritual is strengthened in the freedom of the spirit as useful and necessary for a monk at every time for perfection.

Hence it must be noted that for prayers which are by precept of the Church or the order, the utterance of words is required as a matter of necessity, together with the intention of satisfying the statute by which anyone is bound to pray. Let those see how they satisfy the command of the Church who do not express the seven canonical prayers by the utterance of words, but with closed mouth run through the sequence of prayers by thought alone, with their eyes assisting. But in those prayers which we undertake to say not by command but by our own will and from devotion alone, the fervor of the mind toward God suffices without the utterance of words, because, as we have said, God requires such worshippers as adore in spirit and truth. But the canonical prayers, which they call the hours, we are bound to say, and not merely to think. In both cases devotion of mind toward God is useful; without it no prayers are believed to be effective.

And so, my brothers, when we stand for prayer, we ought to watch with the greatest zeal and attend to the prayers which we pour forth, lest we pray without the fruit of inward compunction. Therefore let every thought of the flesh depart far away; let no occupation with household affairs hinder the mind; nor let the soul then think anything else at all except this alone which is being said. Therefore also the priest, after the preface has customarily been sent before the sacrifice, prepares the minds of the brothers, saying, Lift up your hearts, so that when the people answer, We have them with the Lord, he may admonish them that they ought to think of nothing other than the very God whom they invoke. Let the breast therefore be closed against the adversary and lie open to the Lord alone; and in the time of holy prayer let no one admit any occupation of the world at all.

Great is the usefulness of pure prayer, which, like a faithful messenger, carries out the command of the fervent mind, and, flying, ascends into heaven, where flesh does not reach. For prayer is sustained by two wings, so that it may fly up into heaven; one of these is called fasting, and the other is named almsgiving.
