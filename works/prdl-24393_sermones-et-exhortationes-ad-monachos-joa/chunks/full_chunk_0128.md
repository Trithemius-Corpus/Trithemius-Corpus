Beautiful garments, decorously arranged, do not befit a monk, but the meanest, with which the body may be defended only from the injury of the inclemency of the air. All fastidiousness is forbidden him, as it is to cloistered men, who by reason of their profession made before God are bound to contempt of the world. Humility flees lofty things; it seeks the things that are lowly.

The twenty-third sign of monastic humility shows a monk to be servant-like toward the brethren and voluntarily given to acts of service, and ready for all things whatsoever he has been ordered. For true humility obeys even inferiors with a most ready will, and is never indignant against the one who commands. As far as he is able, he desires to serve all for the love of Christ, who faithfully loves God with his whole heart. For whatever service he has bestowed on the brethren, he firmly believes that the whole has been shown to Christ.

The twenty-fourth sign of humility a monk then attains in truth, when for the love of God he delights to attend to the meaner offices in the monastery, which the rest despise. We read that many once ascended to this degree of true humility; we do not doubt that they are of great merit before God. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Everyone who humbles himself shall be exalted; for God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble. The humble monk imitates Christ; the proud one, the devil.

The twenty-fifth sign of monastic humility is always to seek the lower place for sitting in the assembly of any persons whatever, and to prefer oneself neither to an equal nor to an inferior. It is not for monks to contend proudly about precedence of place; nor does it befit a soldier of Christ to wish to surpass others in honor. For the true monk, who loves humility, content with the lowest place in every respect, does not desire to excel anyone.

The twenty-sixth indication of humility especially befits him who, although learned and educated, on account of the lowly humiliation of himself hides all learning and knowledge. For the monk who is truly humbled is always found readier to listen than to speak. For the divine priest Jerome says: The monk has the office not of one teaching, but of one lamenting, who either mourns himself or the world, and fearfully awaits the coming of the Lord. The arrogance of monks is hateful both to God and to men, and always liable to sin.

The twenty-seventh sign of humility is perfectly attained by him who, for love of regular discipline, delights more to be silent than to speak. The humble lover of Christ loves silence, and, lest he offend in speech, is accustomed to say very few things. Humility is rarely found among the talkative; and he who is swift to speak casts down his own soul. A talkative and garrulous monk is not without pride; and he who is foolish multiplies words. Whoever in the spiritual contest desires to overcome his adversary through humility must not omit to restrain his tongue from much speaking and from every idle word. It is written in the book of Proverbs that in much speaking sin will not be lacking.

Whoever has attained the twenty-eighth sign of perfect humility will avoid laughter and all disordered gestures of those who guffaw. Lamentation most befits a monk, not laughter, as one who is dead to all the levities of the world. But the wise man says: A fool raises his voice in laughter, but a wise man will scarcely laugh quietly.

The twenty-ninth indication of perfect humility takes its beginning from the fear of the Lord; for the sake of his honor, as far as he is able, he flees and detests every sin. For everyone who does not fear to sin has not truly been humble. But the monk who follows Christ according to his strength in true humility is accustomed to flee every sin, and, as far as he can, to exercise himself in the pursuit of virtues. Humility confesses its sin; and as often as it has offended even in the smallest matters, it does not rest until it has amended everything through true penance. He who is truly humble is always found zealous for preserving cleanness of conscience. For the nourishment of humility is charity, which does not permit the one who has it to be idle.

The thirtieth sign of true humility shows a monk confirmed in the purpose of holiness, and makes him subject to all the brethren, even the younger. Whatever the pastor has commanded, he obeys simply; he never debates the commands of his superior; he does not ask the reason why he so commands. If impossibilities are commanded, he is very ready in spirit; he is prepared without contradiction for whatever things are hard and arduous. He is directed by the counsel of his pastor; he believes him, trusts him, obeys him, and is willingly governed by his command.
