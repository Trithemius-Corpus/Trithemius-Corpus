And so I have wished to introduce today’s conference from the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, who says thus concerning Lenten observance: “Although at every season the life of a monk ought to have the observance of Lent, nevertheless, because this virtue belongs to few, we therefore advise that in these days of Lent they guard their life in all purity, and that all alike wash away in these holy days the stains and negligences of other times. This is then done worthily if we restrain ourselves from all vices, and devote ourselves to prayer with tears, to reading, and to compunction of heart.”

If this virtue belongs to few, that they should at every season lead a life of Lenten observance, it is now plainly evident that few monks in religion live according to the purity of the Gospel, since all who profess the soldiery of Christ are bound to direct their effort toward the summit of perfection. For the monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is solitary; and he is then rightly believed to live in the cenobium according to the Gospel and the rule which he has professed, when, having despised worldly possessions and honors for the love of God, he conducts himself in all purity of heart before God and men: vigorous in work, clean in thought, sparing and peaceable in speech. This is the life of a monk, having Lenten observance at every season: to chastise the body for the love of God through abstinence, and to nourish the mind through inward compunction. He lives rightly who lives justly, who loves God above all things, who with the greatest zeal and the greatest diligence observes his commandments in words and deeds. For he is the true monk of Christ who imitates Christ with all his powers.

But if you wish to imitate Christ, O monk, observe most carefully what I am about to say. Flee the world and all comforts of the flesh, so that you may deserve truly to be solitary. Never permit vain, useless, foul, and harmful thoughts to dwell in your heart, but always think something good, and let the memory of the Lord’s Passion never depart from your mind, so that each day, with thanksgiving, you may contemplate the Lord hanging on the cross. Consider God present to you everywhere, who sees, knows, and understands whatever you have either thought or done, since nothing is hidden from him. Keep your heart clean from every defilement of sin, and always have a pure conscience, fervent in the true love of Christ. Let no idle time fly away from you, but let thought always find you engaged in good works. Do not grow sluggish with much sleep, and keep yourself far removed from every sloth of tepidity. Embrace moderation in food and drink, and live as sparingly as is permitted, so that the body, moderately sustained, may always serve the spirit in humility and in no way rise up against the command of reason.

As soon as you perceive some sluggishness arising in you, flee to the memory of the most bitter Passion of the Lord Jesus Christ, and bring back [unclear] whatever can more swiftly drive away sloth. Break off, I beg you, all delays, since sluggishness of mind, when dissembled, grows suddenly; and sloth, which is not at once extinguished by the new fervor of the mind, is immediately strengthened into the grave destruction of the soul. For of all evils in a monk this is the worst: if through sluggishness and sloth he has offered a place in his heart to the seedbed of vices. And you, O monks, must keep watch against this beast not only every day, but every hour and moment, since, once admitted into the heart, it never enters alone; but when it has been stirred, a mass and spring of all vices accompanies it. There is no more destructive plague of the monk than the sluggishness of sloth, through which all fervor of devotion is extinguished; and, faithful service having been interrupted, the mind, driven by laziness and idleness, is cast headlong into the pit of despair. Whoever permits delay from sloth quickly falls away from the purpose of holiness.

Against this Solomon admonishes you in the Book of Proverbs, saying: “Go to the ant, O sluggard, and consider her ways, and learn wisdom. Though she has no leader, nor instructor, nor ruler, she prepares her food in summer and gathers at harvest what she may eat.” And again he says: “Desires kill the sluggard, because his hands have refused to work at anything.” Nothing is worse than idleness for those who profess the purpose of holiness, because it not only accumulates nothing good, but even consumes and scatters everything previously accumulated.
