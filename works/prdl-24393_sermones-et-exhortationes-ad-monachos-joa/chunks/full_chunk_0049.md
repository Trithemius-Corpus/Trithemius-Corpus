*Homily XI.*
Folio xxv.

Let him think that he has spent that day uselessly on which he has not sent ahead some good work for which he may expect reward. Let the good monk strive at all times to cultivate the field of his heart, and, so far as he can, utterly root out all the thorns and briars of evil affections. Let him learn to love the Lord Jesus above all things with a pure mind, and to keep most diligently whatever he commanded. Let him sow in tears of devotion and constancy what he may reap with all the saints in exultation. Let not the continual difficulty of labor frighten his mind; let not the hard struggle of the flesh break his spirit, since he who labors little gathers little fruit at harvest, and he who now sows much in tears for love of Christ brings back on that day a most abundant fruit. For this, brothers, is the appointed end of our journey which we undertake in the present pilgrimage: that, purified by continual exercise in labors and adversities, we may go forth from here clean and pure to our homeland.

*On fleeing the company of the wicked: Homily eleven.*

[unclear] The company of the wicked has drawn many back from the way of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ; therefore, my most beloved brothers, we do not cease to admonish you to walk in the way of the Lord with the good and holy, so that by their instruction and example you may daily become better. For, as the blessed apostle of Christ says, imitating a senarius of a certain Greek, Menander: evil conversations corrupt good morals. He does not truly desire to be good whom the conduct of the wicked delights. Remember [unclear]. No one speaks wicked things unless he is first found corrupt and depraved in his own heart against the purity of the evangelical tradition. As each person is, and as are the things he turns over and performs in the secrecy of his heart by reflection and thought, so such speech more often proceeds from his mouth. For the speech of one who speaks is the truest witness of the heart, as our Lord and Savior says in the holy Gospel, that out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For a good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and an evil man likewise brings forth evil things.

Since, therefore, he who speaks evil is not good, we must beware of all who use depraved, scurrilous, and pernicious speech; in their talk certain shameful things frequently resound, while words of holiness are either never or very rarely heard. Foul speech is the sign of an immodest heart, even if deeds do not follow. Therefore you, my dearest brothers, who have promised, according to the rule and statutes of the holy fathers of the Church, not only to preserve chastity and every kind of modesty in deed until death, by both implicit and explicit profession, but also ought to be far removed from every unclean thought: flee the company of wicked men and the assemblies of the malicious, and at all times avoid those who speak unjust, foul, or idle things. Remember the Scripture which says: He who touches pitch will be defiled by it, and he who associates with a proud man will put on pride. Hence holy Pope Gregory says: The weak ought to avoid the society of the wicked, lest the evils which they frequently see and are unable to correct should delight them to imitate. And Saint John the bishop says: If a good man is joined to an evil one, either they are made equal, or they are quickly separated from each other. For friendships either seek equals or make them. Indeed, the nature of things is such that whenever a good man is joined to an evil one, the evil man is not improved by the good, but the good man is contaminated by the evil. For diversity of things can never have concord, and wicked society troubles many.

There are among you certain brothers, I say it groaning, professors very forgetful of regular discipline: to them the cell is a prison, the choir a siege, prayer nausea, silence punishment, the reading of Scripture weariness. The love of God either never or rarely raises upward the hearts of those whose whole way of life consists in lower things. Whenever I happen to be absent, they immediately gather among themselves, not about Christ, nor about the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom, nor about the integrity of regular observance, but they bring forth a long discourse about the vain and useless affairs of this world; and by conversing they involve themselves in tales of kings and wars, matters about which it is not lawful for a monk to think.
