*On the nine degrees of ascent.*

Hasten, my very dear brothers, to ascend this degree of the spiritual ladder as quickly as possible, so that you may at last be able to arrive at the summit of perfection. For if you, who have been placed under obedience of monastic perfection, have advanced to this fourth degree, you will now ascend the fifth without difficulty. For those who are placed in the fourth do [unclear], since they have not subjected themselves by vow or profession to monastic constitutions. And in this one respect they are inferior to those that follow: because a good work is so much the more acceptable to God, the more it is commended by the merit of obedience.

Therefore those ascend the fifth degree who keep themselves from sins with all their strength, and, placed under obedience, always persevere strong and constant in the purpose of holiness. Loving God above all things with a pure mind, they do good works chiefly for love of him, and in their intention they neither fear punishments nor seek rewards. They obey their prelates humbly for love of God, yet they are somewhat obstinate. They sometimes try to defend their exercises and private observances against the determination of their prelates, and to consider themselves wiser than their rulers in the examination of spiritual matters. Flee this, monks, because the demon lies hidden in ambush; and those whom he cannot conquer in open conflict he strives to overcome by secret plotting. For the holier and more perfect a monk has been before God, the more promptly he obeys his prelate without debate. But if a perfect monk should scorn to obey his abbot, even an imperfect one, in things lawful and honorable, then surely he has lost the name of perfection. Therefore let each one take care to press on with zeal for progress in such a way that he presumes in nothing to violate obedience. Scripture says: Obedience is better than victims, and to listen is better than to offer the fat of rams. Whatever you have offered is another’s; but the truly obedient man offers himself.

The sixth degree, then, belongs especially to those who, living in every purity of mind and body, love the Lord above all things; who do every good thing that they can for the honor of God alone; who do not depart from the commands of their prelates. Great is the virtue of obedience, which doubles the good work that has been done, and, increasing the merit of the worker, presents him to God as a tree with fruit. Do not let the words of men who despise obedience disturb you, my very dear brothers, who prefer the freedom of their will to the subjection of monks, and place a good work done from freedom before the merit of obedience. For even if a monk obeys by necessity of his profession, nevertheless the good will by which, for love of God, he bound himself to obedience commends that necessity in many ways.

There is a necessity by which a man is compelled to do something, where the will has not consented but always resists. This necessity diminishes the praise of virtue, because the good work was never in the will. There is also another necessity, proceeding from the inward inclination of the mind, which increases the praise of a good work and does not diminish it, because it kindles the will more intensely toward that very work. There is a third necessity, which has regard to the end and does not compel the will. For if you wish to cross the Rhine by ship, the fitting necessity demands an instrument. But the necessity by which a monk is compelled to obey his shepherd is not that of the first compulsion, nor that of the second inclination, but rather of the third, since it is ordered toward the end from a preceding will. Therefore it is necessary for a monk, on account of the end, to do that which the abbot commands, so that he may be able to fulfill the vow that he made for love of God. Since, then, this end is very praiseworthy, by which the creature subjects himself to his creator out of love, the necessity of obeying in a monk is of good praise, of more excellent merit, and of more abundant reward, by reason of the vow, than if he did a good work from obedience without a vow, or if it were done without a vow from the freedom of his own will. For the monk has once offered himself wholly, and all his will with him, to God, and has reserved absolutely nothing for himself. Whatever good he does under obedience is referred back to the end of the religion he has undertaken, to be kept for love of Christ; with this no work after martyrdom is compared. He who is continent without a vow from the venereal act performs one virtue, which is called chastity; but he who observes these things by reason of a vow walks at a higher degree, because the several virtues are referred in ordered fashion to the perfection of the whole religion which he has professed. For the whole monastic religion is more than one private virtue. For he who offers to God a good work that he did without obedience offers that alone, leaving to himself what is greater: the power of acting, together with his own will.
