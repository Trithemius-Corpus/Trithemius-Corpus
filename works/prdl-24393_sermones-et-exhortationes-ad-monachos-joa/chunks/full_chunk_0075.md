*On the Lenten fast of monks.*

...to act against his command; let him omit or neglect nothing that has been enjoined on him by him. Let him beware of troubling or saddening his shepherd by a restless manner of life; nor let him murmur against him, nor disparage him, nor say any evil whatever against him. Rather, let him reverence him with the sincere affection of charity, as the vicar of Christ, and not sadden his spirit either by importunate or disorderly words or deeds. Moreover, let him aid his labors as much as he can, and intercede for him with continual prayers before God. Monks sin greatly against God who do not fear to sadden their abbot by their importunate disparagements and wicked behavior.

Therefore whatever monk desires to be good, whoever truly wishes to be devout in the Lord, let him always and everywhere venerate his abbot with pure love as the vicar of Jesus Christ in the monastery; let him humbly obey his commands in all things, and in no way depart from his direction. But let the abbot strive to show himself in all things a worthy shepherd to his monks, one who feeds the sheep of Christ entrusted to him by word and likewise by example, in all gentleness of charity. Let him not disturb the brothers without just and reasonable necessity; let him not be a despiser of the holy community; let him recognize himself as the steward of the monastery's goods, not their lord. Let him not be a mocker of his subjects, not turbulent, not importunate, not a tyrant, but their sincere lover, gentle in speech, mild and upright in conduct, holy in his way of life, clean in conscience, always devoted in mind to God.

Let him at all times bear solicitous care for the souls entrusted to him, and neglect nothing of those things which seem able to conduce to their salvation. Let him not therefore raise himself up on high because he has been placed as ruler of others, but let him remember that he must fear so much the more, inasmuch as he is most certainly bound to render account for all in the dread judgment of God. Let him correct the proud, rebellious, and contumacious as a lord; let him kindly cherish the humble, obedient, and governable as a most gentle father, and wisely distribute to each one his right without acceptance of persons. Let him always consider himself the mother and nurse of the monks; and therefore those whom he can bring back to better things by gentleness he ought not to exasperate with importunate words and harsh invectives. Let him always bear solicitous care for the weak, and provide for all in such a way that they have no just cause for murmuring. In his own weaknesses let him learn how he ought to have mercy on his subjects. For he cannot be a good shepherd of souls who disdains to suffer heartfelt compassion for the necessities of his ailing subjects.

But he can become a good abbot among monks who knows how to adapt himself to the persons of the weak in such a way that, whenever a brother asks something of him, taking upon himself the condition of the person asking, he says in his heart: What would I wish to be done to me in this matter, according to the right judgment of reason, if I were in the person of the one asking? Indeed, if the abbot imagines himself a subject, he will let no one go away from him saddened. We have now said, brothers, what sort of men it befits you to be toward us for the love of God, so that, existing as good monks, you may please the Lord Jesus in truth. You have nevertheless heard what sort of shepherd I ought to show myself to you; whether I do this or not, you yourselves will judge better than I.

For we shall fast in vain and find no fruit of Lenten observance unless we all are concordant before God in the true charity of ecclesiastical union. Or what good will it do to abstain from richer foods and defile the conscience with evil works or thoughts? For in order that the fasts we have begun may be pleasing to God, it is necessary that they be not only alien and remote from vices and sins, but also adorned with the fullness of all virtues. For holy Ambrose says: What profit is it to fast in the belly and to run riot in hunting, to abstain from foods and to wander in sins? Do you think, brothers, that he fasts who at first daybreak does not keep watch at church, does not seek out the holy places of the blessed martyrs, but rises in the morning, gathers his servants, arranges the nets, brings out the dogs, ranges through glades and woods, and, I say, drags along with him servants perhaps hastening rather to church? To his own pleasures he adds the sins of others, not knowing that he will be guilty both of his own fault and of the ruin of his servants.
