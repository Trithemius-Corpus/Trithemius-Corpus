*That monks must live cautiously.*

Justice is the virtue which distributes and preserves for each person what is his own; by it private advantage is neglected so that it may preserve the common advantage and equity, without which no commonwealth can subsist in divine peace. For the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “A kingdom is transferred from one nation to another because of injustices, and injuries, and insults, and various deceits.” The order of justice looks first to God, then to the fatherland, thereafter to parents, afterwards to all in common according to the distinctions of ranks and the merits of dignities. To God, in the first place, at the dictation of justice, we owe religion, faith, worship, devotion, and thanksgivings, and true love from the whole heart with obedient submission. Then to ourselves, purity of mind and body, continence with zeal for perfection, so that through obedience it may be permitted us to return to him by whom we were created. In the third place, to the fatherland we owe advantage, honor, and defense, and, insofar as we can, to procure its benefit together with justice itself. Fourth, we are bound to our parents by honor and obedience, and in their necessity by care and support of every kind. Fifth, to prelates [unclear], to subjects teaching and nourishment, and correction to those who offend. In the seventh place, however, we are bound to give honor to elders and princes, and, without fault, subjection for the love of God. Eighth, we owe reverence to venerable persons, and honor to priests. Consequently we are bound to equals by friendship, to inferiors by condescension, to benefactors by reciprocation, to friends by fellowship, to enemies by patience, to the poor by mercy, to the wretched by consolation, to all by charity. This, however, is true justice: by it we love God from the whole heart for his own sake, and our neighbor in God as ourselves, but enemies for God’s sake, distributing to each person what is owed to him by right and providing for the advantage of all.

But since excessive justice easily incurs sin, temperance is necessary for you in all things; it alone makes men more perfect. Temperance is the virtue which by reason discerns and conquers the motions of the soul and every lust, lest impulse in acting exceed the equity of the law. Temperance is a most excellent virtue: it governs pleasures and preserves all things in due order. Apply temperance to all your actions, words, and also thoughts, and the discretion necessary to the virtues. Whatever good you do with discretion receives the name by the merit of virtue; but what is done without discretion must be called vice. Discretion is the moderator of all virtues, and the greatest ornament of prudence and justice. Discretion is necessary not only for prelates but also for subjects, since it gives order to those exercising virtue, grants modesty to affections, and bestows on all human counsels most beautiful grace and honorableness. Hence a certain wise man said: “There is a measure in things; there are, finally, fixed limits, beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.”

For this, brothers, strength of soul is necessary for you, through which you may become not only steadfast in adversities but also ready and cheerful in difficult things. For who, especially without strength of soul, could despise this world with its pleasures, and persevere in holy monastic religion under the laws of obedience even unto death? Strength of soul takes its powers from the charity of Christ, and stands constant, no less in adversities than in prosperity, in desire for virtue. Strength walks the way of uprightness without fear, and to enemies it appears terrifying with a certain holy pride. But strength of soul seems fearful of [unclear]. Strength despises the injuries of the small and easily overcomes all the pleasures of the flesh. Strength lies hidden in patience, is shown in adversity, and is openly declared through patience. Strong of soul is not the man who avenges injuries brought upon him, but the man who patiently tolerates them; and if he has received his adversary into his power, he thinks that he has been able to take vengeance. The strength of monks is to despise the world, to conquer the flesh, to spurn all the honors and pleasures of the world, and to place all hope and confidence in God alone. The measure of strength is to be necessary, neither rash nor [unclear], because, walking on the right way, it declines neither to the right nor to the left. That monk must be judged strong who is temperate in all things, and moderate and just. Hence the most holy pope Gregory says: “The strength of the just is to conquer the flesh, to oppose their own pleasures, to extinguish the delight of the present life [unclear] strengthen; they despise earthly things, seek heavenly things; they can be killed, they cannot be bent, nor can they endure falsehoods. Through weakness they fear [unclear], nor, when harmed, do they ever keep silent from the truth.”
