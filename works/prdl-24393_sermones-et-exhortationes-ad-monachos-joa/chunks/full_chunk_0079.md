Let us therefore consider, brothers, before whom we stand, and to whom, in the sight of the angels, we recite the psalms in prayer; for the monk who, at the time of prayer, [unclear] vain thoughts [unclear] are very necessary to him: but, summoned by his servant, he leaves the king and turns himself to the servant’s vain and useless conversations. Truly anyone who did this would be judged foolish by all, and would rightly seem deserving of the severest punishments.

But what does the monk deserve who, standing before the highest king of all things and speaking with him about the things that pertain to the salvation of his body, admits into his mind thoughts that are harmful, or even destructive? If one who mocks and despises an earthly king is punished by beheading, what does one deserve who despises the heavenly emperor? Therefore I admonish you, brothers, and with all the sincerity I can I ask that, having purified your hearts, especially at the time of prayer, from the stain of wicked thought, you receive in the mind only what you sing aloud with the mouth.

As the angels stand always before God with fear and the greatest reverence, singing an everlasting hymn of praise, so let us also take care, my brothers, to stand before him with a pure heart, a clean conscience, and most fervent devotion, so that we may be able to sing his praises much more in spirit than in body. Let us therefore separate our minds from all earthly affairs; let us cleanse our consciences from all enticements of the flesh; let us put on the fervor of the Holy Spirit; and let us drive far from us all sluggishness and idleness. For just as a heavy load weighs down the back of a slothful man, so drowsiness at the time of psalmody is accustomed to oppress the minds of monks.

Nothing harms monks more than sloth, which little by little enfeebles all the powers of the mind and extinguishes the whole fervor of the spirit. For when a monk has begun to fall away from the fervor of holy devotion, countless evil thoughts enter the mind; and he can now drive them away so much the less, the farther he is convicted of having withdrawn from his former fervor. Hence drowsiness arises [unclear] by the display of services; the mind is burdened by sadness and melancholy, which the monk, growing lukewarm in spirit, strives with all effort to relieve not by spiritual but by earthly and fleshly little consolations. And as he proceeds longer in this alienation of mind, he is hardened, now almost insensible, against the exercise of compunction; and the more he longs for the consolations of the flesh, the farther he wanders from the inward fervor of the spirit.

For what makes you drowsy at the time of psalmody except sloth? For the mind that has fallen into torpor soon makes the body doze. But again I admonish you, brothers, as I have more often been accustomed to do, that you especially avoid idleness and drowsiness at the time of prayer, since, just as drunkenness brings forgetfulness into the soul, so also drowsiness makes the mind unsound and foolish before God. Wash away this vice of drowsiness, O you young men, by which we grieve that some of the seniors have long since been infected through long habit; among them dozing in choir is familiar, and compunction of mind toward God is exceedingly rare.

Beware, I say, this evil habit of sleeping, which, after it has once led a monk away captive, rarely allows him to be free. Therefore at the beginning of conversion young men especially must fight against this most pernicious enemy of religion, as someone said in verse: “Resist at the beginnings; medicine is prepared too late when evils have gained strength through long delays. But on that account do not put yourself off into coming hours; he who is not ready today will be less ready tomorrow.” For it is difficult to cure a sickness that has become old through long duration.

Let us see with how much diligence and attentiveness servants stand before earthly kings and princes, so that we may not be ignorant of how it befits us to stand in prayers before God, who alone is king of all and lord of the universe. The eyes of all look to the lord, and all and each hang most attentively upon his nod, lest anything commanded by him escape those standing by through negligence. From this, then, let us consider with how great purity we ought to stand before the Lord God in the assembly of prayer, day and night, from whom we desire to obtain the kingdom of eternal happiness.
