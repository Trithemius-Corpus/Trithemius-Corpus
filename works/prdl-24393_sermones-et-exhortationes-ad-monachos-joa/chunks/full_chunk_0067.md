*On the abstinence and fasting of monks.*

...to bear speech: to admit no vain, useless, or foul thoughts; to commit nothing that is unbecoming to a servant of God; and to omit nothing that pertains to the consummation of righteousness. For unless the mind has fasted from vices and sins, bodily abstinence will profit nothing toward the salvation of the soul.

Hence the holy priest Jerome says: “Your abstinence and fasting are pleasing in proportion as they are offered with holy conduct, so that what in others are coverings for vices may in you become ornaments of virtues.” For what good is it for the body to be made thin by abstinence, if the spirit swells with pride? Or what virtue is there in not drinking wine, and being drunk with anger, lust, hatred, and envy? Or what will it help us for eternal salvation, brothers, that according to the rule of our holy father Benedict we do not eat meat, if we do not subdue the vices of the flesh?

For the thrice-holy pope Gregory says: “In vain is the body worn down through abstinence, if the mind, divided by disorderly movements, is scattered among vices.” Hence Scripture commands: “Sanctify a fast.” To sanctify a fast is, with other good works joined to it, to show abstinence of the flesh worthy of God. Indeed, if abstinence from foods alone made a man virtuous, there would have been no need to add “sanctify”; but because fasting alone does not suffice for the sanctification of the soul, it is necessary that it be adorned with the ornament of all virtues. For those who abstain from foods and do evil imitate the demons, for whom there is always fasting and never any lack of sin.

A fast acceptable to God is to restrain the body by abstinence from the desire of evils, and by the spirit of compunction to mortify the deeds of the flesh for the love of Christ. Let the mind of the one fasting be fervent in the love of God; let his conscience always be clean from vices, and the fasts of humble servants will please the Most High. Let the monks’ fasts be daily, as the canon of the holy teacher Benedict established; let their hearts be clean, as the Lord Jesus commanded in the holy Gospel; let their refection be moderate, fleeing satiety, because just as from excessive fullness the body is corrupted by diverse and various illnesses, so too an unregulated movement of lust is aroused in it. For modest and moderate food is both medicine for the body and a support of virtue for the soul.

For Saint John Chrysostom, bishop, says: “Nothing is so pleasant as food well digested and cooked; nothing so healthful; nothing so sharpens the senses; nothing so drives away sickness as moderate refection. For sufficiency with moderate nourishment produces health and pleasure together. But abundance makes disease, brings on troubles, and generates various illnesses. For what excessive hunger does, immoderate fullness of foods is also accustomed to do. Hunger indeed in a few days takes a man away and frees him from this penal life; but excess of foods consumes and makes the human body putrefy and rot, torments it with long illness, and at last consumes it with a cruel death.”

Therefore, that you may live sound in mind and healthy in body, my most beloved brothers, keep this rule of living which the holy fathers prescribed for you by word and showed beforehand by living example: fleeing all luxury of foods and drinks, grant to the body only so much as, nature requiring it, is enough for good health. Let food quiet hunger, but not stir the body to lust; let drink quench thirst, not [unclear] the mind. [unclear] expelled our first parents shamefully from the kingdom of paradise and innocence: they did not devour a bird, a fish, a calf, or a pig, so that you should think food was in fault; rather, against God’s command they presumed to take a very cheap apple, so that you may know that not food, but appetite, is liable to the vice of sin.

For it can often happen that some man takes more lavish foods without fault, provided he regulates his appetite, while another receives cheaper foods not without guilt of conscience, when through immoderation he exceeds the measure of temperance. Therefore let none of you think himself secure from the vice of gluttony because he eats neither luxurious nor delicate foods, since it is not food but the appetite for food that is held in fault. For it is quite possible for one eating beans and vegetables to contract the guilt of sin, and for another taking the most delicate birds as food not to sin.

Hence one of the holy fathers says: “A belly filled with any foods at all brings forth seedbeds of lust, nor can a mind choked by the weight of foods govern the rudders of discretion. For not only excess of wine is accustomed to make the mind drunk, but also the excess of all foods, while it is unsteady.”
