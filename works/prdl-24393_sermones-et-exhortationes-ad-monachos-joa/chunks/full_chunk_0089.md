Indeed, if true humility is lacking, the whole gathering of the other virtues will be nothing other than a kind of ruin of a house built without foundation on sand. For in the spiritual building humility lays a stable foundation; charity orders the breadth of that house; the wall is raised on high by the continuous joining of good works in the fervor of the spirit; in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ and in firm hope of the promise of eternal protection and blessedness it is set straight at the summit; and its length is arranged in the most beautiful order in the long-suffering of patience.

Therefore, my brothers, again and again I admonish, adjure, and beseech you to take care to found the building of spiritual exercise in the depth of humility, because no good work is pleasing to God that has been offered outside true humility. Now there are many things which are accustomed to stir the servants of God to true humility. For the consideration of their own worthlessness humbles some within themselves before God, because, when they consider that they have already often experienced themselves to be wretched and weak, they place all their hope and confidence in God, humbled. Others, weighing the multitude of God’s benefits toward them, fear the judgment of ingratitude; and therefore in the sight of God, with great fear and reverence of gratitude, they always walk humbly.

And who among us, brothers, is there to whom the compassion of almighty God has not granted many benefits, even singular ones? If we wish to be grateful for them, above all we must be humble. Others, converted by divine mercy after a multitude of sins to penitence, diligently recall to memory how many evils they have committed against the law of the almighty; and for these, humbled in bitterness of spirit, they detest their own impieties with horror. But we offend in many things; hence it is necessary that we humble ourselves always in mind and heart, and even in body, so that grace may abound where sin previously abounded.

Often, moreover, the providence of divine mercy permits certain persons to sin enormously for a time, so that afterward, converted to penitence, they may learn to remain in true humility. A humble penitent is higher than one who has not sinned but is proud, because humility justifies penitents, while pride condemns the arrogant eternally, even if they have done nothing else evil than this alone, that they are proud.

Others, carrying in their heart by daily meditation the bitter passion of Christ Jesus, humble themselves so much the more, the more they know that they are very insufficient debtors for such great and wondrous benefits of their Redeemer. The Son of God humbled himself, being made man for us, and, filled with many reproaches, voluntarily underwent the most shameful death of the cross, to free us from the destruction of perpetual death. What Christian man, and especially what monk, will not greatly humble his soul before God when he has recalled to memory such great humility of his Creator and Redeemer? Indeed, the man whom Christ’s humility does not stir to meekness: no admonitions of men will profit in him. In vain are we called monks, unless according to our strength we have humbly followed Christ. For this reason he said in the holy Gospel that he is the way, so that we may imitate his humility, and the way of life of so great a master may become the spiritual form of the disciple.

And so, brothers, as much as we can, let us labor with all our strength, since for us he humbled himself into such great humility. Therefore the highest medicine for inducing true humility in the servant of God is this: if daily and very often he recalls to memory the most bitter passion of Christ Jesus.

Tribulations, adversities, and illnesses, together with poverty or want, have also made many humble who perhaps in prosperous and favorable circumstances would have perished, puffed up. And we trust that the poverty which we often say conduces to our salvation does so, since we have a great cause of humility, but find no matter at all for elation and pride. Others, by continual exercise of virtues and by the memory of the death which they must necessarily suffer shortly, have come to great humility. Whom, even the proudest, would not the intent meditation of death humble? Or who is so fierce in spirit that he would not little by little lay aside his swelling pride, if he considered more diligently that he will die shortly?
