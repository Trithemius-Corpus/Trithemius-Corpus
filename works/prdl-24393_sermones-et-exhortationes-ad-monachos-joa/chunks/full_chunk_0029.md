...how much more accustomed study is to be varied, and to make use of alternations against weariness. Hence Jerome again says: “Let prayer often interrupt reading, and let the pleasing alternation of holy prayer come upon the soul continually cleaving to it.” The monk who does not love the secrecy of the cell is not a true soldier of Christ, since he bears the name and habit of a monk without the works; and he who ought to have been solitary for the love of God, having turned back in mind to the world, loves the pursuit of vanity. Hence the blessed pope Gregory says: “He who lives removed bodily from men, but plunges himself in thought amid the tumults of human intercourse through desire for earthly things, is not solitary, although he dwell in solitude. But if someone is pressed bodily by the crowds of the people, and yet suffers no tumults of secular cares in his heart, he is not in the city, even if he dwells in the midst of men.” And this is what holy Jerome says against Jovinian: “The wise man,” he says, “can never be alone. For he has with him all who are good and who have been good; and he has a free mind, and whatever he wishes he brings forth, and he transfers himself wherever he wishes. For what he cannot do in body, he embraces in mind; and if there is a lack of men, he speaks with God.” Therefore a good man will never be alone.

And so, remaining in your cell, always beware of idleness, since nothing so enfeebles all the powers of the mind as idleness, after it has produced acedia from itself. If you have loved God ardently, you will never be idle, because true love of Christ does not allow torpor to arise in the soul, but always impels the lover toward the delight of the beloved. And just as a certain man wise according to the flesh truly said, “Let him love who does not wish to become slothful,” so experience has taught us in spiritual love that the mind which it has begun to inhabit it does not allow to grow torpid with idleness and sluggishness. For divine love always impels one toward better things; and the more it has advanced by burning, so much the greater increase of itself it takes on. But the [workshop?] of this love is a clean conscience; if a monk desires both to have and to preserve it clean, it is necessary first of all that he despise the vanities of the world and the business of temporal things. For it is not possible that a double love reside in one breast; nor can a man succeed in loving Christ who does not wish to despise the world. For God, although in the Trinity of persons he is one in the essence of substance, is either loved by us alone, or is not loved with a partner.

Therefore, so that the conscience may remain clean in all purity, and so that the mind may have strength to love God above all things, let the monk love the secrecy of the cell, and never withdraw from the exercise of inner sweetness. For in the cell of a good and wise monk there is the greatest tranquillity; and if anywhere there is an earthly paradise, it is in the monastery of those who are converted to the purity of the cenobitic institution. There is peace there, piety, goodness, light, virtue, honorableness; there is joy there, purity, sweetness, and great tranquillity of life, where the devout praying monk speaks with the Lord, and the future sweetness of the heavenly kingdom is tasted beforehand by the contemplative soldier. O cell of monks truly serving God, paradise: you are indeed the little garden of most honorable delight, in which the mind is joined to God through love and is sweetly raised up through desire. The monk who loves, venerates, and cultivates you is a true soldier of Christ, and serves the Lord in the fullness of virtues. But he who abhors and deserts the secrecy of the cell despises the workshop of his own salvation. Therefore, my dearest brothers, I beg you to begin to inhabit the secrecy of the cell with desire of mind; without love for it no one will be able to come to the perfection of virtues in the cenobium.

*On the manual labor of monks. Homily VII.*

“Because you shall eat the labors of your hands,” says the prophet of the Lord, “blessed are you, and it shall be well with you.” If all are reckoned blessed who live from the proper labor of their hands, certainly no one idle eats bread without guilt and fault. For almighty God imposed this punishment upon the first-formed Adam for the transgression of the original command, announcing to him that in the sweat of his face he would work food from the earth with his hands.
