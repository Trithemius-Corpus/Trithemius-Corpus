From the beginning the disobedience of the first man destroyed the way of eternal happiness, which the obedience of Jesus Christ restored. For he was made obedient to the Father for us, even unto death; and by his most innocent passion he restored to us the happiness we had lost. Hence the way to salvation is humble obedience, as our holy lawgiver Benedict cries out in the Rule: “Listen, my son, to the precepts of the master, and incline the ear of your heart, and willingly receive the admonition of a loving father and carry it out effectively, so that by the labor of obedience you may return to him from whom you had departed by the sloth of disobedience.”

Obedience is the tree of eternal salvation, which grows in the garden of monastic humility. Obedience is the dwelling-place of the Holy Spirit, and the quiet tomb of one’s own will. Obedience is the merit of heavenly happiness, and the sole lesson of monastic discipline. Obedience is the extermination of all pride and arrogance, and the persecutor of all vices. Obedience conquers every malice, and extinguishes every disordered pleasure of the flesh. Obedience makes the conscience pure and clean, and preserves true humility in the mind. Obedience is the remedy for the wound of the soul, and the foundation of all monastic observance. Obedience is the faithful guardian of all good works, and the discipline of a sincere mind. Obedience is the beginning of mortification, and the glorious ornament of monks. Obedience is the preserver of peace and concord, without which the vigor of monastic discipline perishes. Obedience always directs the edge of the mind toward God; and he who is truly mortified in spirit despises all the consolations of this world. Obedience makes a monk close to God, and far removed from all filth of soul. Obedience is the adornment of the soul and the garment of beauty; without it no one will come to the kingdom of eternal brightness. Obedience is the mother of victory; and whoever knows how, for the honor of God, to be subject in humility will obtain the crown of martyrdom in heaven. Obedience separates the monk from the world; and he who follows it with his whole heart will be great before the Most High. Obedience is the consummation of Christian perfection; in its humble workshop, the more a monk advances, the holier he becomes. Obedience is sure security at the hour of death; for a monk, when he is about to depart from here, will fly forth more securely the more ready he has been for obedience to commands. Obedience is the rewarder of the humble, and the chief emblem of merits. Obedience is the rule of Christian gentleness, and the strongest inner nourishment of charity. Obedience is the shelter of chastity, and the ornament of most pure innocence. Obedience is the wisdom of monks; and to acquiesce in the commands of prelates is the highest philosophy of cloistered men.

Obedience and humility are the wealth and riches of monks; and to possess continually in the mind the memory of the Lord’s passion is a sweetness greater than all the feasts of the world. Obedience and humility make the monk; but he who is rebellious and proud wears the monastic habit without cause. Obedience in the mind is the voluntary mortification of the members while they are alive; its merit is of such dignity that a man has nothing greater that may be offered in honor of God. Let each one offer for the love of God what he can: without doubt, he has offered more than all who has slaughtered his own will for God through obedience. It is indeed a great good to distribute one’s resources for Christ’s love to the uses of the poor, and to relieve by alms the want of the needy. But by as much as the whole outweighs the part, by as much as the free choice of the will is nobler than necessity, by as much as heaven stands apart from earth, by so much more has he offered who, for the love of God and by laying aside his will, has forever denied himself. For whatever temporal substance you have offered to God is not yours but another’s, and is of less value than what is proper to you, both in esteem and in reward. You have one thing proper to yourself which, offered to God, is pleasing above all things. What is this, if not free will, which the almighty Creator placed in your power? A good will is greater than all riches, for nothing richer can be offered to God, nothing will be judged more acceptable.

This most precious gift, my brothers, you once offered to God when, denying yourselves and all worldly things for Christ’s honor, you put on the monastic habit.
