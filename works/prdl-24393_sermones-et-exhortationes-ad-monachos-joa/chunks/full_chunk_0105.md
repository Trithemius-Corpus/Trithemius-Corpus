*On the brotherly charity of monks.*

It is [unclear] to love one another mutually, and, with regard to charity, to direct one’s attention rather to the soul than to the body. Thus by reason and understanding one is distinguished from brute animals, so that the regard of our love is referred to the mind, by which we understand, not to the body, which we have in common with beasts. And far from holy monks be all affections of the flesh, for they are not ignorant of the rule of mutual and sincere charity.

If love for one another is pure, upright, and conceived solely from the fear of God, so that no advantage of any corruptible thing intervenes, but the simple intention of charity remains; and true charity, simple and pure, does not seek the byways of evil conspiracies, nor arm itself with the murmur of contention for the devising of iniquity. He who possesses true charity of brotherhood in his heart preserves peace in all things and stirs up no matter or causes of destructive dissension. Charity, spending its one [unclear], does injury to no one, and whatever it does not wish to be done to itself, transfers to the person of no other.

Do not think that you have true brotherly charity when you love one another mutually for the sake of the flesh or the advantage of any temporal thing whatever; for where the cause of love is not the Lord, the affection of the one who loves is always vicious, either in part or in whole. But he will rightly love his brother who loves him with the love of God, and for the sake of the common participation in the kingdom which we await. Therefore, as Saint John the bishop says, we have been baptized into one body of the Church, so that we may not be dissolved; in that harmony and friendship which exists mutually, let us preserve forever the charity and mutual agreement of one body. Just as no one outside the unity of the Catholic Church can obtain salvation, so no one is believed able to be saved without brotherly charity.

Charity, therefore, is necessary for you before all things for salvation; without it, the remaining works of virtues are altogether fruitless. For there can be no good thing, no reward salutary unto life, which has not drawn its origin from it. Charity is a certain road to the compendium of virtue; if anyone enters upon it by the straight path of the journey, he will need no guidance of a leader. Pure, simple, and upright charity is not accustomed to harm one’s neighbor, nor to bring evil upon anyone. Charity is accustomed to spend upon all the affection which it grants to its own advantages, and whatever it hopes may conduce to its own salvation, this it desires to be common to all people in Christ Jesus.

Charity, free from any disturbance, is exercised in the office of love; and though it loves all people most greatly, nevertheless it is provoked on behalf of none to a zeal of bitterness. It is always solid, always constant, always stable and firm, because it is neither touched by passions nor agitated by movements of unrest. It offers itself common to all, and so spends its love upon each individual that it sees its own as common to all. Charity does not neglect itself in those things that pertain to the reason of being and of the soul; but in those things which regard the advantage of the body, it often puts the causes of others before its own interests. Great indeed is the dominion of charity, which compels a person to make another’s disadvantage his own.

Thus you also, my brothers, who have solemnly taken up the service of Christ, who through the steep path of obedience pant after eternal life: if you do not wish to run in vain in the [unclear] of so laborious a contest, take care before all things, we exhort you, always to preserve brotherly charity inviolate among you. For unless you have sincere and perfect charity toward one another, you labor without fruit in regular observance. Abstinence from eating flesh will contribute nothing to the salvation of your souls; the renunciation of all private property will profit nothing toward the attainment of eternal happiness, if brotherly love does not hold a place in your hearts.

Therefore, desiring to be saved with Christ in truth, love one another mutually; and as Christ loved you, so, we pray, love one another. What each one of you desires to be done to himself, let him, without envy and for the love of God, bestow that very thing upon his brother at all times. Let divine love conquer in you all quarrels of the flesh and all [unclear] contentions; nor let destructive dissension of the flesh find a place where the fullness of brotherly charity ought continually to dwell. And unless all in the monastery, according to the law of Christian institution, are [unclear], each of you must always necessarily abstain from harming another. But if through contentions and injuries you harm one another mutually, you are certainly neither monks nor true Christians. For it befits all cloistered persons and monks to be outstanding in the observance of charity, and to be far removed from envy, hatred, and rancor.
