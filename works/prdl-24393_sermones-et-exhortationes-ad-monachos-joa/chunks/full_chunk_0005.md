*Epistle*

John Trithemius, called the Sponheimite, to the reverend father and his teacher, lord Johannes de Colenhausen, abbot of the monastery of the holy martyrs Marcellinus and Peter in Seligenstadt, and formerly my predecessor at Sponheim: reverence and honor.

I do not know, teacher ever to be venerated by me, under what unlucky star that monastery of Sponheim stands as having been built, since rarely does an abbot die in it; rather, almost all, afflicted by weariness, have, now for almost seventy years, as I find from the annals, resigned of their own will. We are not wholly ignorant why you too, after the example of those who went before, abandoned us poor men before the time: we who rightly accuse our poverty and the rebellious rashness of certain men. I now experience, alas, the cause which impelled your mind to leave the abbacy of Sponheim, utterly barren and unfruitful, over which you presided for thirteen years after the reform, not without continual anxiety of mind and infinite labors. I pass over the wrongs of very many of the brothers which you endured, all of which indeed are daily doubled upon me. You ordered me to send to your fatherhood the book of my exhortations to the brothers, which recently, amid the raging occupations of worldly affairs, I composed by lamplight; and although it is unworthy of your reading, I nevertheless did not wish to resist the command of a parent. Therefore I pray your gentleness kindly to apply the file of correction: if anything is lacking, add it; cut away what is superfluous; and what is unsuitable either hand over to the flames or at least set in order. Farewell, sweetest father, mindful of us. From Sponheim, on the sixth day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1386 [editorial note: likely a printer’s error for 1496 — Trithemius was born in 1462, making 1386 impossible].

*All things. I.*
*Folio III.*

It befits the soldiery of Christians, brothers, and especially of monks, to be subject to the various calamities of this world, and, like gold in the furnace, to be continually exercised and tested by tribulations. For it belongs to monks to surpass the common little rule of the soldiery of Christians by a higher manner of living, and to obey not only the Lord’s commandments but also his counsels. It belongs to monks utterly to despise riches, honors, and all the pleasures of the present world for love of God, and, having overcome fleshly desires, to be continually at leisure for God alone. For a monk is so called as one solitary from unity, who even by the very word of his name is admonished to contempt of the world. Therefore the office of a monk is to remain with himself in solitude, cleansed and purified from vices, and sweetly to love Christ above all things. For the goal of monks, to which regular institution is directed, is Christ; whoever refuses to imitate him is neither a monk nor even a Christian. Hence, my brothers, we ought to know that whoever does not follow our Lord and Savior in true patience and humility gains nothing for salvation from being called a Christian or a monk. For he himself is the one who says in the holy Gospel: Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven, he shall enter the kingdom of heaven.
