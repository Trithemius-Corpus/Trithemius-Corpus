Certainly, my brothers, the things we have promised are great, and a greater necessity lies upon us to keep what we have professed. For unless we faithfully observe everything that the Rule of our blessed father Saint Benedict commands us, the justice of almighty God will judge us severely as perjured and impious transgressors. For it is very dangerous not to render to God what has been promised, and through forgetfulness of the holy institution to violate one’s purpose.

Therefore, to satisfy the duty of pastoral care, I am accustomed daily to admonish you to the observance of regular discipline; nor hereafter shall the necessary word of salvation ever be lacking to you, so long as my spirit remains in this prison of the flesh. But you, I pray, take care not to be fruitless hearers, since the matter concerns the soul, which, once lost, is never recovered. Therefore, all of you who have come together in the monastery to run the course of intellectual combat, all of you who for love of Christ have once renounced your own will and the world: take up holy obedience as the helmet of salvation, and you will carry off a sure victory over the enemy.

For obedience is the strongest armor of monks, which no adversities will be able to harm. For Scripture says: “He who keeps the commandment will experience no evil.” In Proverbs also it is said thus: “The obedient man will speak of victories.” Obedience makes a monk invincible in all temptations; and he who follows in all things the counsel of his elder will never suffer loss to the salvation of his soul. For obedience is a short and rough road, by which one quickly ascends to the summit of perfection. But the monk who is a true soldier of Christ never trusts his own judgment, but in all things follows and observes the direction of his pastor.

Indeed, he who is a true lover of Christ, the monk who wholly desires to be faithful, loves his abbot as the vicar of God, and, always obedient to his commands, counsels, and ordinances in all things, submits cheerfully. In order that a monk may be able to do this confidently, no boy, no unlearned man, no carnal man, no one lacking discretion, nor anyone light-minded or inexperienced in spiritual combats, is to be ordained abbot, but a man mature in character, learned in the divine Scriptures, walking in the spirit in the true way of life of holiness, pious, gentle, tranquil in mind, and discreet, one who knows how to show judgment to the wicked and mercy to the well-disposed.

For whenever a restless, turbulent, indiscreet, and inexperienced man is appointed abbot over monks, the integrity of regular discipline is at once shipwrecked. Subjection, and with it the obedience of humility, must perish when subjects cannot have firm confidence in their pastor. For if the abbot is a mocker of his brothers, or an over-harsh despiser of the weak, who will open to him any secret whatever of his heart? Therefore the greatest care must be taken at the beginning that such a pastor be appointed for sheep bereft of one, who seeks nothing other than the honor of God and the salvation of souls.

The monk who has obtained such a pastor should give thanks to God, for in these times of ours he rarely occurs who knows how to show himself in all things a worthy and suitable prelate. He is deservedly to be loved by all his subjects and, as the vicar of Christ in the monastery, honored with continual veneration. Let the thoughts of all be opened to him without fear, since he seeks nothing other than the progress of each one. If the physician of sick bodies must be prudent, experienced, and endowed with the highest discretion, how much more fitting is it that the abbot be an excellent man, since he is the physician of souls?

Blessed are the monks to whom God’s mercy has granted a useful and suitable pastor, provided that it has not also denied them the ability to understand this very thing. Otherwise a good pastor of bad sheep is not recognized in his uprightness, but rather, through the ingratitude of the depraved, is tormented with the Lord. For often the judgments of monks are blind whenever an opinion is sought about prelates; and those who by their conduct corrupt the integrity of the order rarely judge truly and rightly about pastors. Because of the ingratitude of subjects, rulers are very often changed for the worse; and monks who despise a good and useful pastor in the present life have received, by the just judgment of God, a foolish and useless successor.

Those who are subject in simplicity walk rightly, and those who truly imitate Christ do not pass judgment on the one presiding over them.
