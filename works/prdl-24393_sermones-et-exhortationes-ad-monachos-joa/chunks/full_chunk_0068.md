Therefore great discretion is needed, so that food necessary for the support of life alone is supplied to the body, and no excess of indulgence occurs. Hence the holy Pope Gregory says: “It is a great labor of discretion to give something to the demanding body and to deny it something; by not giving, to restrain gluttony, and by giving, to nourish nature.” For through abstinence vices are to be extinguished, not the flesh; and through food the body is to be nourished, not sins. Thus, then, it is necessary that the monk maintain the art of continence in his diet, so that he kills not the flesh but vices. For often, while we pursue the enemy in the flesh, we severely slaughter, through immoderate abstinence, the spirit of devotion which we love. More often also, while we have compassion on the [unclear], as though sparing a fellow-citizen, we nourish an enemy for battle against us. But when the vice of gluttony rules, men lose everything they have done bravely; for when the belly is not restrained, all the virtues are overthrown together. For one does not rise up to the conflict of spiritual combat if the enemy placed very near within us, namely the appetite of gluttony, is not first subdued by us, with God’s help; because unless we overcome the things that are nearer to us, we hasten in vain to fight against those placed farther away. For war is waged in vain in the field against foreign enemies if within the walls of the city there is a citizen lying in ambush. We never overcome the assaults of demons unless we first overcome gluttony in ourselves.

Let no one, brothers, deceive himself by empty persuasion, so as to believe that there is no sin among beans and vegetables, if the mind loosens too far the appetite of receiving by excessive pleasure. For in taking food one can everywhere be [unclear], when appetite is extended beyond the rule of nature. See, we never eat meat either in the monastery or outside it; here in this open countryside we see fish very rarely; no one among us has ever seen more sumptuous foods; we neither can nor indeed ought to have delicate fare. Our foods are barley, oats, beans, peas and vegetables, milk also, cheese and eggs. From these we receive the daily supports of our life; and hunger stirs appetite so that disgust for them may not arise. Outside the time of the Lenten fast, fish are so rare for us that scarcely once in a month a few are brought from the river Nahe, and those very small. In the whole Bursfelde reform there is no monastery less provided with the necessity of fish. But can we therefore not fall into the vice of gluttony, because we cannot find more sumptuous foods? Indeed, since the vice is not in the food, as has already been said, but in the appetite, the eater of peas or beans can sin no less in eating than he who is accustomed to eat the most sumptuous and choice foods. Poverty does not make us wholly [unclear]; [unclear] simple and rustic [foods], by which nature may somehow be sustained. This, my brothers, I pray, let us take care of: that in that which produces loathing of delicacy our conscience not be polluted by excess. Let us take with patience and thanksgiving, from what the Lord has given, as much as necessity itself requires; and let us not think that the snares of the devil cannot harm us, since he overthrew the first men in paradise not by more sumptuous food, but by a small and common apple. For the vice is not in the food but in the appetite, when either we desire things forbidden to us, or, eagerly gaping after permitted things, we use them otherwise than we ought.

Nor does abstinence befit anyone more than a monk, who, since he has been specially called to the soldiery of Christ, ought not by his profession to have leisure for any delicacies. Our poverty imposes upon us a certain necessity of abstinence; for we can restrain ourselves to temperance all the more easily, since the less we overflow with foods, the more purely we fast in holiness of mind. For temperance is more easily preserved in poverty, but in riches it is indeed more quickly endangered. What the food of monks ought to be, blessed Jerome makes clear, saying in a certain letter to Paulinus: “Let your food be cheap and taken in the evening: namely vegetables and legumes; and sometimes consider it the height of delicacy to eat a few little fish. He who desires Christ and feeds on that heavenly bread does not greatly seek what dung he may make from precious foods.
