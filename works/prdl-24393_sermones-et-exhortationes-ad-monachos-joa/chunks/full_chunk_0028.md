For, as Saint Augustine says, he is less goaded by pleasures who is not where there is an abundance of pleasures; and he suffers less trouble from avarice who, placed far from human beings, does not see riches. Since, therefore, the holy Spirit of God loves the secrecy of the mind but shudders at publicity, it belongs to you, brothers, to love the solitude of cells as the workshop of all virtues, and never to estrange yourselves from its dwelling without a reasonable cause. For a monk who loves secrecy, not an idle one, who has learned to remain steadfastly in his cell, sometimes enjoys many holy consolations, of which the wandering and restless man is necessarily deprived.

Just as a fish dies if it is kept too long outside the water, so all the devotion of a monk perishes if he wanders too often outside his cell. Let us imitate John the Baptist, whom Chrysostom says is the chief of monks: he, a monk, as soon as he was born, lived in the desert, was nourished in the desert, and as a monk awaited Christ in solitude. For just as it is difficult for a tree placed beside the road to preserve its fruits until maturity, so it is difficult for a monk who often wanders outside his cell and is occupied in the affairs of the world to keep the fruits of righteousness unstained until the end.

Let us therefore withdraw, monks of God, from tumults, and let us plant ourselves in the secrecy of monastic solitude, so that neither the world may have anything in common with us, nor we with it. For a mind that is shaken by the cares and anxieties of the world is rarely truly rooted in the love of God. Indeed, movement of the body often alters the state of the mind, and a person who is always immersed in outward things is never illuminated by the fervor of the divine Spirit. For quiet of mind is born from the firm steadiness of the body, and the man who loves solitude for the love of God remains stronger and united with himself in holy exercise.

Whoever among you, brothers, is an eager lover of his own salvation should learn to remain quiet in his cell, and, as far as he can, should strive to occupy himself continually, with desire, in the works of divine love. But be quiet in your cell, not idle; tranquil, not drowsy; always devoted to spiritual exercise, not slackened by torpor of soul. The instruments of spiritual exercises in the cell are four: to read, to write, to pray, and to meditate. A fifth, however, is granted to few, who after true purity of heart are raised to the mountain of contemplation of the Lord.

Finally, these four exercises of monks dwelling in the solitude of the cell must be so moderated that they produce neither weariness nor torpor of mind, but that a suitable time be assigned to each activity, so that, while alternation lightens the mind, the little body is not burdened beyond its strength. Therefore the reading of the divine Scriptures should have its own suitable hour for study; when it senses that a desire for devotion is present, it should at once be changed into fervent prayer. Manual labor, moreover, which is enjoined on the monk dwelling in the tranquility of solitude, is very useful, lest harmful lukewarmness arise in the mind.

For the reading of holy Scripture stirs the monk to the charity of God; meditation on the Lord’s passion and on the benefits of God inflames the mind to the fervor of most ardent love; prayer strengthens the weak soul; and manual labor, continued temperately, preserves one from torpor and weariness. When we pray earnestly and devoutly, we speak with our God, who is present everywhere; but when we read in the holy Scriptures, we are taught by the Lord himself what we should seek or flee. Prayer, if it is ardent in the love of God, purifies the mind from sins and vices; reading, however, instructs and forms it, as Saint Isidore says: “By prayers,” he says, “we are cleansed; by readings we are instructed. Both are good, if possible; if both are not possible, it is better to pray than to read.”

Therefore the monk who wishes to spend his time in the cell usefully and fruitfully should strive continually to occupy mind and body in these exercises by turns, because a soul that keeps watch in such labors gives no entrance to perverse thoughts. Hence the divine Jerome, admonishing a monk, says: “Always do some good thing, so that the devil may find you occupied.” For he is less assailed by the enticements of the flesh who, always usefully occupied, admits no weariness of mind.
