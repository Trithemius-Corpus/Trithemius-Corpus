Although you live chastely, O monk, pure and holy, and as long as the spirit remains in the body, do not presume to have confidence in chastity from clay. The food of chastity is fasting and abstinence, and, in every action, the support of wholesome continence. But when you have attained all things, do not trust in yourself, because he whom we know to have eaten nothing fell from heaven. The mortification of the body is both very necessary and useful for preserving the beauty of chastity; but however much you have advanced in either, do not trust in yourself, but, always watchful and hoping in the Lord's help, be afraid. For unless you keep yourself continually and most earnestly in the fear of the Lord, the house of your conscience will quickly be overthrown. He runs in vain who presumes to guard chastity by his own strength. For unless the Lord causes the dwelling-place of chastity to be built, the one who builds the wall of abstinence labors in vain. And unless you become, with all your strength, a co-worker with divine grace, you will in no way obtain the beauty of chastity. For God will not make you chaste without your will, nor will you attain this great gift unless he, through his mercy, comes to the aid of your weakness. Therefore yours it will be, O monk, to offer the desire for chastity with good will, and, in humble confidence, to await certain increase from the Lord. Apply fasting, and the Lord's help will not be lacking. Chastity is a gift of grace, not of nature; and to live in the flesh as though beyond the flesh belongs to angelic strength, not human. Nevertheless it is not difficult for a man of good will to preserve chastity, especially one who is separated from the tumult of worldly people and lives solitary in the midst of monks. A monk, too, who, young and innocent, enters a monastery of good reform, if he keeps his mind continually occupied in the fear of the Lord, is less assailed by lust. For the experience of lust is pernicious, since, as someone truly said, it seeks to conquer souls, not to satisfy the veins. But a monk who is a lover of lust can easily extinguish the heat if, fleeing idleness and fullness, he is willing to occupy himself continually in spiritual exercise. For among all vices none assaults chastity more greatly and more dangerously than fullness and idleness. If a monk overcomes these two evils, he will easily preserve chastity. Hence the most holy princes of monks, wishing to cut away the causes of lust, established for us a spare and meager diet according to a fixed measure, by which the body is sustained for necessity and is not fattened for lust. They also decreed that scantiness of sleep was useful for monks, because immoderate rest expands the flesh no less than gluttony. But they wished idleness, as the most pernicious plague of the soul, to be far from cloistered persons, dividing the time most wisely so that a monk would never be found idle. Hear these things, brothers, and grasp them in your mind, you who carry a great treasure in a fragile little body. Always flee idleness; do something good, so that whenever the tempter, the devil, approaches, he may find you usefully occupied. For truly, he who is idle lies open to the snares of the tempter; but he who is occupied in good work has closed every entrance to snares. Therefore the mind must be occupied with continual exercise, so that it is never found idle. Blessed is that monk who occupies his mind at all times in good exercises and never sleeps in the sleep of torpor and sloth. Blessed is the spiritual soldier of Christ who always keeps watch in the fear of the Lord and immediately restrains, from the beginning, all the disordered motions of the flesh. For it is not enough to guard true chastity unharmed, if someone plays with unclean thoughts as though deliberately. There must always be vigilance against the adversary of our salvation, who is never conquered more quickly than if he is choked off immediately from the beginning.
