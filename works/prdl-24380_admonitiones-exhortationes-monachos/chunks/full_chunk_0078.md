He does not threaten insult; he stands fixed in his holy purpose, and however much the wicked may rush upon him, he is not moved. Just as timely rain increases the hay in the meadows, so a monk’s merit grows from humble endurance of injuries. Humility is the sinew of all virtues, and steadfast patience under injuries for the love of God is the mine of good works.

Therefore, when you are about to go out on the road from the monastery, prepare your heart to endure injuries, and strengthen your mind against the insolence of mockers. When scoundrels shout after your back, repeating the name “monk,” remember Christ mocked and spat upon before the governor, who did not answer a word, but kept patience in silence. Imitate this Leader, O monk; always listen to this Teacher, who was meek and humble of heart.

Do not answer a word to those who mock you; do not turn your face back toward them, but say with the prophet: “Those who sought evils for me spoke vanities, and all day long they meditated deceits. But I, like a deaf man, did not hear, and like a mute man not opening his mouth. And I became like a man who does not hear, and who has no rebukes in his mouth.” This Christ did; this Jesus taught; this the King of all commands. If you wish to be his disciple, you must always be prepared for every injury.

When you are on the road, do not neglect to say your canonical prayers with whatever devotion you can, and always turn over something good with yourself in your mind, either by praying or by meditating. The Lord walks with you, and his holy angels accompany you as you walk. Therefore keep your heart clean in all things, lest you offend the majesty of such great companions. The Lord is wholly eye and ear, and nothing is hidden from his knowledge.

Keep constant watch over your words, and let deliberation of the heart precede every word of your mouth, so that you say absolutely nothing for which you might have to repent. The mouth of a monk speaks few things, and reasonable things, and his lips never go beyond measure in speech. All his conduct is set toward the edification of his neighbors; and he who teaches many by his character speaks useful things even when silent.

He who is a true lover of God walks most humbly on the road; and he who fears the Most High restrains his mouth from loud laughter. In your whole life consider yourself mortal, and live as one dying, as though already breathing out your last breath. Happy is the kingdom, and rich before God the province, in which monks rise with Boötes, since where there is no fear of God, the theaters are filled with cloistered men.

A monk on the road is like a man who speaks while sleeping; when he has awakened from sleep, he remembers none of the things he had dreamed. For thus the monastic canon commands. Nor should anyone presume to report to others whatever he has seen or heard outside the monastery, because it is very destructive. But if anyone has presumed to do so, let him be subject to the regular punishment. Likewise also he who has presumed to leave the cloisters of the monastery, or to go anywhere, or to do anything, however small, without the abbot’s command.
