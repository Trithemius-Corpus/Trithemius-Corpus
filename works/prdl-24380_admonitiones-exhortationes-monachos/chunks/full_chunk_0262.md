If we consider how perilous a thing is the rule of souls, how heavy a burden is pastoral care, and to how strict an examination it is liable, it would be a wonder if ever in this life our heart presumed upon security. For Scripture says: “A most severe judgment shall be made upon those who are in authority.” For, as blessed Benedict declares in the Rule, the abbot will have to render account to God for the souls of all the monks committed to him. Therefore he says: “Let the abbot who has been ordained always consider what a burden he has taken up, and to whom he will render account of his stewardship.” It is certainly a heavy and dangerous burden to render account for the souls of many, when scarcely anyone is able to answer for himself. O what will abbots devoted to earthly things have to answer to the supreme Shepherd, who destroy the souls of their brothers by evil example and feed them neither by word nor by deed? What shall I say about certain abbots of our order, who neither read the Rule of Saint Benedict, which they profess, nor, if they read it, understand it? I lie if I have not known abbots established under our chapter who are wrapped in such great darkness of ignorance that they do not understand the smallest sentence from the Rule. They neither know nor love the divine Scriptures. Submerged in the vanities of the world, they vanish away. They serve the desires of the flesh, not the Spirit. According to the command of our lawgiver, the abbot must be learned in divine law, so that he may know whence to bring forth new things and old; chaste, sober, merciful, etc. What will those men say, what will they teach, what will they exhort, who understand nothing of the holy Scriptures, nothing of regular discipline? O good God, to what misery our order has come, which once was most learned and full of most holy men. Where now is the lover of regular observance? Where is the study of the Scriptures? Where is the preservation of holiness? All have perished together, and in only a few do we find traces of former honor. Our abbots, except for a few, either given over to avarice or carried away by the vanities of the world, think of nothing less than knowledge of the Scriptures, although many consider themselves learned; but from their works the character of their erudition is known. Now the avaricious man is thought learned. Now he is called religious who joins field to field, who keeps watch over earthly profits, who gives free rein to his appetite for gathering riches. But believe me, fathers, because I am about to speak the truth: temporal riches do not make discipline stable in the order. O how terrible a sentence those abbots will undergo who neglect the Rule, who do not go before their monks by word and by example of holiness; who despise spiritual exercises; who devote themselves to earthly gains; who contemn the discipline of monastic honor. Would that they were wise and understood, and foresaw their last things, and either governed the burden of so great a rule with solicitude, or, if they could not, humbly laid it down.

Happy is he who stands solicitously upon his watch; who always does the will of the Lord; who, without pretense, lives according to the purity of the Rule.

But for the present these things which we have said concerning the state and reformation of our order will, as I believe, suffice; and let their reading, with the end of the chapter, also come to its close. Moreover, all and singular things which either in this treatise or in others I have written or shall write, I submit to the examination of the sacrosanct Roman Church, whose faith, doctrine, and definition I wish always and everywhere to preserve, teach, believe, and defend as long as I live. But if anything, which God forbid, has been said by me otherwise than she holds, teaches, and believes, I now by these presents simply revoke it and wish it to be held as not said. By this my declaration I wish and judge the supreme pontiff, the vicar of Christ, to be in all things the judge and moderator of my faith and doctrine.

The present treatise was read at table in the provincial chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict of the Province of Mainz, celebrated at Hirsau, in the year of the Lord 1493, on Jubilate Sunday and on the following days until the end. Moreover, with all the abbots gathered there willing and consenting, it was decreed that it should be printed, and that in future provincial chapters it should always be read publicly at table, as is clear in the statutes.
