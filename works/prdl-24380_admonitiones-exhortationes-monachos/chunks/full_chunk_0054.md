Homily IX.

On the guarding and origin of thoughts. Homily IX.

Solomon, that once most wise king, in the book which he entitled Proverbs, exhorting his son to true wisdom, said among other things: “My son, attend to my wisdom, and incline your ear to my prudence, that you may guard your thoughts, and that your lips may preserve discipline.” For it is no small part of wisdom, dearly beloved brothers, if a monk knows how to discern his thoughts prudently and to guard their movements carefully at all times. It is a great thing to think no evil; but it belongs to no less prudence to be able to repel evil thoughts from their very beginning. For the depths of the human heart are, by profound judgment, mobile, varied, manifold, and always unstable; to observe its beginnings and restrain its movements is the highest prudence and a great sign of wisdom.

If I call the heart of man a mill driven by perpetual motion, I shall not have strayed from the truth, especially if anyone wishes to weigh its restlessness exactly. For what else is the human heart than a kind of mill, always grinding something while in motion? The human heart is never idle, never rests without motion, but is always doing something; and just as an empty mill consumes itself by its own motion, so the heart of man shamefully runs away from itself when the thought of good does not bind it fast. It is always moved toward some other object with the desire of its own love; it is always grinding, always acting, always occupied with something else that gives delight, whether it is good or evil. As the body is not moved without the soul, so the mind of man does not remain fixed in the good unless it has been continually restrained by holy exercises.

Therefore the wise man said in Ecclesiasticus: “Gladness of heart is the life of man, and a treasure without failing of holiness, and the rejoicing of a man is long life. Have mercy on your soul, pleasing God, and restrain yourself, and gather your heart in his holiness, and drive sadness far from you.” What is the gathering of the heart, if not the diligent guarding of thought? For he gathers his heart in the holiness of the Lord who continually observes the movements of his thoughts in the fear of God. And therefore Scripture says: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.”

Now thoughts take the beginnings of their movements from those things which are presented by the senses; and they wander the more dangerously, the less they are considered from the beginning by the judgment of reason. Therefore whoever desires to be watchful over his thoughts, whoever wishes to gather his heart in the holiness of the Lord, must first guard his outward senses most diligently and place the bridle of prudence upon each of them. Hence Scripture commands thus concerning hearing and sight: “Hedge your ears with thorns, and do not listen to a wicked tongue,” and, “Make doors for your mouth, and bars for your ears.” “Blessed is the man who stops his ears lest he hear bloodshed, and shuts his eyes lest he see evil.” In holy Job also we read thus: “I made a covenant with my eyes, that I should not think about a virgin.” But against those who have fallen into the pit of sins through the service of the senses, the Lord bears witness thus through the prophet Jeremiah: “Death has come up through your windows; it has entered your houses, to destroy the little children outside and the young men from the streets.” Death enters our houses through the windows whenever a wicked thought is admitted into the mind through the service of sight or hearing.
