On Continual Preparation for Death.

A man is judged foolish among men who prefers dross to gold; but he is considered most foolish before God who places temporal pleasure before everlasting goods. Ecclesiasticus 33.

He who loves a serene conscience should flee idleness; for, as Scripture says, every idle man is in desires, and idleness has taught much evil. The mind must be occupied with good exercises, and the fire of holy conduct, which burns with charity, must be nourished by continual practice. For an idle man lives neither for himself nor for his neighbor, but is always given over to his belly or to sleep: he opens his mind to vices, and keeps it closed and empty of virtues. Idleness, the kindler of lust, quickly extinguishes the fervor of spiritual devotion.

Abstinence also is especially very necessary for a monk, so that his conscience may remain clean. For, as Saint Jerome says, a full belly readily foams over into lust; and he who feeds his servant delicately will find him rebellious. The lover of serenity of conscience should be constant in holy prayers; for as dogs flee a stone, so demons flee devout and continual prayer. He should also carefully beware of sloth, and likewise sadness of mind; and as soon as he feels some temptation creeping in, he should have recourse to the consolation of the Scriptures. Indeed, a foul thought will not enter a mind which the word of God has filled. Whatever we do, whatever we think, whatever we undertake, let us always know that God’s presence is at hand, and we shall easily overcome disordered delight. A good conscience never forgets the presence of God, but always meditates that its judge is present. Act now, my brothers, so that you may have consciences pure from every crime; and if anyone feels himself burdened by some fault, let him return without delay, through true repentance, to necessary purity. For unless you have clean and good consciences at all times, you live as apostates, not as monks.

On Continual Preparation for Death. Homily 25.

The highest philosophy of cloistered men, my dearest brothers, is continual meditation on death. Hence a certain wise man, considering the usefulness of this philosophy, admonished every mortal, saying: Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever. Meditation on death draws a man back from the pleasures of the flesh and brings in contempt for all corruptible things. Meditation on death despises present things through voluntary poverty, and drives the mind toward desire for future things. Meditation on death removes pride far away and plants true humility in the heart of the one meditating. Meditation on death makes a monk attentive to the observance of regular discipline and never allows him to be idle. Meditation on death cuts off all the vanities of the world and preserves the mind in the highest temperance. Meditation on death makes one despise the vain and passing honors of the world, and think of nothing other than the future judgment of God. Meditation on death
