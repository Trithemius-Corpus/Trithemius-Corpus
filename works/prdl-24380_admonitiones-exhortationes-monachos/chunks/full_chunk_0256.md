*On the State and Ruin of the Monastic Order.*

The presidents, judging that our order could not long subsist in observance without a special remedy, determined through the provincial chapter to come to its aid; in which, through the diligence of the presidents, all would be instructed to live according to the Rule, and no one could deviate from the reform once begun. For in the chapter of abbots vices are to be corrected, exhortations to virtues are to be made, so that the bad may find something to fear, and the good may hear something by which they may become better. Therefore various things are read for edification, so that increases may always be present to good minds. There the abbot is instructed concerning his governance; there he is admonished concerning the danger of pastoral care; there what he is to do is prescribed, and what he is to avoid for the love of God is forbidden. Finally, when we gather thus for chapter, many useful things come about for us, which, if we seek the salvation of souls, are very necessary. For, to pass over the rest, mutual acquaintance in charity is there conceived, and experience shows how greatly this conduces to love of regular observance. There, from acquaintance, brotherly charity is both preserved and increased; otherwise it would quickly grow lukewarm if it did not have the nourishment of mutual recognition. Here an easy access to uniformity lies open, where, from the conference of the fathers, a mutual fire is prepared for honorable conduct, and from the fellowship of holiness an example is offered.

These are the camps of God, O Fathers, and the defenses of our order, by which all the devices of our adversaries may be reduced to nothing. But there is not one sincerity in all, nor does equality of manners appear among you. Whence could such a disparity of manners arise in one order, where capitular unity is seen? Who has divided between you and the Rule of Saint Benedict, O Fathers, long-haired with matted hair? Why do you not look to uniformity, you who celebrate assemblies of unity? If you love the holy order which you profess, why do you not love uniformity in it? What is the shadow of a long-haired head to you? What ornament do you think the summit of a curled crown bears? Believe me, Fathers: a face covered with hair is reckoned a disgrace in a monk, not an honor. O how beautiful this assembly would be if, as it ought, it came together into one discipline of regular observance. What shall I say? I praise the assembly, but I rebuke the deformity among its members. For because of the disorder of the order, the present unity grows cheap. The outward habit indicates what the inward garment reproves in the life of many. Truly, Fathers, this disparity of manners and habits offends the provincial chapter no little, since it is known to have been instituted for this reason, that equality of life might be preserved above all things. For outward discord is the sign of inward discord. Therefore, that we may be of one mind in the observance of regular discipline, let us strive first of all to be conformable in habit and tonsure.
