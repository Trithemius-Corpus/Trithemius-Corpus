A good conscience does not fear to appear in the sight of the judge, nor does it dread the accusing judgment of synderesis. A good conscience has hope in God and, without the unrest of hesitation, the best confidence, because one who remembers having committed no evil comes confidently to the judge’s examination.

A good conscience is richly rewarded by the Lord God and is changed into everlasting joy. A good conscience is not one that commits no evil, but one that, preserving purity for the love of God from every sin, also adorns itself most fittingly with the practice of all good works. For a good conscience, according to the prophet’s admonition, turns completely away from evil and always and continually does good with the most zealous intention.

A good conscience especially adorns a monk and makes him utterly alien to worldly actions. A good conscience follows the Lord Jesus in simple purity and is stained by no spots of error. For a conscience that is good, and as long as it is good, cannot be erroneous, but always remains grounded in the truth of the Holy Spirit. But a conscience that has been erroneous is not good. For error is opposed to truth and has no share at all in goodness. When the conscience is in error, however, the danger is near, and the descent into hell very steep.

There are many among cloistered people who are weak and far too feeble in spirit, who are not always directed by the right judgment of their consciences, being preoccupied by various causes. And often, indeed, either a small or a great error of conscience arises from ignorance, namely whenever one who is less experienced in spiritual practice doubts whether the way he is considering in his mind is good or evil. For who, walking uprightly in his simplicity, understands everything? In doubtful matters the counsels of elders must be sought; and what they have decided, the monk should not doubt must be embraced as the judgment of truth.

Sometimes the conscience errs from negligence, when a monk neglects to examine himself by the scrutiny of reason and does not labor to search out the truth in doubtful matters. From this a pernicious error arises in the conscience, through which reason is gradually darkened. Pride also frequently makes the conscience erroneous and far removed from the knowledge of truth. For the proud man, although he understands that he does not know, nevertheless desires to seem wise and learned to everyone; for this reason he is ashamed to confess his ignorance.

Sometimes, however, error of conscience arises from singularity, to which presumption is always said to be joined. And this plague is frequently troublesome to monks and to those who profess holiness without the necessary foundation of humility. For the sheep that walks singular paths is more quickly seized and strangled by wolves.

Sometimes too an excessively disordered affection hurls the conscience into error, namely when a person loves his own will more than is expedient. For whoever is led by the affection of his own sensuality is frequently blinded in the judgment of truth.
