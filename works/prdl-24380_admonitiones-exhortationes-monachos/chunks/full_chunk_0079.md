He who fears the Lord keeps the commandments of the rule; and he who is a lover of discipline always walks with maturity. A fool, walking on the road, multiplies words and asks the names of all the mountains. A humble brother, speaking with God in the hidden place of his heart, is like one silent, and walks rejoicing in his simplicity. Whatever the constitution of the fathers has commanded, he considers most diligently; and wherever he may be, he carefully preserves all things.

The monk who, when sent on the road, has kept himself clean and pure from every stain of fault, will return joyful to the monastery, and, as though he had escaped the danger of the sea, hastens with longing to the harbor of rest. But he who has lost modesty has become insolent, and returns sad and sorrowful, as though going to prison.

The true monk of Christ does not seek or love the friendship of worldly people, but despises it; but he who multiplies familiarity with those unlike himself gradually falls away from the purpose of holiness. Cursed is the cloistered man who places his confidence in human beings, and whose heart has departed from the Lord his God. Do not seek the friendships of men, but take care to obtain the fellowship of the invisible angels. Frequent friendships with mortals do not suit a monk; through them both the vigor of the mind is dissolved and vain occupations are brought in. For just as dripping water over a long time hollows out a stone, however hard and solid it may be, so excessive familiarity with worldly people gradually softens monastic rigor. Have charity toward all, but familiarity with few. He who has God as a friend easily scorns familiarity with men; and he who speaks with angels takes little delight in the conversations of mortals.

Whenever on the road you see the sign of the Lord’s Passion, or of the crucified one, do not pass by unless you say: I give you thanks, Lord Jesus Christ, for the pain of your wounds, for the pouring out of your most holy blood, and for your most innocent death, which you endured for us on the altar of the cross; and I ask you, through your bitter Passion, have mercy on me now and in the fearful hour of my death. Repeat this very brief prayer often as you go, and never pass by an image of the crucified one unless you have given thanks to the Savior.

Do not quarrel with the one who walks with you on the road, lest on returning to the monastery he falsely say something evil against you. He who provokes his traveling companion to impatience is like one who drives a mad dog to bark by beating it. The wicked man is overcome by gentleness, and he who is humble of heart calms the one stirred up in spirit. In the midst of worldly people do not pour out speech, lest perhaps they take up mockery against you.
