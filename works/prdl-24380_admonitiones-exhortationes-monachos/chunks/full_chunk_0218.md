O happy necessity of monks, which took its beginning from the treasure of a good will and is ordered toward the end that is God, the unchangeable good. It is of greater praise, more excellent merit, and more abundant reward to do a good work out of obedience by reason of a vow than if it were done without a vow from the freedom of one’s own will.

The monk, moreover, once offered his whole self and all his will to God, and reserved absolutely nothing for himself. Whatever good he does under obedience is referred back to the end of the religious life he has undertaken, to be preserved for the love of Christ; and no work after martyrdom is comparable to this. One who is continent from sexual activity without a vow practices one virtue, which is called chastity; but one who observes it by reason of a vow walks on a higher path, since the individual virtues are ordered toward the perfection of the whole religious life that he has professed. For the whole monastic religious life is more than one private virtue.

For one who offers God a good work that he has done without obedience gives that alone, leaving to himself what is greater: the power of acting, and at the same time his own will. But the monk who does something for God from the vow of obedience has more excellent merit, since he offers God both the act and the power of acting. For no one has offered more to God than the monk who has given himself.

Some who climb this sixth step, however, have hidden within themselves a certain defect of pride, because, while they look incautiously to themselves, they do not entrust themselves in all things to the divine will as they ought. This defect takes its beginning from a certain very subtle vanity, when the soul, trusting in its own purity, asks that something secret be revealed to it by the Lord. And in this one respect the climbers of the sixth rung differ from those of the seventh: those who are bound to God flee the signs of vanity. For signs have been given not to believers, but to unbelievers; hence the monk who asks the Lord for a sign shows that he has not yet perfectly submitted himself to the divine will.

Those ascend the seventh step who most zealously keep all that has been prescribed, and live subject to God in all things without the stain of vanity. They flee every sin, both venial and mortal, as far as they can; they perform the works of virtue for the love of God alone; they obey their pastors in all things; they seek no miracles of vanity. Whatever almighty God has ordained concerning them, they receive gladly; they do not in any respect at all depart from the observance of monastic purity. Yet in one thing they are lower than those who follow, because perhaps they suffer some defect of which they are unaware, by the impulse of which they sometimes commit something of their own sensuality.

Whoever has deserved to ascend the eighth step grants nothing further to sensuality. Here is the sweetest rest of the soul; here is perfect tranquility of mind. Nothing flawed in beauty is left in this step; nothing is here that can sadden the soul.
