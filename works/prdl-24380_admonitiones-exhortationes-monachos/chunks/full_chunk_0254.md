Who among the absent and rebellious, in all these many years, has ever been compelled to pay the double penalty? What use are statutes to us, what use are laws, what help are the privileges of the supreme pontiffs, if in a time of necessity they are not put into practice? Behold, Pope Benedict XII provided for us fittingly in all things, when he promulgated that the restraints of censures should be imposed against the contumacious and rebellious. Why then do we delay? Why do we not put into practice against the disobedient the remedies granted to us?

Truly, fathers, this dissimulation of ours which we practice is not praiseworthy. For the rebellion of a few, if passed over in silence, will be an occasion for many to resist. But if a small number is compelled to obedience by discipline, the boldness of the multitude is restrained by fear. Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste, and every religious order at variance with itself is gradually scattered. It is no fair appearance of order where confusion troubles unity; for in every commonwealth the first road to desolation is the disobedience of subjects.

Therefore, venerable fathers, if you desire to continue the provincial chapter, if you wish to oppose the dismembering of the monks, if finally you wish to defend your order from the hands of enemies, take care first of all to recall the contumacious to obedience, lest what you acquire outwardly by diligence you lose inwardly through the division of your subjects. This indeed must first be attempted by the gentlest exhortations. But if they contemn these with hardened neck, they must be struck with harsher rebukes. If, however, they care neither for gentle words nor harsh ones, then ecclesiastical censure should be promulgated against them. For fools are not corrected by exhortations, but become proud.

In these matters, however, moderation must be observed, and necessary discretion, without which no rebellion is ever softened. For it is better to have weak sheep than none. But who is so fit that he can preserve discretion everywhere? It is difficult to apply moderation to all deeds. He who wishes to heal infirm souls must be both severe and compassionate, and tempered as though from a certain mean between the two, so that severity is not excessive nor compassion lax. For the one breaks, the other loosens. But the fairest manner of coercing the rebellious and contumacious is that which the long experience of elders commends.

On holding the provincial chapter. Chapter X.

When you come together as one, most excellent fathers, let us diligently investigate what the celebration of the provincial chapter requires. For the greatest strength of our stability is placed in it, if it is celebrated rightly. For it is the one fortress of our order, without which our whole religious life would be thrown into confusion.
