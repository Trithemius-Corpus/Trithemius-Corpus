Our foods are barley, oats, beans, peas, and vegetables, also milk, cheese, and eggs. From these we receive the daily supports of our life; and lest disgust at them arise, hunger stirs up appetite. Outside the season of the Lenten fast, fish are so rare for us that scarcely once in a month are a few, and very tiny ones, brought from the river Nabe. In the whole Bursfeld reform there is no monastery less provided with fish in time of need. But can we therefore not fall into the vice of gluttony, because we cannot find more sumptuous foods? Indeed, since the vice lies not in the food, as has already been said, but in the appetite, the eater of peas or beans can sin no less in eating than one who is accustomed to eat the most sumptuous and choice dishes. Poverty does not make us wholly secure from gluttony, although it lessens the occasions of sinning in more delicate fare.

We do indeed have simple and rustic food, by which nature is somehow sustained. This, my brothers, I beg us to take care over: that by excess our conscience not be polluted by that which produces nausea in the delicate. Let us take with patience and thanksgiving from what the Lord has given as much as necessity itself requires; and let us not think that the snares of the devil cannot harm us, since he overthrew the first human beings in Paradise not with richer food, but with a small and common apple. For the vice is not in the food, but in the appetite, when either we desire what is forbidden to us, or, greedily gaping after what is permitted, we use it otherwise than we ought.

Abstinence befits no one more than a monk, who, since he has been specially called to the warfare of Christ, ought not by his profession to be free for any pleasures whatever. Our poverty imposes on us a certain necessity of abstinence, because we can restrain ourselves to temperance all the more easily, the less we can find delights into which to flow away. Let us therefore, I beg, turn the hardships of want into weapons of salvation, so that the less we are refreshed with richer foods, the more purely we may fast in holiness of soul. For temperance is more easily preserved in poverty, while in riches it is more quickly endangered.

What the food of monks ought to be, blessed Jerome makes clear, saying in a certain letter to Paulinus: Let your food be cheap and taken in the evening, namely vegetables and legumes, and sometimes count it the highest delicacy to eat a few little fish. He who desires Christ and feeds on that heavenly bread does not greatly seek from what precious foods he may make dung. The lover of Christ loves abstinence, honors fasting as the one remedy of soul and body, and with the greatest care guards against his heart being weighed down with excess; nor does he take more food than nature, content with little, demands.

But the monk who casts aside fasting, who hates abstinence, the nurse of virtues, who gulps down food greedily, who drinks wine or strong drink, and whatever can intoxicate, to satiety, is unworthy of the very name of monk, and cannot be Christ’s [unclear].
