Homily VI.

…will depart to be tormented. For since our mind, or soul, was created in ignorance and privation of all things, it must necessarily come to knowledge of things by continual action, movement, and art. The end, indeed, makes the human intellect equal to the angelic, changing its potentiality into act; and it will make from privation a habit, from emptiness fullness, from the imperfect the perfect, from one deprived one possessing, from darkness light, from night day, from lunar solar, and from earthly heavenly.

This end, my dearly beloved brothers, is the beginning of eternal salvation; and no one can happily come to it who does not strive with all his strength toward the summit of perfection. But since it is difficult amid crowds always to keep unbroken this necessary effort of advancing, where a thousand occasions for sinning are offered, the holy fathers, founders of the monastic order, taught us by word and example to flee the world, and to avoid crowds not only in mind but also in body.

Of those who were stronger in spirit, many withdrew to the desert, so that they might become nearer to the Lord in mind the farther they had withdrawn in body from the world. Of these we read the saying of Isaiah the prophet: “The wilderness shall rejoice and shall blossom like the lily; budding, it shall bud, and shall rejoice with gladness and praise.”

Others, however, desiring to benefit many, established cenobitic houses, in which they placed a multitude of monks, whose way of life was separated from the peoples and nevertheless made social among themselves. For it is not suitable for all to sit in the desert, for whom it is more fitting to serve the Lord in the midst of brothers. For our monasteries too have their own solitude, so that anyone who wishes to be solitary can be so in the midst of brothers, if he keeps up the seclusion of the cell in which he dwells.

For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is called solitary, because in mind and body he is separated from the tumult of this world. Therefore, as I am about to deliver today’s sermon to your charity, I warn you with fatherly concern, and I beg that I may not have spoken so much in vain, since I seek nothing other than your good and desire nothing more than your progress in spiritual conduct. I ask you, I say, and exhort you in the bowels of brotherly charity, to love the quiet of the cell as the workshop of your salvation, in which there is great peace for those who love God, and great advantage for bringing the intellect to perfection in the knowledge of the highest truth.

For the prophet Isaiah says: “In silence and in hope shall be your strength.” Whoever does not love the solitude of the cell and the quiet of silence by no means loves purity of mind; rather, wandering in body, he always drinks in new vanities in spirit. Hence blessed Jerome says: “Why do we desire the crowds of cities, we who are reckoned from our singularity? For to me a town is a prison, solitude a paradise.”

If, brothers, the desert and solitude delighted us, this place would offer great convenience for our purpose, where, apart from poor country people, a guest is rarely met; no one disturbs our quiet, no one troubles us, no one introduces spectacles or rumors.
