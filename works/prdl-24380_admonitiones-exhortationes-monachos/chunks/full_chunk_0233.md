The Pentichus of Johannes Trithemius

...namely, necessity, fittingness, usefulness, and honorable propriety. But you, perjured monk, who are led by will alone, who despise God and the order, feign necessity, display false fittingness, lie about usefulness, and fashion honorable propriety by fraud. Let necessity be evident and manifest; fittingness true; usefulness not private but public; honorable propriety not fictitious but genuine. Otherwise the dispensation will be unjust.

I bring forward nothing for which I should be angry with the Supreme Pontiff in this dispensation; I blame nothing in his actions, since I know well that he was deceived by the false accounts of monks. You are the guilty ones, you the deceitful ones, you who must be marked with iniquity, O perverse monks, wicked apostates, who by lying, deceit, and fraud seek what you despair of obtaining by just prayers.

What necessity presses you to abandon the order and pass into the state of secular life? What fittingness urges you to break what, once promised to God, no one may revoke? What usefulness is there in putting off the monastic habit, abandoning the order, denying your profession, transferring from the monastery, and not merely breaking but utterly casting away the vow made to God before all the saints? Or what honorable propriety is there in a monk dedicated to God returning to the world, breaking faith, committing perjury, perpetrating sacrilege? O transgressors and impious men, how will you ever be able to excuse so unspeakable a presumption?

None of these four accords with your transfer; none of them confirms your dispensation. For what necessity could have driven you to this rashness? What cause for this dismemberment could you have alleged before the Vicar of Christ? The poverty and ruin of the monasteries? But you could quickly have restored this if you lived according to the Rule of Saint Benedict. For you became poor through wicked conduct; by holy living you would again be enriched. Look around you at monasteries brought back to regular observance, how, after extreme poverty, by the Lord’s bounty, they again advance toward their former wealth; and indeed quite justly. For the Lord first promised us in the Gospel, saying, “Seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things will be added to you.” But you put the love of God after other things, neglect his worship, devote yourselves to games, feasts, drunkenness, and gluttony, and do not regard the work of the Lord; you care nothing for regular observance. Therefore, because spiritual things were neglected, by the just judgment of God even temporal things failed you. Thus the necessity you pretend is false, and the dispensation you allege is insufficient.

But perhaps you say: We are not sons of peasants, but of nobles; we are noble, delicate, and weak, and cannot keep the observance of cobblers, farmers, and shepherds. Fasts, abstinence, vigils, disciplines, and the other exercises they have are unbearable to us; we cannot imitate them, nor should we.

What then? Are you not a monk because you are noble, because delicate, because tender, because weak? There is nothing by which you can be defended against Saint Benedict, who in his Rule provides abundantly for every condition, every rank, every age; who provides for the delicate and the weak with the greatest discretion. Are you alone among the noble, tender, and delicate who have professed the monastic order? Many very noble young men, sons of emperors, kings, dukes, and princes, serve in our order in holiness and learning, under regular observance.
