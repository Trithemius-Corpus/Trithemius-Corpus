For those who do not fear God will by no means acquiesce in our exhortations. Come now, most reverend Fathers, you who have assembled in chapter for the honor of our order, you who labor for its restoration: recall to mind its former state, and consider into how great a calamity it has fallen in this time. Behold, monasteries are being snatched from the hands of Saint Benedict; our order is being destroyed all around by the enemies of God; monks are returning to the world; the impious tear apart our religious life; strangers are endowed with our goods. What shall I say of internal wars? What shall I report of civil evils? It is no wonder, Fathers, take heed, if we are put to shame from without, since we are torn apart by our own people within.

Our abbots kick against discipline, tear apart the unity of the order, neglect the statutes of chapters. They spurn visitations; they despise your censures, O Fathers. Unless you strive very quickly to bring them back to regular observance, you will not be able to keep them long under the order. Why then do you dissemble? How long will you sleep? When will you awaken? When will you fight for the inheritance of Saint Benedict? O Fathers, Fathers, arm yourselves with zeal for God, for the order, and for discipline, because it is time. Fight for your lawful rights. Defend the rights of holiness; stand manfully against transfers of the order.

Behold, already in our own times, in the province of Mainz alone, six monasteries have been transferred from our order; and unless we watch more carefully, we shall suffer graver losses day by day. Thus, while we dissemble, not to say grow sluggish, the order is gradually diminished, until at last it is entirely scattered. Why do we not meet such great dangers? Or, while the others perish, do we wisely trust in our own security? Did not the poet truly say: “Your own affair is at stake when the neighboring wall is burning”? Yesterday the danger was theirs; tomorrow a like danger will hang over us.

Each one of you, I do not doubt, loves his own monastery, and would by no means consent that it be transferred from the order, so that he desires not only its present happiness but also its future happiness to be prolonged for eternal years. Why then do you not defend in common what each of you fears to lose individually? I fear, Fathers, that in this dissembling our very wicked tenacity is being exposed. We love, and yet we lose, a monastery; we fear a few expenses, and lose spacious dwellings. We are unwilling to lay out a small sum of money, and we allow most abundant monasteries to pass from the order to outsiders. We put private advantages before the common good, and we do not care to preserve the honor of the order. We say many things, we determine many things for the useful defense of the order; but all of us are afraid to spend coins. O feeble zeal, which stinginess renders useless. If we do not give money for our brothers, when shall we lay down our life? If we do not contribute a few expenses for the preservation of the order, when shall we give our bodies? He who is grasping in a small matter: when will he be generous in a great one?

Now I shall speak to you, Fathers, who dissemble while the order has been placed amid such great dangers and needs aid. Blessed Benedict made you rich from poverty.
