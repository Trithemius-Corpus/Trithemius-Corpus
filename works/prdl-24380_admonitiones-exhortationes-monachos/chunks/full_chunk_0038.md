Therefore the reading of the divine Scriptures should have its own suitable hour for study; and when it senses that the desire for devotion is present, it should at once be changed into fervent prayer. Manual labor, too, which is appointed for a monk dwelling in the quiet of solitude, is very useful, lest harmful tepidity arise in the mind. For the reading of the holy Scriptures stirs the monk to the love of God; meditation on the Lord’s passion and on the benefits of God inflames the mind to the fervor of most ardent love; prayer strengthens the weak soul; and manual labor, continued temperately, preserves it from sluggishness and weariness.

When we pray earnestly and devoutly, we speak with our God, who is present everywhere; but when we read in the holy Scriptures, we are taught by the Lord himself what we should seek or avoid. Prayer, if it is ardent in the love of God, purifies the mind from sins and vices; reading instructs and forms it, as holy Isidore says: “By prayers,” he says, “we are cleansed; by readings we are instructed. Both are good, if both are possible; if both are not possible, it is better to pray than to read.”

Therefore the monk who desires to spend his time in the cell usefully and fruitfully should strive continually to occupy mind and body by turns in these exercises, because a spirit that keeps watch over such labors gives no entrance to perverse thoughts. Hence blessed Jerome, admonishing the monk, says: “Always do something good, so that the devil may find you occupied.” For he is less assailed by the enticements of the flesh who, always usefully occupied, admits no weariness of mind.

Indeed, when every monk, as a careful guardian of his soul, is carried by turn from the practice of one good work to another with the desire of holy fervor, he is strengthened in the constancy of all virtues; and he falls into weariness the less, the more he has been accustomed to vary his occupation and to use alternating tasks against weariness. Hence Jerome again says: “Let prayer frequently interrupt reading, and let a pleasing alternation of holy activity continually kindle the soul that clings to God.”

The monk who does not love the secrecy of the cell is not a true soldier of Christ, because he bears the name and habit of a monk without the works; and he who ought to have been solitary for love of God has turned back in mind to the world and loves the pursuit of vanity. Hence blessed Pope Gregory says: “He who lives bodily removed from men, but in thought plunges himself, through desire for earthly things, into the tumults of human intercourse, is not solitary, although he dwells in solitude. But if someone is physically pressed by the crowds of the people, and yet suffers no tumults of secular cares in his heart, he is not in the city, even if he lives in the midst of men.”
