Homily XV.

He has by no means tasted the zeal for contemplation. I have introduced these things, brothers, so that you may understand that nothing is so difficult that habit, continued over a long time, does not make it easy. This holy man, as he himself confesses, did not suddenly arrive at the height of this abstinence, but gradually over time, by degrees, little by little subtracting a little from his food, until at last he was able to abstain altogether. For, as we have said, he had abstained from every perishable food for almost twenty years.

By the example of this man, my brothers, let each of you learn daily to subtract something from his food and drink for the love of God, and accustom himself to give his body not as much as it can take, but as much as is sufficient to sustain nature. For nature, as Boethius testifies, is content with few and very small things. Many dishes produce many illnesses, and gluttony of the belly dulls the mind and torments life. For the wise man says: “The sleep of the laboring man is sweet, whether he eats little or much; but the fullness of the rich man does not allow him to sleep.”

The belly should be filled without satiety, and one should rise from table without disgust, so that we eat not as much as we can, but as much as, at nature’s demand, we ought. Monks especially must avoid variety of foods; for whoever uses varied dishes, trying to put into his stomach more than nature requires, feasts not without the vice of gluttony. The strength of our body must be restored by moderate and simple food, not shamefully oppressed by varied and many foods, because the body was not created for food, but food for the body. That variety of foods brings diseases upon the body we prove from the temperance of the ancients, whom a uniform simplicity of foods preserved for a very long time in this life.

Our most holy founder, Benedict, the most modest sacred teacher of all the leaders of monks, prescribed for us a rule adorned with the highest discretion; in it, wishing to show monks the measure of foods, he says: “We believe that for the daily meal, whether at the sixth or at the ninth hour, two cooked dishes are sufficient for all tables, on account of the different infirmities, so that anyone who perhaps cannot eat from one may be refreshed from the other. Therefore two cooked dishes should suffice for all the brothers. And if there are fruits there, or fresh vegetables, let a third be added.”

If I understand our lawgiver’s mind soundly, he allowed monks two cooked dishes where the necessity of infirmity requires it, not for the filling of the appetite of the healthy. For a monk who is healthy is rightly excepted from this indulgence, so that he is refreshed by only one cooked dish. But in my judgment the sick man is rightly permitted to be refreshed from both, since no excess is to be feared in him.
