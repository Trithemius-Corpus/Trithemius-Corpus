Whoever among you have come to the monastery to run the course of intellectual combat, whoever for the love of Christ have once renounced your own will and the world, take up holy obedience as the helmet of salvation, and you will bring back a sure victory over the enemy.

For obedience is the strongest armor of monks, against which no adversities will be able to do harm. For Scripture says: “He who keeps the commandment will experience no evil.” Likewise in Proverbs it is said thus: “An obedient man will speak of victories.” Obedience makes a monk invincible in all temptations; and he who follows in all things the counsel of his superior will never suffer loss to the salvation of his soul. For obedience is a short and rough road, by which one quickly ascends to the summit of perfection.

But the monk who is a true soldier of Christ never trusts his own judgment, but in all things follows and observes the direction of his shepherd. Indeed, he who is a true lover of Christ, who wholly desires to be a faithful monk, loves his abbot as the vicar of God, and in all things always cheerfully submits, as one obedient, to his commands, counsels, and ordinances.

But so that a monk may be able to do this with confidence, no boy, no unlearned man, no carnal man, no one lacking discretion, nor one light-minded or inexperienced in spiritual combats, should be appointed abbot, but a man gray-haired in character, learned in the divine Scriptures, walking in the spirit in the true conduct of holiness, devout, gentle, tranquil in mind, and discreet, who knows how to show both judgment to the wicked and mercy to the well-disposed. For whenever an unquiet, turbulent, indiscreet, and inexperienced man is set over monks as abbot, at once the integrity of regular discipline suffers shipwreck.

Subjection and humble obedience must perish together when subjects cannot have firm confidence in their shepherd. For if the abbot is a mocker of his brothers, or a harsh despiser of the weak, who will open to him any secret of his heart? Therefore at the beginning the greatest care must be kept, that such a prelate be appointed as shepherd over destitute sheep, one who seeks nothing other than the honor of God and the salvation of souls.

The monk who has obtained such a shepherd should give thanks to God, since in these times of ours one is rarely found who knows how to show himself in all things a worthy and suitable prelate. He is deservedly to be loved by all his subjects, and honored in the monastery with continual reverence as the vicar of Christ. Let the thoughts of all be opened to him securely, since he seeks nothing other than the progress of each one. If the physician of sick bodies must be prudent, experienced, and endowed with the highest discretion, how much more fitting is it for the abbot, who is physician of souls, to be an excellent man?
