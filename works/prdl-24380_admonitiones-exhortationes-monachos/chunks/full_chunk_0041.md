He placed four in this monastery, over whom he set as abbot John, from the noble family of Budisbach, commonly called “of the house of Carbonaria”: a man such as you have seen and heard, worthy of eternal memory. He found this place in extreme poverty and, presiding over it for almost fourteen years with great patience, restored it to the condition in which we now live.

Therefore, since lack of temporal goods still presses heavily upon us, there would be continual need of labor by which we might feed ourselves with necessary sustenance and free the house of God in which we dwell from debt. But what are your labors, or how much can they help us in such great matters? We are few in number and unfit for rustic labors; nor does the institution of the priestly order permit us to spend the whole day in the field turning stones with our hands and to neglect the divine service.

We are wretched, crushed by excessive poverty and by lack of all necessities. If we labor much, we gain little from it; and if we have worked little, we shall certainly fail still more in spirit. For man, as Scripture bears witness, is born to labor, just as a bird is born to fly. Therefore, lest you eat your bread in idleness, you must always be bound to some honest and useful labor, by which both the mind may be relieved from weariness in regular observance and our heavy poverty may be repaired, at least in some small measure.

In harvest time we have been accustomed to employ you outside the monastery only for haymaking, and in the days of autumn for the vintage, when, that is, the labor is both very necessary and the scarcity of hired men increases the price and wages of servants. At other times we have determined to keep you within the monastery, because your labors, in comparison with the monastery’s expenses, contribute little to its advantage, and, as it seems to us, very few of you willingly come forward of your own accord for rustic pursuits.

There is also another consideration that stirs my mind not to occupy you readily in external works of the hands without reasonable cause and evident necessity. For I find by experience that labors of this kind do not agree in you with regular observance, but rather destroy than promote the integrity of monastic discipline among you. I am ashamed to say it, and nevertheless I am compelled to utter it: your labors outside the monastery and the cell bring more destruction to the purity of souls than salvation.

For what do you labor at, and what are your pursuits in your labors? Are they not to wander about, run here and there, indulge in feasting and cups, and spend the rest of the time in vain and useless conversations? You do not have within yourselves that holy fervor of the Spirit of God with which we read that the monks of our ancient institution were once inflamed; for the common necessity they all and individually labored with their hands with such desire, such zeal, and such effort that you would have thought the whole business of the monastery rested in the foresight of each one.
