Since, however, all men naturally desire the good, it is most shameful to love sin, because every sinner abandons the good and turns himself to evil.

Sixth, it strongly draws the mind back from sin if it remains always occupied, by habit, in meditation on the Lord’s Passion. For who will be able to devote himself to the pleasures of the flesh, if he has thought of Jesus Christ, the ruler of the world, hanging for him on the cross? The death of Christ freed the world from perpetual death; whoever carries this benefit with him in memory will easily abstain from every crime of vice.

Finally, if anyone considers with the whole attention of his mind that God is present to him in every place, and knows and understands all things, he will without doubt always abhor every sin. For God is everywhere with us: not only present, but truly inward and most present. No secret is hidden from him; nothing remains concealed from him, but everything lies open and manifest to him. He searches the hearts of all; he knows the most secret thoughts, he who alone has true knowledge of all things. For what mortal dares to sin in the presence of such majesty, which he believes sees all things, without the greatest shame, if he considers who and how great he is who sees?

Therefore, whenever an evil thought troubles the mind, whenever the disordered movements of the flesh disturb the soul, think of the Lord God, the avenger of evils, as present; recall to memory the bitterness of the Passion of Jesus Christ; devoutly implore the help of heavenly grace. Harder than stone is the man whom these things, if carefully considered, cannot call back from the intention of sinning.

Seventh, what recalls the mind of a studious monk from the desire of sinning is that by continual reading he should go through the accounts of the martyrs’ sufferings. Indeed, whoever often recalls to memory the invincible constancy of the holy martyrs of Christ cannot fail to become stronger and more robust in resisting sins. For this reason the brave deeds of the saints were committed to writing: so that, whenever we who are weak read such things, we may rise up to imitate those who have gone before.

Do not pay regard, brothers, to those who, with proud rashness, deny faith to the histories of the saints, and think that the glorious things narrated about the most blessed friends of Christ are fables. For the virtues and good things which they do not find in themselves, they disdain to believe that the saints of God possessed. For there are many, and especially among those who think themselves great because of their learning, who too rashly reject the histories of the holy fathers; and since they themselves cannot enter upon the arduous path of virtue, with windy loquacity they invent that no one, or only a rare few, could have done so. Whatever is read before them from the lives of the saints, whether arduous or great, they cry out that it is invented and fabulous; and, being placed as they themselves are in the darkness of vices, they suppose that those whose histories they mock lived in the same way before them.
