*The Pentichus Book of Johannes Trithemius.*

You were so solicitous for your religion; why do you abandon us at the end? Why do you not look to your inheritance? Why do you not now, even now, come to the aid of your order as it totters? Have you cast off care for it? Or has your soul abhorred your sons? Come to our aid, help us, bring assistance, because your religion has never been surrounded by such grievous evils. Behold, it is being destroyed from without by lovers of the world; behold, it is being torn apart within by perverse monks, so that no one can easily discern which is the greater crime: for monasteries to be transferred into the hands of secular clerics, or to be kept longer under the hands of such criminal monks.

Alas, holy Father, what shall I say? You, once instructing abbots, commanded them to be solicitous concerning weak monks. Now shepherds have no care for the sheep, no diligence for the salvation of souls: all seek the things that are their own, not the things of Jesus Christ. Behold, your sons, Father, having zeal, desire to come to the rescue, but they accomplish nothing, hindered by the rashness of the wicked. Who, amid these things, will truly come to our aid? The bishop? But he cares for his own affairs, seeks his own, despises ours. Princes? But they sell favor, reject discipline, seek temporal things. There is no one who grieves over the crushing of your order; no one who comes to its aid; no one who provides help. All who can restore it neglect it; others, who seem to have zeal for discipline, try to help, but cannot.

O abbots, abbots, you who are the occasion of all evils in your monasteries. You who, given over to vanities, do not at once correct monks who do wrong: what a harsh sentence of condemnation you will receive. To you, believe me, to you will be imputed the damnation of your monks. You, placed as their rulers, have neglected to instruct their morals. Woe to you. It would be better if you had never been abbots. You, devoting yourselves to avarice and lust, neglect the work of the Lord, despise the care of souls, think nothing of the path of observance. You who ought to correct the vices of monks commit vices, and, lest your own be reproved, you conceal those of others.

You see, most adorned Fathers, what the condition of your order is, what the abbots are, what the monks are, since those who [unclear] the wicked are lacking; among them only those who [unclear] occur. Why, then, do you delay? Why do you not think about the reformation of such men? Why do you not restrain the wicked pursuits of the monks? Their ill repute confounds us; their examples scandalize us, because the greater they are in number, the better in conduct they are believed to be by those who do not know.

Rise up, Fathers, because the days are both short and evil; reform, according to your powers, the deformed order; raise up what has been cast down; restore what has collapsed. For unless you apply a remedy quickly, you will shortly suffer grave loss. For dissolute monks, enemies of regular discipline, always aspire to worse things, and, lest they be saved in the order, they strive to abandon it. You have the aids of law for beginning reformation; you have the Rule; you have statutes. This alone is required of you: that you put them into practice. Therefore, if you love your order, indeed because you love it: do not neglect, do not delay,
