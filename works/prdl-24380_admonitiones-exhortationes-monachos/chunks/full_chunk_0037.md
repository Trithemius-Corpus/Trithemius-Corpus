But I see few among you who love inner quiet, and I grieve deeply, because I know very well that a monk cannot make progress in spiritual conduct if it is clear that he does not love the quiet of the cell. But he who knows how much good the secrecy of solitude brings to the soul loves his cell and does not readily go outside, always fearing that in the turmoil he may lose what he has gathered with great labor in hiddenness. For, as Saint Augustine says, he is less stirred by pleasures who is not where there is a crowd of pleasures; and he suffers less trouble from avarice who, placed far from men, does not see riches.

Since, therefore, the holy Spirit of God loves the secrecy of the mind and shudders at public exposure, it belongs to you, brothers, to love the solitude of your cells, the workshop of all virtues, and never to estrange yourselves from its dwelling without a reasonable cause. For the monk who loves secrecy and is not idle, who has learned to stand firm in his cell, sometimes enjoys many holy consolations, of which the wandering and restless man is necessarily deprived. Just as a fish dies if it is kept too long out of the water, so every monk perishes in devotion if he wanders too often outside his cell.

Let him imitate John the Baptist, whom Chrysostom calls the prince of monks: as a monk, as soon as he was born, he lived in the desert, was nourished in the desert, and as a monk awaited Christ in solitude. For just as it is difficult for a tree planted beside the road to preserve its fruits until maturity, so it is difficult for a monk who often wanders outside his cell and is occupied in the affairs of the world to keep the fruits of righteousness unstained to the end.

Let us therefore withdraw from the road of worldly tumult and plant ourselves in the secrecy of monastic solitude, so that neither the world may have anything in common with us, nor we with it. For a mind shaken by the cares and anxieties of the world is rarely truly rooted in the love of God. The movement of the body often alters the state of the mind, and a man who is always immersed in external things is never illumined by the fervor of the divine Spirit. For quiet of mind is born from the firm steadiness of the body, and the man who loves solitude for the love of God stands stronger and more united to himself in holy exercise.

Therefore whoever among you, brothers, is a zealous lover of his own salvation should learn to remain quietly in his cell and, as much as he can, should strive to occupy himself continually and with desire in the works of divine love. But be quiet in your cell, not idle; tranquil, not sleepy; always devoted to spiritual exercise, not slackened by torpor of soul. The instruments of spiritual exercises in the cell are four: to read, to write, to pray, and to meditate. A fifth is granted to few, those who, after true purity of heart, are raised to the mountain of contemplation of the Lord.
