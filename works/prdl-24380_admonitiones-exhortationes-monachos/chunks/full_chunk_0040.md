Homily VII.

He inflicted punishment for the transgression of the original commandment, declaring to him that by the sweat of his face he would have to work his food from the earth with his hands. And since we all sinned in Adam, we are necessarily subject to the same penalties to which he is known to have been subjected. He labored with his hands for the food by which he might live, nor did he eat his bread in idleness.

Hence our holy father Benedict, detesting the idleness of monks in the Rule which he handed down to us, says: “Idleness is the enemy of the soul, and therefore at fixed times the monks ought to be occupied in manual labor, and again at fixed hours in divine reading. For then they are truly monks, if they live by the labor of their own hands, as our fathers and the apostles also did.” Hence Saint Paul also says in the epistle: “He who does not work, let him not eat.”

In the beginning of the monastic state this was the custom of monks: that they admitted no one into their fellowship without labor, not so much because of the necessities of food as because of the salvation of the soul, lest through idleness occasion be given to destructive thoughts. At that time monks had no rents, no tithes, no revenues, no annual proceeds of any kind; but all, living in common, procured food and clothing for themselves with their hands, burdening no one by the exaction of debt.

The holy apostles of our Savior also, although they had the power to live from the preaching of the Gospel, preferred to labor with their own hands, lest they should seem to have burdened any of the faithful, and so that they might have not only what they themselves could live on, but also something from which they could supply necessities to the needy.

But after monks began to have rents and revenues, the former zeal for simplicity soon failed, and pride gradually grew along with the wealth of temporal things, to such a degree that in time monks refused all manual labors. As long as monks were poor and humble in birth, they worshiped God in truth; but afterwards, when they began to abound in riches, duplicity drove out simplicity, and the arrogance of nobles uprooted the humility of monks. For the integrity of regular observance had produced an abundance of temporal goods; but little by little the riches of the monks utterly extinguished the purity of the order. And when the integrity of regular observance had been taken away, soon in many monasteries the riches of temporal goods also failed by the just judgment of God.

Among these was the present monastery of Spanheim, neither the last, my brothers, nor the first. For many years it remained miserably desolate in both respects, to such an extent that the only five monks living there, and living a dissolute life, did not have the means from which to obtain the necessities of life.
