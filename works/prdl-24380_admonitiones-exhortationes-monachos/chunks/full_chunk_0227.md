I fear, however, that, living now without order, you will receive a place among those where no order, but everlasting horror, dwells. For what other dwelling a man is to receive who, from a bad monk, has become a worse canon, I do not sufficiently understand.

O senseless men, enemies to yourselves, who will teach you to flee from the wrath to come? Who will be able, or will dare, to defend your wicked cause against Saint Benedict in that dreadful examination? Whom will your rashness have as advocate? Saint Benedict, prince of monks, will stand as witness against you; he will accuse you and condemn you. Who will excuse you? Do not flatter yourself, wicked despiser of your regular vow; do not soothe your conscience. If you have Benedict as your adversary, you will find none of the saints your friend. For he who offends one citizen of the heavenly country offends them all. For where there is supreme and blessed tranquility, there too there will be an indivisible unity of minds. Who, then, will be able to deliver you from the hand of the Lord, if all the saints stand against you?

Call upon those who now deceive you, who take from you the habit of the order, who lead you away with flattering speeches, who exist as accomplices of your apostasy, so that they may defend you from the tribulation that is at hand. But if the just man will scarcely be saved, where will the apostate and the destroyer of a holy order appear? On that day the justice of a saint will not deliver the impious man; nor will one wretch bring help to another. Rather, those whom you now win over to yourself with gifts and presents as collaborators in so great a presumption, you will then have as witnesses and companions of your unhappiness. There will be no defender of a depraved action there, no advocate of iniquity; for there all things will be bare. There, each person’s accusing conscience will be enough for his damnation. There the army of saints will stand against those who have wickedly profaned the dwellings of monks, who have impiously destroyed the order of the divine fathers.

But the authors of these things, which I seem to myself to have called wicked unjustly, try to excuse their deed in this way, saying: The improper way of life of monks, driving monasteries into extreme poverty, cannot be corrected and amended unless it is transferred into another state, on account of many reasons which they invent for themselves; reasons which, God granting, we shall also show in what follows to be empty. Finally they say that to correct bad monks through transfer not only is free from sin, but is also a great part of justice and piety.

But where can these things be discerned? How will you teach that what you say is true? To convert bad monks to goodness is praiseworthy and holy; but to make them canons is not to reform monks, but to corrupt them. A bad monk will never be a good canon; but a good monk will not cast off his order, but will cherish, cultivate, and love it. I do not now wish to contend with words; I do not wish to be excessive or vehement. I do not wish to depict the effect of this transfer, which I do not doubt will gradually come to light.
