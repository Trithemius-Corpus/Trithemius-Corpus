The Pentichus Book of Johannes Trithemius.

...with the things promised, to separate the good from the wicked, the truthful from the false: what place will you receive, what success will you have, you who deny the holy purpose of religion, who break your vow, who despise your order, who violate your faith?

Would that you weighed the brevity of this life. Would that you feared the examination of future recompense, so that, pierced by saving teachings, you would heed through repentance the voice of the Lord, who says: “I do not desire the death of the one who is dying, but that he return and live”; and through the prophet: “Return to the Lord your God, for he is merciful and gracious, patient and of great compassion,” one who revokes his sentence if you wish to wash away your guilt with tears. But you do not fear the judgment of God; you despise his clemency; you scorn the patience of his long-suffering.

Therefore the Apostle says to each one of you: “Do you not know that the patience of God leads you to repentance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart you store up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each according to his works.” He said that the judgment of God is just because it is late, because it is long and greatly deferred, so that God’s long patience may provide for your life. But how do you do penance, when you do not acknowledge your guilt? You will grieve, but too late, when there will no longer be a place for repentance, but the repayment of vengeance.

Then, therefore, punishment will be rendered to the impious man and the sinner, when repentance can no longer profit the sinner. O how guilty, how wretched, how unhappy they remain, who, for the very brief pleasure of this present time, deserters of God and of their own order and rebels against God, have done the will of the devil, so that they must be tormented with him in unquenchable fire. Would that these things clung to their hearts. Would that they thought on these things, who desert their order, who break their vow, who so boldly lie both to God and to his saints. But we strive in vain: they never receive our words, who despise the words of God, of the Church, and of Saint Benedict.

Therefore this cause of the order must be carried on not with words but with deeds. Let us therefore arm ourselves, Fathers, with all our strength, and let us prepare for the struggle with an incorrupt mind, devoted virtue, and firm conscience, against those who desire to destroy our order, who strip off the monk, who take away monasteries, who cast aside the Rule, who exist as rebels against their own visitors. Let us arm ourselves, I beseech you, Fathers, with zeal for regular discipline and for the honor of the order, sparing no labors, no expenses, lest this pernicious plague, fostered by our silence, gradually infect the whole order.

Never has such necessity weighed upon our order; never has it been entangled in so many evils. Behold, monks put off the habit; they transform monasteries into dwellings of clerics; and while we attend to private advantages, while we flee labors, while we spare expenses, while we consult our own greed, the order perishes, religion passes away, the worship of God vanishes. Many monasteries have already, through the malice of the times, not to say through our own sloth, been transferred from the order; and unless we wake up in another way, greater losses will come upon us.
