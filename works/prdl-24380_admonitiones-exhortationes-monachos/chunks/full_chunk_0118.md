Among all professors of the Christian faith today, there is no class of men to whom the virtue of patience seems more necessary than to the company of monks. For, as the charity of many grows cold in these our days, to use Paul’s expression, we have become a spectacle to mortals, so that wherever a monk comes among worldly people, he is marked out to be mocked and derided by the fingers of almost all who see him.

You know why I have said this; you are not unaware of what recently happened in the town of Bingen. A drunken crowd of youths and boys accosted a certain monk passing along the public road, and, as though he, by traveling, had caused the rain, threw him into the mud; and by treating the man outrageously, it made him a pitiable spectacle of its own shamelessness. No one was there to restrain the insolent men. Yet the monk’s patience overcame their frenzy, and, full of the love of Christ, when he was being badly handled, he answered nothing else but “Thanks be to God.”

Therefore, the more he showed himself tranquil in mind and spirit, the more those worthless men dragged him into the fountain, thinking that he was heated with wine, when the love of Christ had made him glad and joyful. He left to all monks a great lesson in patience: although he could have avenged himself when wronged, he would not.

Would that we, my brothers, were as patient for the love of God and for our sins in the smallest things as that good man was in the greatest. There are some among you, I say it unwillingly, too insolent and impatient, who are moved to anger by a word, even one that is not insulting; and while they are justly corrected for their negligences, they murmur unjustly, adding sin to sin through impatience.
