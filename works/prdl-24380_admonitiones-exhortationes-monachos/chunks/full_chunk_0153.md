For it is not sincere charity toward one’s neighbors to seek gifts of corruption, or, in the manner of boys, to love with an affection of likeness someone who comes running because of distributions of little dainties. The friendship of monks, among good men who are zealous for the same purpose, ought to be common, and far removed from every carnal attachment. Let it be pure, simple, upright, lasting without injury to anyone, and let it have in itself nothing of suspicious conspiracy. This is not the rule of brotherly charity: to seek private forbidden meetings for consolation and, without the prior’s blessing, to spend time in furtive, indeed prohibited, conversations. You bestow your love upon your neighbor not rightly, but rather ruinously, whenever you come together, contrary to the rule of regular observance, for the comforts of the flesh. The monk’s love ought to be solid and most remote from every transgression of regular discipline, since unless charity is mute with regard to carnal conversations, it does not have in itself the fire of divine love.

Now examine your conversations, brothers, and see whether your hearts are also radiant with true charity. For charity that either gives to a neighbor, or receives from him, consolations harmful to the spirit was never sincere. Therefore take care to direct the eye of the mind not toward the beauty of the flesh, but rather toward contemplation of the soul, if you wish to love your neighbor rightly and praiseworthily. For when the flesh is loved without regard for the soul, a love is generated that is not sincere, but carnal and impure. Consider that your brother has a soul like your own, and always remember that you both await an equal happiness. By mind we mean soul; by soul, not by body, we are immortal. What is immortal is what each is commanded to love in the other, and to order the regard of charity toward the soul rather than toward the body. For just as we are distinguished from brute animals by reason and understanding, so the gaze of our love should be referred to the mind by which we understand, not to the body, which we have in common with beasts.

Let all carnal attachments be far from holy monks, who are not ignorant of the mutual and sincere rule of charity. Let love for one another be pure, upright, and conceived from fear of God alone, so that no advantage of any corruptible thing may intervene, but the simple intention of charity may remain. But simple and pure charity does not seek the byways of evil conspiracy, nor does it arm itself with the murmur of contention for the contrivances of iniquity. Whoever possesses true brotherly charity in his heart preserves peace in all things, and stirs up no matter or cause of ruinous dissension. Charity, giving each one his due, does injury to no one, and whatever it does not wish done to itself, it never transfers onto the person of another. Do not think that you have true brotherly charity when you love one another with regard to the flesh, or for the advantage of any temporal thing whatever, since where the cause of love is not the Lord, the affection of the one who loves is always faulty either in part or in whole.
