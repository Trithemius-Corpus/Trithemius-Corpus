*The Pentichus Book of Johannes Trithemius*

*How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our Province would be. Chapter XI.*

How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our Province is, you yourselves, O Fathers, know, for you are not ignorant of the condition of the monks of this time. I know it is known to you with what labors, expenses, and sweats the Fathers once reformed our order; concerning these things we find records in our age both few and weak. Among all the reforms of our order in our province, however, three have lately shone forth above the rest, namely the Castellan, from Castellum; the Melk, from Melk; and the Bursfeld, from Bursfeld, having taken their names from them. All these, just as they were fervent in the beginning, so, gradually growing lukewarm, are approaching their end. For the first two, as though worn out with age, have been reduced to a small number; but the last, which is also the later one, because it is still younger, seems also to be more steadfast. Nevertheless even this itself now seems in certain of its members to be growing lukewarm and again declining toward the laxity of its former life. Thus once the Cluniac reform, known throughout the whole world, when it spread itself far and wide, gradually failed, as though its strength had been lost through expansion. For it is said in the proverb: everything that grows old is near destruction. For any reform, however celebrated or holy it may have been, unless it is often renewed through the diligence of prelates, gradually vanishes. For as much as it grows with time, so much is it diminished in fervor. For when those who had shown zeal for the order in deed passed away, new abbots arose after them who, neglecting the holy fervor of reform, relapsed into their former deformities. And, to touch on matters nearer at hand, where is that reform which Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa, as legate, began with incredible zeal? Where are those terrible oaths of all the abbots of our province, by which they bound themselves to regular observance in the hands of the same Cardinal before the altar of Saint Stephen at Würzburg? Where is the regular observance that was promised? Behold, Fathers, you have under your chapter one hundred twenty-seven abbeys; of these, from the three observances already mentioned, scarcely seventy have remained under reform. Yet there are, I do not doubt, some who think themselves excellently reformed, but their conduct denies it. Would that the abbots would heed the threat of our holy lawgiver, often repeated, by which he says that the governance of souls is subject to the strictest accounting. Would that they would drive the sheep entrusted to them to the pastures of heavenly life, lest they themselves perish eternally together with the sheep. Consider the conduct, both of abbots and of monks, without the law of monastic honor, smoking all around; when it is known, it is a shame for me to speak of it, and a horror for you, O most revered Fathers, to hear it.
