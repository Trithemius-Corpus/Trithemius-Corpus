Rise up, you also, most excellent Fathers, authors of various reforms, and see that your labors have now perished, your constitutions are neglected, your exhortations spurned, the protests of your solicitude despised. You made the monastic order glorious in life and learning, beloved by God, venerable to the angels, holy to yourselves, reverenced by men.

Where now are those merits? Where now are those works of the just? Alas, venerable mother Religion, to what great calamity have you come! Those who once glorified you now despise you; those who honored you now hold you in contempt. You have abandoned the commandments of your lawgiver; you have cast aside the study of the Scriptures; therefore you have become contemptible. The sons whom you bore have done this evil to you.

For soon, when monks began to savor earthly things, to neglect sacred reading, to set inward things after outward, your excellent color was changed into foulness. But what then? He who falls often sets himself to rise again; but you, once fallen, have never wholly risen. What did the Cluniac reform do? What the Specuensian? What the Italensian? What the Castellensian? What the Melicensian? What, most recently, the Bursfeldensian? And if there were any other reforms besides these, what could they do for the whole order, spread throughout the entire world? For even if the Cluniac reform poured out the light of its honorableness in all the provinces of the world, nevertheless it left many monasteries in various kingdoms in their former deformity. For from that time when our religion first fell, there has never been any general reform.

O how easy is the descent to Avernus; how ready the will to offend; how very great, after a fall, the difficulty of rising again to righteousness! All, so to speak, have fallen; few have risen from their deformity; many are rotting away in their pleasures.

I grieve over you, O flower of monastic beauty; I grieve, and indeed vehemently. And if I cannot aid you otherwise, I pour out hot and most bitter tears for your contrition. But you also, most honored Fathers, who love the honor of your order, who grieve over the contrition of our mystical Joseph, who desire to come to the eternal inheritance, attend to the state of our religion, inspect its desolation, think upon a remedy.

Behold, all around, wealthy monasteries are snatched from the hands of Saint Benedict; behold, the cloisters of monks become dwellings of clerics, and from evil pursuits monks are transferred to the worst. You err, O prudence of the world; you are deceived, power of the age: never will you be able to make a good canon out of a bad monk.

But you also, transgressors, take heed; apostates of the order, listen. What madness drives you to destroy monasteries for Saint Benedict which you did not build? Or do you think yourselves free from damnation if, having cast off the monastic habit, you sin as clerics? Will the presumptuous license of sinning save you in the strict examination?
