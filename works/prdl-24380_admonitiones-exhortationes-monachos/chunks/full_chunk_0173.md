The wise man says: A kingdom is transferred from nation to nation because of injustices, injuries, insults, and various deceits. The order of justice first regards God, then one’s country, thereafter one’s parents, and afterward all people in common according to distinctions of rank and the merits of their dignities. To God, first of all, at the dictation of justice, we owe religion, faith, worship, devotion, thanksgiving, and true love from the whole heart under obedience. Next, to ourselves we owe purity of mind and body, continence with zeal for perfection, so that through obedience we may be permitted to return to him by whom we were created. In the third place, to our country we owe advantage, honor, and defense, and, as far as we can, the promotion of its welfare together with justice itself. Fourth, we are bound to show honor and obedience to our parents, and in their need care and every kind of support. Fifth, to our prelates and superiors we owe obedience and humble subjection for the love of Christ. Sixth, prelates owe teaching and nourishment to their subjects, and correction to those who do wrong. In the seventh place, we owe honor to elders and princes, and subjection without sin for the love of God. Eighth, we owe reverence to venerable persons, and honor to priests. Thereafter we are bound to offer friendship to equals, condescension to inferiors, requital to benefactors, fellowship to friends, patience to enemies, mercy to the poor, consolation to the wretched, and charity to all.

This is true justice: that we love God with the whole heart for his own sake, and our neighbor in God as ourselves, and our enemy for God’s sake, distributing to each person what is owed to him by right and providing for the benefit of all. But since excessive justice easily falls into sin, temperance is necessary for you all, and it alone makes people perfect. Temperance is a virtue that discerns by reason and conquers the movements of the soul and every lust, so that impulse in action may not exceed the equity of the law. Temperance, an excellent virtue, rules pleasures and preserves all things in due order. In all your actions, and also in your words and thoughts, apply temperance, and the discretion necessary to the virtues. Whatever good you do with discretion receives the name by merit of virtue; but what is done without discretion must be called vice. Discretion is the moderator of all virtues, and the greatest adornment of prudence and justice. Discretion is necessary not only for prelates but also for subjects, because it gives order to virtues, bestows modesty upon affections, and grants to all human counsels a most beautiful seemliness and honesty. Hence a certain wise man said:

There is a measure in things; there are, in short, fixed limits, beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.

For this, brothers, strength of soul is necessary for you, through which you may not only become steadfast in adversities, but also ready and cheerful.
