Homily XV.

What good is it not to drink, and yet to be drunk with anger, lust, hatred, and envy? Or what will it profit us for eternal salvation, brothers, that according to the Rule of our holy father Benedict we do not eat meat, if we do not tame the vices of the flesh?

For the thrice-holy Pope Gregory says: “The body is worn down in vain by abstinence, if the mind, distracted by disordered impulses, is scattered among vices.” Hence Scripture rightly says: “Sanctify the fast.” To sanctify the fast is to show abstinence of the flesh worthy of God by joining it with other good works. For if abstinence from food alone made a man virtuous, there would have been no need to add, “Sanctify”; but because fasting alone does not suffice for the sanctification of the soul, it must be adorned with the beauty of all the virtues. For those who abstain from foods and do evil imitate the demons, who always fast and are never without sin.

A fast acceptable to God is to restrain the body by abstinence from desire for evil things, and continually to mortify the deeds of the flesh by the spirit of compunction for love of Christ. Let the mind of the one fasting be fervent in the love of God; let his conscience always be clean from vices, and the fasts of humble servants will please the Most High. Let monks have daily fasts, as the rule of our holy predecessor Benedict established; let their hearts be clean, as the Lord Jesus commanded in the holy Gospel; let their refreshment be moderate, fleeing satiety, because just as the body is corrupted by many and various diseases from excessive fullness, so the disordered motion of lust is stirred up in it. For modest and moderate food is both medicine for the body and a support of virtue for the soul.

For Saint John, the golden mouth, the bishop, says: “Nothing is so pleasant as food well digested and cooked; nothing so works health, nothing so sharpens the senses, nothing so drives away sickness, as moderate refreshment. For sufficiency with moderate nourishment produces both soundness and pleasure. But abundance causes disease, brings troubles, and generates various illnesses. For what excessive hunger does, immoderate fullness of foods is also accustomed to do. Hunger indeed carries a man off in a few days and frees him from this penal life; but excess of foods consumes and makes the human body rot, wastes it with long illness, and at last destroys it by a cruel death.”

Therefore, in order that you may live in mind and be healthy in body, my dearly beloved brothers, keep this rule of living which the holy Fathers prescribed for you by word and showed beforehand by living example: fleeing all luxury in foods and drinks, indulge the body only so much as is enough for good health, as nature requires. Food should allay hunger, not incite the body to lust; drink should quench thirst, not overthrow the mind or the senses; clothing should ward off cold, not make the soul grow soft in love of vanity. Always remember, brothers, that gluttony shamefully drove our first parents from the kingdom of paradise and innocence; they did not desire a bird, or a fish, or a calf.
