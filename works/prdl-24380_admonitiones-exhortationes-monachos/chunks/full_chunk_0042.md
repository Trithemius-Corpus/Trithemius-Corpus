Which of you, brothers, is an imitator of such men, so that he regards the need of our whole community as his own, or strives, according to his strength, to relieve it by laboring in the sweat of his brow? They, while laboring with their hands, prayed to God with unceasing hearts; but you, intent on tales, satisfy neither the duty of your hands nor that of monks in your minds.

In the time of the regular fast, too, they either diligently kept the measure of abstinence appointed for them, or, if they could not do so because of the great extent of their labors, they ate for this reason: that they might be made fit and capable for their labors. But you do not eat in order to work, but rather work in order to eat. When, according to the Rule, you ought to fast, you take occasion from work to break the fast.

Moreover, how filthily and contemptibly some of you treat their clothing: what lover zealous for regular discipline could bear it calmly? Whose property is it, my brothers, that you wastefully consume? Everything that belongs to the monasteries of God’s servants is Christ’s patrimony, and all of it is dedicated to God; nor is it lawful for anyone to misuse the things of Christ, or to divert them to another use. Do you not know what our holy lawgiver Benedict says to you in the Rule? “If anyone treats the things of the monastery in a dirty or negligent way, let him be corrected. If he does not amend, let him be subjected to regular discipline.” And in the chapter on the cellarer of the monastery he says: “Let him keep all the vessels of the monastery and all its substance as if he were looking upon the sacred vessels of the altar.”

Finally, I cannot be silent, and certainly ought not to be, about how filthily and negligently many of you treat everything, as if you were either not seen by God, or as if the sloth of any of you caused no harm to the property of the community. Know, brothers, that I forewarn you and announce to you that for all these things, as also for your other sins, you will have to render an account to the Lord God.

I therefore reduced for you the labors outside the monastery, lest by working badly you should sin more gravely, and I enjoined on you manual work: writing, binding books, and things of this kind, which you can accomplish within the enclosure of the monastery with tranquility of mind and body. Would that you performed even these works of your hands diligently for the love of God, so that you may not eat your bread wholly idle. There is no manual work which, in my judgment, is more fitting for a monk than to write books of sacred reading and to prepare necessities for others who write. For this holy labor may very often be interrupted by prayer, and one may keep watch no less for the food of the mind than of the body. Necessity also presses us to devote ourselves diligently to writing books, if we wish to have at hand the means by which we may occupy ourselves together usefully in spiritual study. For you see that the whole library of this community, which once was distinguished and great, has been so scattered, sold, and alienated by the misshapen monks before us that no more than fourteen volumes were found here by us.
