For the monk who professes the cenobitic rule is commanded to be a disciple of Christ the Savior; and if he desires to please him in truth, he must become his imitator. For thus he says in the holy Gospel: “He who wishes to be my disciple, let him follow me.” And what “let him follow me” means, the holy apostle John teaches in one of his letters, saying: “He who says that he abides in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked.” And the Lord himself again says in the Gospel: “He who wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me. He who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me. And he who loves son or daughter above me is not worthy of me. And he who does not take up his cross and follow me is not worthy of me.”

Your teacher has not spoken obscurely, O monk, nor wrapped his meaning in riddles, but has set forth a plain saying, by which you can put forward no excuse. For Christ himself is the example and rule of good and perfect conduct for every Christian, and especially for the monk; and the cloistered man who, with all his powers and with his whole heart, does not follow him, boasts in vain of the profession of faith. For just as a Christian is so called from Christ, so a disciple is named from imitation of his master. Therefore imitate your master in your conduct, so that you may prove yourself a true disciple of Christ.

For the Lord Jesus Christ, whose disciple you profess yourself to be, O monk, though he was rich, became poor for our sake; but you, though you are lowly and needy in the school of poverty, seek abundance of all things, and though your teacher commands you to despise everything for love of him, you in turn desire to possess everything in the monastery. Your Lord and teacher was obedient to the Father for our sake, even to the death of the cross; but you, full of pride and impatience, either refuse to obey your superiors for love of him, or obey unwillingly and with indignation.

For the sake of your salvation, he not only endured with the utmost patience throughout his whole life the words and insults of wicked men, but also for love of you bore blows, livid wounds, and dreadful injuries. He did not refuse even to undergo a most shameful death, so that he might call your soul back from the error of death to the life of eternal light. From the day of his birth he lived in the world poor and humble, filled with many tribulations and insults, so that by his teaching and benefits he might restore you, a most ruined man, to the knowledge and love of God.

For what would we be, if the Son of God had not appeared as man, except utterly lost? And what are we, if we do not imitate our master with all our powers, except ungrateful?
