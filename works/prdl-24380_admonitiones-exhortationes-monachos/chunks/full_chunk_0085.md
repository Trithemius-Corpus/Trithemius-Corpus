A monk can be a martyr without sword and fire, if for the love of God he everywhere keeps true patience in his soul. For the true monk is not puffed up by praise nor saddened by blame; he shows himself patient under injuries; amid insults and reproaches he joyfully and humbly keeps silence. No one is stronger than the patient man, who knows how to conquer himself and humbly to endure every adversity with a tranquil mind for the love of God.

Every impatient man, however, is proud and arrogant, because he does not follow Jesus, the teacher of humility, and therefore he is indignant at the just rebukes that he has deserved. As the furnace tests gold, so insults and injuries test the monk, whether he truly loves Christ. Humility and patience in adversities are the true marks of monks; whoever is not clothed with their adornment boasts of the name in vain. He who is not humble cannot truly be a monk; and he who does not keep patience in adversities cannot be a true disciple of Christ.

Hence King Solomon said in the book of Proverbs: “A patient man is better than a strong man, and he who rules his own spirit than one who storms cities.” For a man’s learning is known through patience, and his glory is to pass over wrongs. The furnace tests the potter’s vessels, and the trial of tribulation tests righteous men.

The monk who follows Christ in truth is patient under all injuries; and he who is not forgetful in meditating on the Lord’s passion exults and rejoices in his soul amid insults. For all, as holy Christophilus says, who wish to live piously in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution; and whatever evil befalls them, they overcome it with the greatest constancy.

And although patience is necessary for a monk in every place and time, still in the daily examination of faults it must be guarded with especial care and more diligent attention, where the correction of morals is at issue, and whatever has been committed against the standard of monastic purity is amended in each person by the senior. Therefore, before we enter this place, all things must be considered in the morning, and the soul must be strengthened against what may happen, so that, if perhaps a harsher rebuke should occur, it may not overthrow the mind from its state of tranquillity.

For even if we have resolved never to bring insult upon any of you or do injury to anyone, still we do not deny that it can happen that someone may think himself rebuked beyond his desert, if he weighs our fatherly correction by the words rather than by the spirit. As God knows, we seek your salvation in all things, and nothing else; we sincerely intend to cut out vices by the roots according to the statutes of our religion, not to harm anyone. We cannot please everyone in all things, but we are compelled especially to displease those who, sinning against the rule, daily promise themselves impunity for their crimes. Of these King Solomon said: “Evil teaching belongs to those who abandon the way of life, and he who hates rebukes will die.” The pestilent man does not love the one who corrects him, nor does he go to the wise. He who does evil deliberately flees.
