Do not rouse Saint Benedict against yourselves as an adversary, if you desire to find the friendship of the other saints; for if one is offended, none of the others will be your friend. Consider the end of those who long ago transferred themselves from the holy order, and likewise fear for your own. Where are the abbots and monks who once withdrew from the order and handed over the monasteries of the holy Father Benedict into the hands of secular men? All are either dead or will die soon. You also, who are still alive, who have withdrawn from the order after the example of those before you, when you do not expect it, will go to those whose imitators you are. Would that, from another’s danger, you feared your own, and, while there is time, took up repentance with your whole heart.

I have heard from many with what great bitterness of mind, at the point of death, many transgressors of the order were constrained, who from monks had become canons, or who had even been the authors of such transfers. For they said that they grieved over no sin so much as over that one, because they knew they had given consent or help to the transfer of the order. If the transfer of monasteries from the order is good, why did those who were dying grieve that it had been done? But if it is evil, why do you do it and undertake it?

Let all who occupy the monasteries of monks remember with how severe an anathema they are marked by blessed Pope Gregory: those who do not fear to turn the possessions of monasteries consecrated to God to alien uses. Let Thiergandus, once archbishop of Trier, be an example to them. When he had taken the monastery of Saint Andrew as a dwelling from Pope Adrian in the city of Rome, and had been warned in a third vision by the same Apostle and by Saint Gregory not to occupy the dwellings of monks any longer, and, though ordered to depart from there, pretended not to notice, he perished with all his people by a harsh death. Whether those who occupy the monasteries of monks are secure before God, we leave to their own consciences. A man who possesses another man’s goods without his will is bound to restitution. Let him who occupies the goods of any saint against that saint’s will consider what he deserves. We have now used a long enough discourse against the transgressors of our order; let us now come to what remains.

How these dismemberments by apostate monks are to be opposed. Chapter VII.

Although both my spirit and necessity itself offer abundant matter for inveighing against the destroyers of our order, nevertheless, since those are not present who might be called back to repentance by exhortations, I think enough has been said against them. For all who so wickedly oppress the honor of their own order are senseless; and I judge that they must be resisted not only with words, but with deeds [unclear].
