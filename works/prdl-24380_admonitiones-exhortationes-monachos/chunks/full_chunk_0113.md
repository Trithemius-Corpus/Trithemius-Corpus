The monk who always thinks he will die permits no idle time to pass. For Scripture says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” On the contrary, concerning the forgetful and the sluggish it is written: “They are a nation without counsel and without prudence. Would that they were wise and understood, and would foresee their last things.” The memory of death brings watchfulness into the soul and extinguishes every disordered thought of the mind. Whoever rightly fears death is never sluggish in good work.

For the wise man says: “Whatever your hand is able to do, do it earnestly, because neither work, nor reason, nor wisdom, nor knowledge will be among the dead, where you are hastening.” For the monk who is a true worshiper of God spends no moment of time uselessly, but by night and by day, in the exercise of good work, he continually practices watchfulness of mind with the greatest devotion; and, though sleeping in body, in his soul he says constantly to God’s messengers: “I sleep, and my heart keeps watch.” Sleep is the image of death and the binding of the bodily senses; but one who is a true lover of Christ keeps watch in mind even while sleeping in body, and, praying without ceasing, cries to the Lord with the affection of charity: “Before you is all my desire; dreaming of you, I think of you, Jesus crucified, my love.”

I blush, my brothers, and am deeply ashamed to bring forward a discourse on the watchfulness of monks, because when I recall to mind the practice of keeping vigil among the holy Fathers who went before us, I recognize with sorrow how far our weakness stands from their perfection. They, kindled with divine love, often passed whole nights without sleep in the praises of God; but to us even a span of eight hours for sleeping sometimes scarcely seems enough, of which we do not cease complaining that the five before Matins and the three afterward are too short.

For the true abstinence of monks consists not only in sparing use of food and drink, but also in the continuation of vigils for the praise of God, because that mortification of the flesh is foolish in which the belly is restrained from foods and the whole body is fattened through sleepiness. For the body is refreshed for its subsistence not only by food and drink, but is also restored by the quiet of sleep. What sort of fasting by monks is it to be sparing by abstaining from foods and drinks, and to indulge in sleep to satiety? It makes little difference whether you refresh the body with food or with sleep, since in both satiety is a vice.

Therefore the ancient men and the first leaders of the monks, observing temperance in sleeping no less than in eating and drinking, remained most continent in both. Some of them, standing on their feet, and others lying on the bare ground, granted nature only the scantiest sleep.
