Through pride the devil fell from heaven; through humility man ascends. We are called monks in vain if we have not been humble, since, just as fire is extinguished if it lacks the fuel of wood, so a monk without humility is judged utterly unworthy of his name. By Christian humility the devil is conquered, and, as though struck by a nail, is overcome and flees. No jewel is seen more splendid than humility in the adornment of monastic life; if a monk despises it, he has denied both his profession and the humble Christ.

After humility comes prudence, which always directs all things according to the rule of reason, and does nothing except what is right, honorable, and praiseworthy. Prudence knows what it ought virtuously to seek, and it knows well what, according to the right judgment of reason, it ought to avoid. True and perfect prudence is that by which we seek that highest, unchangeable good, and order all things to their due and right end. Prudence preserves the observance of regular discipline stable in its vigor, and brings wandering monks back to the complete reform of the purpose they have lost. Prudence directs the monk on the right way of salvation, and does not allow him to turn aside in either direction. Prudence preserves peace and concord among the brothers, and orders right action in each matter.

Prudence is very necessary for you, my brothers, so that in every action you may know what you should undertake and what, in turn, you should flee. True prudence, not of the flesh but of the spirit, teaches you to despise the world and to desire only those things that are future and eternal. Prudence teaches a man to stand firm within himself, and not to turn aside from his purpose of holiness either in prosperity or in adversity. Prudence teaches him to order present things well, brings past things back to memory for instruction, and provides for the future, lest anything happen unexpectedly. Prudence corrects offenders usefully and fruitfully; all that it either does, says, or thinks, it arranges according to the judgment of reason toward its due end.

Be therefore prudent as serpents and simple as doves, because prudence without goodness is malice, and simplicity without rational prudence is called folly. For the most holy Paul, Apostle of Christ, says: The prudence of the flesh is death. Be prudent, lest you be circumvented by the deceit of others; but be simple, lest you circumvent anyone. For this is the office of the prudent man: neither to be capable of being deceived, nor to wish to deceive anyone. A prudent man does not abandon the judgment of reason, and does nothing for which he must repent. For “prudent” is so called from foreseeing.

Justice accompanies prudence, and it is the most splendid of all the virtues. For without justice all the other virtues are nothing. Justice is the preserver of religion, the mother of grace, the nurse of observance, the fosterer of truth, the begetter of peace, and the source of piety. Justice is the virtue that distributes and preserves to each person what is his own; it neglects its own advantage in order to preserve the common advantage and equity, without which no commonwealth can long subsist in peace.
