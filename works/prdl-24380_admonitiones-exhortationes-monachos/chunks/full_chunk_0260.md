Behold, the rich men of the world violently seize the goods of monasteries; behold, the worshippers of avarice invade the possessions of the order; behold, secular clerics receive the abbeys of St. Benedict in commendam. None of these things would happen if abbots and monks had persevered in regular observance. Let us therefore impute to ourselves what we suffer, since we did not resist the rashness of the wicked from the beginning. To us, I say, this fault is justly ascribed, since, intent on our own advantages, we do not care for the losses of the order, as though they were someone else’s. Let us rise up, I pray, Fathers; let us rise up, even at last, though late, after so many losses, so many dangers, so much destruction of souls, and let us do what lies in us, beseeching God’s mercy for the rest, that he may supply our deficiency, direct our zeal, and bring our holy efforts to completion. And if we cannot bring the whole order back to the unity of good conduct, let us at least strive, as far as possible, to reform those monks who are in our province. But if complete conformity cannot be had in all things, let it at least be reformed in the three substantial things, without which the life of monks must be judged nothing other than the foul brothel of an unbelieving soul. Reform, moreover, must begin with the abbots, and thereafter be continued among the monks. For unless the shepherd has been brought back to sincerity, the health of the sheep will not long endure. For it is commonly said: When the head is sick, the other members suffer. Therefore every adornment of the body must begin from the head. There are certain deformed abbots who wish to compel their monks to reform, but they themselves do not wish to submit to regular observance, not even in the least; hating in themselves the good that they love in others, and not blushing to do themselves what they forbid their subjects. Do not trust such men, O Fathers, for they are the very ones who confound us: they spurn visitations, they despise the decrees of chapters. Let experience make us cautious, whom zeal at another time, without knowledge, made penitent.

*That visitors ought to be solicitous for the reform of our order.*

*Chapter XII.*

The chief force of reform, however, is placed in the diligence of the visitors. Therefore it is said in the constitutions of the Fathers that suitable men should be appointed to the office of visitation: men who, by word and example, can effectively instruct those to be visited in uprightness of conduct. For a Visitor must be learned in divine law. He must be holy and irreproachable in life, so that by word he may teach the teachable, and by example instruct the stubborn. For one without the other is not sufficient in a ruler of souls. For if he is unlearned, how will he instruct others? If his conduct does not provide those to be visited with an example of good action, how will he exhort them to reform? He will indeed hear the reproach of the proverb, in which it is said: Physician, heal thyself.
