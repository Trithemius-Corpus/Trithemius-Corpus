I do not know, my ever-venerable predecessor, under what unlucky star this monastery of Sponheim was built, since an abbot rarely dies in it, but almost all, overcome by weariness, for nearly seventy years now, as I find from the annals, have resigned of their own accord. Nor are we wholly ignorant why you too, following the example of those before you, abandoned us poor men before the time: we rightly blame both our poverty and the rebellious rashness of certain men. I now experience, alas, the reason that moved your mind to leave the abbacy of Sponheim, utterly barren and unfruitful, over which you presided for thirteen years after the reform, not without continual anxiety of spirit and endless labors. I pass over the wrongs you endured from very many of the brothers, all of which are indeed now doubled against me every day.

You instructed me to send to your Paternity the book of my Exhortations to the brothers, which I recently composed by night amid the raging occupations of worldly affairs. Although it is unworthy of your reading, I nevertheless did not wish to resist a father’s command. Therefore I beg your gentleness kindly to apply the file of correction: and, if anything is lacking, add it; cut away what is superfluous; and what is unsuitable either consign to the flames or at least put in order. Farewell, sweetest Father, remembering us. From Sponheim, on the sixth day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1486.

Sermons and Exhortations to Monks by Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, afterward of Würzburg; of these sermons there are two books: the first is called the Book of Homilies, the second the Book of Sermons.

In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ begins the first book of Exhortations to Monks.

On the Spiritual Warfare of Monks. Homily I.

It belongs to Christian warfare, brothers, and especially to that of monks, to be subject to the various calamities of this world, and, like gold in the furnace, to be exercised and tested by continual tribulations. For it belongs to monks, in the warfare of Christians, to surpass the common rule by a higher manner of living, and to obey not only the Lord’s commandments but also his counsels. It belongs to monks to despise utterly riches, honors, and all the pleasures of the present world for the love of God, and, having overcome carnal desires, to be continually free for God alone. For a monk is so called as one solitary, from oneness; and by the very word of his name he is admonished to contempt of the world. Accordingly the office of a monk is to remain with himself in solitude, clean and purified from vices, and sweetly to love Christ above all things.

For the goal of monks, toward which the regular institution is directed, is Christ; whoever refuses to imitate him is neither a monk nor even a Christian. Hence, my brothers, we must know that whoever does not follow our Lord and Savior in true patience and humility gains nothing for salvation from being called a Christian or a monk. For he himself says in the holy Gospel: Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven, he shall enter the kingdom of heaven. And what the will of God is, the holy Apostle disclosed, saying: This is the will of God, your sanctification. But since the necessary completion of this sanctification commanded to us requires great endurance of evils in all things, and humility, because no one is holy by his own strength, we must, my brothers, keep continual consideration of our calling within ourselves.
