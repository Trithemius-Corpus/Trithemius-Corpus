Great is the virtue if you do not harm the one by whom you were harmed; great is the courage of soul if, for the love of God, you forgive the one inflicting injury; great is the glory if you observe Christ’s command and pour out prayers for those who wrong you. No one has known the strength of his own soul whom no notable insult has yet burned. Let no one boast that he is meek or patient who has not been sated with reproaches. There is no patience without adversity, nor meekness without insult, because if you wish to keep the proper bounds of endurance, an insult-inflicter must first be present.

“If I had lived,” says the lazy and fleshly monk, “in the days of the martyrs, I would have feared to undergo no kind of torment for the love of Christ; I would willingly have exposed my body to tortures, so that I might have gained the crown of martyrdom.” Great rashness of folly. You have never been able patiently to endure a word for the love of God, and you promise the constancy of the holy martyrs? You fear a slight correction, you who promise that you will give your body for Christ. If martyrdom delights you, hear what the most blessed Pope Gregory says: “If, with Christ helping us, we strive to preserve the virtue of patience, then we live in the peace of the Church and hold the palm of martyrdom. For we can be martyrs without sword and flame, if we truly preserve patience in the soul. To die at the hands of a persecutor is martyrdom in open deed; but to bear insults and love one who hates you is martyrdom in hidden thought.”

True patience is to endure another’s evils with equanimity, and not to be gnawed by any grief against the one who has inflicted the evil. What will you say now, O lazy monk, who, unable to endure the very light martyrdom of words, boast so insolently of constancy in shedding blood? Learn, miserable man, to have patience in small things, and consider that, as Scripture says, “He who is unfaithful in little, who will trust him with what is greater?”

You murmur at everything done in the monastery; when rebuked, however gently, for your grave faults and negligences, you bear it most impatiently. No one in the monastery walks rightly in your eyes; no one besides you, in your estimation, lives religiously. You alone, as it seems to you, are holy in the monastery; you, the most learned of all, allow no one to know what you alone do not know. You judge yourself worthy of all honors above everyone, because you not only lack patience, but possess neither true humility nor obedience.

This is not the road by which one goes to the kingdom of heaven, when a monk is proud in the school of humility and a Christian lives in conduct contrary to Christ. But the monk, the solemnly sworn servant of Jesus Christ, so that he may imitate his Lord, must not be proud, but humble; obedient, not rebellious; gentle, not cruel; not insolent, but meek.
