For through many years in the order of the holy Father Benedict there shone men most excellent in every learning and in holiness, as almost all who have written the histories of the ancients testify. And in truth, to speak without envy, the holy religion of Benedict easily surpasses all orders, if you choose to consider its dignity, its wide dominion in the world, its men distinguished in learning and holiness, and finally their multitude. For this venerable planting brought forth eighteen supreme pontiffs, more than two hundred cardinals, sixteen hundred archbishops, and three thousand five hundred bishops. But if anyone calls this total of illustrious prelates into doubt as excessive, let him turn over the chronicles and deeds of the ancients, and he will find that only a few have been designated out of many.

Yet let no one think that the order’s former splendor should be measured from its present state, since there is no fair comparison between our men and those men of old, for in our times monks do not shine, as once they did, either in knowledge of the Scriptures or in holiness of conduct. Perhaps even now you will find devout monks, though few, but simple men, unlearned, and without any training in the liberal arts. But for the government of souls in this age devotion is not enough, if knowledge of the Scriptures is lacking. But if our monks are neither devout nor learned, they are rightly judged unworthy of the government of churches. Many, indeed, walk well as subjects, who, if they were raised to the governance of others, would never continue in the tranquility of their former life. And, to say briefly what I think, neither are churches worthy of monks, nor monks of churches at this time. The cause, however, is not hidden from the prudent, and in due time hereafter it will be brought to light. Now, if it is pleasing, let us inquire briefly what the present state of our order is like.

On the Present Lamentable State of Our Order. Chapter III.

My eyes have failed from weeping; my inward parts are troubled; my liver is poured out upon the earth over the destruction of my order, because the humble little one has failed in it, because, cast down without honor, it lies open to the hands of the enemy. Let no one despise me as I mourn; let no one accuse my grief. I do not seek earthly honors; I do not covet ecclesiastical dignities. Rather, I groan that learning and holiness have failed from my order. What have we to do with empty honors, we who seek life in heaven? Alas, the crown of our head has fallen; monastic devotion has grown lukewarm; charity has grown cold; the honorable discipline of the Rule has for the most part failed. Where now in our order is that most holy devotion, once rich in most learned men? Where is the venerable learning in every variety of Scripture? Where is the abbots’ solicitude for the care of the Lord’s flock?
