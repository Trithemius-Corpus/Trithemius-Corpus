For it can often happen that a person takes richer foods without fault, while he governs his appetite, whereas another takes poorer foods not without guilt of conscience, when through immoderation he exceeds the measure of temperance. Therefore let none of you think himself safe from the vice of gluttony merely because he eats neither rich nor delicate foods, since it is not the food, but the appetite for food, that is held at fault. For it is quite possible for someone eating beans and vegetables to incur the guilt of sin, and for another taking the most delicate birds as food not to sin.

Hence one of the holy fathers says: “A belly filled with any foods at all produces the seedbeds of lust; nor can the mind prevail in managing the rudders of discretion when it has been choked by the weight of foods. For not only drunkenness from wine is accustomed to intoxicate the mind, but also excess in all foods makes it unsteady and wavering, and utterly strips it of every contemplation of integrity and purity.”

For this reason great discretion is needed, so that only the food necessary for sustaining life is given to the body, and no excess of indulgence occurs. Hence the divine Pope Gregory says: “It is a great labor of discretion both to give something and to deny something to the body that demands food, and by not giving to nourish nature.” For through abstinence the vices are to be extinguished, not the flesh; and through food the body is to be nourished, not sins. Thus it is necessary that a monk maintain the art of self-restraint in his diet, so that he kills not the flesh but the vices.

For often, while we pursue the enemy in the flesh, we severely slaughter, through immoderate abstinence, the spirit of devotion that we love. Very often too, while we show too much sympathy to the flesh and spare it as though it were a fellow-citizen, we nourish an enemy for battle against ourselves. But when the vice of gluttony rules, people lose everything they have done bravely, because when the belly is not restrained, all the virtues are at once overwhelmed. For one does not rise up to the struggle of the spiritual contest unless the enemy placed within ourselves, namely the appetite of gluttony, is first subdued by us with God’s help; since unless we overcome the things that are nearer to us, we hasten in vain to fight against those set farther away. For war is waged uselessly in the field against foreign enemies while a citizen plotting within the city walls is present. We never conquer the assaults of demons unless we first overcome gluttony in ourselves.

Brothers, let no one deceive himself with empty persuasion, so as to believe that there is no sin among beans and vegetables if the mind, with excessive pleasure, loosens the appetite for receiving them. For guilt can be present everywhere in taking food, when appetite is extended beyond the rule of nature.
