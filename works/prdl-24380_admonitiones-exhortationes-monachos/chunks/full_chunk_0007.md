You have professed the unconquerable warfare of Christ under a twofold condition: the one when, at the font of holy baptism, renouncing Satan, you swore to the words of Christian instruction; the other when you solemnly put on this garment of monastic religion. A great necessity of making progress binds you, if you weigh your profession with due consideration, since the meaning of the Christian name commands that you must keep the Gospel precepts, and the vow that you voluntarily made requires that you preserve the appointed rules of monastic discipline.

It is no longer enough for your salvation to fulfill those appointed things which are set before Christians in common to be observed, unless you also most diligently keep the monastic institutions which you promised. “Vow,” says Scripture, “and pay.” And again another Scripture says: “If you have vowed anything to God, do not delay to pay it; for an unfaithful and foolish promise displeases him, and it is much better not to vow than, after vows have been promised, not to pay.” And Saint Augustine admonishes us how necessary it is to pay to God what has been promised, saying: “There are certain things which, even without vowing them, we owe to the Lord, and there are certain things which, unless we have vowed them, we do not owe. But after we promise them to the Lord, we are necessarily constrained to pay them.”

And now, my brothers, carefully consider what you have vowed to the Lord your God, and diligently weigh under what necessity of paying what you promised you have been placed. For you vowed to your God, not to man, obedience, and conversion of your way of life according to the Rule of our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and perpetual stability in this order of religion. But the obedience which you promised to God you owe to his vicar, a man, whom you have learned from the Rule you profess to hold the place of Christ in the monastery.

What the conversion of your way of life ought to be according to the Rule is plainly seen in the fourth chapter, marked “On the Instruments of Good Works,” in which you find the sum of all monastic perfection briefly comprehended. Indeed, what our blessed father commands to be done concerning novices you have all heard, and you know that it was done in the beginning of the profession of each one of you. For he says: “The one to be received shall promise in the oratory, before all, concerning his stability, the conversion of his way of life, and obedience, before God and his saints, so that if he should ever act otherwise, he may know that he will be condemned by God, whom he mocks.”

Consider now, therefore, brothers, under what necessity of regular observance you have been placed, since unless you complete in deed what you promised when you received the monastic habit, you will be judged before the almighty Lord not as servants of God, not as friends, not as monks, but as mockers. Therefore always remember those things which the Rule commands you, according to which you promised to shape your ways, and labor like true soldiers of Jesus Christ, always keeping watch in the fear of the Lord, since, as Scripture says, unless you hold yourselves steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown.

Monks who break their covenant are not monks, but mockers.
