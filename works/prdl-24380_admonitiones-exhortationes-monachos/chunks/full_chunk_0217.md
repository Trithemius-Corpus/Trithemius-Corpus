For love of him they chiefly do good works, and in their intention they neither fear punishments nor seek rewards. They humbly obey their prelates for love of God, and in no respect at all do they depart from the purity of regular observance. Among these there are some whom the weakness of the flesh sometimes makes unstable in mind, and somewhat obstinate in their opinions against obedience. They sometimes try to defend their exercises and private little observances against the determination of their prelates, and think themselves more prudent than their rulers in the examination of spiritual matters. Flee this, O monks, because the devil lies hidden in ambush; and those whom he cannot conquer by open attack he strives to overcome by hidden contrivance. For the holier and more perfect a monk is before God, the more readily will he obey his prelate without debate. But if a perfect monk disdains to obey his abbot, even an imperfect one, in lawful and honorable matters, he has certainly already lost the name of perfection. Therefore let each one take care to devote himself to the pursuit of progress in such a way that he presumes in nothing to violate obedience. For Scripture says: Obedience is better than sacrifices, and hearkening more than offering the fat of rams. Whatever you offer is something external; but the truly obedient man offers himself.

The sixth degree, then, especially befits those who, living in all purity of mind and body, love the Lord above all things; who do every good they can for the honor of God alone; and who do not depart from the commands of their prelates. Great is the virtue of obedience, which doubles a holy good work, and, increasing merit for the worker, presents him to God like trees with fruit. Let not the words of men who despise obedience move you, my dearest brothers, men who prefer freedom of will to the subjection of monks, and set a good work done from freedom above the merits of obedience. For even if a monk obeys from the necessity of his profession, nevertheless the good will by which he bound himself to obedience for love of God commends this necessity in many ways.

There is, however, a necessity by which a man is compelled to do something where the will has not consented but always resists. This necessity lessens the praise of virtue, because the good work was never in the will. There is also another necessity, arising from the inward inclination of the mind, which increases, not lessens, the praise of the good work, because it kindles the will more intensely toward that very work. There is a third necessity, which has regard to the end and does not compel the will. For if you wish to cross the Rhine by boat, a fitting necessity requires the instrument. But the necessity by which a monk is compelled to obey his shepherd is not the first, of coercion, nor the second, of inclination, but rather the third, because it is ordered to the end from a preceding will. Therefore it is necessary for a monk, on account of the end, to do what the abbot has commanded, so that he may be able to fulfill, for love of God, the vow that he has made.
