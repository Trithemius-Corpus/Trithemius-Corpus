Homily XV.

What was the reason that almighty God forbade our first parents to eat the fruit, under threat of death, except to show them the occasion of merit through the good of obedience? For, as Scripture bears witness, he created two intellects from the beginning, namely the angelic and the human, to whom, through grace, he might communicate himself for the enjoyment of eternal happiness. Yet although he created both by the gift of his grace, he most rightly ordained that they should become forever blessed also by their own merit, so that in them both mercy might have its place, and conversion might obtain the merit necessary for salvation.

But the angelic intellect was immediately divided in two: some, falling of their own accord through pride, while others, humble and subject through love of their Principle, cleaved to him. Those who apostatized from their Principle departed in misery; but these, for the merit of their cleaving to him, were endowed with everlasting blessedness.

The human intellect, however, was sent down into flesh; and soon, at the devil’s persuasion, it transgressed the command of God and feasted on the food forbidden to it. From this followed the ruin of the whole human race, which no one could take away except the Son of God, made man from the Virgin for our sake. For what condemned our first parents except the transgression of the command? God was not concerned about the food, but about obedience. For if man had abstained, as almighty God had commanded him, surely by this obedience he would have merited, in his innocence, the enjoyment of eternal happiness.

For it is established by the tradition of the Scriptures that man cannot be saved without the merit of free will, since, even if the grace of God works all things in all, nevertheless he will save no one without the cooperation of his own will, except only infants baptized in the name of the Trinity, and those who cannot have the free use of reason. For just as free will does nothing toward salvation without the grace of the Savior, so the grace of God will not save an adult without the merit of a good will.

But the first man, placed in Paradise, transgressed the command of God through intemperance, and did not have the merit of obedience in a good will; therefore he was cast out, wretched and unhappy. Yet the mercy of almighty God determined from eternity to restore the one who had fallen through weakness, knowing that he had been deceived rather by the suggestion of the envious serpent than by his own malice. He pardoned this guilt through the passion and death of his only-begotten Son, born in time of the Virgin, so that the one who perished by his own rashness might be restored to salvation by the obedience of the mediator.

For it was impossible for the human race, lost through disobedience, to come to the mercy of the Creator except through the obedience of such a mediator, who would be God and man, in whom there would be both the power of divinity and the innocence of nature.
