Let this be for the monk a true mirror of perfect humility, in which, looking each day, he may examine himself.

The first mark of true humility, then, each person can recognize in himself when he is able to regard himself as lower and more worthless than all people, not with painted speech, but by the humble judgment of the heart. This is the greatest sign of deep humility: that a monk, always advancing toward a better way of life, should believe himself to be a useless and unworthy servant of God. True humility belongs to the heart, not to speech; it is the pursuit of the mind, not merely of bodily display. That monk is truly humble who loves God above all things, who always keeps himself pure in every respect from every transgression of God’s commandments and of the rule, who does great works of devotion and justice, and who, by a true estimate of himself within himself, thinks lowly things of himself.

The second mark of true and deep humility is attained by the one who, running well in the fear of the Lord and persevering, at all times confesses his negligences before God and men. For the more he has advanced in good works, the more he sets his sins and negligences before the eyes of his mind. He carries continual contrition in his heart for his offenses; and always pierced in spirit, praying and groaning, he asks the Lord with tears for pardon and mercy. Nor does he pray only for his own sins, but also for the sins of the whole world. The memory of one’s own offenses is the greatest remedy against the lifting up of the mind.

The third mark of humility is that he desires to keep in secret with himself all the good works he does, and, out of zeal for humility, hides his progress. For the humble man does not boast vainly, nor does he seek praise from men, who hopes for the glory of eternal blessedness. Whoever among you thinks himself humble and a true monk, let him not despise this sign of deep submission and abasement for God’s sake.

The fourth mark of true humility is that, willingly descending into the abyss of contempt for himself, he regards all his brothers as better than himself before God, even those who are openly sinning; forgetful of his good deeds, he wisely considers his own evils; but passing over others’ evils in silence, he reveals at the proper time the good things they have done. This is the condition of true humility: always to admire and proclaim the good works of others, and to make little of his own and prudently hide them for the avoidance of pride.

The fifth sign of true humility is that, having attained it, he is quiet in his simplicity, and does not speak of others’ defects unless compelled either by reason of office or by the command of charity. Yet in correction the humility of a prudent man preserves moderation, so that the one whom he cannot make better by rebuking more severely, he strives to admonish gently and meekly. At times he also wisely passes over rebukes, and waits for and watches for a more fitting time.
