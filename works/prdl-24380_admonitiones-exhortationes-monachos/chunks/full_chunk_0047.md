Pay attention, I beseech you, dearly beloved brothers, to what I say to you, and consider how greatly you are bound to pray without ceasing. It is not enough for a monk, striving toward perfection in the school of Christ the eternal King, to discharge the canonical prayers; nor is it sufficient for a man solemnly dedicated to God by vow to join in the common prayers of the order in choir, because he who is commanded always to pray without ceasing is bound, beyond vocal prayers, also to spiritual prayers.

For the prayers instituted by the Church or by the order fall under the merit of obedience and under the law of necessity by reason of the command. But that manner of praying which is called spiritual, useful to a monk at every time and necessary for perfection, is strengthened in the freedom of the spirit. Hence it must be noted that for the prayers which are prescribed by the Church or by the order, the utterance of words is required of necessity, together with the intention of satisfying the statute by which one is bound to pray.

Let those see how they satisfy the command of the Church who do not express the seven canonical prayers by uttering the words, but, with closed mouth, run through the sequence of prayers by thought alone, their eyes serving them. But in those prayers which we undertake to say not from command but by our own will and from devotion alone, the fervor of the mind toward God suffices without the utterance of words; for God seeks, as we have said, such worshipers as adore in spirit and in truth. But the canonical prayers, which they call the hours, we are bound to say, and not merely to think. In both, devotion of the mind toward God is useful; without it no prayers are believed to be effective.

Therefore, my brothers, when we stand for prayer, we must keep watch with the greatest care and attend to the prayers we pour forth, lest we pray without the fruit of inward compunction. Let every thought of the flesh depart; let no occupation with household affairs hinder the mind; nor let the soul then think of anything else at all except this alone, what it is praying. For this reason also the priest, after the customary preface before the sacrifice has been given, prepares the minds of the brethren, saying, “Lift up your hearts”; so that, when the people answer, “We have them lifted up to the Lord,” they are reminded that they ought to think of nothing other than God himself, whom they invoke.

Let the breast, then, be closed against the adversary and open to the Lord alone; and during the time of holy prayer let it admit absolutely no occupation of the world. For great is the usefulness of pure prayer, which, like a faithful messenger, carries out the charge of a fervent mind and, flying, ascends into heaven, where flesh does not reach. For prayer is sustained by two wings, so that it may fly up into heaven: one of these is called fasting, the other is named almsgiving. Hence Saint John Chrysostom says: “Nothing is more powerful than a person who prays, who prays with fasting; for he has double wings, and neither wanders nor grows sluggish.”
