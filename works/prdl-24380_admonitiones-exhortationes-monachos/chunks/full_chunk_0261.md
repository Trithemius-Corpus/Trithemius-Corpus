Nor undeservedly. For indeed, by what presumption are you led, O Polylogue the Visitor, to try to reform others, when you still lie in your own deformity? You are polluted with the filth of vices, and will you be able to cleanse others? Believe me, the Holy Spirit will not give power to your words, when by a contrary life you offend against his grace. A sermon lacks efficacy in the heart of the hearer when the preacher’s conduct reproves it.

What shall I say? I lie if the stubborn man does not often rebuke the stubborn, the foul man defame the foul; and he thinks that he has escaped being conscious of himself, as though conscience were not enough. There are, I say, among them those who, vicious in life, accuse others: in public accusers, in secret guilty parties, at once judges against themselves and offenders. Outwardly they condemn what inwardly they do; they willingly admit what, when they have observed it, they charge as a crime. A boldness wholly suited to the abusive, and an impudence fitting for the impudent.

Often the miser rebukes the covetous man; the lustful man, the unchaste; the proud man, the arrogant; the criminal, the foul. What, Fathers, what do you think? Can deceivers of this sort be found among the visitors themselves? I do not dare to say it; yet I experience it. Many say many things; few show by deeds what they speak. They cannot reform themselves and their own, and they strive to compel others to observance. They say great things, they do the least things: foolish presumption, which, neglecting its own affairs, is carried off to those of others. He who is not sufficient for himself, for whom will he abound? He who cannot bring himself and his own to amendment of life, when will he be able to amend others?

I have said these things against those who are such, who condemn in others what they scorn to amend in themselves. For the rest, I would not wish anything to have been said as an injury to zealous, good, and suitable visitors, of whom I have known several. But you, most excellent Fathers, who have to choose visitors, take care to appoint the better men to that office, if you desire to bring monks to regular observance. For the highest point of regular discipline lies with the visitors: when continued it grows, when neglected it fails.

Indeed, if the visitors diligently fulfill their office, they will preserve reformed monasteries in regular observance, and will easily restore deformed ones to a better state. But if they are negligent, they quickly destroy even those things which have been well begun by others. For nothing so confounds unity; nothing so disturbs peace; nothing so harms charity as the ignorance of visitors, which grows hot not with reason but with undiscerning will. Experience has very often taught us how great disturbances, dissensions, angers, quarrels, slanders, and hatreds unskilled visitors have stirred up in congregations of monks, visitors for whom everything rests on command, and nothing on knowledge and charity. But I have said enough about this, lest I seem to accuse those whom, because of the dignity of their office, we are commanded to treat with honor.
