You believe yourselves to be perfect and holy cultivators of great religion and inward devotion, yet you do not regard fraternal charity or true humility as necessary in yourselves. You seek sensible devotion in prayers, and with tearful eyes you already think yourselves friends of God. But what then? Do tears in prayers make a man holy, when he prays with a certain exaltation of mind and without true humility? God gives tears; the soul, struck by adversities, gives them; the devil too, deceiver of the proud, gives them. God makes the mind humbled in true charity fruitful, like a meadow, with sweet tears; nature moves the weeping person in adversity; the devil pours streams of water upon the proud under the likeness of devotion.

Humility is the one virtue which, in the breadth of charity, makes the hearts of men truly devout. Whatever you may do in your singularity profits you nothing, O monk, if you do not have true humility with the fervor of twofold charity. For to rise before others for the night praises, to say long prayers in private beyond the custom of the order, to continue a fast beyond what the rule has established while others lawfully eat, to remain in secret while the rest go out to the common labor: two things make these meritorious, and if they are lacking, all such acts will be wholly fruitless. Indeed, whatever works of a monk charity and humble obedience have not commended will be judged empty of merit before God and will lack reward.

But you who with haughty brow despise the precepts of your elders, who do not do what they command but what pleases you by your own rashness, who prefer your own inventions to the holy common exercises of the order, are still far removed from the way of life of the saints. It is not lawful for you by your own rashness to abandon the things that belong to monastic institution, and to put new and vain things that please you before necessary things. Whatever work you do is displeasing to God if you despise that to which you are bound. The inspiration of divine grace teaches humility, persuades obedience, and follows true charity in all things; it keeps the commandment, admits no pride, despises no one, but pursues the perpetual integrity of regular discipline.

But the prompting of the devil, under the appearance of good, cunningly trips up the unwary monk and makes him subject in all things to his own judgment. For everything that pertains to preserving monastic integrity he falsely claims has been badly instituted, is contrary to him, and for that reason must be set after his own pursuits. But so that you may learn wisely to distinguish the grace of God from the promptings of the ancient enemy, mark what we say. First, then, whoever thinks himself inspired by the grace of God should carefully observe whether day by day he becomes purer in conscience as the tinder of sin decreases.
