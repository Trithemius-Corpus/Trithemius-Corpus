A monk, however, who is commanded to imitate Christ and renounces the world on this condition, that he may live in greater purity, must beware of everything that overturns a manly state of mind; and, out of desire for the heavenly homeland, he must despise all the vain joys of this world and preserve himself always unconquered in every maturity of mind and body.

And just as immoderate laughter is forbidden to monks at every time and place, so too speaking words that move the hearers to laughter is prohibited. Here now, most dear brothers, pay attention to yourselves, and carefully consider how you ought to restrain your tongues. Often recall to memory what the prophet says in the psalm: “I said, I will guard my ways, that I may not sin with my tongue. I set a guard over my mouth; I was mute and humbled, and kept silence even from good things.”

Think of yourselves in every place as standing before the Lord God, who is present everywhere, who hears all things, sees all things, knows all things, understands all things, from whose knowledge nothing is hidden, and to whose strict examination all things are reserved. Live in his sight in such a way that you may be able to come securely to his judgment.

Whenever, by permission of us or of the prior, you come together for conversation, you must with the greatest care avoid scurrilous, idle, and laughter-provoking words, according to the command of our Lawgiver. Let your speech be full of modesty, not loud, not frivolous, not rash, not contentious, lest it become liable to an exact accounting. Likewise flee much speaking, because it is not without sin and makes a monk burdensome to all people.

Let the elders speak not just anything, but words of saving doctrine, by which they may instruct the minds of the younger men in the path of righteousness and kindle them more fully to the love of God. For, as our holy Lawgiver says, speaking and teaching belong to the master; keeping silence and listening belong to the disciple. Therefore let the elders speak useful things and teach; but let the younger men listen in silence and learn. And if anything must be asked of the prior, let it be asked with all humility, reverence, and subjection, lest anyone seem to speak more than is fitting.

A man is known by his words, and he who multiplies utterances in his mouth is foolish in heart. A man’s speech is the witness of his conscience; and he who loves modesty and innocence of heart must have modest speech, without vanity. For you are ministers of the living God, my brothers. Let no vain, useless, or idle word come from your mouth, but only what is good for the building up of the hearers, so that it may increase charity in their minds and enlighten the heart unto the knowledge and love of the highest truth.

Precious is the tongue of the monk that speaks nothing at all except what is holy; and worthy of blessing is the mouth from which nothing is brought forth except what is divine. Let each one keep his heart clean from every wicked thought, because he who is good in heart does not speak evil.
