Do not leave the monastery anywhere without the abbot’s blessing, because, since you ought to be dead and buried to this world, nothing further will be lawful for you without the command of your superior. Know yourself a soldier of King Jesus Christ; you must follow your commander, without whom there is no salvation. He, fastened to the cross, is taken down not by his own hands but by the hands of others; and you, if you desire to be saved, must have your will governed by the abbot’s command.

For a monk who goes out anywhere from the monastery without the abbot’s permission is like a little sheep in the darkness, when the house is consumed by fire. But the true monk of Christ, even when he is outside the monastery in body, remains in the cloister in mind; and he is no less alone among many than he is accustomed to be solitary in his cell. The lover of Christ thinks of Jesus even in the midst of a crowd; and he who is a true worker of virtues keeps his heart firm in the fear of God in every place. For even if he is not in the monastery in body, yet in heart, because of the vigor of his spirit, he always stands firm, as though alone in the cloister. The spirit is not polluted by the movement of the body when the solitude of the mind is preserved.

You must live with the same holiness on the road, O monk, with which you are commanded to conduct yourself in the monastery, and you must walk with a certain greater caution, lest you scandalize anyone by a more lax example, where there seem to be as many judges of monks as there are observers present. The eyes of all secular people look up to monks, and they seek in their works the holiness which they hear them profess. A monk gives more offense by one reckless word than a man given to the world does by ten thousand. For however frivolous and buffoonish a worldly man may seem, still he has not learned to tolerate calmly the insolence of monks, because with rustic simplicity he believes all cloistered men to be not what they are, but what they ought to be. Hence an evil word or deed is ascribed by them to a monk as condemnation, while in a worldly man it is counted as pardonable.

And not without reason. For the monk who has professed the holy and approved rule must hold all his effort directed toward perfection, and must far surpass the conduct of secular men, in word and deed, in all purity of mind and body. For a light or idle word, which is granted pardon among secular people, is judged ruinous among cloistered men.

Gold is more precious than all metals, and is more desired by mortals, and in nothing is a stain or corruption of falseness more quickly charged. And the defect which is discerned in gold with judicial severity is overlooked in the other metals because of the baseness of their nature. Gold gladdens the eyes by its beauty, and strengthens the inner parts of life by its virtue. Gold melted in the fire is not diminished; and the true monk of God is not changed from his purpose of holiness in the tribulation of persecution. A small stain on a golden vessel greatly displeases in the sight of a powerful man; if an earthen vessel is crooked, no one detests it. Against a monk who sins in small matters, the great outcry of many against him is immense.
