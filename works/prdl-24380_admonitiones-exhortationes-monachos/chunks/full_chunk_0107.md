For he is shown to be but little religious as a monk at other times, who on these holy days is not found more religious, more fervent in the love of God, and more devout.

Hence the holy Pope Gregory says in a certain homily: “Lent must be observed with the utmost care, so that during it the fast, apart from Sundays, which are withdrawn from abstinence, is in no way broken, unless illness prevents someone; for these days are the tithe of the year, which we are commanded to offer to the Lord.” Saint Ambrose likewise says: “He keeps Lent who by fasting and watching ascends to Easter. For just as at the remaining time of the year fasting is a reward, so in Lent not to fast is a sin. Those fasts are voluntary, but these are necessary; those come from choice, these from law. To those we are invited; to these we are compelled. And therefore whoever passes beyond the appointed number by eating on one day is accused not as a violator of one day, but is charged as a transgressor of the whole of Lent.”

In these sacred days all desires of the flesh must be restrained; the body must be chastised through abstinence, so that if perhaps at other times we have lived less temperately, we may in some way make amends for it by this Lenten fast. Therefore, dearest brothers, let us follow the admonitions of our most holy father Benedict, and with as much diligence as we can let us exercise ourselves in holy works, withholding something each day from our body, for the love of God, in food and drink, so that we may prove ourselves true servants of God.

Then our holy founder adds, saying: “Yet this very thing which each one offers he should suggest to his abbot, and it should be done with his prayer and consent, because what is done without the permission of the spiritual father will be reckoned to presumption and vainglory, not to reward. Therefore all things must be done with the abbot’s consent.”

The words of our holy lawgiver are clear, brothers, so that no one can have recourse to the veil of ignorance. For if all things must be done with the abbot’s consent, then without his permission absolutely nothing is to be done. Indeed, the more some good work is adorned by many virtues, the more it must not be doubted that it is of greater merit before God. Therefore, when a monk, by the leave and blessing of his abbot, withholds from his body some food or drink for the love of God, he will receive from the Lord not only the promised reward of eternal fullness for that abstinence itself, but also the kingdom for humility, and for the merit of holy obedience he will obtain without end the fellowship of all the saints in heaven.

For the monk who, once professed, has submitted his head to monastic institutions has resigned his will to Christ in the hands of the abbot; and if afterwards he again usurps it for his own judgment, he has now shamefully [unclear] Christ.
