If we look to divine worship, they perform it so confusedly, so disorderly, so laxly, that neither do the words convey sense, nor does the melody sound the proper tone. For they are devoid of all instruction in the good arts, so that they understand absolutely nothing of what they sing. Hence it comes about that, without feeling, without devotion, without any savor of inward sweetness, they do not so much discharge the canonical hours as confound them. In their hands the book of divine Scripture is never seen; no practice of edifying discourse is ever taken up; no account is ever taken of the formation of morals. The whole day is consumed in obscene gossip; all time is enslaved to games and feasting. There is never any mention of reform. The madness of men at odds with one another rages mutually, and among the cowls, peace broken, the monastery, with law despised, [unclear] insane with lawsuits. Spear there, and sword, and bow are at hand, so that you would think what you see is not a monastery but a stronghold of men at war. As witnesses of their private ownership, contrary to the statutes, contrary to the laws, contrary to the rule, they each inhabit separate houses, in which they pursue not the studies of monks but those of clerics. They neither fear nor love God; they think nothing of the life to come, since they put the concerns of the soul after the pleasures of the flesh. They do not read the rule of monks; they do not heed the statutes; they despise the decrees of the Fathers. They scorn the vow of poverty, know nothing of chastity, and hold obedience cheap. And would that, since they refuse to be chaste, they would at least deign to be continent. The filth of vices smokes all around, and what everyone knows, we alone, who are ordered to amend it, do not know. Why should I say much? So many, so great, so varied are the unlawful things they commit there against the Rule of Saint Benedict, that I would more readily think a monk could be formed anew than that the life of these men could be amended. For nothing of monastic honor remains inviolate among them; rather everything lies neglected, spurned, despised, and torn apart. Anger, quarrels, dissensions, envy, detractions, curses, oaths, insults, and things like these are not judged among them to be sins, because what is public has begun to be lawful. A place lies open for vices and crimes; virtue alone does not deserve entry. They laugh if anyone begins to speak before them about penance, and, as Mantuan says, they despise the gods above and Acheron. There is no fear of punishment, no dread of the Judge. Among them you would see, as in a spring of all vices, pride, greed, lust, gluttony, sloth, anger, hatred, and every pleasure of the flesh abounding; if they can grasp it, they rejoice; if they cannot, still they eagerly desire it. The beasts have rotted in their own dung, and, as if reason had been lost, they do not wish to be recalled to penance.
