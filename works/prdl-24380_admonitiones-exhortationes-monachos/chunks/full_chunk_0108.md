Let the monk, therefore, be always humbly subject to his abbot in all things for the love of Christ; let him presume to do nothing at all against his command, and let him omit nothing, or neglect nothing, that has been enjoined upon him by him. Let him take the greatest care lest by restless conduct he either grieve or disturb his pastor; let him not murmur against him, nor disparage him, nor say any evil whatever against him. Rather, let him revere him with the sincere affection of charity as the vicar of Christ, and not grieve his spirit by troublesome or disorderly words or deeds. Let him also assist him, as far as he can, in his labors, and intercede for him continually before God with prayers. Monks sin greatly against God when they are not afraid to grieve their abbot by their troublesome disparagements and corrupt ways.

Therefore, whatever monk desires to be good, whatever monk truly wishes to be devout in the Lord, let him always and everywhere venerate his abbot with pure love as the vicar of Jesus Christ in the monastery; let him humbly obey his commands in all things, and in no way depart from his direction.

The abbot, for his part, should strive to show himself in all things a worthy shepherd to his monks, one who feeds the sheep of Christ entrusted to him both by word and by example, in all gentleness of charity. Let him not trouble the brothers without just and reasonable necessity. Let him not be a despiser of the holy community. Let him recognize himself as steward of the monastery’s goods, not lord. Let him not be a mocker of his subjects, not turbulent, not troublesome, not a tyrant, but their sincere lover: gentle in speech, mild and upright in conduct, holy in his way of life, clean in conscience, always devout in mind toward God.

Let him at all times bear solicitous care for the souls entrusted to him, and neglect nothing that seems able to conduce to their salvation. Let him not on that account exalt himself on high because he has been set as ruler of others, but let him remember that he must fear so much the more, as he is most certainly bound to render an account for all in the dread judgment of God. Let him correct the proud, rebellious, and contumacious as a lord; but let him kindly cherish the humble, obedient, and tractable as a most gentle father, and wisely distribute to each his due without respect of persons. Let him always consider himself the mother and nurse of the monks; and therefore he ought not to embitter with troublesome words and harsh invectives those whom he can bring back to better things by gentleness.

Let him always bear solicitous care for the sick, and make provision for all in such a way that they have no just cause for murmuring. In his own weaknesses let him learn how he ought to have compassion on his subjects. For he cannot be a good shepherd of souls who disdains from the heart to suffer with the needs of his sickly subjects.
