Therefore the monk who desires to serve Christ in true purity of mind should always be occupied in some spiritual exercise, lest idleness give birth to vice and harmful thoughts find a place where they may gain strength. Just as rust consumes iron, and a garment is eaten away by worms, so idleness weakens the mind; and the virtue of the soul, long left unused, gradually fails and is utterly enfeebled. Flee idleness, then, and overcome acedia.

But since we cannot remain firmly and without any change of mind in one state of divine love, it is necessary for us, lest we fall away entirely, to renew our holy purpose every day. Thus, whenever through the negligence of human weakness we begin, however slightly, to grow sluggish from spiritual fervor, we should examine ourselves, and by the remembrance of things to come and by contemplation of the Lord’s Passion, immediately recall our conscience to its former purpose of making progress.

Above all, however, we must beware of putting off this holy effort to make progress until tomorrow, because it has often been harmful to postpone to a future time a good that could have been done sooner. Hence the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “Do not delay turning to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For his wrath will come suddenly, and in the time of vengeance he will destroy you.” Augustine also says: “Why do you put off the good until tomorrow, saying, ‘There will still be a long life’? For who knows whether it will be long? And if it is not good, what does it profit that it will be long?”

Without a good life, the conduct of a monk should be called death rather than life. Therefore the remedies of an amended life must not be delayed by any hesitation, lest the time for conversion perish through tardiness, when no further delay for repentance is granted. For he who promised forgiveness to the penitent did not promise tomorrow. No one who fears God delays turning to repentance, nor postpones today’s salvation until tomorrow. Every person who knows that he is set in tepidity of mind, and who puts off the amendment of life from day to day, is deceived by the hope of a longer age; for if he knew that he would die tomorrow, he would undoubtedly change his purpose today. The hope of a longer life has miserably deceived many, who, while they put off the amendment of their conduct each day, suddenly fall into the depth of death.

Who among you, brothers, does not resolve in his heart to take up the purpose of an amended life before he dies? For I believe that each of you still intends to exercise greater devotion toward God before the danger of death comes upon him. And why do you wait so long? Why do you put it off from day to day? Behold, you who now live in health, and do not give yourselves to the spiritual exercises of divine love when you can, while the Lord patiently waits: the final day will come for each one of you, and it is already hastening, when, as the angel of death presses hard, the power whose effect you have put off for so long will be taken away from you.

Therefore again and again I ask you, brothers, and with all the earnestness I can I admonish you: turn to the Lord our God with your whole heart while you live healthy and unharmed, and do not further delay your will, lest you lose even the power granted to you. Think, and often think again, that we are not citizens of this world, but strangers and pilgrims; we do not have here a lasting city, but we seek the one to come; and every day we are hastening toward death, though we do not know it.
