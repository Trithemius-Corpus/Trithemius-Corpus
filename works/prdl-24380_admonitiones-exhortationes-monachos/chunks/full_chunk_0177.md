Their righteous deeds will never be delivered to oblivion: they had compassion on the ruin of the order, put their shoulder under the burden, and, with the Lord granting his aid, restored it in many places. Indeed, many different particular reforms in the order flourished many times at various periods; but there was never any general one that brought the whole order back to the integrity of regular observance. Finally, we see that the particular restorations, which we have said were many, have almost all collapsed again.

Most recently, however, in the year of the Christians 1420, when the general council was being held at Constance, a certain new and, up to now, last particular reform took its beginning in Germany. It takes the name by which it is called from a certain monastery in the regions of Saxony, then the poorest of all, which is called Bursfeld. The first author and chief of this most holy reform was John Rode, abbot of the distinguished monastery of St. Matthias near Trier. Having been appointed general visitor of the order by the aforesaid Council of Constance, lest, while preaching to others, he himself should be judged reprobate, he first displayed reform in himself. For he reformed both himself first of all and the monks of the said monastery; he issued constitutions in conformity with the Rule, and obtained confirmation of the holy work from the apostolic see.

Among all the monasteries of the order, the humble and rather poor monastery of Bursfeld first received this reform, which afterward it extended to Reinhausen and to a few monasteries of Saxony near it. At length, when John Rode, abbot of St. Matthias and chief of our reform, had died, and no suitable successor for extending the undertaking that had been begun could be found in that place, John of Hagem, abbot of Bursfeld, who had already reformed certain monasteries, a holy man and very zealous for the order, was elected at the urging of Jacob, archbishop of Trier. Content with his poverty, he did not agree to leave Bursfeld, lest he should seem to have preferred riches to poverty. He therefore remained in his own monastery and was appointed over others in the place of the first reformer. Since he seemed comparable to his predecessor neither in learning nor in zeal, he was able to accomplish little for the continuation of the undertaking begun, nor could he enlarge the reform among many.

Meanwhile it happened that Nicholas of Cusa, priest-cardinal of the holy Roman Church and legate of the apostolic see, came into Germany. Since he burned with great zeal for our order, he established and granted many things for its reform. Hearing of the good reputation of the aforesaid abbot at Bursfeld and of his most fervent purpose for regular discipline, he summoned him to himself, appointed him general reformer of the order, and through his industry reformed several monasteries.
