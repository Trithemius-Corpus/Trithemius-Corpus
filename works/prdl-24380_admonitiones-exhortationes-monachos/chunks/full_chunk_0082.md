There are two things, my most beloved brothers, which this place requires of you: one, that in correction you always maintain humble patience; the other, that you willingly receive our exhortation, such as it is. Desiring you to be ready for the first, our holy lawgivers commanded that the brave deeds of the martyrs, whose imitators you ought to become, be read; and then, so that you may be teachable in spirit, they ordered one passage from the Rule to be added. Therefore, so that you may be strong by imitating the holy martyrs, and patient under necessary rebukes, the account of their passion is read here in your ears; and so that you may prepare your minds for saving doctrine, one reading is recited to you from the Rule which you profess. The holy martyrs of Christ, by their example, exhort you to patience; and blessed Benedict admonishes you by his teaching to be ready even for injuries.

Therefore let none of you, most beloved brothers, refuse in this place humbly to accept penance daily for his negligences; let no one disdain it. This place is the public expiation of sin, in which the severity of judgment is so tempered that the kindness of discretion is not violated. For I desire to be the physician of your souls, not their destroyer. Therefore come without fear to the examination of our judgment, and humbly accuse yourselves openly of the negligences you have committed. I do not wish to burden you beyond the Rule, so as to transfer the most secret offenses of conscience from sacramental confession to the public examination of faults, as we know certain men have done to the grave harm of the souls of their subjects. For thus says our lawgiver Benedict in the Rule: “But if the cause of the soul’s sin is hidden, let him reveal it only to the abbot or to spiritual elders, who know how to heal their own wounds and those of others, not to expose or make them public.”

There are two places in the monastery for the expiation of sin: one for those sins that are manifest, and the other for those that are hidden. Manifest sins are to be expiated publicly before all in the daily proclamation of faults; hidden sins, however, and those whose disclosure would scandalize the weak, are to be referred to confession. For the one whose sin is manifest must also be punished publicly; and the one who has sinned in secret must also be corrected in secret. There are certain rulers of cloistered men who do not fear to bring the hidden sins of their subjects to public examination; in my judgment, they do not carry out the rights of their office with sufficient prudence, since they close off the way of salvation to their subjects and constrain the freest confession under the bond of ill will. How dangerous this is, no one of even moderate wisdom fails to understand. For the holy founder of monks rebukes this rashness of a shepherd when he says: “Let the abbot show the greatest concern, and with all shrewdness and diligence take care not to lose any of the sheep entrusted to him.
