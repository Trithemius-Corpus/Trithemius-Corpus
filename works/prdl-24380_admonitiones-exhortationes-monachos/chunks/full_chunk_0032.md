Homily V.

Rarely does a monk rise again who has once fallen. And alas, how many faint-hearted, lax, and idle cloisterers are found today, who frequently collapse under the burden of spiritual warfare and rarely rise again to the true fervor of the spirit. Their condition is indeed pitiable: they have a name that they are alive, and they are dead. Neither the memory of death has been able to lead them to true compunction of heart, nor has the recollection of hell, set before them, been able to soften them.

Monks of this sort, after they have long remained thus sluggish in accidia, are either oppressed by evil habit and collapse, or in a certain way become as though in hell, so that they never fully breathe again into the light of the Lord’s mercy, nor reach the freedom of the spirit, which alone is nearest to the kingdom of heaven, and makes the Lord’s yoke sweet and his burden light. These evils befall them because their will has not yet been cleansed from carnal passions, nor do they will the good as they know it, since they are enticed and drawn away by their own concupiscence. For they love the passing consolations of the flesh; and even if at times they interrupt them, they never wholly break them off. Hence it is that they very rarely direct their affections toward God; and if they are ever moved to compunction, they do not persevere long, even for an hour, but are soon wearied in soul by sluggishness and inertia, and suddenly grow disgusted, through sadness and melancholy, with the good they had begun.

But a mind tossed by these storms cannot be filled with the fervor of divine love; for as flesh placed by the fire and then repeatedly removed is never well cooked, so a soul that often relapses into accidia is by no means truly illumined by the divine Spirit. For Scripture says to the Lord: “Those who go far from you shall perish, and you have destroyed all who work iniquity.” For the farther the mind has withdrawn through accidia from the fervor of divine love, the nearer it is to hell and despair, and the more rarely and with greater difficulty does it grow strong again for true repentance.

Therefore, whoever among you, my brothers, desires to flee from the midst of Babylon, whoever longs to come to the inward fervor of the spirit, should strive always with the utmost effort to drive away from himself torpor of mind and accidia, and daily to renew the holy purpose of divine love. It is very hard, I admit, to resist the perverse habit of the flesh, and in an old heart, against custom, to think new thoughts. Certainly, brothers, no one with a lukewarm will can rise from the depths of hell, nor will anyone be able to overcome an evil habit without great violence and the daily practice of its contrary. For even if no one becomes holy by his own strength, but it is God who works all things in all, nevertheless it is plainly certain to us that the kingdom of God suffers violence, and he who neglects to do violence to his own carnal impulses does not reach the fellowship of the Lord’s saints.

Hear now, monk, whoever you are who are lukewarm toward the love of God and given to accidia; hear, I say, and tremble, because unless you immediately rise from the evil habit of a sluggish life, unless you do violence to yourself by the practice of the contrary habit, unless you [unclear] your heart to the fervor of divine love.
