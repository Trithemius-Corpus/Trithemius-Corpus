Concerning monks sent on the road.

Let the word be restrained in the mouth until reason brings forth a judgment of equity, and let not speech that can instruct the hearers be hidden. Do not trust the songs of flatterers, for when they have shot down an innocent man, they soothe the ears with disordered words. A monk who feeds on quarrels is like a slave, and like a swaggering man who stirs up disputes under the name of religion.

In the presence of learned men do not be eager to speak, lest perhaps, when you do not understand the parable, you be laughed at by those standing by. He who recognizes himself as a monk does not use profound speech; and he who teaches little ones makes the question plain. When you go to men unlike you in your way of life, do not boast in anything; but if you are asked, answer humbly in few words.

When you are set upon a journey, always think of God, and consider the reverence owed to your elder as accompanying you. Do absolutely nothing which you would not wish to be made known to him, since, as the holy Hierodulus says, he is believed to act in Christ’s place in the monastery, and therefore is deservedly honored by all. Live, then, as though you always had him present before your eyes; and what you would not presume to do or say while he looked on, do not in any way attempt while he is absent. For it is foolish consolation, when conscience is gnawed by guilt, for a man’s sin to lie hidden and for one who knows himself sick unto death to flee the judgment of his shepherd.

The monk who does evil which he wishes to hide from his prelate has violated the integrity of the monastic rule and has grievously sinned against the fifth degree of humility. For it commands thus: “The fifth degree of humility is this: if, by humble confession, he does not conceal from his abbot all the evil thoughts that come into his heart, and the evil things done by him in secret.” These are the admonitions of Father Benedict; these are the holy teachings of the monastic institution. If you, monk, despise or neglect them, you are proved forgetful of your own salvation.

It is a sure sign of humility if a monk does not refuse to open his heart to his elder, and does not wish any of the evils he has committed to be hidden from his shepherd. He who confesses his fault with humility will be strengthened against the adversary of his salvation; and he who conceals his thoughts will advance in evil. For there is no thought of evils so small that it does not gradually grow if neglected, and no vice so slight that it does not gain strength if it is dissembled.

Therefore evil thoughts must be driven back at once from the beginning, because when a depraved thought is not immediately extinguished, it gives birth to delight; delight to consent; and consent hurls one into action. Thereafter the action of evil, repeated often, begets habit; habit, necessity; necessity brings in despair; despair, contempt; contempt, obstinacy; and that gives birth to the eternal death of the soul. For Scripture says: “When the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.”

Is not your abbot, O monk, the shepherd and physician of your soul? Am I not, my dearest brothers, after God, the one who feeds your mortal bodies with necessary food? Am I not compelled to minister the word of salvation to you? If you, the sheep, flee the shepherd, what pastures of the spirit do you expect from us? The sick man who flees the physician despises his own salvation and willingly kills his soul. Therefore the monk who walks in the way of malice and turns aside from the paths of justice is near to death, and he slips so much more deeply into the abyss of vices the less he reveres the judgment of correction. But he is truly a monk who guards his soul from all iniquity and does nothing at all which he would wish his shepherd not to know.

He who hides his way is not pure; and he who flees the presence of the judge increases suspicion. Like a sheep wandering in the wilderness and seized by the wolf is the negligent and impure monk who withdraws himself from the knowledge of his shepherd and is devoured by demons. A diseased sheep running to destruction, and a monk hiding every sin, have no salvation unless they return to the physician.

Monks returning from the road, on that very day, according to the monastic rule, are to be prostrated before the eyes of all the brothers, so that whatever has been committed through negligence on the road may be expiated by the common prayers. For the common prayer of many greatly avails to wash away sins, if it has been poured out to the Lord with the fervor of devotion.
