It is good for me to rest under the rule of most pious Father Benedict; if in it I have done any wrong through frailty, I trust that I have him as a pious advocate before the strict judge, whose order I love, venerate, and keep with whatever diligence I can. But what patron you will have, I do not know, you who cast off the order, offend all the saints, and publicly lie to God.

For whoever, after profession of the order, lives otherwise than he promised, the Rule shows that he will be condemned as a mocker of almighty God. For it says: “Therefore, if he has acted otherwise, let him know that he will be condemned by him whom he mocks.” Hear this, sons of Belial; take heed, deserters of the order, and, if there is any feeling in you, shudder: “let him know,” it says, “that the perverse monk will be condemned by him whom he mocks.” What greater mockery, what crime is more wicked, than to deny the faith promised to God, to set aside the vow, to lie to him, to withdraw service from him, to tear away from him what is his?

I wonder, and indeed greatly, whether such wicked apostates can ever, even for an hour, have true joy, because of the confusion of conscience and the remorse of synderesis. But, to state plainly what I think, I cannot be induced by any arguments to believe that they will ever afterward live without the gravest torment of their own conscience. For by probable experience we know that the monks of whom we speak, after transfer from the order, become almost mad, prone to every shameful work, desperate, without the charity of God, without devotion, without affection, without piety, without conscience, without honor, fierce, disturbed, senseless, and obstinate, so that they care nothing, notice nothing, and wish to hear nothing about God, his kingdom, purgatory, hell, death, repentance, virtue, or their way of life; and that saying of the wise man is fulfilled in them: “When the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.” And this also: “A savage, troubled conscience always presumes.”

Hence such apostates, as if freed from a daytime prison, boldly cast themselves headlong into every vice, and compensate for the shortness of time by the magnitude of their crimes. They are offended if anyone wishes to speak before them of the love of God, the study of the Scriptures, or the honorableness of life. Nor is it any wonder if they cannot hear the words of God, to whom after such unspeakable apostasy they have become faithless. They hate the one who reproves in the gate; they do not admit words of salvation; they despise the sermon of repentance. Therefore the wise man says: “Consider the works of God, that no one can correct him whom he has despised.” If God could ever despise any man, those are among the first who, after their vow, become faithless to him. Therefore, being despised by God, they rarely flee to repentance. Therefore let them know that they are to be condemned by him whom they mock. For they are guilty of a double sacrilege, because they unjustly take themselves from God and their monasteries from Saint Benedict.

And whoever has supplied occasion, favor, counsel, or help for these dismemberments has done this irreparable loss to the order and to Saint Benedict. For the rule of law says: “He who gives occasion for loss is deemed to have caused the loss.” Therefore let such men see how they are liable to the holy lawgiver of monks and bound to restitution, and how they will answer at the Last Judgment to God and his saints, whose temporal goods they unjustly usurp for themselves or for others. But woe to those who unjustly take what belongs to another, which they can never repay either in this life or in the life to come. And woe to you, apostate and denying monks, who confound right, despise equity, and violate faith and covenant.
