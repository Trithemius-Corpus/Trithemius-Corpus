So too the true monk, a lover of Christ, remains the same when sent onto the road among men as he has learned to live in the secrecy of his cell, because he walks before the Lord pure in mind as well as in body. For if he is a true monk, he is not polluted by the dust of another way of life, nor is he changed from the uprightness of his holy purpose by anyone’s presence or conversation. Amid the disorderly talk of worldly people he thinks of other things, and the dissolute conduct of others does not delight his mind. He who is with himself in heart is not divided even in the midst of those who are making an uproar.

Therefore, monk, you who desire to be good, gather your mind back to yourself, and before you leave your cell, set your heart in order for the journey. Let the fear of the Lord always be your companion on the way, and let the guard of monastic discipline never depart from your side. Fence in your ears with the memory of the last things, lest the hearing of vain speech pollute the integrity of your mind. Lower your eyes humbly to the ground, lest they look upon what it is not fitting for a servant of God to desire. Do not take part in the conversations of carnal men; and if you cannot avoid their presence, take care not to lend them your mind. Even in the midst of the wicked, do not transgress your rule, and do not abandon your customary seriousness. Do not fear the insults and reproaches of [unclear], and let not your heart be troubled by the words of men of darkness. The preservation of monastic discipline is justice; and those who despise the simple persecute the innocent with laughter. Preserve your holy purpose in all things, and the Most High will avenge the injury. Do not say in your heart, “I am suffering violence,” nor be ashamed at the words of those who mock you. It is the glory of a monk to endure insult, and the crown of beauty to rejoice in reproaches.

He who is patient under injuries is stronger than Atlas; and he who is not saddened when mocked is greater than all. If you have fixed your desire in heaven, nothing on earth will disturb you; and if you are a true monk, your heart will exult at injuries. There is a judge in heaven who knows all things, and there is the Most High God who cares for all. He is the just rewarder of merits, and he will remember his poor ones. The more you cast down your soul now in this life, for love of him, into the depth of humility, the more in future glory you will be exalted above the rest. For by patience one ascends into heaven, and by humility one comes to the kingdom. For Christ himself also had to suffer, and so enter into his glory. A delicate soldier is one who, while his emperor is enduring insults and mockeries, seeks pleasures; and while he hangs on the gibbet of the cross for his army, pursues delights. Swift is the destruction of both: the soldier who goes out naked to war without weapons, and the monk who disdains to embrace patience and humility amid insults. The monk who is a sincere lover of Christ is not angered by injuries, against those who inflict them.
