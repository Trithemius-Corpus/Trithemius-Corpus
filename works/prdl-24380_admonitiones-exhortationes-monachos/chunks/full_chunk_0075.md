Homily XII.

On monks sent out on the road. Homily XII.

It belongs to monks to flee the world and the crowds of men, and, out of love for solitude, to avoid them at all times as far as they are able. A monk is so called from solitude, just as one is named from unity. He cannot be a monk who follows after throngs of people; and he who takes delight in cities has not yet tasted monastic sweetness. If you are a monk, you ought not to leave the solitude of the monastery without reasonable cause; and if you are bound to silence, do not take pleasure in the conversations of worldly people.

Whoever loves wandering about in the city has abandoned his conscience; and he who is often outside his monastery does not preserve the purity of inward progress. He who delights in going around the homes of his relatives and in the banquets of his neighbors does not regard himself as a monk; and he who goes to the feasts of drinkers kills his own soul. He who sleeps in the house of his kinsman is seeking comforts; and he who avoids the assemblies of the religious follows a destructive freedom.

A monk in the house of his father and mother is like a lamb in the midst of wolves, and like a hare surrounded by hunters’ dogs. If you are a monk, if you are God’s servant and soldier, what are you doing in your parents’ house? Or what are you seeking in the midst of the people? A monk who remains long outside the monastery among secular people is like a man who has buried his only-begotten son beneath deep waters. For just as fish have no life on dry land, so monks have no salvation of soul in the world. Outside the river a fish dies, and the purity of a monk’s conscience is quickly violated outside the monastery.

The life of monks is in the monastery, and the death of souls is in the midst of secular business. It is a great virtue of the soul to flee the crowds of men, or, if it is wholly necessary to be among them, nevertheless to withdraw from them in mind. We are sometimes compelled by necessity to make a journey through the world; but the purity of conscience is polluted so much less by encounters with men, the less the desire of the mind is drawn away from the secrecy of the cell.

Therefore every good monk must be admonished by us that, whenever by command of his superior he is sent on the road for the necessity of some matter, he should never wander outward in spirit. He who does not guard his senses is quickly corrupted when evil is set before him; and he who casts away the fear of the Lord will necessarily return impure to the monastery. When the Rhine overflows, by its muddy waters it stains the beauty of the meadows lying open all around; and when a monk gives his attention to the tales of worldly people, he disturbs the purity of his conscience.

Before, then, you set your foot out of the monastery, arm yourself with holy prayers; and before you go forth, do not fail to commend your way to the Lord with prayers. Then carry out what the lawgiver commanded, and entrust yourself to be protected by the prayers both of the abbot and of all the brothers. Prescribe for yourself a rule of severity in regular observance, under the steady remembrance of which you may meet every flowing-away of the mind [unclear].
