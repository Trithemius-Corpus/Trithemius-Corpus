We see many monasteries founded under the Rule of Saint Benedict in which, apart from the name and the habit, we see no trace of regular discipline. Over this, O fathers, take up a lament; bewail the ruin of our most sacred order, if perhaps God may turn to pardon and leave a blessing behind him. And you transgressors, who are the cause of these tears, return, even late at last, to your heart; return, and fear the indignation of Benedict, your lawgiver. Consider the desolation of the order which you are bringing in to your own harm; fear the wrath of the Almighty, which you provoke by your crimes.

Woe to us: the city of our religion is being destroyed by enemies all around us; the houses of discipline are being reduced to nothing, and no one is found to resist; no one to set himself as a wall before the house of God. We all seek what is our own; we all ignore the common need of the order, until little by little we too fail. Our inheritance has been turned over to strangers, our houses to outsiders. Monasteries consecrated as dwellings for monks are now becoming stables for clerics and fortresses for fighting soldiers.

O what shall I say? I am ashamed to utter what men without conscience are not ashamed to perpetrate in the cloisters of monks. They disturb the solitude of religious men; they change houses of prayer into dwellings of merchants. I suffer with your calamities, O venerable religion of Benedict; I groan that you are exposed to plunder by every passerby. There is none to console you among all your dear ones, because those who could help you will not, and those who wish to come to your aid cannot.

O with what beautiful grace you once flourished in the Church of God, when you bore men distinguished in every holiness and learning, and, like one young, strong, and steadfast, you used to drive away from yourself every dishonorable way of life. But now, as if worn out by old age and the long oppression of days, you have failed, and the strength of your youth has been taken away, because your might is no longer in you. Where now is your zeal? Where is the beauty of your face? Where is your honor? Where is the power once widely spread throughout the whole world? Where is that zeal for letters, venerable to God and the angels, whose fervor once made you exalted above all orders? Where are your sons around your table, learned and most holy men? All have failed, and you have been brought almost to nothing. You have lost the beauty of your youth, and the strength of your adolescence has been scattered.

To whom shall I compare you, or to whom shall I liken you, O separated daughter? To whom shall I make you equal, and how shall I comfort you, mother of monks? Great as the sea is your ruin. Who will heal you? O if you had guarded against this ruin which you now experience, how blessed all nations would call you. But you said in your abundance, “I shall never be moved.” Plenty has made you poor, and the abundance of temporal things has stripped you of all your adornment. You have borne insolent sons, disobedient, rebellious against holy institutions; hiding beneath the cloak of your honor, they have stripped you of all your beauty. Alas.
