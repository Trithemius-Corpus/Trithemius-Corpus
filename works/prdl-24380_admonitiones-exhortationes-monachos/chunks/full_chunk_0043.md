The art recently invented at Mainz in our own days brings forth many volumes daily into the light; but it is by no means possible for us to purchase them all, since we are still weighed down by the greatest poverty. Therefore I warn and exhort you, who do not very readily turn to external labors of the hands, to labor as diligently as you can, for the honor of God, in writing books; for just as idleness is the enemy of the soul, so moderate labor is the preserver of the spiritual life.

For the monk, because he is a soldier of the eternal King, must never be idle. Idleness indeed gives birth to sloth, and sloth brings upon the soul the want of deadly destruction. But the labors of monks must be so moderated that they are never empty of divine converse, but that amid the works of the hands meditation on the Scriptures may burn in the heart; and after it has led the mind into the ardor of the spirit, it should at once be changed into most devout prayer.

Let the monk, therefore, be a soldier of Christ, like a clean animal of God, which chews the cud, always having in the mouth of his heart the food of the holy Scriptures, lest his mind lie open to vain and harmful thoughts, or a foul or idle word burst from the mouth of the speaker. Let him always remember, while he works, the saying of Scripture: Blessed is the man who meditates on the law of the Lord day and night.

For everyone who truly loves the Lord Jesus daily turns over in his heart, with tears and prayers, both his teaching and his passion. For he is not a true lover of God who forgets his commandments even for a moment. Indeed, as Scripture says, those who love the Lord seek the things that are pleasing to him, and those who fear his holy name will be filled with his law. Hence one of the saints said to the Lord: I have hidden your words in my heart, that I may not sin against you.

The soul, therefore, must always be stirred up and daily renewed by spiritual goads, lest it grow dull through laziness or languish in sluggish idleness. The exercise of monastic discipline in labor, the pursuit of reading and prayer, and the careful keeping of vigils are true incentives to virtue. Nothing, however, is more destructive to the holy purpose of a monk than idleness, since it both stirs the flesh to lust, weakens every virtue of the mind, and extinguishes the fervor of the spirit. For idleness in the cloister is the tinder and nourishment of all evils.

Wherever, then, you may be, whether in the field or in the cloister, always flee idleness as the most destructive plague of the soul, adorning and exercising your mind either with prayer or with meditation on the Scriptures. Gladly keep company with good and zealous lovers of God; but avoid the negligent and those who despise their own salvation. For he who touches pitch will be defiled by it; and he who makes himself the companion of sinners will not be free from guilt.

Consider your words carefully, so that you do not disparage anyone at all, nor listen to another who disparages someone; for the slanderer and the willing listener alike are servants of the devil, the one by speaking, the other by listening. Labor like a good soldier of Christ Jesus, and diligently do with your hands whatever...
