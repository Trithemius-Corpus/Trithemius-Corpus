Homily XIII.

With the heart one believes unto righteousness, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation. Although this saying of the lover of Christ is to be understood chiefly of faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, nevertheless it seems rightly applicable to true penitence. For without confession of the mouth, contrition of the heart alone is not sufficient for penitence, especially from that time when the statute of the Church decreed confession to be necessary for salvation.

Therefore the third thing necessary for the truth of penitence is confession of the mouth; without it neither contrition nor the purpose of future amendment is sufficient. I know that none of you is ignorant what this saving confession ought to be like. Yet I say it, because perhaps it will not be useless: confession must be one’s own, entire, free, humble, spontaneous, and shamefaced. For the holy bishop Ambrose says that a shamefaced confession greatly helps God in forgiving sins to men, and that the punishment which we cannot avoid by defense we lessen by humble shame.

A great thing is the due confession of sins, because it quickly restores to spiritual health the soul of the one confessing, wounded by offenses; it also justifies the humbled mind and grants pardon for sins. For all our hope after the fall of sin consists in the humility of confession, because guilt is not dismissed unless it is revealed in confession, through the priest, to God who knows all things.

For in confession there is place for mercy, as Solomon says in the book of Proverbs: He who hides his crimes will not be directed; but he who has confessed them and abandoned them will obtain mercy. It is also written in the Psalm: I said, I will confess against myself my injustice to the Lord; and you forgave the wickedness of my sin. The holy evangelist John also says in his first epistle: If we confess our sins, God is faithful and just, so that he forgives us our sins and cleanses us from all iniquity.

And the holy bishop Augustine says: Confession is the salvation of souls, the scatterer of vices, the restorer of virtues, the conqueror of demons, and the heaper up of all good things. It closes the mouth of hell and opens the gates of the heavenly Paradise. Cassiodorus too, commending the power of true confession, speaks thus: O blessed confession, which takes away everlasting disgrace, because whatever guilt penitence finds, it renders absolved for endless ages.

The fourth thing necessary for true penitence is satisfaction: by it, namely, we cut off and remove all the causes of sins, and for those things which we have wrongly committed we appease the Lord with holy works. Hence the divine Pope Gregory says: We by no means make satisfaction if we cease from iniquity, unless we also pursue, with contrary lamentations, the pleasures which we loved. The most holy Pope Leo also says in a sermon: By prayer God’s favor is sought; by fasting the concupiscence of the flesh is extinguished; by alms sins are redeemed; and at the same time, through all these things, the image of God is renewed in us.

Confession, moreover, is the voluntary accusation of the offender arising from shame, and, through the keys of the Church, making satisfaction for sins, with the obligation, by penance and at the command of the priest, of chastising in oneself those things which one has brought forth. Confession must be simple, humble, pure, faithful, frequent, bare and open, discreet, voluntary, one’s own, shamefaced, entire, secret, full of tears, made at the proper times, strong, and truly accusing oneself from the heart; for true penitence requires all these things, which so laments past sins that thereafter it commits no more things that must be lamented.
