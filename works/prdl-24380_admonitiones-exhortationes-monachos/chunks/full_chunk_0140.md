On the true obedience of monks.

...against abbots. But I, who desire you to be instructed in everything holy and useful, exhort and admonish your charity as a father: from now on do not lend your ears to these meddlesome wanderers, who disparage the conduct of their pastors and strive to hide their own crimes by denouncing others. It is not the necessary integrity of monastic discipline to shift the blame for the rebellion and disobedience of subjects onto the head of the pastor.

The man who is a true monk does not rashly judge another’s affairs; and the man who is a son of humble obedience does not investigate pretexts for rebellion. But he who yields consent to the passions of the flesh can never be at rest; and he who scrutinizes another’s life is a despiser of his own salvation. A monk founded in the love of God does not pass judgment on the command of his elder; and he who always beholds Christ as present venerates his pastor as an angel of God. Boldness makes a monk degenerate; and presumption has hurled many into prison. A petulant, wanton, and insolent monk has lost the purpose of holiness; and unless he is brought back to his senses by harsh chastisement of the flesh, he has already set his foot toward hell.

He who, with curious rashness, weighs the command of his elder is not obedient; and he who is saddened by an order is not a true disciple of Christ. For true obedience does not murmur, does not hesitate, does not grieve, does not seek a loophole, does not delay, does not excuse itself, nor does it show the least sign of unwillingness. At the command of the elder, for the love of Christ, it always rejoices, hastens gladly, and obeys cheerfully with speed.

For what else is a monk but an unceasing combatant, who, armed with the virtues of humility and obedience, always keeps watch in the arena of struggle, and, under a threefold battle line, fights more strongly as victor against himself? Blessed is that monk who, doing violence to himself, has learned to overcome the will of the flesh, and by the continual practice of spiritual warfare climbs the steep mountain of true obedience. Truly blessed is that monk who, having despised every consolation of the world, has placed his whole self in the love of Jesus Christ, and now desires nothing further among visible things.

It is not for monks to seek riches, honors, and pleasures in this world; nor does it befit a soldier of Christ to think of anything except Christ. O monk three and four times happy, who, for the love of Christ, mortifies his own will even to the end of life, and perseveres humble in true obedience. How great a good humble obedience is before God, my brothers, no one can sufficiently declare, since divine testimony willed to prefer it to all sacrifices. It is obedience that reconciles the soul to God and, because of the shedding of blood, crowns the martyrs.

For the monk who, for God’s sake, of his own free will binds himself to the perpetual obedience of religion, if he lives rightly according to his vow, certainly bears a long martyrdom.
