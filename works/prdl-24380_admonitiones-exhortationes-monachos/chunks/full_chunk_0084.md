Would that you knew how effective a medicine it is for a soul wounded by sins to receive public accusations and corrections with humble patience, and even, for the increase of perfect satisfaction, to bear injuries and insults with equanimity. I am certain that humble patience in adversity far surpasses every kind of satisfaction; and the readier the mind is to endure insults and injuries for love of Christ, the more easily and swiftly it will obtain from the Lord pardon for all its sins and a fuller indulgence.

For this place in which we now speak is a workshop of purification and expiation, in which zeal for regular discipline holds primacy, discretion presides over examination, and charity directs and completes all things. Therefore in this very place patience is greatly necessary for you; without its presence no satisfaction will be complete. The monk who is a zealous lover of his own salvation loves this place as a workshop of expiation, and never leaves it without having been made better than he was before.

For this place is fearful and terrible to demons, but dear and greatly venerable to the holy angels of God; in it the fault of humble monks is washed away, and whatever is committed openly is also expiated publicly before all. But if some hidden fault has been committed by any one of you, the penitent’s secret confession will likewise cleanse it away. In both cases pure confession with humility is required, and both here and there patience is judged necessary for you.

Therefore, whoever you are who desire to be a good monk, prepare your soul in all patience, so that in adversities as well as in prosperity you may keep a most constant tranquillity of mind. When you have been publicly accused by some brother for your negligences, bear it patiently with all humility, faithfully bringing to mind how innocently the Son of God was accused for you. For if he, just and innocent, who committed no sin, suffered insults, why should you not patiently bear rebukes from a senior, you who offend in many things every day?

He most patiently endured countless injuries for your salvation; and why should you not, for your sins, bear humbly and patiently a few brief admonitions? For if you are justly accused of a fault in judgment, you must necessarily be patient, since you yourself have deserved what you suffer, and your humble endurance of rebukes will be reckoned to you as satisfaction for sins. But if you did not deserve the public accusation in judgment, nevertheless bear it patiently for love of Christ; for it will be reckoned to you not only as satisfaction for former sins, but much more as justification.

Therefore, whether a monk is accused justly or unjustly, he has no cause for just self-defense, since if he has sinned, he suffers by his own deserving; but if he is innocent, he is all the more bound to patience. For no one unless patient and humble will be able to imitate Christ; but since a monk is especially bound to be Christ’s disciple and imitator, it especially befits him to be patient.

It profits us greatly, if we love Christ, whether we are accused justly or unjustly.
