I submit these sermons to your examination, and with humble supplication I earnestly ask that the file of your mastery may correct them. I have arranged a few out of many in order, since I neither committed them all to writing, nor did an excessive length of the book seem to me either necessary or useful. For on feast days, when we are free from the turmoil of worldly business, we devote ourselves to reading according to the rule of the Scriptures. And lest I seem to occupy the place of shepherd in vain, what I have labored over by night I am accustomed, after None, in place of the regular study, to speak and to recite from a little sheet before the brothers in chapter. For I find that this care of mine not only profits the brothers for edification, but also helps me toward skill in composition. Farewell, venerable teacher, and pray for us.

From Spanheim, on the twelfth day of the month of September, in the year of the Christians one thousand four hundred eighty-six.

In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ begins the second book of exhortations to monks by Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, afterward of [unclear].

That monks must live cautiously. Sermon I.

Many things indeed, most beloved brothers, are necessary for your consideration in this life, if you desire to attain the glory of eternal salvation. For you know, as Scripture bears witness, that your adversary the devil, like a roaring lion, goes about seeking someone to devour; and we know that he must be resisted with great watchfulness in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ through works of charity. Therefore let no one presume on his own strength, lest he trust in vanity; for unless you humbly keep yourselves in the fear of the Lord, you will quickly fall into the snare of the devil. “The greater you are,” Scripture commands, “humble yourself in all things,” for God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble.

For it befits a monk to be humble and a true despiser of this world. A monk ought always to be diligent in the observance of regular discipline and continually zealous in the fear of the Lord. A monk ought to be chaste and modest in mind and body alike, so that he may serve God, as he promised, in all purity. A monk must not live according to his own will, but according to the rule and according to the direction of his shepherd, conforming to the norm of cenobitic instruction. A monk [unclear] ought also to love holy silence and to occupy his mind usefully in the reading of the Scriptures. A monk ought at all times, even until death, to be diligent, so that he may be perfect in the observance of the commandments of God and of the rule.

But so that you may know what things are necessary for you, who live in holy religion, for perfection, I shall describe for you a certain path of virtues, by which you must direct your way to the kingdom of heaven. All the virtues, however, cling together by the bond of mutual connection, so that whoever perfectly possesses one seems to have them all; and whoever lacks even one is said to possess none at all.

The first of all the virtues necessary for salvation is the most holy catholic faith, separated from every pollution of errors and heresies, and rooted originally in the teaching of our Lord Jesus Christ. For, as Scripture bears witness, without faith it is impossible to please God; and whoever does not believe in the only-begotten Son of God is already judged, and the wrath of God remains.
