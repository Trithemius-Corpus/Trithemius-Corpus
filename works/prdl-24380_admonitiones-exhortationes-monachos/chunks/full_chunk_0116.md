Homily XVII.

If one who mocks and despises an earthly king is punished by beheading, what does one deserve who despises the heavenly emperor? Therefore I warn you, brothers, and with all the sincerity I can I beg you, to purify your hearts, especially in the time of prayer, from the stain of wicked thought, so that you may turn over in your mind only what you chant with your mouth. As the angels always stand before God with fear and the greatest reverence, chanting an everlasting hymn of praise, so let us also take care, my brothers, to stand before him with a pure heart, a clean conscience, and most fervent devotion, so that we may chant his praises much more in spirit than in body.

Let us therefore separate our minds from all troublesome earthly affairs; let us cleanse our consciences from all enticements of the flesh; let us put on the fervor of the Holy Spirit, and drive far from us all laziness and cowardice. For just as a heavy burden weighs down the back of a slothful man, so drowsiness during psalmody is accustomed to oppress the minds of monks. Nothing harms monks more than acedia, or sloth, which little by little weakens all the powers of the mind and extinguishes the whole fervor of the spirit. For as soon as a monk begins to fall away from the fervor of holy devotion, countless evil thoughts creep into his mind; and he is now able to repel them so much the less, the farther he is shown to have withdrawn from his former fervor.

From this arises drowsiness in the performance of the divine offices; the mind is weighed down excessively by sadness and melancholy, and the monk, growing lukewarm in spirit, strives with all his effort to relieve it not by spiritual consolations, but by earthly and carnal little comforts. And when he goes on longer in this estrangement of mind, he is hardened, as though insensible, against the exercise of compunction; and the more he pants after the consolations of the flesh, the farther he wanders from the inward fervor of the spirit. For what makes you drowsy during psalmody except acedia? For the mind that falls into sluggishness soon makes the body doze.

But again I warn you, brothers, as I have been accustomed to do very often: above all, flee laziness and drowsiness in the time of prayer, because just as drunkenness brings forgetfulness upon the soul, so too drowsiness makes the mind senseless and foolish before God. Beware this vice of drowsiness, O you young men, with which we sorrow to see some of the older men already infected by long habit; for in choir dozing is familiar to them, and compunction of mind toward God is very rare. Beware, I say, this evil habit of sleeping, which, once it has led a monk away captive, rarely lets him go free. Therefore at the very beginning of conversion, young men especially must fight against this most destructive enemy of religion, as someone said in verse:

Resist at the beginnings: medicine is prepared too late,
When evils have grown strong through long delays.
But make haste, and do not put yourself off to the coming hours;
He who is not ready today will be less ready tomorrow.

On the sleep and wakefulness of monks.
