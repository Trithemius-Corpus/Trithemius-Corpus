Therefore, my brothers, let no one be proud of the grace of the Holy Spirit which he has received; let no one vainly exalt himself, because, unless we are humble, we shall in no way be able to share in the mercies of the Lord. For, as the sacred lover of Christ, Paul, says: he who thinks himself to be something, when he is nothing, deceives himself.

Third, the grace of devotion is withdrawn from a man because of sloth and acedia, when the mind is little exercised in good works. For, as Scripture says, idleness is the enemy of the soul, and everyone who pursues leisure will be in want. For the wise man says: Desires kill the sluggard, for his hands have refused to work at all. Just as fire is extinguished without fuel, so the spirit of devotion, without the continual exercise of charity, fades away and grows thin.

Therefore let no one presume upon his own strength in this life; let no one trust securely in his former way of life, because, so long as someone can become better from worse, he can also become worse from better. For our whole life upon earth is a trial, in which, if we do not advance in good, we fail; and there, as holy Pope Leo says, we fall into the danger of failing where we turn back from the desire to make progress. Therefore flee acedia, and always keep watch in good works. Consider what happened to the slothful servant, who hid in a napkin his master’s money, which he had received for trading. Trade, I beg you, while there is time; as long as you live, always be occupied in good pursuits.

Fourth, the grace of devotion is very often withdrawn from those who, placed under obedience, follow their own will. For the monk who loves his own will most justly loses the grace of God. But he who, for God’s sake, humbly subjects himself in all things to the obedience of his shepherd, who despises the will of his own heart, is filled with various gifts of spiritual grace and enlightened by the light of inward fervor. We can offer nothing more precious or acceptable to God than if, for love of him, we deny our own will. Finally, the very profession of monastic discipline lays upon us, by the necessity of salvation, the duty of despising our own will and humbly submitting to him who has been set over us. Therefore the monk who follows the impulse of his own will is judged as an unclean vessel and unworthy of spiritual grace. Hence the grace of God is deservedly withdrawn from the monk of self-will, who, living otherwise than he promised, lies to the Lord.

Fifth, the grace of devotion is withdrawn from the monk who thinks that he has it by his own merits. He does not understand that sin dwells in him; if he has received anything good, he ascribes it to his own merits. Against this sluggishness of mind the holy prophet said to the Lord: Lord, by your will you gave strength to my beauty.
