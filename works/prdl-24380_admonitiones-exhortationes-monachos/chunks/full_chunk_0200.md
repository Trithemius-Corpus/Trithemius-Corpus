To know God in the kingdom of eternity and to love him is to possess, by enjoyment, the very glory of happiness. For man was created for this: that he should understand God his creator as the supreme good; that, understanding him, he should love him above all things; and that, loving him, he should enjoy and possess him forever. This is the end of the monastic institution; this is the destination of the Christian faith: that God, whom we now know through faith and love with all our heart through works of charity, we may, in the blessed life, understand perfectly and have as the everlasting object of our enjoyment.

For the attainment of this eternal happiness, most beloved brothers, all good things must be done even with the greatest labor, and all adversities must be endured with the greatest patience, since the very brief exercise of virtue brings forth for us an eternal reward. Hear what holy Bishop John says on these matters: If every day, he says, it were necessary for us to endure torments, indeed if we had to tolerate even gehenna itself for a short time, so that we might see Christ reigning in eternal glory and be joined forever to the number of his saints, would it not, by equal reasoning, be fitting to endure everything sorrowful that can befall us, so that we may deserve to possess the glory of so great a good? But one does not arrive at the supreme good except through the greatest labors; for, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel, the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent seize it.

The fifth thing that ought to draw a man back from sin is to consider the dignity of the human condition, which, by the natural guidance of reason, detests vice and loves the good of virtue. Every man who lives according to the command of reason is a lover of virtue; but he who, becoming filthy in the dung of vices, rolls about in it, conducts himself as a beast, not as a man. But for you, my brothers, vices must especially be detested and virtues loved, since you have despised the world for this reason: that in the monastery you might lead a life estranged from sins and adorned with holy virtues.

Virtues, therefore, are to be cultivated by you. I say virtues are to be cultivated, not images of virtues; not with incense, not with sacrifice, but with love and with the purpose of a wholly upright will. For to cultivate virtues is nothing other than to embrace them in the soul and, for the love of God, to keep them inviolate even to death. For, as Lactantius says, the whole duty of virtue is not to sin; and therefore there is no true virtue except that which, through faith in Christ, tends toward that end where the supreme good of man is, than which nothing better can exist. But he is not virtuous who does not love God, since charity is the sum of all virtues. Therefore, if you love God with all your heart above all things, you are truly virtuous. Thus there is one virtue and one way of the good, by which, through faith and love of Jesus Christ, one arrives at the kingdom. He who truly loves God does not sin, because of his love of virtue.
