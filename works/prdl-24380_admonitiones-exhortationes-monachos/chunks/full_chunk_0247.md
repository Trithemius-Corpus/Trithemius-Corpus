Behold how many are found among us, if I may say so with your pardon, who distribute the substance of the monastery to frivolous persons, prepare banquets for birds and dogs, enrich parents and kinsmen from the monastery’s goods, and squander the rest in vain and voluptuous feasts.

Few there are who grieve over the desolation of our order; fewer think about a remedy; very few supply help. Is this how the holy order of Benedict, once most excellent among all, is defended from the enemy’s assault? Wake up, Fathers, while watchfulness is useful, lest too late you repent of having slept. Look at the professors of other orders, how carefully, how zealously, how steadfastly they defend the rights, privileges, and monasteries of their religion, sparing neither expenses nor labors nor contributions, so that they may preserve their orders in their strength.

Are you inferior to them? Are you poorer than the mendicants? If one chooses to consider the number of monasteries of the order, we are more numerous; if the substance of temporal goods, richer; if power and authority, undoubtedly superior. What do we lack, except zeal and constancy, that they possess? Why then do we hesitate? Why do we grow feeble in idle inactivity? Why do we not keep watch for the defense of our order?

Do you not know, O Fathers, that the laws aid those who are watchful? That is not good patience by which God’s honor is neglected, the worship due to him is diminished, and the integrity of the holy order is violated. What shall I say? You grieve over monasteries already transferred, and yet you do not undertake to preserve those that are to be transferred. You put private interests before common ones; therefore your zeal bears little fruit. Each of us, as I see, thinks himself safe if, when his neighbor’s house is burning, his own wall does not at once grow hot. But O folly, O stupidity deservedly to be blamed: he who, wrapped in so many dangers, now raises himself up in confidence of his own security will shortly, once fallen, be compelled to lament.

Therefore, Fathers, if we desire to preserve our houses unharmed from fire, let us meet the fire with water while it is still far away. For if the flame is allowed to pass as far as the neighboring houses, thought is taken for a remedy too late. Hence the poet says: “Resist beginnings; too late is medicine prepared when evils have grown strong through long delays.” Dangers that are ignored gain strength with time, and what at the beginning easily admits a cure gradually becomes an incurable wound.

At the same time take care that an error now neglected does not, in the course of time, grow without measure, when no one will be found who will steadfastly oppose himself for the order. But if you say that litigation was always brought against the authors of the dismemberment, and that they nevertheless prevailed, I answer with that Virgilian saying: “Because the herdsmen came too late.” After the confirmation of letters, a sentence once issued is not easily revoked. For if Pilate was unwilling to change what he had once written, how will the supreme Pontiff, the Vicar of Christ, revoke what has once been decreed? A sentence is more easily prevented than revoked.
