Christ Jesus, appointed to enlighten every man coming into this world, gave mortals knowledge of God; and, dying for the lost, he sealed for the redeemed the greatest cause for love. He instructed the intellect through faith, by which we know God; he stirred the affection through his passion, so that we may deservedly love him above all things.

Therefore, if we love our Lord and Redeemer with unbroken sincerity, it is altogether fitting that for love of him we should discipline our body. For our abstinence will lead us to merit in a twofold way: namely, when by fasting we satisfy the statutes of the fathers through obedience, and when by abstaining out of charity toward God we show ourselves grateful to our Redeemer, who endured death for our sake.

Abstinence increases merit, commends obedience, bestows wisdom and learning, purifies affection, enlightens the intellect, grants victory against demons, perfects religion, obtains pardon for sins, subdues the unlawful motions of the flesh, wins efficacy for prayers, and displays to one’s neighbor an example of virtue. Abstinence purifies the mind, adorns the soul with virtues, and heals the weak little body. Whoever loves abstinence is wise; whoever loves fasting is a strong ruler. A sober mind is the dwelling place of wisdom; but whoever pursues banquets will die in poverty of spirit. It is hard for a man to become wise who lives a life of pleasures.

Nature is content with few things, and the health of life is preserved in moderation. Long-continued voracity has harmed many, and more have perished by drunken excess than by the sword. For habit seems to alter nature, and the longer it has grown strong, the stronger it will then be in any undertaking.

There is among the Swiss a man named Nicholas, whose abstinence has turned the people of all Germany to admiration; and today, as you have heard, he is named by everyone with the title of holiness. He was once a peasant in a certain Swiss village, and according to the custom of the pagans he used to pronounce judgment among fourteen for quarrelling villagers. It happened at one time that he could not revoke a sentence of his associates which he thought unjust; and fearing danger to the salvation of his soul, he left house, wife, children, and all the business and possessions of the world, and withdrew alone into a neighboring wood for love of God, where he lives a solitary life.

And behold, today it is the twentieth year since, entering solitude, he has taken no human food. I am saying things very well known to all; nor do I think there is any man living in Germany who has not heard the fame of this miracle. And lest anyone perhaps in the future believe that we have been deceived by simulation, we call as witnesses the Swiss, together with the bishop of the church of Constance, who observe his habits and life with the utmost care. He is a man of very keen intelligence, and though he is utterly ignorant of letters, nevertheless, like another Antony the hermit of old, he understands the sense of the Scriptures extremely well. We saw the things which he most eloquently debated with a certain master concerning the sacrament of the altar, things at which he who [unclear] heavenly matters by [unclear] will not be able to marvel enough.
