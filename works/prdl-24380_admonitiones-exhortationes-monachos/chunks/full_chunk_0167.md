We are born so that we may shortly die. Let death seem terrible to those whose whole life is defiled in the pleasure of the flesh, and for whom no good things are reserved in heaven. But we, who have placed our hope in the Lord Jesus Christ, the Savior of all, and for love of him have despised the pleasures of the world, why should we fear death?

Nevertheless, since death is the most terrible of all terrible things, we shall be able to drive away an indiscreet fear of dying if each day we prepare ourselves as though we were already about to die. Yet it is one thing to have dread of death, another to have trembling fear. For the Lord Jesus too felt the dread of death in advance, but not trembling fear, so that he might show the properties of the two natures. Therefore continual meditation on death is altogether necessary for us who are weak, so that the mind, not yet perfected in the charity of Christ, may be stirred, as though by a certain violent impulse, to the grace of compunction.

For by the remembrance of future death the mind is pierced with a useful dread, like a nail driven into wood. The remembrance of death introduces arduous repentance and true sobriety of mind. The remembrance of death is necessary for the unwise, whose conversion begins from servile fear, so that through filial fear it may be raised by degrees into charity. The remembrance of death, and of the strict examination of God, the judge to come, is especially necessary for those who by evil habit and daily practice are drawn into sin.

And there are among you some who are too lukewarm and negligent in the spiritual warfare; admonishing them, we exhort them paternally to remember that they are mortal, and to live henceforth in such a way that they do not fear to die. For it is very dangerous to live in that state in which a man would dread to die. We must live so that we await death securely; and this can be done only if, preserving ourselves in all purity of mind and body, we continually burn with the exercises of divine charity. Let us live, as far as possible, innocently, according to the rule of the holy Father Benedict, which we have professed, and we shall have good hope, because almighty God, who does not allow a man of good will to perish, will have mercy on us. Let us always think of God as present with us everywhere: he sees all things, knows all things, understands all things, rewards all things. And let us live in that purity in which we have promised to serve his most holy Majesty.

THE END.

Here ends the first book of exhortations to monks, written by my own hand, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the ninth day of the month of June, in the year of Christians 1486, in the fourth indiction, in the twenty-fourth year of my age. I dedicated this work to my predecessor, now abbot of Seligenstadt, most dear to me.

LETTER

Johannes Trithemius, though unworthy, abbot of the monastery of Sponheim, to the reverend father and lord Johannes de Colenhausen, abbot of Seligenstadt, formerly of Sponheim, my most dear predecessor: with prayers, ready and prepared to please him in spirit.

Recently, venerable predecessor, I sent you my first book of exhortations to monks, containing twenty-five homilies. After I learned that it had been approved by your examination, I judged that this second book also, which I now send, should confidently be inscribed with your name. Into the former I gathered twenty-five homilies for the instruction of the brothers, which I had delivered before them; in this one I have arranged seven sermons, according to the order in which they were spoken in their presence.

I do not fear to undergo the examination of your paternity, because I always had you as a gentle predecessor, and long ago found you to be a sincere lover of truth. For you flourish with a keen, gentle, and deep intellect, which can easily discern between true and false, and necessity requires you to correct all things sufficiently. Therefore I pray that, according to the gentleness innate in you, you patiently bear the rudeness of my talent, since I am both a little child in the knowledge of the Scriptures and a novice in the order of prelates.

Practice in the Scriptures, as I trust, will give me experience, and continued diligence in writing will give me progress. The occupation of household affairs and the poverty of the place often draw me back from salutary study, and turn my mind elsewhere against the purpose of my will. Only on feast days and Sundays is leisure granted me for writing, on which, out of reverence for God, I am not accustomed to admit any business of the peasants. Sometimes also, after the customary singing of matins and lauds, I am accustomed to spend what remains of the night on the study of letters, and, as far as I can, to compose something by lamplight for the instruction of my brothers. I desire in all things to follow in your footsteps, and, if I can, to observe forever the praiseworthy custom of teaching subjects which you introduced. I know that I am bound to this.
