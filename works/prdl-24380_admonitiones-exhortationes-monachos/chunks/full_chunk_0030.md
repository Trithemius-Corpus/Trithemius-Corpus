Hence it is written: “With all vigilance guard your heart, because from it life proceeds.” By the name “heart,” however, we understand affection or desire, whenever it inclines toward the love of some thing, clinging to it so much that it entangles the whole will. Hence the psalmist says: “If riches abound, do not set your heart on them,” that is, do not love them from the desire of the will.

Nothing is more changeable than the human heart, which is altered by almost every single thought and can scarcely remain fixed in the same purpose unless it is restrained by a certain forceful intention. Saint David, not ignorant of this, said to the Lord: “But I said in my abundance, I shall never be moved. Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty. You turned your face away from me, and I was troubled.”

Therefore, my dearly beloved brothers, if there is anyone among you who desires to serve our Lord and Savior in true purity of heart, if anyone wishes to be a monk both in deed and in name, let him learn first of all to trust not in himself but in God, and let him take care always to renew the good purpose of holy conduct by fresh instruction, not only daily, but, as far as he can, at every hour and moment.

For the fervor of spiritual conduct, toward whose practical exercise the whole institution of cloistered life is known to be ordered, cannot remain long at the same point; for if it does not always advance toward what lies ahead, it necessarily falls back to lower things and, what is worse, weaker things. In the arena of spiritual exercise there is no standing still in a lingering halt, because it is the property of the human heart, by its own natural motion, always either to advance toward better things or to fall back into what is evil. Hence, if you do not advance in spiritual conduct, you necessarily decline; if you do not go forward toward holier things, you certainly go backward.

Since experience has made this clear to us, I do not cease to admonish your charity to stretch your desire in good conduct always toward further things, lest you fall into the destructive danger of failing precisely where you do not renew the desire of advancing. The fervor of a spiritual purpose is directed toward the love of God and is preserved in no other way than in him. For the love of God is not absurdly compared to fire, because just as fire, when the fuel of wood has been removed, is necessarily extinguished, so the love of God, if the continual exercises of good works are lacking, soon fades away and is endangered.

Hence that saying of the Savior in the Gospel: “I came to send fire upon the earth, and what do I desire except that it burn?” O monk, happy is that conscience in whose breast nothing burns except the love of Christ, who loves God above all things, who despises all pursuits of vanity.

Now consider your consciences, brothers, most diligently, and weigh them with exactness: whether you love God above all things, and whether the fire of divine love is already burning in you, so that you may apply the necessary fuel, lest at some time through your lukewarmness you feel the little flame of holy fervor utterly extinguished.
