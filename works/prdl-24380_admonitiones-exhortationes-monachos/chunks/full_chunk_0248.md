It touches all, because what is snatched from one is judged to have been taken from all. Whenever a monastery is seized from our order, our common inheritance is in part wasted. If anyone has attempted to occupy your house or field, you immediately accuse him before the judge, immediately ask for an injunction, and until you conquer your adversary you do not cease from the lawsuit. Why, then, do we all not do in public causes of the order what each person does in private ones? Will what one man does be impossible for many, unless perhaps where there is a multitude there is also confusion, so that what is entrusted to several is preserved more negligently? For we gladly embrace the command that each man be nearest to himself. But while, with our body drawn in on every side, like a hedgehog, we roll our own affairs about within ourselves and cast aside care for the order, at last, caught by the snares of hunters, we think too late about the remedy of flight.

Therefore now, reverend Fathers, with the goodwill that befits a monk and with all the attention I can, I beg you: come to the aid of your order, which is nearly falling, and oppose these dismemberments as quickly as possible. Do not put money before such necessities; do not love coins more than the order. Shameful is the keeping of riches by which the commonwealth is not helped in necessity. What will an insatiable desire for gold profit you, if the desolation of your order, so holy, so venerable, so glorious, should prevail? If our predecessors had watched over the order with no greater care than we do, long ago in many monasteries the name of our religion would have perished. Let us therefore imitate the constancy of those whose inheritance we have received. If we love God, if we love the honor of the order, if we desire to preserve it, let us think in time about a remedy. Let us contribute a little to the preservation of the order, that we may deserve to receive much; let us spend a part, that we may be able to preserve the whole; let us offer worthless things, that we may be able to acquire precious ones; let us give earthly things, that we may be able to possess heavenly ones. Let us spend money and also prayer, since by the former man is appeased, by the latter God.

Whatever we have belongs to the right of our holy father Benedict; when we spend anything for the preservation of the order, we do not give him what is ours, but restore to him what is his. Indeed, we are bound to him not only for the souls entrusted to us, but are also obliged to give an account for the substance of the monastery. Yet an account of revenue is never rendered more safely than when money is opportunely expended in the causes of the Lord. But if money is spared in the Lord’s necessity, suspicion is generated of the servant’s unfaithfulness.

At the beginning of this chapter we set down the title concerning the manner of resisting the dismemberments of the wicked; lest we seem to have passed over these matters untouched, let us briefly explain them. And although this treatment does not pertain to me, nevertheless, since I am a foster-son of your same order and a lover of the chapter, the admonition of a disciple will not, as I think, be burdensome to you. It is yours, O Fathers, to distinguish the true from the false; let it suffice for me to have explained what I think.
