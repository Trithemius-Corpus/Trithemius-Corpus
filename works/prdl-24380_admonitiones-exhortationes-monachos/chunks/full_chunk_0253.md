If judges lack authority to restrain the rebellious, legislation is made in vain. For we always strive after what is forbidden, and unless compelled we resist commands. For where there is no fear of power, the condition of law is quickly despised. The preservation of law, therefore, is fear of power. But where the relaxation of power looks the other way, the vigor of law totters; for a constitution, however holy or necessary, is quickly snatched into oblivion if authority is lacking by which contempt may be punished.

Take away power, and the commonwealth will be nothing other than a confusion full of sedition. For rustic savagery is not led by reason, nor quieted by gentleness. Among our own people too, God amend it, there are many abbots and monks coarser than peasants, more savage than beasts, who are not bent by gentleness to the observance of God’s commandments and the Rule, nor are ever persuaded by exhortations, however holy. If the authority of those who preside does not oppose such men, they will never be led by any obedience to their head.

For this reason the supreme pontiffs, founders of this most sacred provincial chapter, established for us many useful remedies, by which we may compel the abbots subject to the chapter to contribute to the support of the order. We have authority; do we hesitate about power? And what is authority worth without power? If you wish, most honored Fathers, that I speak more openly about what I have meant by the power of authority, you will pardon me if I mark your timidity, not to say negligence.

Behold, we have learned that one hundred twenty-seven abbeys are established under our provincial chapter, yet we do not receive from all of them the subsidies of the contribution due. Why is this? We receive the authority to demand it, but we do not exercise the power. We receive what is offered willingly; we are afraid to compel the disobedient. For I am certain that many abbots of our province contribute nothing to the support of the order; indeed, if they are asked, they object as though by prescriptive right.

What are we doing? Why do we dissemble so long? We apply spurs to the one who runs willingly; to the one lying down, indeed kicking back, we do not even apply words. From this arises contempt for our statutes; the authority of the chapter is despised; the inheritance of the order is laid waste on every side. These things must be referred to you, O Fathers, who neglect to tame the rebellious at the beginning.

Consider what this dissembling will finally bring forth; and if you love your order, think as quickly as possible about a remedy. A body is weak and close to dissolution when it is deprived of the support of its members. Behold, out of one hundred twenty-seven abbots, how few you see in the present chapter, though all were bound to attend by statute. Where are the rest? Where are the procurators of all the absent?
