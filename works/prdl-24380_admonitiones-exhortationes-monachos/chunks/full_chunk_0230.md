The boldness of fools, if not at once checked, gives the rest occasion to presume similar things.

Nothing is more unhappy than deformed abbots. They care for nothing less than the rule; they pursue nothing more eagerly than lust, greed, pleasure, pride, and the vanity of the world. Thus, when the head is infected, all the members of the body are corrupted. Now in monasteries where the rule is despised, where discipline is not observed, what else is seen but a kind of spring of all vices? No obedience, no poverty, no chastity is maintained among them for love of virtue; among such men, to live without blame is a crime. These are corrupt men, perverse monks, craftsmen of deceit, authors of fraud, ministers of iniquity, friends of wickedness: they flee discipline, despise observance, destroy order, confound unity.

And truly, Fathers, unless you quickly provide a remedy for these evils, in a short time you will see the order diminished, and you will grieve over it.

But perhaps you say: “Our vigilance has never failed; our diligence has never ceased. We have always set ourselves against the rashness of the wicked; we have always tried to prevent the dismemberments of monasteries, and we have never accomplished anything. What do our labors, our efforts, our heavy expenses profit, when princes and rulers of churches oppose us with such power?”

I know, Fathers, that what you say is true. I know what has been done; I know it has been continuous. But I beg you not to take my words amiss if I dare to answer those objections. Your vigilance in the causes of the order is known to me, your labors are clear, the greatness of your expenses is proven, and continuance has always been applied in these matters. But I should like you to remember, O Fathers, two verses of Horace, in which he says:

“There is measure in things; there are, finally, fixed limits,
beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.”

We have many and varied adversaries; we must not fight against all in one way, but in different ways, in varied ways, in discerning ways. We are monks, professors of humility. Our struggle must be begun with prayers, entreaties, and supplications; it must not be provoked by fierceness. God is high; no rash sentence is to be brought forth against God [unclear]. The divine powers are appeased by prayers, not by threats. But enough of this; for I believe, Fathers, that you understand what I mean. Nor are these things to be explained everywhere to everyone, but only to those who have zeal for the order and the power to carry it out.

Against those who transfer themselves from the order, or who have already long since transferred themselves.

Chapter IV.

Whether I am moved with sufficient justice against those who try to dismember themselves from our order, I do not sufficiently know; nor, if I did know, would I easily dare to say, since almighty patience tolerates them so long and scourges them so late.
