What he cannot embrace with the body, he embraces with the mind; and if there is a lack of human company, he speaks with God. Therefore the good man will never be alone. And so, remaining in your cell, always beware idleness, because nothing so weakens all the powers of the mind as idleness, once it has produced listlessness from itself. If you have loved God ardently, you will never be idle, because the true love of Christ does not allow sluggishness to be born in the soul, but always impels the lover toward the will of the beloved. And just as a certain carnally wise man truly said, “Let him love who does not wish to become slothful,” so experience has taught us in spiritual love, because it does not permit the mind in which it has begun to dwell to grow sluggish through idleness and laziness. For divine love always impels one toward better things, and the more it advances by burning, the greater increase it takes to itself.

The workshop of this love is a clean conscience; and if a monk desires both to have it and to preserve it clean, it is necessary first of all that he despise the vanities of the world and the business of temporal things. For it is not possible that a double love should reside in one breast, nor can a man succeed in loving Christ if he is unwilling to despise the world. For God, though in the Trinity of Persons he is one in the essence of substance, will either be loved by us alone, or he is not loved with a partner. Therefore, so that conscience may remain clean in all purity, and so that the mind may be able to love God above all things, the monk loves the solitude of the cell and never withdraws from the inner exercise of sweetness.

For in the cell of a good and wise monk there is the highest tranquility; and if anywhere there is an earthly paradise, it is in the monastery of those who are preserved according to the purity of the cenobitic institution. There is peace, piety, goodness, light, virtue, honor; there is joy, purity, sweetness, and great tranquility of life, where the devout monk in prayer speaks with the Lord, and the future sweetness of the heavenly kingdom is tasted beforehand by the contemplative soldier. O cell, paradise of monks truly serving God: surely you are the little garden of most honorable delight, in which the mind is joined to God through love and is sweetly lifted up through desire. The monk who loves, reveres, and cherishes you is a true soldier of Christ and serves the Lord in the fullness of virtues. But he who abhors and deserts the solitude of the cell despises the workshop of his own salvation. Therefore I beg you, my dearest brothers, begin to inhabit the solitude of the cell with longing of mind; without love for it, no one in the monastery will be able to attain the perfection of virtues.

On the Manual Labor of Monks. Homily VII.

“Because you shall eat the labors of your hands,” says the Prophet of the Lord, “you are blessed, and it shall be well with you.” If all who live by the labor of their own hands are judged blessed, surely no one eats bread without guilt while idle, and the idle man is blameworthy.
