Let the one among you who is spiritual not despise his disorderly brother, but rather bring him back, by humble admonition, to the pursuit of piety. For Scripture says: “Bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ.”

Indeed, if, agreeing together in brotherly love, you have always been of one mind with one another through faith in Jesus Christ, without doubt you will easily overcome your adversary the devil. For where the true charity of Christ makes the monks dwelling in a monastery of one mind, there God is worshiped in all holiness; there the invisible presence of the holy angels is believed to be, through whom the rashness of evil spirits is restrained. In such a congregation of holy men God dwells, whom the army of the heavenly kingdom praises together with the voices of the monks singing psalms. A multitude of monks burning in the love of God is a paradise of heavenly delights. A congregation of monks who are of the same mind in the Lord is a city adorned with a multitude of towers.

Watch, my brothers, I beg you, watch in holy prayers, and shake off drowsiness and sluggishness of mind especially at that time when you stand in church before the Lord to sing psalms. For if, standing before God in psalmody, you are fervent in prayer, you will without doubt feel his grace present with you. For he is very far removed from the purpose of a monk who, while praying, thinks of something else, and lets his mind be stirred not by divine things but by fleeting matters or occupations.

For you know what our holy founder Benedict says in the Rule: “If we wish to suggest anything to powerful men,” he says, “we do not presume to do so except with humility and reverence; how much more must we supplicate the Lord God of all things with all humility and devotion of purity? And let us know that we are heard not in much speaking, but in purity of heart and compunction of tears.” Since, then, when we sing psalms we believe that God is present everywhere, great reverence is needed in prayer, so that our mind may agree in all things with our voice.

Let us therefore consider, brothers, whom we attend upon, and to whom, in the sight of the angels, we recite the psalms in prayer. For the monk who, at the time of prayer, occupies his heart with vain thoughts is like a man who, standing before a king or emperor and speaking with him about matters very necessary for the safety of his body, when called by his servant leaves the king and turns himself to the servant’s vain and useless conversations. Truly, whoever did this would be judged foolish by all, and would rightly seem deserving of the severest punishments. But what does the monk deserve who, standing before the supreme King of all things and speaking with him about the salvation of his soul, suddenly turns his inner eyes to useless occupations and, neglecting the presence of the eternal King, admits vain or even harmful thoughts into his mind?
