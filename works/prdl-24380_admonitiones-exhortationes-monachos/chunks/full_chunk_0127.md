Patience, therefore, is necessary for all mortals, and it especially befits those who live in the cloister, since, just as adversities are never lacking to the righteous in this world, so no one will be justified without patience. Now the principal degrees of patience, as the diligence of the ancients distinguished them, are three.

The first and lowest is that of those people who neither seek out adversities nor bear them willingly, but when they befall them are weighed down in spirit and cast down with sorrow; they also sometimes complain and murmur for a time against those who inflict them. But after the agitation of their inflamed mind has passed, they return to themselves, and seem patiently to endure the insult inflicted on them without revenge. Yet the patience of these people, not being of great virtue, is always very near the fault of an unquiet mind, and therefore is often to be reckoned of no merit, and sometimes of lesser merit. And although in our times such people are called distinguished by the virtue of patience, nevertheless, compared with the holy men of old, they do not deserve this venerable name of Christian long-suffering and endurance. For that does not seem to be true patience which has been darkened by bitterness and indignation of an agitated mind.

The second degree of patience is said to belong to those people who, although they do not seek to suffer evils, nevertheless, when they come, receive them freely and kindly, and embrace them most patiently as though they were a gift sent by God. Such people are truly to be called patient: inflamed with divine love, they bear with equanimity whatever adversity has occurred, and preserve, even in tribulation, the same tranquillity of mind that they formerly had in prosperity. I would not doubt that that most holy Idumaean Job was of their number: struck not only by extreme adversity, he bore all things patiently, but even when placed in the whirlpool of evils, he gave thanks and blessed the Lord, saying: “The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done. Blessed be the name of the Lord.”

All Christians ought to keep this degree of patience, and especially, before others, those who live in the cloister. For the wise man says: “A man’s learning is known through patience.” For the more learned anyone is in the law of the Lord and in the knowledge of the ordering of rational nature, the more patient he will be in adversities. What if I, as a lover of truth, speak more boldly: that adversities profit us more than prosperities, if we are learned and not forgetful weighers of our own condition?

The third degree of patience, surely the highest and most perfect of the remaining ones, is rightly granted to those who not only, when there is need, bear adversities with equanimity for the love of God and the benefit of the brethren, but who also, inflamed with a more fervent spirit of charity, willingly thrust themselves forward and hasten eagerly to receive insults and injuries. And this kind of patience is divided in two ways, just as a twofold manner of martyrdom of the saints is found. For some, when they could have lain hidden during the persecution of believers, with the charity of Christ burning in them, with no one compelling them, but of their own free will, presented themselves to the tribunals of their persecutors [unclear].
