As long as we are placed in this corruptible life, dearly beloved brothers, we offend daily in many things, and however holy our way of life may seem, we are not without sin. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and this earthly dwelling depresses the mind as it thinks on many things. Scripture says: “There is no righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin.” Likewise the blessed evangelist John says: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.”

The soul, indeed, too much delighted by commerce with the flesh, sins, and its strength is diminished by despair of spirit. The mind is deceived by the assault of sensible things, and readiness to sin is strengthened by long habit. The weakness of the flesh weakens the fervor of the spirit, and the frailty of the will resists the holy purpose of the mind. Hence Solomon, that most wise king, says in the book of Proverbs: “Seven times in a day the righteous man will fall and rise again, but the wicked will fall into evil.”

By the testimony of this saying we are warned not to grow lukewarm in the purpose of holiness, even if it happens that we have fallen seven times in a day. As often, monk, as you fall through sin from the purpose of holiness, so often do not be slow to rise again to your former fervor. It belongs to human frailty to fall, but to stubborn diabolical obstinacy to remain cast down in the mire of wickedness. The demons sinned once, and in their obstinacy were made hardened and stubborn, never thereafter to return to repentance. But human beings, as often as they have fallen, can always rise again to repentance and obtain by tears and prayers the grace of God which they had lost by sinning.

For the heart of man is flexible and can be moved in either direction, because no man in the flesh should ever be believed able to become so evil that he cannot, if he wills, come to his senses from his wickedness and, by God’s gift, reform his mind toward goodness. Again, no one becomes so good in this mortal life that he cannot become evil, if he misuses the grace of the Lord. Since this is so, my brothers, you must be admonished by today’s exhortation to walk carefully, so that he who, as his conscience bears witness, is already walking in the paths of justice may persevere daily in them by making progress, and he who knows himself guilty before the Lord may amend his life as quickly as possible.

If human frailty daily drives you on, or, to speak more properly, hurls you down and makes you sin, let the love of God and of your own salvation also urge you daily to expiate your fault humbly through true contrition. For in this way the righteous man falls seven times in a day and rises again, because even if he slips through human frailty, nevertheless, always persevering in the purpose of holiness, he is strengthened more and more in soul in the love of God. For while he preserves his conscience free from grave sins, he is unwillingly defiled by daily negligences; and when he sees that he cannot live without faults, humbly acknowledging his own weakness, he groans all the more. But if he has even been overtaken by a graver fault, he at once rises again to amendment through repentance.

Therefore the ancient governors of monastic discipline, considering the condition of human frailty, established various kinds of expiation, by which monks of good will might daily advance toward what is better, and the wicked might be compelled to the observance of regular life. For in the Rule the holy Father Benedict commands thus, saying: “With all solicitude let the abbot take care of offending brothers, because it is not the healthy who need a physician, but those who are sick.”

For this reason the first leaders of regular observance, with provident consideration, established that every day after Prime, in the place appointed, a convent of faults should be held, in which, with either the abbot or the prior presiding, the actions of all the brothers should be brought into the open by fair examination. This place, my brothers, bears the figure of the purgatory of souls, because here not only are past sins expiated, but we are also fortified against future ones. For in this place, which we call the chapter-house, whatever you have done wrong openly is corrected and expiated through salutary penance. Here daily, as the paraphraser pronounces it, you learn your monastic law, according to whose integrity you have promised to live; here each day you hear the exhortatory word of salvation; here you listen to the victories of Christ’s martyrs and their many kinds of sufferings for the orthodox faith briefly recited.
