If the man who unjustly claims another’s house or field for his own use sins mortally, of how great a crime is he guilty who turns the house and possessions of God and the saints into dwellings for secular men? And if a man is bound to make restitution to another man for things unduly taken away, how much more are those who bring monasteries owed to God and Saint Benedict bound to restitution? If a sin is not remitted unless what was taken away is restored, when will those be able to be absolved from guilt who turn the dwellings of God’s servants to alien uses? For monasteries once taken away are never restored to the order. Woe to those who did not fear to establish for themselves this disposition, which they will never be able to revoke by any satisfaction.

O monk, O abbot, you who revoke the vow solemnly made to God; you who cast off the habit of holy religion; you who transfer monasteries consecrated to Saint Benedict into the hands of clerics: what will you answer when accused before almighty God? Perhaps you will say, “We changed our vow for a better one.” Whom will you persuade of this? Certainly neither God nor sound-minded men, since they are not ignorant of the difference between vows. To live as a canon is good and holy, if one has been called by God to that state; but if one has been called to the rank of monastic profession, it will never be lawful for him to descend to a canonry. For no one should doubt that canons are inferior to monks.

Tell me, whoever you are, who are an accomplice in the depravity of monks, who transfer them from their order, who defend causes against God and Saint Benedict, who are regarded as the supporter and patron of their apostasy. Tell me, then: how does it seem to you? Is it lawful for monks to become secular canons? If it is lawful, why do you obtain the dispensation of the Roman pontiff at such great expense, with such labors and importunate prayers? Lawful things have no need of dispensation. But if it is unlawful, who will make it lawful? Dispensation? I greatly doubt it. Whether the pope can dispense in this matter or cannot, it is not mine to discuss, since it is agreed that he has the fullness of power. Far be it from me, then, to detract even in the least from the authority of the supreme pontiff. For he is the vicar of Christ, and it is not lawful for a man to judge anything concerning him.

Are you therefore secure, if he does what he seems able to do? Believe me, you are not secure, because you gave a false account of the reason for your transfer; you fraudulently deceived the vicar of Christ, because both the narrative was false and the supplication, not to call it extortion, was importunate. Finally, the bull of your transfer, carefully and cautiously written, does not absolve you from the order, nor turn aside your vow, nor remove your religious life, nor consent that you lay aside the monastic form. Do you think that the supreme pontiff wishes to stain his conscience with your sins? Read the bull attentively; consider the words; note the intention of the one dispensing, and you will find that even after the dispensation you are a monk before God and the Church. For I have both seen and read the bull of this transfer, in which the pope consents that the monks may have prebends and benefices, and nevertheless commands that they render to Saint Benedict what they have vowed. He grants them the habit of canons, but by no means removes the monastic form.

See how secure you are, you who transgress the limits of the indult, who fabricate falsehoods as truths, who lie to the vicar of Christ, who no less extort bulls than obtain them. In matters that belong to positive law, dispensation makes lawful what otherwise was not lawful, if a reasonable cause requires it. But a vow made to God you are commanded by divine law to pay; from this the pope never intends to absolve you without necessity.

But I ask you, tell me: what is the cause of your transfer? Perhaps you will answer as all those transferred would answer: “There are many causes that necessarily drove me to this matter.” What, then, are they? The poverty of the monastery, the incursion of ravagers, the exaction of princes, the bad governance of abbots. These are the causes of our transfer.
