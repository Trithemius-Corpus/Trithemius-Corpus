Homily XVI.

Let him rise. As soon as you sense any sluggishness arising in you, flee to the remembrance of the most bitter Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, and call back to mind whatever can most quickly drive away acedia. Break off all delays, I beg you, because sluggishness of mind, if ignored, suddenly increases, and acedia, if it is not at once extinguished by a renewed fervor of mind, is immediately strengthened into grave ruin of the soul.

For among all evils in a monk this is the worst: if through sluggishness and acedia he gives place in his heart to a seedbed of vices. And you, monks, must keep watch against this beast not only every day, but every hour and every moment, because once it has been admitted into the heart, it never enters alone; rather, the whole heap and spring of all vices accompanies it. There is no more destructive plague for a monk than bodily acedia, by which every fervor of devotion is extinguished, and, in place of faithful service, the mind, through the interruption brought by idleness and leisure, is driven headlong into the pit of despair. Whoever allows acedia to linger quickly falls away from the purpose of holiness.

Against this Solomon admonishes you in the book of Proverbs, saying: “Go to the ant, sluggard, and consider her ways, and learn wisdom: though she has no guide, nor teacher, nor ruler, she prepares her food in summer and gathers at harvest what she may eat.” And again he says: “Desires kill the sluggard, because his hands refused to work at anything.”

Nothing is worse for those who have professed the purpose of holiness than idleness, because it not only accumulates nothing good, but also consumes and scatters everything previously accumulated. For the fervor of holy devotion rejoices in progress and advances; but through cessation it grows sluggish and little by little, not to say suddenly, vanishes. For sloth is the root of despair, and the mother and nurse of all vices. For just as a garment both produces and nourishes the moth, so the most destructive plague of acedia both gives birth to and fosters despair.

This beast is accustomed to trouble no one more than monks, and especially those who lead a withdrawn life in solitude. Therefore you must take the greatest care, monks, against this enemy of the salvation of souls, and you must fight it bravely not by fleeing but by resisting. You must diligently consider that no one has attained the honor of holiness by sluggishness and sloth, nor has anyone merited the kingdom of eternal happiness without great and continual labor.

Therefore the mind must always be renewed by daily and fresh increases of virtues, and this dangerous journey of life must be measured not by what has passed, but rather by what remains. Let no one promise himself security in this life, which is entirely called temptation, full of as many dangers as there are affections in the soul; but let each person take care that one who could become better from worse does not become worse from better.

And especially in these holy days let us all take care, as much as we can, to become better, adding for ourselves, according to the counsel of our holy forerunner, something to the fixed measure of our service: namely special prayers, and abstinences from food and drink.
