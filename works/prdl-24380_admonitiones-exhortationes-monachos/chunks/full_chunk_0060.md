Homily IX.

The mind of the thinker is defiled whenever, by thinking about and turning over those things that are opposed to God, it takes delight in them. And these perverse thoughts separate the mind of the thinker from God and gradually hurl it down into the depth of evils. For an evil and perverse thought, when it has penetrated the mind, first poisons it with the venom of delight; then it darkens reason; afterward it draws the will into consenting to the evil suggestion; finally it completes the sin in deed. From then on, an evil once presumed is committed with greater license, and all shame or fear is taken away through habit. Sin is joined to sin, and little by little the habit of doing evil is strengthened. But when time is granted to an evil habit, there soon arises a certain necessity of sinning. Here is the hardest rule of death; here is the utmost depth of evils. Whoever has once entered this abyss of sins rarely, indeed not easily, rises again to purity of heart without the special grace of God and the greatest labor of repentance. By his own will the sinner has fallen into this pit of death; but he will not be able to rise again without the mercy of God. Most hard is the yoke of evil habit; if anyone has once submitted his neck to it, he rarely regains his former freedom of spirit. All these things perverse thought brings about when it is admitted into the mind and, through delight, is changed into consent and deed. For no thought is so small that it does not gradually grow if it is not driven away from the mind.

Blessed, then, is that monk who casts every thought of his upon the Lord; who always and everywhere denies a place to wicked thoughts; who despises all the perishable riches of the world; who scorns all the customary comforts of the body. He prepares purity of heart for his Lord; he loves nothing in this world except the Lord; he orders all his thoughts to the glory of the Savior and observes his commandments most diligently. What is happier than that monk who shows himself to the world not as a citizen but as a pilgrim? He lives in the world as though he were not in the world; indeed, he is greater than the world, for his whole way of life is in heaven.

Consider now, therefore, most beloved brothers, the brevity of this time, and always remember that you are mortal and soon to pass away. For we do not have here a lasting city, but we seek the one that is to come; and since we know that so many thousands of people have gone before us through death, let us consider how soon we too must necessarily die. A short time is now granted to us, yet a very acceptable one, in which we may work the fruits of eternal life. For now is the time for labor; now is the hour for sowing good, because after death each person will receive the reward of his labors, when no further opportunity for labor is granted. Therefore, my brothers, anticipate this hour of death with holy works, and do not now fail in the sowing of labor, so that in eternal recompense you may be able to rejoice without end.
