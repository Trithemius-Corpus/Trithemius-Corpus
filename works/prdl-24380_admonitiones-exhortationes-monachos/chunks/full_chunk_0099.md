Woe to our times, in which many clerics and monks have now shamefully cast aside the virtue of abstinence; and those who ought to have shown the people of God the way of salvation no less by example than by word, given to feasting and continual drinking, and sunk in pleasures contrary to all clerical honor, hasten to go before them into the perdition of eternal death.

Scripture says: “The beginning of man’s life is bread and water.” But now our monks and cloistered men buy for themselves, at great expense, whatever is sold most dearly and whatever is found most luxurious; they bury it by gorging it into their bellies, and through intemperance corrupt both soul and body. Amid banquets they debate fasting, and when they are drunk they praise temperance. There each man boasts that he is the conqueror of vices, although he has already been overcome by gluttony and drunkenness.

“Take heed,” says our Lord Jesus Christ in the holy Gospel according to Luke, “lest your hearts be weighed down in gluttonous excess.” It does not befit a man dedicated to God to eat or drink to satiety, nor is it lawful for a monk to eat more than nature requires. Let eating calm hunger, not bring on fullness. Let belches, which satiety is accustomed to produce in banqueters, be far away; let vomiting and indigestions never be heard of among monks. It is fitting that all we cloistered men use a great moderation of discretion, so that the body is nourished with foods and drinks in such a way that, serving the spirit, it can live, but not so that, fattened, it begins to grow wanton against the command of the mind. Let satiety always be far away, since nothing so inflames the body and stirs the generative members as undigested food and a belly heated with wine or beer.

Hence blessed Jerome, admonishing Eustochium to temperance, the guardian of all virtues, says in a certain letter: “Let the bride of Christ flee wine as poison,” since wine and youth itself are weapons against adolescence, which is more inclined to gluttony. Avarice does not so shake youth, nor does pride puff it up, nor ambition delight it, as do those vices of the flesh which seem to us bound together with pleasure. We shall easily lack other vices if we keep watch even moderately in the fear of the Lord. But this enemy of ours is always shut up within; wherever we go, we carry this adversary with us. Wine and youth are a double fire of pleasure. Why do we add oil to the flame, or why do we supply fiery fuel to a little body already burning? Gluttony has overthrown many who were walking chastely in the middle of the road, and those whom the devil could not conquer by his own incitements, excessive drinking of wine has cast down into the depth of lust.

Therefore Benedict, the most holy founder of our way of life, when listing the instruments of good works, set among the chief ones: “to chastise the body, to embrace vigils, to love fasting.” For no one truly follows Christ who, nourishing his flesh delicately, lives in a monastery without daily abstinence. For moderate abstinence, pleasing to God, does many things in us.
