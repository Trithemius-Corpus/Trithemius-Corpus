O initiates of the illustrious heavenly prince, let it not hereafter please you to submit your free neck to the services, command, and yoke of both a vile maidservant and a little prostitute.

If you count for nothing the laws of men, judgments, and threats, if finally you esteem reputation as worthless, let at least fear of perpetual fire draw you away from the foul fever of ancient lust.

“If anyone,” cries the Apostle, “has violated the temple of the Lord by forbidden intercourse,” if he has befouled the temples of his own flesh with the stains of prostitutes, the Lord God will destroy this sacrilegious man with grievous destruction.

Whom do the terrible threats not move, which the sacred volumes everywhere resound against the lovers of foul flesh? Or who would not call them foolish and stupid? Why, wretched man, do you weave snares for yourself, while you blindly love the present danger and do not fear the stern judgment of God?

Hardly ever will you set a limit to your crimes unless you flee far from the serpent’s breath. Who carries coals in his tunic and keeps it whole, or stands unharmed in the midst of fires? You cannot avoid the death-bearing plague unless, cutting away the root of the old evil, you drive out idleness, cups, and leisure.

What was stronger than fearsome Samson, who alone, with immense strength, was able to lay low a thousand men with a single jawbone? Yet a vile woman destroyed him, worn down, alas, by continual complaints. Therefore do not trust the tears of a wicked woman; let her prayers, together with her threats, be suspect to you. By its pestilential breath the flame of lust silently breaks into the inmost parts of the heart.

You should rightly shudder, recalling the crime of adultery in David, than whom scarcely anyone could be found more religious, not to mention his son, who in wisdom shone far beyond all other men. Yet both prudent Solomon and his excellent father were captured by women’s enticements; and by falling grievously do they not silently warn us to shun fierce dangers from afar?

You, therefore, who cast off all delays and shake out the death-bearing serpent, men, lest the Dragon hold you captive any longer: if the pestilential plague is unwilling to go out with gentle steps, drive it out of the house. Though, when driven out, she may cry out, murmur, [unclear], tearing reputation to pieces, pour forth insults against the house from every side, weep, and wail, let none of these things move manly hearts; they perish like the briefest smoke.

Rather, let terror, care, fear, and shame press your hearts with just dread and torment them, lest excessive [unclear] and the greatest punishment [come upon] you, when the spirit, filthy with many crimes, shall be set before the judgment of its Lord. Let not the cries, tears, reproaches, threats, and fury of a vile prostitute move you; but let the avenger of crimes, just and upright, terrify you, full of shameful deeds, and black Cocytian Orcus, and Acherontian Styx. Lest at some time, forgotten amid innumerable filths by the vows and goads of wicked Cypris, the avenging devouring flame of wickedness burn you, plunged utterly into the terrible lake, which always crackles with sulphur and fires.

*Another Poem on the Dignity of Priests*

Venerable men, priests of God, heralds of the Most High, lamps of the day, shining with the ray of charity and hope, receive with your ears the words of my mouth.

You serve in the sanctuary of God; beware lest you be barren or empty, if you wish to live with the true stock.

You are the protectors of the catholic law, the salt of the earth, the light of men, the shepherds of sheep, the walls of the House of Israel, correctors of morals, judges of the Church, teachers of the nations.

If the protection of the law falls, the law is abolished; if the salt has lost its savor, with what shall it be salted? Unless the light appears, the way will not be known; unless the shepherd keeps watch, the sheepfold will be broken into.

You have begun to tend the vineyard of God, which you ought to water with the streams of doctrine, to root out thorns and thistles far away, so that the roots of faith may be able to sprout.

You are oxen threshing on the threshing floor, prudently separating the grain from the chaff, the laypeople, who are frail and inconstant. Whatever laypeople see to displease you, they say without doubt is not permitted to them; whatever they see you fulfill in action, they believe is permitted and free from fault.
