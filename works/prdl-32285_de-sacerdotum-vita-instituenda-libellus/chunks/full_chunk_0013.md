But I greatly fear that, before long, more serious things will happen against the clergy. But enough of these matters. You, however, consider not what others are doing, but what must be done by you. For many are called to the priesthood in the Church of God, but few are chosen. Therefore we must take the greatest care that we who are admitted into the Lord’s portion may also be chosen. What does it profit to exercise the priesthood here for a very short time, if one should happen to be separated from the sight of God? Therefore nothing is better than to fix one’s desire on heavenly things, to persist in the study of sacred reading, to despise present things for the love of God, and to think on the things to come. Hence Peter says in the Aurora: Nothing is better for man than if, by reading divine things, he fixes his mind there whence life comes to him. And so continual study must be devoted to the reading of the Scriptures, because reading nourishes the mind and supplies fuel for the virtues.

Therefore, so that you may not lose the value of time, read the sacred Scriptures, write books, devote yourself to devout prayers, and at the proper times labor also with your hands. Do not blush to learn what you do not know, because then you will be able usefully to instruct others, if you always seek to be informed by those more learned. Do not be ashamed to take on the name of disciple, if you desire to attain the honor of mastership. For the Holy Spirit, who teaches man knowledge, is a lover of humility. When you sit at table to take food, remember that first the sacred letters must resound, so that [unclear] may not be defrauded. For in the Council of Toledo it is read thus: Out of reverence for God, the universal synod established this for priests, that since idle tales are often accustomed to be introduced at tables, in every priestly banquet a reading of the divine Scriptures should be mingled in. For by this both souls are built up toward the good, and tales are not necessarily forbidden. For the word of God is food of the spirit, because man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God. The ancient fathers and priests bestowed the greatest diligence on the holy Scriptures, so that they might both healthfully feed the mind and provide useful things for the instruction of posterity. Let us adhere to their footsteps, that we may deserve to be found worthy ministers before God.

*On Avarice and Cupidity, Which Must Be Fled. Chapter Five.*

Although at the beginning of our discourse we argued against avarice and cupidity, nevertheless we are led by a certain reason to speak again in this place about this very matter. For action is never taken sufficiently against this vice unless it is utterly rooted out. But our Savior, knowing how difficult it is for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God, said to those following him: Unless someone has renounced all that he possesses, he cannot be my disciple. Hence the Psalmist admonishes us, saying: If riches abound, do not set your heart upon them. And the Apostle says: Having food and clothing, let us be content with these. What more do we seek, who are ministers of God? Cupidity is disgraceful in a priest of God, avarice is disgraceful, and the continual hunger for gold is damnable. You, therefore, most beloved brother, if you desire to live in freedom of spirit, do not seek perishable monies; do not love riches. Cast your thought upon the Lord, and he himself will nourish you, he himself will guard you. Nor do I want you utterly to cast aside domestic providence, but not to go beyond measure through avarice. Cupidity is the root of all evils, and it does not allow a man to live for God in freedom of spirit, but always torments the mind with bitter cares, and does not permit him to think of anything other than riches. Moderation, however, is the safest thing, which is neither oppressed by poverty nor lifted up into pride by abundance.

We see among the priests of God worshipers of idols, who are always watchful in gathering monies, who devote themselves to avarice, who think nothing or little of eternal life. They live as though they had been born for gain; they cast far away piety toward the poor; they desire nothing other than to seek and find gold. You would see among them certain wretched, rich misers, who have the greatest supply and abundance of temporal things, but lack the capacity to use what they have acquired. All the days in which they dwell on earth they live miserably; they do not presume to eat and drink according to the necessity of the body; they always desire to add something to the heap of money. They are watchful for profits; they have absolutely no care for the salvation of souls. They grieve more over a lost denarius than if ten souls from those committed to them should perish.
