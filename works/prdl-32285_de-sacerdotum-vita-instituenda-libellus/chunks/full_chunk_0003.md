Therefore, when we shave the hairs of the head in the form of a crown, let us despise all the cares of the world. And just as the head is the more eminent part of the body, so the mind is the supreme power of reason. For this reason we wear crowns on the head, so that, freed from earthly cares, we may be raised more freely in mind to the contemplation of God. Hence we cloistered men, who are called monks, adorned with very ample crowns, leave fewer hairs on the head, so that we may show more fully that we are dead to the active life and at leisure in contemplation of heavenly things. Now attend to the definition of the name, and take care, by worthy merits of life, to correspond to the lot assigned by the heavenly Creator. With all vigilance keep your heart, lest desire sink you into the depth of avarice, lest the fire of lust drag your soul down with sinners into the mire of luxury, lest the haughtiness of pride draw you with it to hell, to be tortured without end. Beware lest through the cares of this world you fall away from the lot of God; beware lest you lose the reputation of a good name; beware lest you proceed in the way of those who despise God. Consider the ruin of many priests around you, and always be afraid for your own similar danger. Nowhere among mortals is life more secure than when danger is always feared. Hence it is written: Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil. Indeed, he who fears danger takes heed; he who neglects it falls into it. It is fitting that the minister of Christ, the priest of God, should be a man, not a boy, so that he may know and understand what sort of burden he has undertaken, for the administration of which he will have to render account in the strict judgment of God.

Let him therefore be prudent, sober, chaste, and quiet; let him be modest, kindly, gentle, so that he who is appointed teacher of an unlearned people may suffer no contempt on account of his own manner of life. It belongs to the priest, when questioned, to answer concerning the law, constantly to rebuke the vices of his subjects by the words of holy preaching, and for love of truth to fear no adversities. It is fitting that he who exercises the priesthood among the people of God should be a man learned in the divine law, so that he may know how to bring forth in due season things new and old, lest among mute dogs unable to bark he lose the dignity of his office. It is now your task, O Nicholas, to rebuke the vices of others with diligent admonition, and to show yourself free from vices, lest, if you rebuke crimes while yourself vicious, you make yourself liable to condemnation. For no one can confidently reprove in another what he remembers, with conscience as witness, that he himself has committed. Therefore he who sits to judge the sins of others must be irreprehensible, because he who commits in himself what he rebukes in others does not amend, but provokes. Priests are found similar to hypocrites when they are angry with offending brothers over lighter matters, and yet do not wish anyone to be indignant with themselves over weightier ones. Wretched is the condition of human frailty: sinner condemns sinner, and in an equal crime he pronounces an unequal sentence. I should be lying if very often an adulterer did not rebuke his neighbor for fornication; a judge vicious in many things condemns a few vices in another, and, so that he may be able to conceal his own depravity, he makes manifest the offenses of all. Against such a one it is said by the Lord: Hypocrite, first cast out the beam which you carry in your own eye, and thus you will see the splinter in another’s. Why does a sacrilegious man reprove a thief? Why does a criminal rebuke one who is vicious? Altogether fruitless will be the correction which an equal foulness of life stains. Therefore, so that you may be free from the reproach of hypocrites, beware, when correcting another, of offending in a similar sin. Correction will never become useful among subjects while he who presides bows his neck to public sins. Indeed, the right of correction is lost when the conduct of the corrector is stained by the vices of his subjects. The authority of the priesthood becomes worthless if public iniquity corrupts the honorableness of conduct. Therefore let every correction take its beginning in its own author, and only then proceed securely into public. Let him whose own conscience gnaws at him in a similar crime suspend for a time the sentence of correction. The condition of believers is now for the most part corrupted, and as the people are, so also is the priest. All devote themselves to avarice, from the greater even to the lowest.
