*Chronicle of Trithemius*

[Sifridus?], bishop of Cyrene, vicar in pontificals of lord Adolf, archbishop of Mainz, by his special commission, and finally ordained by the same man, as is customary, presided over this monastery from the prescribed day of his institution for thirteen years, eleven months, and three days, in great poverty, want, and patience. He came from a certain town of the Wetterau named Butzbach, of knightly stock, which bears the arms of Colnhausen. Placed at letters in his boyhood, he learned most of the first rudiments. Afterwards, when he was about fourteen years old, having left the schools, at the urging of a certain young nobleman, a friend, he began to ride. But since he was slender and delicate in body and did not seem able to endure the labors of horsemen and the burden of arms, after four or five years he gave up the practice of riding along with the office of soldiering, on the advice of his people, and returned again to the schools.

Having therefore recovered the studies which he had for some time interrupted, he went to the general gymnasium at Erfurt, and in a short time, by learning many things thoroughly, made considerable progress. He was inclined to devote himself to mathematics and astronomy, and became sufficiently learned in that science. But because of weakness of the head he could not apply himself to the contemplation of these arts to the degree that the will of his mind required. Therefore, after he had devoted himself to letters for some years in the aforesaid gymnasium, he was made doctor of philosophy by the consent of all, and in that faculty he easily surpassed many masters in learning and knowledge.

After this, having left the public study of letters, he went to his native place, and there began to remain in the house that had once been his father’s, together with a servant. But when household care and domestic anxiety began to vex him too much and make him restless, afflicted with excessive weariness, he did not know what he should do or what state of life he should assume. To take a wife, because of occasions which we deliberately pass over, did not seem safe enough for him; to enter the priesthood and live in secular society he judged dangerous, and so he did not wish it; to remain as he was he could much less endure. Therefore at last, after much deliberation, he resolved to leave the world and enter a monastery.

But since he was very slender and of a delicate constitution, he feared to undertake the arduous path of the rule, lest perhaps afterwards he should be compelled, with great shame, to abandon what he had taken up imprudently and too rashly. And so, for the space of one year, proposing and fearing, he stood in doubt, and could not conclude what was more useful for him. He shrank from the order of the Carthusians as too austere; he did not wish to enter the religion of the Mendicants, because of many things by which he was moved; but to enter our observance, because of the almost continual and perpetual abstinence from meat, he likewise dreaded. But at last, anticipated by the light of divine mercy, he broke off delays and laid aside all fear; having arranged his affairs, he came to the monastery of St. James near Mainz, asked to be received as a monk, and obtained it.

Having become a monk, he lived religiously in all things without offense to anyone, so that he even seemed to be set before the rest as an example. Then ordained priest, and afterwards cellarer of the aforesaid monastery, since he seemed sufficiently suitable, he was finally, as we have said, appointed the first abbot of this monastery in the reform. He was of middling stature, neither too short nor again tall in height, lean in body, slender, and slight in strength; subtle in intellect, mild, very affable, but of swift and vehement anger, whose disturbance of mind came quickly and passed quickly; yet his anger harmed no one more than himself. The reform that had been introduced he continued well, as far as lay in him; he always lived religiously, and at all times conducted himself with the highest maturity and honesty, without complaint.

1470. In the tenth year of John Kolnhausen our abbot, on the tenth day of the month of May, the twentieth provincial chapter of our order was celebrated in the monastery of St. Peter at Erfurt. Presiding in it were Gunther of the same place, Melchior of St. Ulrich at Augsburg, Sebald of St. Giles at Nuremberg, and Paul of St. Mary at Elchingen, abbots of monasteries of our order. In it many statutes were renewed for the [unclear] of the order.

In the same year, on the eleventh day of the month of September, the annual chapter of the union and observance of Bursfelde was celebrated in the monastery of St. Matthias near Trier. In it John, abbot of this monastery, and the present monastery deserved to be incorporated into the union and participation of all the indults, privileges, and graces of the same, in the customary form, with the necessary and customary letters prescribed.
