He was an excellent man, good and magnificent in spirit, who by his prudence and magnanimity defeated and overcame many rivals and adversaries of the church of Trier. Certain canons of the greater church who were rebellious against him he firmly compelled, with the help of the Supreme Pontiff, to subjection and obedience. At his first entry he found the church of Trier altogether impoverished: all its castles, towns, tolls, and revenues pledged, and all its storehouses empty. Nevertheless he was compelled to give and pay sixty thousand florins to John, bishop of Liege, formerly coadjutor of Rabanus, by reason of his administration.

Also in the aforesaid year, John, bishop of Wurzburg, for what reason I do not sufficiently know, was captured by the Domicelli of Hirtzhorn and detained in their custody for four months. There are those who write that the cause was [unclear], and that he had burdened his church with many debts, had not restored at the appointed time a loan received from the aforesaid Domicelli, and for that reason had been captured and detained by them.

In the same year also, on the seventh day before the Kalends of November, Albert, king of the Romans, died, not yet having completed two years in the kingdom, and was buried in Alba, leaving his wife pregnant. He was a prince renowned for liberality and courage. But it seems to me that there is an error in the histories. For if he was elected on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of April in the preceding year, and died on the sixth day before the Kalends of November in this year, he held the kingdom for no more than one year, seven months, and seven days. Therefore four months and twenty-one days were still lacking from the two-year period, as is plainly evident to one who calculates well the intervening months and days.

In his aforesaid year Gobelinus, the nineteenth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, weighed down by illness, departed this present life and rested in the Lord on the eighteenth day before the Kalends of October, after he had completed in the abbacy seven years, one month, and twenty-four days. He was certainly a man constant in spirit and provident in intelligence, not to say crafty, who during the time of his rule endured many adversities with constant spirit and was readier to conquer than to yield. The greater damage which in the time of his rule he brought upon this monastery was the alienation of the books of privileges, which even to the present day have not yet all been restored to us. The withdrawal of bodily residence also harmed the monastery not a little, since the monks, as though deprived of their head, observed no order at all, either in spiritual or in temporal matters. For from the time of Abbot Philip, the last, down to the most recent reform of this place, for almost seventy-nine years, the providers and procurators appointed successively by the abbot, both monks and laymen, are found, for the most part, as appears from their manual registers, with few exceptions, to have been unfaithful to the monastery. *The providers of the monastery were thieves.* However few had dealt unfaithfully, each escaped wealthy, and always after the account had been completed they were found to have spent, in a notable sum, more for the monastery than they had received from it in all things by reason of their office. When asked whence they had received the surplus, one lied that he had received a loan from his mother, a friend, or any other person brought in besides. And so, since the monks were laboring, as it were licitly, under the open vice of private property, what else was to be expected from them than to steal, subtract, commit fraud, lie, deceive, and bring the monastery down to extreme poverty or want? Their pitiable way of life brought such ruin and desolation upon this monastery that up to the present day it could scarcely in any degree be restored, even with the greatest labors.

*Concerning Fridericus Nackheim, twentieth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for five years, seven months, and four days, and concerning various deeds of that time*

After Gobelinus, the nineteenth abbot of this monastery, had died in the year and on the day noted above, and had been buried according to the custom of ecclesiastical tradition, on the third following day, that is, on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of October, the brethren assembled in the chapter-place with the purpose and intention of establishing a new pastor over themselves. They were few in number. After deliberation among themselves, they at last unanimously elected as abbot Fridericus de Nackheim, a professed monk of this monastery, constituted in the priesthood. Confirmed by Theodoric, archbishop of Mainz, and blessed by his vicar in pontifical matters, he presided over this monastery for five years, six months, and four days.
