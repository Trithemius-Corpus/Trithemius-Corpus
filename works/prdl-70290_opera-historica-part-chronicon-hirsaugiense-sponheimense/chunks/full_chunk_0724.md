*XXXVI.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg, to Georg von Rotenburg, most distinguished doctor of medicine, greetings.*

When, in the year just past, on my return from the March of Brandenburg to the Rhine, I directed my journey through Erfurt, I made a most diligent inquiry for you, most distinguished sir, desiring to see and speak with you because of our old acquaintance and affection, mindful of the goodwill and kindness once honorably shown to me on the Mount of Saint Peter by you and your sons, young men of great talent, in the presence of the whole congregation of fathers; for this I shall be grateful to you as long as the power of remembering remains with me. But I sought and did not find you, because you were not in the city. Therefore, after waiting three days with longing for your arrival, since you did not come at all, I departed sadly. For I was held by a great longing for you. And quite often indeed I wished to send letters to you, but I always lacked a sure and faithful messenger who would carry them through.

Today at last, by the Lord’s will, the bearer of this letter, a priest and gymnosophist of Erfurt, met me quite opportunely, through whom I might satisfy my desire. And so I write you innumerable greetings; I signify, as best I can, my love toward you; and I desire that our old friendship remain firm and endure forever. As for the rest, the misfortune of our affairs and the change in our weak condition, whatever adversities have met us, we believe you have learned from the abbot of Saint Peter’s.

I suffered a grave insult and injury from my monks at Sponheim, with certain scoundrels attaching themselves to them; by them my spirit was so provoked into displeasure with the place and the people that my indignation advanced to nausea. From that there then arose in me a horror of my monastery, which over time grew so strong that it finally persuaded me altogether to abandon the ungrateful monks and to provide for myself honorably wherever I could. Meanwhile there was offered to me by the most reverend father, lord Lawrence, bishop of Würzburg, the abbey of St. James, very poor but quiet, and sufficiently suitable and appropriate for my studies. Accepting it for love of peace and tranquillity, I resigned Sponheim, together with its Spatic persecutors, of my own free will.

For twenty-three years I endured the greatest labors in the abbey of Sponheim; for these I received the envy of rivals, to whose rashness I thought it better to yield than, by continual cohabitation, to endure destructive madness. I left behind two thousand volumes, old, precious, and very rare, which with incredible zeal and love I had brought together at Sponheim during the time of my presidency, not without many expenses and labors, in every variety of writings: very many manuscripts, many printed books as well, Greek, Latin, and also Hebrew, of which before me there had been none there.

I confess that my spirit was broken, that so great a store of books had fallen entirely to unlearned and ungrateful monks; and, to restore it wholly to itself, I had to recall the image of my death. I began the experience of death in life; by a certain cosmetic art I tempered what was necessary with what was voluntary, so that one might pass into the nature of the other. And so little by little the pain began, as though afterward, to cool down; and, carried away toward future things, to say with holy Job: Naked I came forth from my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return into the earth whence I was begotten. And that saying of the holy Christ-bearer Paul: We brought nothing into this world; without doubt, neither can we carry anything away.

Therefore, among all the things of this world, the chief thing in which I was wont to take delight for the sake of the final end was that very library; for love of peace it was indeed necessary to despise it, though the separation was beloved to a spirit that had been accustomed to use the familiar consolation of writings in adversity. Yet with God’s help I overcame this intolerance of mind, making a virtue of necessity, so that I might turn more freely toward the highest good, which no one has ever rightly loved with a companion. I rejoice indeed that at last in life I have been released from useless cares of earthly occupations, so that henceforth I may philosophize more quietly in tranquillity. I gladly followed the counsel of Saint Ambrose, who says: It is better to depart with favor than to remain amid quarrelling.
