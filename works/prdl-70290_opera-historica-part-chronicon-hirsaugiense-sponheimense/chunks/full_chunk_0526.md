*Chronicle of Trithemius*

*The gathering of the people at Niclashausen.* A poor and unlearned man at the little village of Niclashausen said that the blessed Mary, Mother of God, had appeared to him in the fields and had given him commands that, preaching publicly to the people, he should rebuke the vices and greed of the clergy; that tolls and transit-dues were not to be paid to princes; that all waters, fisheries, woods, and hunting-rights were common to all; that the commands of priests and bishops were not to be obeyed, nor their judgments heeded; and that neither tithes nor rents were to be given to anyone. To this fool there was a gathering of peoples from all Germany, as to some most holy prophet; much money was offered, miracles were fabricated, and many things were done contrary to the purity of the Christian faith. That foolish man would stand in some house and, while a certain fugitive monk whispered the word to him, would preach to the people through a window; they listened to him all the more gladly, the more he attempted to injure and diminish ecclesiastical liberty and the lordships of princes. Wondrous madness of fools. When the fame of the new prophet had been stirred up from distant regions, a very great multitude of people flowed together, and they adored that wretched and wholly foolish man on knees bent to the ground, crying, while he heard and tolerated it, “Holy man, have mercy on us.” But they also tore away pieces of his clothing for a shrine and relics, and a man considered himself fortunate if he had deserved to touch, see, or hear him. There was a continual and uninterrupted running thither; people of both sexes and ages lay at night in the fields, meadows, and neighboring woods, and many impurities occurred. At last Rudolph, bishop of Würzburg, in whose parish Niclashausen is, sent his armed men, seized the false prophet himself, caused him, after he had confessed the deceit and presumptuous impiety, to be burned, and so the pilgrimage of fools to that place ceased. 1477.

*[Unclear] rashness of foolish men.* In the eighth year of John Kolnhausen, our abbot, Pope Sixtus IV, at the instance of lord Frederick, duke of Bavaria and count in Sponheim, and of Margrave Christopher of Baden, appointed him apostolic commissioner over the incorporation of certain prebends of the newly collegiate church in Wolf on the Moselle in the diocese of Trier, which the aforesaid princes had handed over to the Brothers called the Grey Brothers of the Common Life, to be possessed in perpetuity, and he confirmed the whole transaction.

*The false prophet [unclear] died by burning.* In the same year Eberhard the Elder, count of Württemberg, who afterwards was raised to be duke by Maximilian, king of the Romans, at [unclear], established a gymnasium or general school in his town of Tübingen, in the diocese of Constance. In the province of Mainz he also raised the parish church of St. George into a collegiate church, and caused both it and the university, provided with a sufficient stipend, to be confirmed by Pope Sixtus IV and fortified with the necessary privileges, after the custom of other universities.

*The University of Tübingen is established.* Also in the same year the duke of Lorraine, together with the confederated Swiss, attacked Charles, duke of Burgundy, by whom he had been driven from his duchy the previous year, and defeated and killed him. After his death the aforesaid duke recovered Lorraine. The king of France brought Burgundy under his scepter, down to the present day; and other princes, seizing some things, diminishing Charles’s dominion, scattered it, and just as it had grown little by little, so it suddenly failed. He left one only daughter, named Mary, betrothed to Maximilian, duke of Austria; with her he obtained Holland, Hainaut, Zeeland, Brabant, together with the duchy of Luxembourg, and granted them to Philip his son, the son of himself and Mary. For the duchy of Guelders he had many labors and wars, but down to the present day he has by no means been able to obtain it.

*Charles, duke of Burgundy, is killed.* In the year noted above, on the twelfth day of the month of December, died the illustrious and most Christian prince-elector [Frederick?], buried among the Friars Minor at Heidelberg, in their habit, with great pomp: a lover of the clergy, promoter of all observant monks, defender of the poor and of widows, a most upright advocate of churches, who did as much for peace and the defense of all his people asking his aid as no prince in Germany had done in three hundred years past. He, as my opinion holds, if he had been emperor of the Romans, the Turk would not have torn so many kingdoms, so many provinces, so many cities from Christian dominion; or if the emperor had been such as this man was. When he had died, our most excellent prince, happily ruling today, in this very year undertook the governance of the paternal duchy, which he had governed in the name of guardianship after his father’s death for twenty-six years, being in the twenty-eighth year of his age: a renowned prince, gentle, brilliant and subtle in intellect, strong no less in counsel than in arms, a lover of peace, and a very keen defender and guardian of his subjects.
