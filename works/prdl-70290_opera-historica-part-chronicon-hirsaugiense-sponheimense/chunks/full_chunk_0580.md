*Chronicle of Trithemius*

Meanwhile it happened that the annual chapter of our order was held at Mainz at the end of the month of August, according to custom. Its presiding officers sent two prelates to Heidelberg, to our abbot Johannes Trithemius: namely Conrad, abbot of St. Stephen at Würzburg, and Johannes, abbot of Schuttern, who, in the name of the whole chapter, that is, of all the abbots then present in great number at Mainz, were to ask him to return here to the monastery of Sponheim, promising that they would take pains to remove all impediments from the midst.

By authority of the chapter they had appointed two others, Thomas in Seligenstadt and Johannes in the Rheingau, abbots of monasteries, to approach Duke Johann, who was staying in Simmern, and, if his mind were offended against him in any matter, to recall him to favor on behalf and in the name of all the fathers of the order. When he had heard these things, Johannes Trithemius is said to have given this reply:

“From the time when I began to be a monk, I was always willing, ready in mind to obey in all things the commands of my fathers; rashly I never departed from their statutes. Only on this occasion I can by no reasoning give consent to their commands, so as to return to Sponheim to remain there again, because I have no confidence that I shall henceforth have peace with them. For it is manifest how badly certain of them have plotted against me; whether through malice or through the simplicity of ignorance, I do not judge, but commit it to the judgment of almighty God. They have raised up many rivals against me; wherefore, if I returned to the monastery, neither would I be able to have trust in them, nor do I know that their minds would trust me perfectly. It is better, therefore, that we be separated from one another than that we live in perpetual dissension; for the salvation of souls must always be set before the advantage of bodies. And a shepherd who considers that he cannot make the sheep entrusted to him better provides salutarily for himself and for them if he resigns his office.

“Today the twenty-third full year has passed since I, unworthy, was ordained abbot of Sponheim, always overcooked in the furnace of extreme poverty, continuous in misery and labor, and, what more than all things has been accustomed to burden me, never relieved by the help of my brothers in so many necessary cares. Nor among the reasons for my departure was this the least: that one of my monks, by no means the least, whom I always sincerely loved as an intimate son, whom I defended as the pupil of my own eye against whatever molesters, unmindful of all benefits, lately, while stationed at Mainz, by the counsel and aid of a certain other good brother, issued against me letters full of insults, in the name of the whole convent, although it is clearly established to me that he had no commission or consent at all from the convent concerning this matter. If this was done, or could be done, by the man of my peace, in whom I trusted, who walked with me before the poor little flock in the pastures of the Lord, what will the weaker ones do, whom I have by no means heaped with such benefits? If my right hand, bound to me, could so rashly deny me, what will alien hands do?”

Since, therefore, God by manifest kindness had decreed to free me from such calamities, to the honor of his name, I shall yield gladly and commit myself to divine providence; lest, if I consent to remain longer in office among the ungrateful, I incur danger to the body and detriment to the soul. Nevertheless, before I do this, it will be necessary for me to provide a place for my future dwelling. The Lord God, who does not spurn the prayers of the poor, but sends help to those who ask at the proper time, will himself provide for me, I do not doubt, a place in which I may be able to serve him more freely and more quietly, and work out my own salvation in silence.”

Having said these things, he fell silent; and, both because of the causes noted above and because of certain others set forth by him to the abbots, persisting in the opinion he had begun, he did not consent to return here to the monastery. Whether this action of his pleased or displeased the divine majesty, we cannot know.
