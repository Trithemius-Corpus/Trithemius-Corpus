*Chronicle of Trithemius*

... obtained Landshut together with Lower Bavaria. This Louis, Count Palatine, first took as wife Mary, daughter of the Duke of Brabant; having suspected her of adultery, he later had her beheaded at Werde in Swabia, and had no children by her. In satisfaction for this crime he afterwards founded the monastery of Fürstenfeld, in which he too was buried, as we shall say later in its proper place.

In the same year Conrad, bishop of Speyer, died. Henry, count of Leiningen, chancellor of the imperial court, succeeded him on the Friday before the feast of All Saints and presided for twenty-seven years. This Henry, elected and not yet confirmed, together with his brother, the count of Leiningen, invaded and took by armed force a certain castle in [unclear], called Starckenberg, in the diocese of Worms, which belonged to the principality of the monastery of Lorsch. Therefore the archbishop of Mainz, angered, obtained from the pope judges who would compel them to restitution.

*1246.*

In the thirty-third year of John our abbot, Henry the Landgrave, elected king of the Romans by the princes after Frederick had been deposed, as was said a little above, fought with Conrad, the emperor's son, near Frankfurt, defeated him, and compelled him to flee into Italy.

In the same year Landolf, bishop of Worms, died, and was buried in the monastery of Nonnenmünster, which he had taken away from our order. Cuno, dean of Mainz, succeeded him; when he died within three weeks, Richard of Trier was substituted and presided for about [unclear] years.

In this year, which was the thirty-fourth of John the abbot, Louis, Count Palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria, of whom mention was made above, for the defense of Bavaria against the bishop of Augsburg [unclear] a castle which he had taken away from our order [unclear]. Cuno, dean of Mainz, succeeded him; when he died within three weeks, Richard of Trier was substituted and presided for thirty years.

*1247.*

In the same year the magistracy of Kreuznach was in great dissension with the citizens, because certain liberties and privileges of the city were not being observed for the poor, as it seemed to many at that time; but afterwards concord was made among them.

*1248.*

This year, which was the thirty-fifth of John the abbot, Conrad, bishop of Cologne, a wealthy and high-spirited man, began to build that great and distinguished church of Cologne, laying the first stone on the vigil of blessed Mary ever virgin. Although that work daily increases through the labor of craftsmen, and has thus now been continued without interruption for 453 years, nevertheless scarcely half of the planned building is now seen to have been completed.

The same Conrad the archbishop built anew from the foundations the church of St. Clement, pope and martyr, which had collapsed and which St. Cunibert, archbishop of Cologne, had once founded. Having lost its former name of Clement, it is today called by the name of St. Cunibert. He built many other things and conferred much upon the Church.

In the same year a very great famine oppressed all Germany and killed many.

*1249.*

In the thirty-seventh year of John the abbot, Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, died on the seventh Ides of March; Christian, the second of this name, succeeded him, and presided over the church of Mainz for eleven years, a good man indeed and a lover of the clergy.

In the same year Henry, Landgrave of Thuringia, died; a few years earlier he had been elected king of the Romans, although not all the princes consented to him, nor did he attain the imperial crown. When he died, William, duke or count of Holland, was elected king of the Romans, while Emperor Frederick was still alive with his son Conrad; but he did not attain the crown of the Empire, nor did he live long. Nevertheless he had fairly great obedience, both here and in the lower regions.

*1250.*

This year, which was the thirty-seventh of John the abbot, Emperor Frederick II died at Tarentum, in the fifty-eighth year of his age.

In the same year Hermann, bishop of Würzburg, died; Iring of Reinstein succeeded him.

In the same year also the winter was extremely harsh and very long-lasting, with snow and ice exceedingly abundant; when these at last melted together into one, a very great flooding of waters followed, so great that it overthrew many buildings round about.
