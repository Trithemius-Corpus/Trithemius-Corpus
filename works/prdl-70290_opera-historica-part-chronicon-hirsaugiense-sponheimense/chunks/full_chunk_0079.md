*Hirsau Monastery*

Nevertheless, in all things he did not neglect the care of the brothers. In that conflict which, between the supreme pontiff Gregory VII and King Henry IV, was turning to the peril of many souls, he could by no means be induced to consent, even in the least, to those who were attacking the vicar of Christ. Nor did he wish only to defend and observe the side of the Apostolic See, but he also brought back to obedience to it, publicly and privately, by word and example, all whom he could, without regard for the indignation of the malicious.

He was a great lover of great charity, so that he rejected no one who wished to serve God. He did not consider poverty or the ignorance of persons, but admitted all who came to the service of the Creator, noble and ignoble, rich and poor, learned and unlearned, without distinction of family or nation. In food and clothing he was neither fastidious nor excessive; he loved moderation in all things. At table he never agreed to use special foods or drinks, but as soon as anything of that kind had been set before him, he ordered it to be distributed to the weaker brothers. He taught from the Rule that every mark of singularity in outward conduct was to be avoided by a monk; yet in inward conduct he was, laudably, very singular. For in contrition of heart, in devotion of mind, and in the fervor of divine love he was very singular, so much so that in the daily sacrifice of tears and prayers he offered himself wholly [unclear] to the Lord. His purity of life was so holy, so innocent, and so religious that, since it was confirmed by magnificent signs and miracles, it cannot be doubted that it was most pleasing to the almighty Lord.

Finally, when the fame of his religious way of life, which could not long remain hidden, spread far and wide through Germany and Gaul, many nobles and rich men of this world, and men of great dignity and learning, began eagerly to flock to him and humbly to bind themselves to divine service under his instruction. Of these, several who had been prosperous in the world brought much of their paternal inheritance with them to the monastery for the support of the brothers and the acquisition of necessary revenues.

Throughout all the days of his life he had great trust in the mercy of almighty God, and showed himself such in holiness of morals toward the Lord that in no necessity was he ever abandoned by him. For on a certain day, when the brothers to whom he had entrusted the care of temporal affairs, constrained by the greatest and unavoidable necessity, were urgently demanding from the pious shepherd, who had not a single penny, five pounds of silver to be applied to the common utility of the domestic estate, he began to console them with very sweet words, saying that one must in no way despair of the mercy of God. But they, oppressed by excessive necessity, paid no heed to words of consolation. And so the most reverend abbot, rising from the midst of the brothers in order to beseech the Lord for the pressing necessity of his own, went out; and immediately some unknown man met him, placed the aforesaid weight of silver in his hands, and at once departed.

Many miracles were performed through the man of God William; since they are very fully described in the history of his life, it would be superfluous for us to write them again. Having met on the bridge near the monastery of St. Aurelius a lame man supporting himself on two staffs, he restored him to his former health by invoking the name of Christ. In a grove, when he was about to do some work [unclear], he fed many men from a few loaves, raising himself up with great faith in Christ. Taking pity on Gebhard, a monk, who after the man of God had been carried to heaven after death succeeded him in the abbacy of the monastery, and who was contracted in his whole body, he obtained by prayers sent up to the Lord the health of his right arm, so that he could help himself with it in eating.

At a certain time a fish set before him from theft, though he had been instructed by no indication of human revelation, but inwardly taught by a prophetic spirit, he rejected; and, when the crime was disclosed, he reproved them sufficiently severely. Also a vessel which, through the negligence of the servants, [unclear] was suddenly found again full of wine, and he ordered it to be distributed to the poor. Then, secure in the examples of the supreme shepherd Jesus Christ concerning the mercy of God, he ordered a brother who had labored all day with the brothers in fishing, trusting in the Savior's kindness, to go out again and let down his net for a catch; and at the first draw the brother caught eight pikes of great size. Immediately the holy father invited the poor to a feast, so that he might eat with them the gift divinely offered.

When in a time of famine he was refreshing the poor with a generous hand, and the storehouses, exhausted of grain, now appeared altogether empty, Christ's servant William did not on that account lessen the works of piety; and behold, the grain chest, which in the evening had been carefully inspected, [unclear]. Why do I dwell upon miracles, when the greatest of all miracles is his restoration of the monastic order and of regular discipline, of which I have not yet made mention? For he is the man who strove to reform our order, miserably collapsed in almost all the provinces of the German kingdom, and in many monasteries he also wonderfully reformed it.
