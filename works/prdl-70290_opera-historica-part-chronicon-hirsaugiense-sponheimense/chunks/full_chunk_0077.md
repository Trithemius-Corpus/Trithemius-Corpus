*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

*1069.*

Therefore, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1069, in the seventh indiction, on the holy day of the Ascension of our Lord Jesus Christ, which was the fourth day before the Nones of June, the monastery not yet being completed, William was appointed abbot; and in the presence of many nobles, both clerics and laymen, he was ordained according to custom by Henry, bishop of Speyer, and placed in the seat of the abbacy, not without a flood of tears.

He presided in great religion and holiness for twenty-two years; during these he wonderfully enlarged the state and condition of this monastery, both in persons and in temporal goods. He was born in Bavaria, begotten of honorable parents, by whom, in his boyhood, he was offered to almighty God by his most pious parents in the aforesaid monastery of the holy martyr Emmeram at Regensburg. There, both in conduct according to the purity of the Rule and in literary learning, he advanced so much with time and diligence that, I will not say in the monastery, but not even in another, did he have an equal in his age.

His intellect was most subtle, and learned in every science, both human and divine, as his distinguished volumes show, which he published both as a monk and afterward as abbot. If anyone carefully turns them over with understanding, he will know with what keenness of intellect he was endowed. For from his boyhood, as we have said, being established in the monastery, all that time which could remain over from the regular and other monastic labors he devoted either to prayer or to the study of good letters. No one ever saw him idle, in the monastery or outside it: he was always occupied with some spiritual, or certainly literary, intention.

In those arts which are called liberal he was most learned, so that he far surpassed even his own teachers. For he was a most acute philosopher, and so subtle in disputations that he was completely overcome by no one. In music he shone with singular learning, since in praises of the saints [unclear]. He was so illustriously learned in mathematics and astronomy, in arithmetic and in the calculatorial art, that he was considered by all the most learned in these things, as it were [unclear].

What he published, however, we shall indicate in passing. For he wrote on music, on the composition of the monochord, on the astrolabe and its composition, on the correction of the Psalter, and on the method of ecclesiastical computation. He himself also was most devoted in the sacred Scriptures, in the reading of which, as long as he lived, he was accustomed to have sweet companionship.

He was in every respect a man worthy of honor; even the disposition of his body [unclear] from those who looked upon him. Indeed, he was tall in stature, bald on the front part of the body [i.e. head], [unclear] somewhat drawn out, and the fingers of his hands slender with beautiful length; in the rest [unclear] he had rare [unclear], a face of blue color mixed with whiteness, beautiful [unclear]. His speech to men was such as, with a certain natural adornment of words, has been accustomed to make any excellent orator [unclear]. Hence he was very powerful in exhorting, so that, outstanding most modestly through sweetness of voice and a certain authority of speech, he seemed to have the minds of his hearers in his power, since whatever he wished to say he could persuade his hearers with admirable ease and beauty.

In correcting delinquents, even if he seemed somewhat severe, nevertheless he acted with wonderful prudence and discretion, and with such sagacity [unclear] that they could have worked the salvation of [unclear]. For the love of Christ he afflicted his body with almost continual fasts and vigils, and he [unclear] himself with such tears of holy compunction into a sacrifice of praise that you would have believed he would soon lose the light of his eyes. He was pleasant in brotherly conversation, not dissolute; and there was in him, together with pastoral gravity, a sweet affability, by which he easily drew all into favor toward himself.

He was of such humility that he did not disdain not only to be taught by inferiors in those things which he perhaps seemed not to know, but also many times to be corrected. He cherished his brothers and sons in Christ with a wonderful affection of love, and he strove to care for the causes and necessities of each one no less than his own soul. No one ever came to him, however sad or tempted, for the sake of consolation, who did not depart magnificently consoled by the sweetness of his words.

He was of the greatest mercy toward the poor, and all the sick, needy, and weak whom he found he cherished with fatherly affection, to such an extent that many times he stripped himself of his own garments, with which he might cover the nakedness of some poor man.
