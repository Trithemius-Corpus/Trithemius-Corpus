*Sponheim* ... of the Preachers, which John de Beka, clerk, recounts in his Chronicles on the deeds of the bishops of Utrecht and of the counts of Holland, speaking thus: As the Epiphany of the Lord approached, King William went to Cologne, so that there he might solemnly offer the sacrifice of praise in honor of the three Magi. In those days the lord Albert, later bishop of Regensburg, of the Order of Preachers, was regent and lecturer there: great in necromancy, greater in philosophy, but greatest in theology. He humbly asked the king to deign to dine with him on the solemn day. The king, hoping to see some prodigious sign, agreed to his request.

When, therefore, the solemn masses were finished, the king hastened to the convent of the Preachers. Albert received him gladly and led him out of the house into a certain garden adjoining the monastery, where he found servants of wondrous beauty, who had prepared everything necessary for a banquet. In those days it was a very harsh winter, and the whole surface of the earth was covered with snow. Therefore all those who were with the king began to murmur against the master, because in such dreadful cold he had ordered his guests to dine outside the house, without a fire, in the garden.

But after Albert had sat down at table together with the king, each person being placed according to his rank, while they were waiting for the meal to be brought in, behold, suddenly all that immense mass of snow and ice vanished in a moment, and summer heat, with the rays of the sun shining forth, grew hot. The earth at once brought forth grass, and flowers of wondrous beauty appeared both on the trees and in the grass itself. A wondrous thing: each and every tree of that same garden put forth leaves and flowers, and at once produced early fruit. The flowering vine gave off the scent of sweetness and in that instant brought forth fresh grapes in great abundance before all. A multitude also of birds of various kinds was seen, which, applauding one another and chattering with varied song close by, made them joyful, so that it seemed one should think not January but rather the season of May or June was present. For the long severity of wintry cold vanished completely, and the fierce heat of summer warmth grew so strong that some of those reclining at table were forced, because of the excessive heat, to take off the clothes in which they were dressed, and many prepared shade for themselves by various devices.

The servants, however, were very beautiful young men, lively and indeed handsome, unknown to all those dining there, who brought in food and drink in very great variety and most abundant supply, whence this was done [unclear], with the greatest decorum and discipline of manners. All marveled with great joy, seeing such great marvels as they had not seen before. At last, when the banquet was completed, the whole crowd of servants suddenly disappeared, the singing of the birds fell wholly silent, the greenness of the trees and grasses suddenly [unclear], and the returning abundance of snow again covered the face of the earth. Immediately such cold grew dreadful that all who had earlier put off their double garments, sweating because of the excessive heat, now, touched by excessive cold, took up their clothes again and hastened as quickly as possible to return to the fire.

When these things had been done, Albert obtained from the king a place for his brothers in the city of Utrecht for building a monastery, with freedom and many gifts of gold and silver. The aforesaid John wrote these things in the aforementioned Chronicles of the Utrechters; whether they are true or false, it is not our purpose to wish to judge in this place.

Great tumult was in the kingdom in these times, and various dissensions, because not all wished to accept William as king of the Romans.

In the same year the castle called Richenstein, beside the Rhine, not far from Bingen, was captured. At the same time also, when King William was staying in the lower regions, Mainz and the other cities of those parts which had adhered to him had no defender against their adversaries. Therefore, when the procurators of the cities of Worms, Boppard, Wesel, Bingen, Oppenheim, Friedberg, Frankfurt, Gelnhausen, Fritzlar, and certain others met at Mainz on the day of Saint Margaret, virgin and martyr, they entered into a treaty, peace, and concord in the year noted above, and they laid aside every toll which they had about the Rhine for the preservation of the good of peace, although most of them were entangled in heavy debts on account of the wars and many hardships which they had suffered in the time of the schism after the deposition of Emperor Frederick II.
