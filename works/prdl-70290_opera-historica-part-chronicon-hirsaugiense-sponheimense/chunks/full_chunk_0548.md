They were distressed for a long time by a great shortage of provisions, since, while all those from the Palatinate bought whatever they wished, the people of Bingen alone were excluded from all commerce.

There was also in these times a long dispute, over many articles, between the aforesaid most illustrious Palatine and the church of Mainz; it was the occasion both of the aforesaid removal of the market and of many evils in the land. For indeed, to pass over the other articles, mentioning only one, the people of Bingen, obeying the command of their lords, wished to close the public roads and the bridge over the river Nahe on both sides, near the leper-house and the monastery of St. Rupert, sometimes by day, but always by night, at their pleasure, so that no horseman might pass that way without their consent. Since the most illustrious prince Philip, Palatine, could by no means either tolerate or wish this, since that route necessarily had to be kept open at all times for both his messengers and imperial ones, and since, although he had commanded the people of Bingen once and again that they should not at any time block the bridges and the roads necessary for ascent and descent through that place, but should fortify their own town with an enclosure, they paid no heed, he applied the following remedy. He had as prefect in Kreuznach, whom the common people call an official, Albert Goler of Ravensburg, a Swabian by nation, a man ready in counsel and arms, most daring in all dangers, endowed with singular wisdom and industry; through him he compelled the people of Bingen and their adherents to obedience without any great din of arms. For he, one night, with wonderful ingenuity and skill, burned, broke, and removed the road-closures and barriers, so that no guard of the people of Bingen could in any way frighten him or recall him from what he had begun. And so he opened all the roads that they had closed, both by night and by day, down to the present day, and accomplished many wonderful and illustrious deeds.

*1492.*

In the ninth year of John Trithemius, our abbot, the emperor of the Romans and king held an assembly of princes in September at Koblenz; in it many matters were handled against the king of the French, who, as we have said, had snatched away the wife betrothed to our king. Against him Frederick Caesar strove most earnestly to rouse the spirits of the princes and an army, in order to avenge the injury done to his son. When he [unclear] them unwilling for the triumph over one woman [unclear], after it had been befouled for more than eight hundred years with Mohammedan filth, he restored it to the purity of the Christian faith.

In the same year also Ferdinand, most Christian king of the Spains, after a long siege, great expenses, and incredible labors, powerfully obtained the kingdom of Granada with all its appurtenances from the hand of Boabdil, the Saracen Moorish king, in a most glorious triumph, after it had been befouled for eight hundred years with Mohammedan filth, and restored it to the purity of the Christian faith.

In this same year John Trithemius, abbot of this monastery, at the request of Gerlach, abbot of Deutz, of our order, wrote one book *On the Praise of Scribes*, previously noted, and also another *On the Manner of Celebrating a Provincial Chapter*; both were afterwards printed. He also began in this year a little work *On the Illustrious Men of Our Order*, divided into four books; because it was laborious, he completed it in the following year.

In these times there were certain utterly lost men, foes and enemies of the archbishop of the church of Mainz, whose names we judge unworthy to come in any way to the knowledge of posterity, although they were born from the military class. They devised unheard-of tortures against the clergy, the priests of the Lord: the testicles of those whom they captured, so that they would follow them to appointed places if they wished to be freed, they enclosed so tightly with certain savage iron fastenings, concealing the form and manner of opening them by a certain marvelous invention, that they could not be opened by anyone other than the one who had locked them, by any ingenuity, any skill, or any filing. No cleric could go out securely, if he belonged to the lordship of the church; but priests were captured hereabouts, tortured, despoiled, and beaten, and some even died from the very torments. But all who were under the protection of the most illustrious prince Palatine were secure, whether they were secular priests or cloistered ones. For the most renowned prince by no means wished to permit anyone who lived in his principality to suffer injury from those robbers. The aforesaid enemies of the clergy had their stronghold in the castle of Kaltenfels, not far from here, from which they inflicted very many losses on the church of Mainz by fire and plunder.
