He was a native of Odernheim, born of respectable parents, of knightly stock; in his youth he was handed over to the monastery and imitated the habits he found there. He was, however, worldly, frivolous, and inconstant; he was accustomed to take too much delight in games and carousals, and cared little, indeed absolutely nothing, for religion. But when he had been ordained abbot, he did not at once change his former bad habits; for some years, intent day and night on carousals, dice-games, and other things, he seemed to care not at all about the advancement of the monastery. For not rarely, but often, he shamefully squandered much of the monastery’s substance at dice, sometimes staking [unclear] florins, or rings from his fingers, in that very pursuit of vanity, losing them at the same time. At length, after some years, frightened by fear of the Bursfeld reform, whose fame was then exceedingly celebrated, [unclear] of Nicholas of Cusa, legate of the apostolic see, [unclear] compelled him to leave monasteries bound [unclear]. But afterwards, as far as the prudent administration of temporal affairs is concerned, he sufficiently reformed himself; he gave up the games, carousals, and wandering excursions to which he had previously devoted himself, and applied himself more earnestly to the monastery’s advancement. Nevertheless he did not correct either his own morals or those of his subjects to the purity of the Rule, nor did he take care to amend the defect of spiritual instruction. He found the state of the monastery in temporal matters very poor and exhausted, burdened to creditors in more than one thousand sixty florins; over several years he paid off the greater part of the debts and constructed several new buildings. Among other things, he built the house which is now the kitchen, and that which today is the bakehouse; for their expenses he laid out more than 130 florins, according to the tenor of his reckoning. He also repaired the lower mill from the foundations, planted several new vineyards, not without great expense, and completely repaired two fishponds. He also constructed a number of other things, both in the courts outside and within the monastery: buildings, barns, houses, and stables. We judge it superfluous to make individual mention of them all.

In the first year of Abbot Conrad, Ludovicus, prior of this monastery, died, the twenty-eighth in the order of priors from the beginning of the foundation. After Fridericus, who had died in the sixth year of Abbot Gobelinus, he was [unclear] that he should care for religion. After him, Johannes Mariae was appointed the twenty-ninth prior; for some time he also held the office of cellarer together with the priorate, because of the small number of persons. He remained prior for almost fourteen years, a man born more for temporal affairs than for the cares of monastic discipline. It happened at that time that sometimes the prior himself represented the whole convent, because apart from him there was no monk at all in the monastery. For the abbot had his own dwelling apart; similarly Fridericus of Nackheim, formerly abbot, who had resigned, was accustomed to stay sometimes in the monastery in his own little house, sometimes in Kreuznach. And the monastery, formerly full of monks, was exposed to the mockery of the peasants: abbots, and one monk, of whom the other part was the convent. Nevertheless the number of persons was increased from [unclear] alien monks.

In the aforesaid year of the Lord, on the seventeenth day of the month of April, which was Palm Sunday, during the night the sea in the regions around Dordrecht, with the greatest force, broke through the dike and went beyond its bounds, and suddenly and unexpectedly submerged more than one hundred thousand people, with all their goods and livestock, in a pitiable calamity. On the same Palm Sunday there were also horrible thunders and flashes of lightning in the county of Zutphen, so much so that lightning falling from the clouds into the church of the blessed ever-virgin in the town of Zutphen consumed the tower and bells, seriously injured several people, and killed thirteen in number. On the same day also a great cold wind coming from the north along the ascent of the Rhine brought such a quantity of snow that, all roads being blocked with snow, no one could go anywhere outside the house. And on the penultimate day of the same month of April, in many places the healthy vineyards, injured by excessive cold, perished.

In the aforesaid year Theoderic, archbishop of Cologne, with a great army of his men again entered Westphalia against the people of Soest, but to the great harm and inconvenience of himself and his men. For the citizens, going out and fighting manfully, captured two hundred men from among the better nobles on the archbishop’s side and, bringing them captive into the town, did not set them free until they had redeemed themselves with a great sum of money; from them they extorted more than fifty thousand.
