*Concerning Henry, the twentieth abbot of this monastery, who presided for 8 years; concerning his character and certain deeds of that time.*

After the most reverend abbot Conrad had died and been buried, as we have said, and after the funeral rites had been celebrated in Christian fashion with the customary solemnity, the prior and the brethren, assembling on the day appointed for the election in the chapter place, after the invocation of the Holy Spirit, by unanimous consent elected as the twentieth abbot of this monastery Henry, a conventual monk, who at that time held the office of cantor. But after his election he resisted for a long time, until at last, overcome by the prayers of the brethren, he gave his consent to the election. He presided for eight years.

He was from eastern Francia, born of honorable parents; having been set to letters in his youth, he made no small progress over time by learning, so that, instructed by long practice both in divine and in human philosophy, he became exceptionally learned. Afterwards, touched by divine inspiration, he began to consider in his mind the vanity of this world and the brevity of the present life; and after mature deliberation had first been held, he came to Hirsau and, by putting on the monastic habit, undertook monastic customs. Once made a monk, he lived among the brethren without complaint, always first with the last in the divine service, to which he wholly bound himself. Afterwards, since he seemed zealous in the divine offices and was not ignorant of music, he undertook the office of cantor, which he fulfilled for some years so studiously and eagerly that he yielded to all as an example through admiration. He also used to be intent with the greatest diligence upon the labors of his hands, according to the Rule of our holy father Benedict, planting vineyards, cultivating gardens, and grafting trees of various kinds.

By his own zeal and labor he also greatly adorned the church, [unclear] from day to day he advanced more and more in religion, and was dear and beloved to all the brethren; the fame of his name began also to spread abroad to outsiders, [unclear] with manifold praise. He was elected abbot by the brethren of the same monastery, [unclear] received with much honor and reverence.

Having been ordained abbot at Brauweiler, he took care with all zeal to instruct the monks in the observance of regular discipline and to forbid all the vices of the monks more strictly under a fixed censure. But, affected by weariness because of the poverty of the place and the excessive importunity of the brethren, when he saw that he could make less progress, he repented of having undertaken so arduous a burden of pastoral care. Therefore, after long and mature deliberation, he resolved to lay down the abbacy he had undertaken and to return to Hirsau, whence he had come. And so he then resigned the office of pastoral care and, having returned to the monastery whence he had come, was again appointed cantor, persevering in the same humility after the abbacy in which he had lived before.

After this, when the venerable abbot Conrad had died, as we have said, he was substituted in his place and presided over the monastery of Hirsau for 8 years: a good man, gentle in disposition, who at the beginning of his advancement for some years ordered both his own life and that of his subjects modestly enough and according to the Rule, and took care in all things to show himself a useful pastor in each state. But in the course of time, too much weighed down by the cares and anxieties of secular affairs, he turned his mind away from the rigor of holy observance; and, becoming more lax in the custody of monastic discipline, he relaxed in himself and toward his subjects the fervor of a holy superior. Hence, when 8 years had been completed in the abbacy, he brought back to memory the negligences of his past life and, fearing the punishment of divine vengeance, abdicated the abbatial dignity, as we shall say in its place, and accepted the provostship in Reuth for the support of his life.

As far as the disposition of his body is concerned, he was a man of middling stature, his body somewhat spread out in [unclear]. In his way of life he was very humane and affable to all with a certain sociability; though he was quickly moved to anger, he could be placated by a quick satisfaction. In his time, the holy observance of religion had declined not a little in the present monastery, since he immersed himself wholly in the cares of temporal affairs and did not seem to give as much thought as he ought to the spiritual advancement of his subjects.
