But because report of them had preceded their arrival, the peasants had carried whatever was movable into the cities and fortified places. They did indeed surround several towns with a siege, but since they lacked the engines necessary for assault, they were able to capture none at all. Meanwhile the citizens of Strasbourg, together with the Alsatians, sent letters and messengers to Emperor Charles, then at Frankfurt with the princes, to implore aid; the emperor promised them that he would come shortly with an army. But, though often entreated, since he always promised his arrival and always put it off, he gave the Alsatians great [suspicion] concerning himself of a conspiracy secretly entered into with the English Company, because he allowed so noble a province to be laid waste by the basest little robbers. At last, compelled by shame and necessity, he sent an army into Alsace. As it approached, the band of robbers withdrew in flight; and when our men decided to pursue them, they used such speed in fleeing that our men could not cover in four days the journey they made in one day. But after the robbers’ departure, the emperor’s army, ranging through Alsace, did the inhabitants very great damage, trampling down and destroying the grain in the fields, since it was close to harvest-time, so much so that they seemed to have suffered heavier losses from friends than from enemies. Hence a great famine followed, which grievously afflicted all Alsace for six continuous years.

In the aforesaid year Bohemond, archbishop of Trier, burdened by gout and old age, caused Cuno of Falkenstein, canon of the church of Mainz, formerly coadjutor of Archbishop Henry against Gerlach, to be created canon of the church of Trier, and took him on as coadjutor, entrusting to him the handling of the affairs of the whole church and country. He, triumphing everywhere most gloriously against the enemies of the aforesaid church, soon at the beginning of his appointment accomplished wonderful things. Therefore Bohemond, rejoicing, obtained by his prayers from Pope Innocent VI consent to resign the archbishopric of Trier to Cuno. When the pope gladly assented to this, Bohemond abdicated the pontificate and had Cuno as successor, receiving from him for his own support, for as long as he should live, the town and castle of Saarburg with their appurtenances and revenues; he survived five years.

In the year written above Pope Innocent VI died. Urban V succeeded him, abbot of Marseille, of the Order of Saint Benedict; he presided for eight years, a learned man of most holy life, who caused the cross to be preached against the Turks and accomplished many good things. In his time flourished the devoted servant of Christ, Saint Bridget, formerly queen of Sweden, illuminated by many divine revelations; she scorned worldly rule for love of the heavenly, and, going out from her land and kindred, made pilgrimage to the thresholds of the saints, continually devoting herself to fasts, alms, and prayers. She came to Avignon to Pope Urban V, sent by our Lord Jesus Christ for the confirmation of her rule, which she had made.

Also in the aforesaid year William of Gennep, archbishop of Cologne, died, leaving an enormous treasure in gold, silver, wine, and grain. The see was vacant for ten months.

*MCCCLXIII*

In the fifth year of Abbot Wignand, and the Lord’s year 1363, Gerard, bishop of Speyer, died and was buried in the major church. Lampert of Buren, abbot of the monastery in Gengenbach, of the Order of Saint Benedict in the diocese of Strasbourg, immediately succeeded him and presided for eighteen years; when these had elapsed, by the authority of Pope Urban VI, he was transferred to the noble exempt bishopric of Bamberg.
