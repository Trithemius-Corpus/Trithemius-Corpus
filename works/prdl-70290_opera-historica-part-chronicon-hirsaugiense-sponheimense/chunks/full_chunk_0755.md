*Familiar Letters, 55*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, sends greetings to Brother Theodoric de Eltz, monk of Fulda.*

I congratulate you on your health, dearest Brother Theodoric, and I greatly rejoice in your peace. Your merits have required me, by the most just reason, to do this; and I should deserve to be accused forever of the fault of ingratitude if I so quickly handed over to oblivion the many services you have rendered me. For, to pass over the rest, no forgetfulness will ever erase this one thing from my memory: that when I was at [unclear] with the most serene prince Palatine two years ago, for several days, in the matter of the burned monastery of Limburg, and meanwhile certain retainers of the prince [unclear], while my monks dissembled, not to say consented, had conspired against me in contempt, because they were aggrieved that I was a supporter of the party of the aforesaid Prince Philip, you were the first and only person to report to me their rash presumption, with charity making the journey safe for you through their threats and swords. But what the monks, professed to me in regular fidelity, faithlessly conspiring with my rivals, committed against me, there is no need for me to recount to one who knows.

Nor did you only disclose to me the conspiracy of my rivals, which otherwise, unknown to me, would have been exceedingly harmful; but by your diligence and fidelity you also brought over to me, from the hands of my rivals, our books and instruments of knowledge and of secret matters at Stauronesus and the monastery, and you did not abandon me when I was sick at Neometum and Neuburg. You always stood by me as an unwearied companion and servant when I went to Cologne Agrippina and from there to the March of Brandenburg, while in all these matters not even one of my brothers cared to come. Therefore it is worthy and seems just that, for your services toward me, I should acknowledge your fidelity with fitting benefits, as far as I can with God.

From your letters, then, which you recently sent me, I have noted two things that concern the peril of your soul, unless you provide for your salvation as quickly as possible by fleeing from them. One of them is that, contrary to equity, you have declined from the regular observance of the order to deformed monks. The other is that, by reason of the ministry entrusted to you, pursuing public gain, not without injury to the purpose of the monastic life, you run about here and there as a beggar and wanderer. I beg you, brother, listen to my counsel, whom you affirm always to have been a faithful patron to you.

You have done a thing exceedingly harmful to your soul and, beyond any doubt, dangerous: departing from the lawful ways of our fathers and abandoning the observance of the regular life, which in the monastery of Wissembourg you publicly professed, swore, and solemnly promised before God and his saints; and now you live among deformed monks a life in no way fitting your profession. How will you stand in the judgment of God? Or what will you answer, if you die in such a state, which is altogether contrary to your profession? See, you are now doing those things which the holy father Benedict forbade to you and to all: you devote yourself to meats, games, and drinking; you possess private property; contrary to the monastic institution, you wander about in the world with dissolution of mind; and the pen blushes to express one by one the things that follow from these.

You were not instructed so at [unclear] of the Helvetians, nor did you learn this manner of living with me at Sponheim; nor did you ever see from me examples of such conduct at any time, nor note even the least trace of it. Rather, the contrary was manifest to you in me, you who both at Sponheim and elsewhere were always a witness of my life and conduct for more than four years. For you know, and it cannot be hidden from you, that many kings and princes have often had me solicited, with the promise of a quite ample stipend, to turn aside to their courts; nor even today do they entirely cease from the insistence of their petition once begun. Yet thus far I have always resisted, and I have not wished to comply with their gifts or promises, so that I might in any way withdraw from the regular observance which I once professed, although a greater occasion than yours was given to me for turning aside to others.

Remember, brother, I beseech you, your first instruction; remember your vow; remember your promise, and return to your heart, because what you do is not lawful, but altogether prohibited, damnable, and apostatic: to depart from the holy reformation of the regular life and to migrate to a deformed monastery. It has always been lawful for each person to advance to what is better, and it has always been praiseworthy to increase a vow in good things; but to fall away from good into evil has never been lawful, nor has it been permitted to anyone to violate and diminish a vow solemnly made to God. No necessity compelled you to transfer your purpose of regular observance to those not living regularly; therefore you will by no means be able to be excused. You are a monk professed under regular observance, and for that reason you can in no way be saved outside observance. Therefore either, if you wish to be saved, return to observance; or, if you have persisted in the deformation of life which you have assumed, know that you are to be damned by him whom you have despised as a transgressor of your vow.
