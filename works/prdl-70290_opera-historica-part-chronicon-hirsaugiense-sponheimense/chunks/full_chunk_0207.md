*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

...he was preparing what seemed necessary, when, while bathing in a certain river, the passages of life being closed, he was suffocated, and miserably entered the way of all flesh. After his death, the princes took his son Henry, the sixth of this name, as king of the Romans; Philip, archbishop of Cologne, crowned him at Aachen according to custom, and he reigned after his father for ten years, having reigned with him, while he was alive, for almost twenty-two years. He took as wife Constance, daughter of William king of Apulia, by the counsel and dispensation of Pope Clement III, because she had been a nun for many years and was the only daughter of the aforesaid king. With her he obtained the kingdom of Apulia and Sicily, the pope lending favor and assistance. And although she was fifty years old, nevertheless she bore him a son named Frederick, later Frederick II, emperor, of whom we shall speak in his own place.

*1190.*

In the second year of Abbot Henry, Ida, countess of Spanheim, became a nun of our order in the monastery of Mount St. Rupert near Bingen, in which thereafter, all the days of her life, she lived most religiously, second to none in continual humility and obedience, in prayers and meditations on the divine Scriptures. Her sister was Margaret, a virgin of most holy life, [unclear], who carried out the office of prioress for many years in the same monastery, and, renowned for miracles, at last passed to the Lord. The feast day of her commemoration is observed on the fourth day before the Kalends of November, when she passed from life.

*1192.*

In the [unclear] year of Henry, Philip, archbishop of Cologne, died in Apulia, having been established with the emperor. Bruno III, provost of the major church, succeeded him and presided for three years, and afterward became a monk. There was an obscuration of the sun, and famine followed in the land, and no small barrenness of fields and vineyards.

In this same year also Philip, king of the Franks, and Richard, the renowned king of the English, with their army joined together in Syria, at first waged war prosperously against Saladin. But soon discord between them having arisen at the devil’s instigation, Philip returned to Gaul. Richard, however, vigorously pursued the war he had begun in this same year; but in the following year he too returned home, not without harm to the Christians, whom he left afterward exposed to many dangers in the Holy Land.

In the same year also Pope Clement III died. Celestine III succeeded him and presided for six years and eight months. On the sixteenth day before the Kalends of May, after celebrating his pontifical coronation at the solemnity of Easter, on the following day he crowned Henry VI, king of Rome, as emperor of the Romans.

In these times the Order of the Teutons began in the Holy Land through certain citizens of Bremen and Lubeck, who, crossing the sea with Count Adolf of Holstein under Emperor Frederick I, took poor, sick pilgrims into their care for the love of God and, as far as they were able, supported them by nourishing them under tents. They built at Acre the first house and church of this undertaking; calling it the house of the Teutonic brothers, they gave the order its name. The work of this order is to serve sick pilgrims. Around the same time also began the Order of the Holy Trinity for the redemption of captives, whose more important monasteries are established in France. The work of this order is cheerfully to expend its substance for the redemption of Christians who are held captive among the unbelievers in the Holy Land, and to aid them by ministering to them with all its strength.

In the fourth year of Henry, abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, a grave dissension arose because of the insolence of his servants, who were disturbing the peace of the brothers with excessive importunity. But when the brothers had often asked the abbot either to cast the wicked and restless servants out of the monastery or at least to correct them, and [unclear], certain of the younger men, offended in spirit, calling upon the aid of outside friends, violently drove some of the abbot’s own household out of the monastery. In that tumult one of the abbot’s servants, named Adelbert, was gravely wounded, and after eight days he died from the same wound. From this arose a great dissension between shepherd and sheep, not without danger to souls, until Otto, bishop of the church of Speyer, interposed his authority with the counsel of friends and restored concord.

*Vision of a certain monk.*

*1193.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year 1193 of the Lord’s nativity, Rupert, the fourth prior of my monastery of Spanheim, beheld a wondrous vision of the joys and punishments of the life to come. When he revealed it in public with too little prudence, he was soon gravely struck by paralysis and suffered punishment for almost the space of a year.
