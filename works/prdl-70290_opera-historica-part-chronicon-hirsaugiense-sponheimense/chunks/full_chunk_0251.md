Therefore, assembling armed in a very great multitude, they brought forth a huge army; and coming to Utrecht, they were gladly admitted by the citizens as defenders of liberty. They at once expelled all the nobles from the city with disgrace, distributing their goods to the poor and needy. Advancing from there, they seized many towns, castles, and fortifications of the nobles, and utterly destroyed some. Then, when they were vigorously besieging a certain town named Harlem, situated in Guelders, the townsmen, through the effort of a certain knight and with the aid, it seems, of the aforesaid bishop of Utrecht and the count of Guelders, defeated in battle those confederates of the Sotularian faction, many being captured and more being killed. And thus the Sotularian society of peasants, reduced to nothing, vanished.

*1269.*

In the fourth year of Abbot John, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1269, a great quarrel arose between Berthold, bishop of Würzburg, and the counts of Henneberg, Castell, and Hohenlohe. As it gradually grew exceedingly, they finally came to arms; and when battle was joined near the town of Kitzingen, many on both sides fell wounded.

In the same year certain men proscribed from the city of Cologne fled to Engelbert the archbishop, who was staying at Bonn. They were kindly received by him, because he was not in good accord with the people of Cologne. Having taken counsel, they approached Cologne by night and tried to enter through a hidden underground passage, made by someone for a price, laboring to hand the city over into the hands of that bishop. But when the plan was discovered, they accomplished nothing, although many perished on this evil occasion.

*1271.*

In the fifth year of Abbot John, in the one thousand two hundred seventieth year of the Lord’s nativity, Pope Clement IV died, and the see was vacant for nearly two years.

In the same year peace and concord were made between Engelbert, archbishop of Cologne, and the citizens, through the mediation of Albert the Great, bishop of Regensburg.

In this same year there were many seditions in various cities and several wars among the princes of the land, not only in Germany but in all the neighboring provinces, and many thousands of men fell.

*1281.*

In the following year, which was the sixth of Abbot John and the year of the Lord’s nativity 1271, in the fourteenth indiction, after the two-year vacancy of the apostolic see, the cardinals elected as Roman pontiff the archdeacon of Liège, named Theobald, who at that time was in Asia with the army of the Christians fighting against the infidels. Summoning him to Viterbo, they made him pope, and he was called Gregory X. He presided for four years, a man worthy of so great an honor, if indeed a mortal man ought to be deemed worthy to hold on earth the place and vicariate of our Lord the Savior, who sits in heaven. He celebrated a general council at Lyon in the second year of his pontificate, at which many cardinals, archbishops, abbots, and other prelates of various ranks appeared. Many things [were done] concerning the election of the supreme pontiff, the expedition against the Saracens, the union of the Greek and Latin Church, and the peace of Christians [unclear] from Poland, [unclear], on the fourth nones of March. And he presided for thirty years, a good and [unclear] man, who ruled the church well.

*1282.*

In the seventh year of Abbot John, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1272, Henry of Leiningen, the forty-seventh bishop of Speyer, died on the day of Saint Prisca the virgin, that is, on the fifteenth kalends of February. He was buried in the greater church of Speyer, under white marble. Frederick of Westerburg succeeded him and presided for twenty-four years and five months, a man patient of labors and not ignorant of war.
