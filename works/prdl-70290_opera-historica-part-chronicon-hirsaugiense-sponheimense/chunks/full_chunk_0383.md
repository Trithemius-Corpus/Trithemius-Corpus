Among others there was a certain butcher of Creutzennacht, called Michael Mort, a very brave and exceedingly bold man, who fought so strongly on the side of the count, his lord, against the adversaries that, like another Maccabee, he deserved for himself an enduring name of praise among posterity. For, standing in the midst of the enemy, brandishing his sword on the right and on the left, he attacked them so strongly that he alone killed more than twenty and made a way for himself through their midst with the sword-point. At length, overcome by the multitude of enemies and wounded in the feet, he fell to the ground; then, immediately recovering his strength and standing on his knees, since he could not rise, defending himself strongly and brandishing his sword fearlessly around him, he struck down five more and wounded several of those standing around. Finally, however, having no helper, since his own men had begun in part to flee, he died gloriously. In memory of such courage, a stone was set up in that same place with his image carved on it, and is seen there down to the present day. Moreover, although the battle was fought for a long time with uncertain fortune, nevertheless the victory at last fell to the archbishop. There, on the side of Count John, the count of the Rhine, the count of Veringen, with many other nobles, were captured by the same archbishop; and John himself, count of Sponheim, fleeing with the help of a horse, barely escaped. He was lame in one foot, and when he was fighting bravely with his own hand, surrounded by the enemy, he was seized. Seeing this, the aforesaid Michael Mort, with the other butchers of Creutzennacht, boldly, as we have said, rushed upon the enemy and freed his lord by his own blood. For so great a benefit the count afterward rewarded the butchers in Creutzennacht with a special privilege of his own. Having won this victory, the archbishop plundered all the count’s villages round about and burned many of them. His knights too, thirsting for plunder, spared neither monasteries nor churches. For, entering the nearby monastery called Schwabenheim, they plundered it, and, robbing the courts and buildings around it, set them on fire and inflicted many losses on the brethren.

In the same year Werner, the aforesaid archbishop, took the castle called Rheinberg and destroyed it utterly, because of the robberies and depredations that were being committed from it.

In the same year also Henry, count of Veldentz, entered into a perpetual alliance with Werner, archbishop of Mainz, as is clear from the letter that follows, whose tenor is as follows:

I, Henry, count of Veldentz, make known to all that, in regard to the fidelity which I owe to the church of Mainz, of which I am steward and vassal, and from which I hold ample fiefs, desiring to remain forever in devotion and service to the same church, I promise in good faith, for myself and my heirs who ought to succeed me in the county of Veldentz, that I and my aforesaid heirs will give aid to lord Werner, archbishop, and to the churches of Mainz, against all who injure them, and will assist them perpetually. The archbishop himself promised to do the same in turn for me and my heirs, as is more fully contained in his open letters. In testimony of this matter and for its perpetual validity, I have caused my seal to be appended to the present letters. Given and enacted in the year of the Lord 1330, on the calends of September.

In the sixteenth year of Peter our abbot, Albertus Magnus, formerly bishop of Regensburg, died, a man most learned in every respect [unclear], both in the church of the saints and in secular [unclear]. William, count of Jülich, having secretly entered the city of Aachen with 468 armed men, with the purpose and intention of taking the city, was killed by the citizens together with his first-born son and all his armed men. In vengeance for this deed afterward, when negotiations for an agreement were held, the people of Aachen were compelled to resign to the count of Jülich for all time the right of appointing the provost in the church of Aachen, together with the receipt of the third part of all offerings accruing in the church of the blessed Mary ever virgin there.

In the seventeenth year of Peter our abbot, Rudolf, king of the Romans, after matters had gone prosperously in Austria and Bohemia, held a solemn assembly first at Nuremberg and afterward at Frankfurt. All the castles in which robbers and plunderers of the royal road kept themselves were destroyed. Afterward he moved an army against the count of Burgundy, and first surrounded the city of Besançon in order to obtain it.
