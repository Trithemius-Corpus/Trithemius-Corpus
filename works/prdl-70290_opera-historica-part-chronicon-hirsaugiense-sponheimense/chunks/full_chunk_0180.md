*Monastery of Hirsau*

In the same year, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 1156, the emperor came to Cologne around the feast of All Saints, and was received with sufficient honor both by the citizens and by Archbishop Arnold. There a certain knight named Bernhard, who had been convicted by the duke of the Saxons of having conspired in the murder of Hermann of Winceburg, was beheaded by order of the emperor.

*1157.*

In the following year, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 1157, in the fifth indiction, Volmar, the fifteenth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, departed from this world on the fifth day before the Kalends of February, around the first watch of the night, and rested in the Lord: a man worthy of remembrance among all posterity, since by his industry, wisdom, and piety he did many good things for this monastery of his. He increased its revenues, enlarged its income, restored the ruined buildings of the cenobium, constructed anew several houses and dwellings necessary for the convenience of the brethren, increased the number of monks serving God, and showed himself, by word and example, an excellent pastor to all his subjects. He was buried in the greater church, before the altar of Saint Michael.

*Concerning Hartwig, Sixteenth Abbot of This Monastery, who presided for only two months, and concerning his life and conduct.*

After the most reverend and God-beloved abbot Volmar had been buried, although the brethren were held by the very great love they had for him, and could not erase from their hearts the memory of so most pious a father, not, I say, quickly, but not ever entirely, whom they had always found to be in all things a useful, pious, and most gentle pastor; nevertheless, lest the malice of the times, which then abounded in the land, should introduce anything sinister by the suggestion of depravity, it seemed to them that they should not delay in the election of a new pastor.

Therefore on the same day on which they had committed Volmar to ecclesiastical burial, they came together [unclear] temporal matters, so that one useful and suitable for the rule of the monastery might be considered. Accordingly, after the grace of the Holy Spirit had been invoked, as is the custom, they unanimously and concordantly elected as abbot a monk named Hartwig, a venerable old man, in the year of the Lord 1157, in the fifth indiction, on the octave of Saint Agnes, virgin and martyr, which is the fifth day before the Kalends of February. When the election, according to custom, had been publicly announced in the person of Hartwig, he fell to the ground before the whole convent and tearfully begged the brethren, with supplication, that he be spared so great a burden. “If I had wished,” he said, “indeed, if I had been fit to rule souls, I would have remained in that state in which I had been before my entrance into religion. But because I know my imperfection, and that it is more expedient for me to be subject than to preside, therefore I gladly gave up my house, provostship, dignities, and the benefices that I had, though all my friends and kinsmen opposed it, so that without care and anxiety for temporal affairs I might be able to render pleasing service to almighty God in the simplicity of my heart.”

And when the brethren strongly pressed him to take up the burden of rule imposed on him by God as upon one fit for it, at last, consenting, he said: “To despise your prayers, most beloved brethren, I judge [unclear]; yet I know myself wholly insufficient for so great a weight of rule. Nevertheless I trust in the mercy of almighty God, for love of whom I once despised all worldly things, that he will not long permit me to live in this misery, if he knows before himself that this care of souls, which inexperienced men demand, will harm my soul.” And so, yielding to the very great insistence of the brethren, he undertook the burden of pastoral care for the love of Christ, and went down with certain monks to Hillin, archbishop of Trier, for the gift of benediction. He refused, however, to be blessed by Günther, bishop of Speyer, for certain reasons; and therefore, according to the tenor of the privilege long since granted by the Apostolic See to the monastery of Hirsau, he went down to Trier. Thus on the Sunday of Quadragesima, which in that year had fallen on the fourth day before the Nones of March, he was blessed in the greater church of Trier by Archbishop Hillin, in the presence of several abbots.

While he was on that journey, he visited the most holy bride of Christ, Hildegard, of whose holiness, miracles, writings, and revelations he had heard many things. When he heard her speaking, he was turned to amazement and shed tears for exceeding joy. Having obtained an opportunity to speak with her privately, he opened to her the whole state and grief of his mind, and simply related in what great anxieties he was because of the care of souls imposed upon him. And he humbly asked that, for the love of Jesus Christ, she would deign to inquire into the divine will concerning his state, and what he ought to do regarding those thoughts in which he was engaged concerning pastoral care.
