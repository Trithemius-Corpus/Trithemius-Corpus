*Second expedition of the king of England.* *[unclear].* *The castle of [Duna?] is surrounded in a siege.* *Count Walram of Sponheim.* *Henry, twelfth abbot of this monastery, dies.*

In this same year also Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, surrounded in siege the castle Duna of the count called Sylvester; and to tame his insolence he built a new castle, which he called Mount St. John, and at last compelled the count himself to come to surrender. Walram also, count of Sponheim, perpetrated many things against the aforesaid archbishop; wherefore, stirred to anger, he proceeded against him again, and as far as Kreuznach did much damage in the county by burning and plunder. But Emperor Louis, interposing his authority, made concord and peace.

In the same year, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of May, Henry, the twelfth abbot of this monastery, died and was buried in the body of the church before the altar of the Holy Cross; and the abbacy was vacant for five days. He left to posterity, however, a testament that was not good: he divided the revenues in the possessions of the monastery with the convent, and again made the monks proprietors, just as Abbot Peter had once done in the year of the Lord 1270, as was said above, not heeding the piety of Abbots Dietlib and Willicho, his predecessors, who, cutting this vice off at the root according to the command of the Rule, magnificently increased the substance and revenues of the monastery with spiritual gain. As soon as the division of revenues was made between abbot and convent, and the monks resumed private property, immediately, along with regular discipline, the temporal substance also began to perish; and within a few years they came to such want and poverty that, although the monks in this place were few in number, they scarcely had enough from all the revenues of the monastery on which to live. But now, directing our pen to continue the history, we shall make the narrative.

*Concerning Willicho the Second, thirteenth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for one year, three months, and four days, and concerning his deeds.*

[unclear]

Therefore, after the death of Henry, the eleventh abbot of this monastery, the brethren assembled in the chapter room in the aforementioned year of the Lord's Nativity 1340, in the seventh indiction, on the second day of the month of May; after long deliberation had first been held, they at last unanimously elected as thirteenth abbot of this coenobium the cellarer of the monastery, a monk professed here from his youth, named Willicho, son of John, knight of Sponheim, surnamed Bruder. Confirmed by Henry, archbishop of Mainz, and blessed by his vicar in pontificals, brother Albert, bishop of [Ibina?], he presided for only one year, three months, and four days. He was, as we have said, from Sponheim, of honorable parents of the knightly order. His father John was knight and secretary of Count Simon; his mother was Gertrude, daughter of Wilhelm, knight of Sobernheim. After she died ten years after the birth or nativity of her son, the father took a second wife, Mechthild, daughter of a certain knightly man of Winnesheim. When she had borne several sons and daughters, the preceding sons, by his command, were handed over by their father to various monasteries to serve God. Therefore, when Willicho was in the season of youth, he was made a monk under Abbot Willicho; then, after some years, he was appointed cellarer, and finally, as we have said, was ordained abbot. Immediately at the beginning of his promotion he demanded back from his father the portion of the maternal inheritance concerning him, for which he acquired certain goods for the monastery in Bockenau, as we shall say in the following year. He was a man sufficiently industrious and provident, concerning whom there was sure hope, if he had obtained a longer life, that he would have magnificently increased the substance of the temporal estate for this place.

Since Abbot Willicho, from the first year of his promotion, had asked from his father the portion of his maternal inheritance, and the latter seemed less willing to give anything to the monastery, at last, induced by the counsel and authority of lord Simon, count of Sponheim, he yielded to his son's requests in this way. First, therefore, he gave to him, and through him to this monastery, as a perpetual possession, the court in Nosbach with fields, meadows, pastures, waters, ways and byways, cultivated and uncultivated lands, with all its revenues and appurtenances, together with houses, a chapel, and all things acquired and to be acquired, by a completely free transfer, without any burden of debts, precaria, or impediment of any exaction whatsoever.
