I shall follow the counsel of the Almighty, who permits nothing without cause; trusting in his mercy, I shall not fear what wicked men may do to me: ambitious, ungrateful men, who persecute me for this reason, that their ambition might be satisfied, as the outcome of the matter will prove. As report has it, my prior wrote against me, in the name of the community, [unclear] full of lies, fabricated without reason. Wondrous is the man’s deceitfulness. With almost continual letters he importunes me; with words too, and tears, whenever he comes to me, he prays, begs, asks, both in his own name and in that of the community, that we return to our monastery, promising all subjection and obedience, while in secret he contrives adversities against us. Moved by the scandal of this injury from the right hand, although with the favor and assistance of the most serene prince Palatine and of others I could repel these wrongs, and retain my abbacy even while absent from the monastery at all times, with the Duke and all my rivals not considered, nevertheless I have brought my mind to resigning, preferring to keep my conscience innocent of those evils that might follow, rather than avenge the insults inflicted. Therefore I resolved within myself to yield to such burdens of pastoral care, and henceforth in no way to serve the ungrateful, after the example of our most holy father Benedict and of very many fathers following him, who, provoked by the ingratitude of their own monks, left the pastoral care of sickly sheep and betook themselves to other places to provide for their own salvation. But we shall discuss these matters fully when your charity comes to us at Heidelberg, where I, the abbot of Limburg, await you together with the [Schottern?] abbot, and we altogether hope that you will come. I pray that your paternity may long fare well and safely.

From Budori, on the 20th day of the month of August, in the year of the Lord 1506.

*LXX.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, sends greetings to Henry, Abbot of Bursfelde.*

God alone, best and greatest of all, who is ignorant of nothing, knowing the hidden things of hearts, he himself knows how unwillingly I absented myself from the chapter, having no small cause, under whose pressure I would more than gladly have been present at its celebration, had not the savagery of the tyrant count prevented me, just as it prevented the abbot of Limburg. For by the counsels of many we were advised not to expose ourselves to so great a danger of the roads. For we are not ignorant of his malice, which is so great that he neither fears God nor reveres man. He is full of a demon, almost mad, as one who in the preceding year, when war was raging against the poor, not only the monastery of Limburg but also several other most beautiful churches, doing the devil’s work, utterly destroyed by setting them on fire. Hence he always presumes with a savage and disturbed conscience. If he should capture me again, which God avert, who would free me from the hands of a madman? The issue is for body and life, which I have held should be preferred to the whole world. I have determined with myself what I shall do, having deliberated maturely by the counsel not of one night but of several months. I intend shortly to resign my abbacy and, with God’s help and the prince’s favor, to provide for myself elsewhere, where I can do so more quietly. I do not desire to return to Sponheim, nor do I wish for reconciliation with the Duke, since he who showed himself hostile to me without cause will not hereafter have me under his dominion. There are two men in Sponheim who were the instigators of the whole evil, of whom one is the prior, the other was the cellarer; each aspires to the abbacy. Let the fathers allow them to prevail if they wish; I shall turn myself to the path of peace and avoid the company of the impious. The abbot of St. James at Mainz would have forestalled all these evils, if he had not believed falsehood more than truth. For the cellarer, publicly detected in crime, while I indeed was absent, fearing punishment, fled to that abbot himself; in order to defend his own vices, he falsely invented another’s. The latter believed the liar rather than the truth, and thought the whole thing a Gospel which the brother had devised in his own defense. When I returned to the monastery and had learned the history of the deed, I was indeed deeply grieved not only at the brother’s crime but also at the rashness of the abbot, who was defending such a criminal fugitive against the testimony of all the brothers. Meanwhile, since I had been unwilling to follow the precipitate opinion of many, it happened that I was called again to Heidelberg, where, while I was occupied for a rather long time in the business of the burned monastery of Limburg, that same brother, out of hatred for those who had disclosed his deed, with certain of the lay-brothers consenting to him, brought about so much that some of them were captured, with the aforementioned abbot no less procuring it. What followed afterward your paternity will understand more clearly from the brother whom I sent you. Farewell, best father; pray to God for us.

From Budori, on the 30th day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1506. And in his own hand, very hastily.
