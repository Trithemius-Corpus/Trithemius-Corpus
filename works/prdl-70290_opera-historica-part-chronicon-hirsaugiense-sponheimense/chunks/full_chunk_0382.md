But after 175 years the heir of the aforesaid Hedwig resigned the right of patronage into the hands of Johann of Kulenhausen, abbot, in favor of the new reform, as we shall say in its place.

In the same year Manigold, bishop of Würzburg, died. Andreas, born of Gundelfingen, succeeded him and presided for 22 years; he had many disputes with his citizens, from whom he received and endured many injuries.

*1297.*

Adolf, king of the Romans, puffed up with excessive pride, stirred up many conflicts and wars throughout every land, disturbing the peace. For at his command Hermann, count of Geroldseck, and a certain other count, appointed provincial prefects in Alsace, were most troublesome to Conrad of Lichtenberg, bishop of Strasbourg, and to all the inhabitants of Alsace, and inflicted many losses. Provoked at last by such injuries, the aforesaid bishop, and also the citizens of Strasbourg, the counts of Lichtenberg, the counts of Zweibrücken, the count of Holloch, together with many other princes, counts, cities, and nobles, unanimously conspired against King Adolf, incited by the excessive insolence of him and his men; nor did they desist from the purpose they had begun until, after many princes had been brought to the same opinion, they finally cast him out of the kingdom, as we shall say in the following year.

*1298.*

Sifrid, archbishop of Cologne, died and was buried in the church of Bonn. Wichbold, dean of the greater church there, succeeded him. Bohemund, archbishop of Trier, because of his old age, soon took him as coadjutor; this same Bohemund did not live long.

In the same year, when King Adolf was excessively weakening the Empire, nourishing discords and wars, burdening the people with unusual taxes, and despising the princes, Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, together with certain other princes, summoned from Austria to Mainz the duke, son of the late Rudolf, king of the Romans, promising that, once Adolf had been removed, he should be elected king of the Romans. When he had heard this, he came to the Rhine with many princes and a very great army, having on his side Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, Wichbold, archbishop of Cologne, Bohemund, archbishop of Trier, Conrad, bishop of Strasbourg, and also the bishop of Constance, the counts of Lichtenberg, Ochsenstein, Freiburg, Holloch, Zweibrücken, the landgrave of Alsace, and many other princes from Austria, Hungary, Bohemia, Carinthia, and various other provinces. The bishop of Strasbourg brought to Albert's aid against Adolf 80 armed horsemen and footmen. The people of Strasbourg, moreover, sent in his aid four thousand armed men, both horse and foot, who received him at Freiburg with great honor. Hearing this, King Adolf gathered a great army and besieged Rubiacum, but withdrew with the matter unfinished; going to Speyer, he waited to see what the princes gathered at Mainz would decide to do against him.

Meanwhile Albert came to Mainz with very great power, with no one resisting him, and found the leading princes of the Empire assembled. There, therefore, on the vigil of St. John the Baptist, the princes deposed Adolf from the kingdom on account of his grave offenses and unanimously elected Albert king of the Romans, promising obedience, fidelity, and assistance against all adversaries. When this had been done, Albert, the new king, went out from Mainz and with his victorious army came to the town called Alzey, three miles from Worms toward Kreuznach; there, with his princes, having pitched their tents, he settled, and he laid waste the whole town except for the castle attached to it. Adolf, however, having gathered a great army of warriors from the cities sworn to him, Speyer, Worms, Oppenheim, and Frankfurt, together with his princes, Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, his son-in-law, and also the count of Eisenberg and many others, withdrew to Oppenheim and for ten days awaited the aid of the friends to whom he had written.
