*Familiar Letters*

They do not know necessary things, because they have learned superfluous ones. True knowledge is that which leads to the knowledge of God; it corrects morals, restrains pleasures, purifies affection; it enlightens the intellect in those things that pertain to the salvation of the soul, and inflames the heart with love of the Creator. This is salutary knowledge, which affects the mind in the love of God and does not puff it up, which does not make men proud but makes them lamenting, which does not follow vanity, but, drawing all things to one, is most sweetly made firm in divine love. Brother, we desire you to be devoted to this knowledge, by which alone there is the completion of all the sciences.

Let not the degree of mastership exalt you so as to make you less intent upon the study of the Scriptures; but know that you must [unclear] so much the more, because you have received a manifest sign of knowledge, and especially the name of doctor. I do not esteem highly a bright degree without knowledge, or a great title with little learning; rather, we seek learned men, since we have many doctors. Knowledge commends the degree, not the degree knowledge. It is shameful for a master not to know, and for one who publicly bears the sign of knowledge not to have necessary learning. What is a hoop displayed before a house where no wine is sold? Or what is a royal sceptre in the hands of a fool? A sign is bestowed without the thing signified whenever an unlearned man, and one without knowledge, is raised to be a doctor. And, shame to say, how frequent today in the schools among certain people is that abuse, by which the degree of mastership is given as a sign where a sufficient thing signified is not found.

Does it not seem to you that I have spoken the truth, since the world swarms alike with masters and doctors, among whom fewer learned men and many unlearned are encountered? You must take care, my dear, to adorn mastership with knowledge and priesthood with holy morals, lest you be confounded by a double reproach, if you are regarded both as a doctor without knowledge and as a priest without the merit of holiness.

*The noble office of priest: a degree without knowledge.*

The knowledge necessary to you is, first of all, to know God, to understand the divine Scriptures, to know the canons of the fathers, not to be ignorant of both human and divine law insofar as it concerns your office, to cultivate virtues, and to love God above all things with a pure heart. It pertains to a master to know the principles of the arts he professes, so that he may know how to unravel knots, clarify tropes, and explain all the figures of the Scriptures.

*The seven liberal arts.*

You are called master of the seven liberal arts; let us see how much knowledge you have attained of each one, so that you may understand how their use becomes necessary to you. First, through the art of grammar, which is the foundation of the seven liberal arts, you ought to know how to write in Latin, speak fittingly, and utter correct speech without fault. Rhetoric, surpassing the common manner of speaking, seeks a certain beautiful adornment in speaking, uses tropes and various colors, and strives to set forth the cause proposed to it in beautiful and most pleasing speech; when it has persuaded, it has arrived at the end of oratory with the reward proposed to it. Logic, the rational science, teaches you to discern the true from the false, lest you be deceived by subtlety of speech or fall into the syllogisms of deceitful arguments. For knowledge precedes the cultivation of virtue, because no one can faithfully desire what he does not know; and evil, unless known, can by no means be avoided.

Arithmetic teaches how to multiply given numbers, also to divide them, and in every variety to find the certain root. All things consist in number, and he who is ignorant of arithmetic has the perfection of no science. By knowledge of the art of geometry you will understand the measurement of the earth, arising from numbers and measure: a beautiful science indeed, and adorned with the figures of arithmetic, through which human reasoning rises in no small measure to the knowledge of the highest principle. This measures the broad circuit of the world, describes all the spaces of sea and land, and, when completed, ascends into cosmography. Nothing is more beautiful than this contemplation, in which the mind, transcending the whole structure of the world, marvels at the incomprehensible wisdom of the Creator of all things.

Music, prefiguring heavenly harmony and consonance, is necessary for you both in art and in morals, so that, ordained a priest in the Church of God, you may know how to sing rightly and sweetly with modulated voices, and, adorned with holy virtues, may not depart from the proportion of priestly morals. How necessary astronomy is for you as a master, if you do not know, you are not [unclear]. This teaches the courses of the stars, distinguishes time, divides the year, distinguishes hours and days; from its science arises the ecclesiastical computus, giving the reckoning of movable feasts, which Christian priests ought to know.

Behold, priest, how necessary to you is knowledge of the seven liberal arts, so that you may not be compared to an ape in [unclear], nor to a fool sitting on a royal [unclear]. For grammar contributes this, that you may fittingly understand and utter Latin speech. Logic teaches you to discern the true from the false. Rhetoric furnishes you with the virtue of speaking, so that you may be able usefully to persuade your hearers of the good and true thing that you understand. Arithmetic, moreover, together with geometry, besides the fact that they benefit you in household matters, also furnish a good support for divine contemplation, so that you may be regarded as a master fitted not only for things already discovered but also for those still to be discovered.
