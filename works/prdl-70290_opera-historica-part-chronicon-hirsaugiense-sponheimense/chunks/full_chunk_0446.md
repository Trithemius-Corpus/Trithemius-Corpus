*Sponheim.*

...had him ordained with pontificals, and he presided over the abbacy of this monastery for fifteen years, eleven months, and ten days. He was by origin from Beckelnheim, born of his father Wolfram, knight of Sponheim, and his mother Margaretha of Sobernheim. In his boyhood he had been handed over to the monastery under Abbot Wilhelm and lived among the brothers according to their custom. But when elected abbot, *Regular observance is deficient*, he presided, not very usefully, more with regard for his parents than for God, because he instituted no discipline of regular observance and in many respects made the supports of the temporal substance worse. Under his rule all the monks of this monastery, seeking what was their own, not what was the Lord’s, had money; they first began to disperse, violate, and sell the quite precious library, which up to that point had remained intact.

*1375.*

Crafft, abbot, in the first year of his rule, stripped and laid bare the head of Saint Irmina the virgin in its silver capsule, which Crafft the first, almost two hundred years earlier, had had made for the adornment of the house of God; and, after the silver was sold, he clothed the crown with copper, as it is still seen to be clothed. Other various treasures of this monastery too, which former abbots had bestowed on the church for the honor of God, were afterward alienated and sold by their successors, so that, apart from their old pontifical mitre, nothing remained down to our time.

In these times also there was a grave dissension between Archbishop Friedrich and the citizens of Cologne; many princes formed a [confoederatio?] with the archbishop against them.

*1376.*

In the second year of our Abbot Crafft, Johannes called Scadlandt, bishop of Worms, of the Order of Preachers, died. He was buried in his convent at Coblenz, where he had entered that order. After him Eckhard of Ders obtained the bishopric of Worms for twenty-nine years, a man very learned both in sacred Scripture and in secular letters, devout in life and conduct, a despiser of the world and lover of God. Contrary to the custom of bishops of that time, in order that simony might be avoided, he was accustomed to confer all orders, both minor and major, in his own person, freely and with complete purity. He was rarely seen in public outside the church, but, fleeing the tumult of men, he rested alone with his own household in his palace at Ladenburg, devoting continual zeal to the reading of the Scriptures and to prayer.

In the same year Abbot Crafft had an action with the villagers there concerning the liberty of our court in Flonheim, and obtained the liberty in every respect, as appears in the letters.

In this same year the townsmen of Deutz, siding with Friedrich, archbishop of Cologne, showed themselves public enemies of the people of Cologne. Hence the people of Cologne, provoked to anger, burned and utterly destroyed the monastery and the parish church. Afterward in the same year, on the eve of Saint Pantaleon the martyr, Archbishop Friedrich besieged Cologne for a few days; when he saw that he could not take it, he raised the siege and returned to Bonn. Then, around the feast of Saint Laurence the martyr, the people of Cologne went out again and utterly destroyed the monastery of Deutz, which they had previously left untouched, together with the parish church, leaving absolutely nothing whole, lest the archbishop perhaps place his garrisons there to attack the city. Moved by this presumption of theirs, many princes publicly became enemies and foes of the people of Cologne; among them these were the more important: Cuno, archbishop of Trier, also the bishop of Paderborn, Wenceslaus, duke of Brabant and Luxembourg, the duke of Austria, also the emperor of the Romans, the count of Ziegenhain, and the city of Aachen. With these they had more than eight hundred hostile men-at-arms declared against them. For eight years also the city of Cologne itself was placed under interdict, and the celebration of divine services in it was discontinued.

*1377.*

In the third year of our Abbot Crafft, Wilhelm, count of Holland, Zeeland, and Hainaut, and count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, son of the late Emperor Ludwig and brother of Count Albert, died in custody, insane, as we have said; it was reported that he had fallen into that insanity after drinking poison. His brother Albert, as was said above, after his removal into custody, usurped his territory for himself.

In the same year a great battle took place between Eberhard, count of Württemberg, and the imperial cities of Swabia, near Reutlingen; many fell.

In the same year also, through Cuno, archbishop of Trier, a very firm concord was made between Archbishop Friedrich and the citizens of Cologne, but in the usual manner it was not long observed.
