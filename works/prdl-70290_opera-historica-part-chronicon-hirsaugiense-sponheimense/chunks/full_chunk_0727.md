*Familiar Letters*

... great, knowing you to be a true friend and faithful from the heart. For unless your friendly presence delighted me beyond all things, I certainly would not be a true friend. For who would not take delight in being wholly with himself? Or who would endure being divided? But he cannot be wholly with himself who does not wish to be with his friend. For when Aristotle was asked what friendship was, he answered: “It is one soul dwelling together in two bodies.” And another indeed spoke beautifully of his friend: “half of my soul.” Therefore he who is with a friend is with himself. And I feel that half of my soul is in you, most steadfast of friends, by reason, of course, of the force of love; therefore, although you are absent in body, yet present in spirit, I suffer I know not what. From this it happens that I think I am speaking with you, and, carried away by the greatness of love, I cannot find an end to my words. Therefore I am compelled to restrain myself, lest the letter exceed measure. I beg you, be indulgent to these unpolished writings; if I have written with too little prudence, know that it must be imputed to friendship, by whose driving power love for you has seized me, divided as I am. For as often as I think of you as my friend, I know that half of me, not the whole, is in my possession. Farewell, my other self, loving and mindful of you, since I am a lover mindful of myself through the mirror of friendship in you. Again farewell. From Würzburg, on the 12th day of the month of July, in the year 1507.

*XXIX. To the reverend father in Christ, Lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James at Würzburg, formerly of Sponheim, his most honored teacher: Johannes de Woesbruck, moneyer of the most serene king of Castile and master of the great toll at Bruges in Flanders, sends greeting.*

Reverend Father, almost five years have now passed since I was last in Sponheim with your paternity. Afterward I could nowhere hear or learn anything certain about the same: what you were doing, or how you were faring. For I would hear nothing more gladly than of your good health, your happy condition, and your prosperous fortune in all things. The fame of your name is great among us; you are praised in the mouth of all learned men, and also of noble magnates. There are many in all our land who desire to see you and wish to be taught by your learning.

Among them is one venerable doctor of decrees, named Nicolaus de Pulchro Monte, a man universally most learned in philosophy, in mathematics, in astronomy, in medicine, in theology also, and excellently skilled in many other disciplines of the scriptures. He has been so inflamed by the fame of your name that he has judged neither expenses to be spared nor the dangers and labors of a very long journey to be feared, so that he might come as soon as possible to your paternity. Having asked and received from me letters of recommendation to so great a reverence, last year he undertook a sufficiently dangerous journey to you, because of the unrest of the Sicambri, and came to your monastery of Sponheim on the twenty-fourth day of the month of February. But when your most reverend paternity was not found, he returned with great sadness to our town of Bruges, from which he had set out, leaving his letters and mine at Sponheim with your prior, to be presented to you at hand as soon as possible.

From this it came about that, both of us ignorant of the state of your affairs, we began to pass our life with much weariness, and of necessity ceased from inquiring about you. For it was being reported there that your most reverend paternity was with the margrave of Brandenburg in the March, but about your return to the monastery we heard nothing certain. At last, God so ordering it, from Master Nicolaus Beifelius, whom your paternity knows very well, canon of St. Germanus at Utrecht and doctor of medicine, who, coming from Italy, was with you at [Neometum?], we learned that you were healthy and safe, that your affairs had been well provided for, and that, after resigning the abbey of Sponheim, you had betaken yourself to [unclear], there in peace and tranquility to philosophize. On this we greatly congratulate you, knowing that you are by no means going to lead your life in idleness, but rather, in your usual manner, will always write something that may profit many unto salvation.

But the aforesaid Doctor Nicolaus de Pulchro Monte, who, because of his exceeding desire for you, always thinks of you when awake and dreams of you at night when asleep, can give himself no rest until at last he satisfies his purpose, by which he desires to see you and to mingle sweet conversations with you about true knowledge. For he is a man not unworthy of conversation with you, as one learned and skilled in many languages, and also experienced in many matters and in the customs of diverse peoples. Twice he has been in Jerusalem; he spent a considerable time in Turkey; at Venice also, Naples, Bologna, Paris, Oxford, Salamanca in Spain, and many other places he lived as a pilgrim for love of wisdom and of the scriptures. Therefore, so that at last satisfaction may be given to his holy desire, we both [unclear].
