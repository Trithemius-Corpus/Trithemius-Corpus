*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

*Peter the physician, archbishop of Mainz, dies.*

Master Peter of [Aspelt?], physician and archbishop of Mainz, a man magnificent in every respect, died; he newly founded the Carthusian house outside the walls of Mainz, and first established the college of canons of the church of St. Catherine at Oppenheim, endowing it quite magnificently. After his death the canons of Mainz petitioned for Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, as their prelate. But Pope John XXII, refusing to give consent to their petition, gave them as archbishop lord Matthias of Buseck, a professed monk of the Order of St. Benedict, an excellent man, worthy of such an honor. He was solemnly introduced at Mainz by lord Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, and by apostolic authority was honorably received by the clergy and people; and he presided for eight years. He immediately allied himself with Baldwin; strengthened by the help of his presence, he enclosed with a siege and took the town called [zu den Giessen?].

In the same year the citizens of Cologne, again proceeding to war on behalf of the Emperor Louis against Henry their archbishop, took his castle of Vrechem and utterly destroyed it.

*MCCCXXI.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Sigismund, which was the year 1321 of the Lord’s nativity, Frederick, duke of Austria, with his brother Leopold, entered Bavaria with a strong hand and a great army against King Louis, and devastated the whole region with fire and sword, everywhere inflicting many losses on the poor; meanwhile Emperor Louis kept himself in his fortifications, because of the absence of his army, beseeching strong fortune with prayers.

*MCCCXXII.*

In the following year, which was the fifth of Abbot Sigismund and the year 1322 of the Lord’s nativity, there was apprehended at Cologne a certain Lollard named Walter, the leading master of the school of heretics of that sect of which we made mention above, in the fifteenth year of Abbot Henry II. In the city of Cologne and outside it in many places he had secretly disseminated his errors, especially among little women, and had seduced many. He wrote little books in the German language suited to his errors, in which, by interpreting the words of sacred Scripture badly, he perverted them and applied them to his errors, and distributed them to women to read. When he was seized, however, he vigorously defended his errors; hence, condemned to the fire by sentence, he was burned, leaving behind, as he himself admitted, many hidden professors, cultivators, and disciples of his errors in the city of Cologne and outside it.

In the same year Isabella, wife of Edward king of the English, daughter of the late Philip IV, king of the French, seized Edward himself, her husband, and committed him to custody until death, because of the desolation, as she said, which he had caused in the kingdom with his servants.

*MCCCXXIII.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Sigismund, and the one thousand three hundred twenty-third year of the Lord’s nativity, Frederick, duke of Austria, again gathered a great army, more than five thousand fighting men from Austria and Hungary, intending to fight against Emperor Louis. Duke Leopold of Austria, his brother, met him with two thousand men, and, after many had been hired on every side, they entered Bavaria again and set about devastating everything. But Emperor Louis, having assembled a very great army of more than thirty thousand fighting men, together with John king of the Bohemians, Baldwin also archbishop of Trier, and Matthias archbishop of Mainz, met Frederick, his adversary, in Bavaria between Ottingen and Molendorf. There, battle having been joined, Duke Frederick was defeated, captured by the king of Bohemia, and presented to Emperor Louis under conditions of life; Louis committed him to custody in a certain very strong castle. Afterwards, while Duke Frederick was thus held in captivity, it happened that a certain magician came to Leopold his brother in Austria, promising that by his art, through the ministry of a spirit, he would bring Duke Frederick back to him from captivity safe and wholly unharmed in one hour, if he wished to promise and give him a fitting reward. He answered: “If you do what you promise,” he said, “I will honor you with a fitting reward.” And so, at an hour suitable to him, the magician entered the circle of conjuration together with the duke and called the spirit accustomed to obey his commands. When it appeared to him in human form, by the power of his conjurations he ordered it to bring Duke Frederick, freed from the emperor’s custody, back to him in Austria as quickly as possible without injury. The spirit answered him and said: “If the captured duke himself is willing to come with me, I will gladly obey your commands.” Having said this, the spirit at once flew off into Bavaria, flew through it after assuming the form of a pilgrim, and entered the prison where Duke Frederick was being held captive. When he had seen him, the spirit sent to him said: “If you wish to be freed from captivity, mount this horse; they will bring you safely and without injury into Austria to your brother Duke Leopold.”
