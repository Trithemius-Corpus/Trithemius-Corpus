*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

...wrote down the legends and histories of many saints, both in prose and in various kinds of verse. Of these we have made mention in the second book of the illustrious men of our order. Theodoricus too, a monk of the monastery of St Alban the martyr outside Mainz, is held to have been renowned among writers in these times; since he was a learned man, he wrote much both in metre and in prose, the titles of which we have recorded more fully elsewhere.

In these times there also flourished Lanfranc, formerly abbot of the monastery of Caen of our order, archbishop of Canterbury in England. Since he was, by the testimony of all learned men, the most learned man of his time in every secular and divine science, and had gloriously directed public schools among the French and the Italians for not a few years, at length, moved by the divine Spirit, he despised all earthly things and, receiving the habit of holy conversion in the monastery of Bec, humbled himself with such humility that he was thought by all to know nothing. For the man who had been great in the world wished, for Christ, to be seen as small in the monastery and to lie hidden in the abjection of humility. Hence, when one day he was reading according to the monastic rule while the brethren were seated at table, and in the course of reading pronounced something as he ought, the prior, who was not very learned, interrupted the reader's voice and, not knowing who he was, ordered him to read otherwise than he ought. But the servant of Christ Lanfranc, correcting what he had pronounced rightly, said [unclear]. The divine [unclear] could not conceal his learning [unclear]; obeying the command of his abbot, he held a public school for monks in the monastery, and left after him many very learned disciples. Then, after some years, he was made abbot of the monastery of Caen [unclear].

In the same year, on the sixth day before the Kalends of October, died Meginher, abbot of the monastery of Hersfeld, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters, in the German regions [unclear], great in the virtues of Christ, and in this age a singular example of monastic living. He was buried on the seventh day before the Ides of December in the monastery of St James, which he himself had built. He had for not a few years a lawsuit with Burchard, bishop of Halberstadt, concerning the tithes in Saxony. Against his dishonesty, when neither secular nor ecclesiastical laws had any effect, and when the wise abbot, having brought his complaint before the law, had told a story to deaf tribunals, at last he appealed to the tribunal of Jesus Christ the judge. When he died shortly afterward, the bishop, by divine vengeance, followed him with his bowels burst, in a pitiable death; yet before he died, wailing and crying out, he said that he had been summoned to God's judgment by the abbot of Hersfeld, and ordered the tithes to be restored to them. He also sought [unclear] from the monks through a messenger.

In these days there was also renowned Ruthard, monk of the same monastery, who succeeded the aforesaid Meginher in the abbacy of Hersfeld, a studious and learned man, since for a long time he had publicly presided over the monastic school at Corvey and left learned disciples after him. He had once been ordained abbot in that same monastery of Corvey, but, falsely accused, as is believed, of certain crimes, after losing the abbacy he had spent some years as a private person in various monasteries; at length, on account of his learning, he was made abbot at Hersfeld. He wrote a very fine little book *On the Tranquillity of Life*, in which he consoles himself by many forms of reasoning, saying that a mortal man ought not to be disturbed on account of whatever [unclear], and certain other works, the titles of which for mortals in other places I have already set down.
