*XI.*
*Of Johannes Trithemius. 523*

*To the Reverend Father in Christ, Lord Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, most revered teacher and friend, Rogerius Sycamber, claustral canon in Hegen, sends greetings.*

I received your letter, most learned Trithemius, exhorting me to the love of Christ, with great reverence and joy. Would that you might often send me such exhortations, by which I may always be able to advance in the love of God and become better than myself. For your letters are sweet to me and [unclear] than honey, nor can I read them so often that they do not please me more when reread.

But I rejoice greatly with you that you have found a place quiet from the cares of secular affairs and suited to your studies, hoping that from now on you will compose many things for the common benefit in leisure and monastic quiet, which previously at Spanheim, because of the manifold cares both private to the monastery and public to your order, you were compelled to interrupt. But I was greatly afraid, after you did not wish to return to your monastery of Spanheim, that you would betake yourself to the courts of princes, since there are many, as I have heard, who greatly desire to have you with them.

But what good man would not rightly be angry with your monks at Spanheim, reproach and curse them, who, out of their envy and malice, did not fear so injuriously to confound you, a man most gentle, excellent, and learned beyond all, dear to kings and princes, and to be revered by all good and learned men, such as the order of Saint Benedict has had no one for three hundred years, and to provoke you to indignation? I, no blind examiner of cloistered men, when ten years ago, with the consent and will of my superiors, at the request of your paternity, I stayed with you for the space of six months, as you know, in Spanheim, your monastery, and copied out for you, at your request, two volumes of my compositions with my own hand, carefully noted the manners and inclinations of your monks, and found almost all of them, except Henry of Cologne and Johannes Damius, of whom one is now dead and the other provost of the nuns in Neuenburg, to be haters and contemners of all good arts, and detractors of your useful studies.

I wondered at your moderation in food and clothing, and I took up your continual observance of regular discipline, together with your constant study of the scriptures, as an example for my spiritual edification; nor shall I forget it as long as I live, but I shall take care, according to my powers, to be your imitator. I have also heard from many that that envious Duke Johannes does not love learned men, nor has anyone in his court who is well disposed toward studious and learned men. I believe this to be true from the fact that, when I was with you in Spanheim at that same time, since for some cause unknown to me he necessarily had to write to the Roman supreme pontiff and to the college of cardinals, he could find no one in his whole duchy of such learning who could write in Latin. Afterwards, if I remember rightly, he was compelled to send his chancellor to you and to ask that you write in Latin to the pope and the cardinals.

Therefore I rejoice and am very glad that you have found a place suited to your studies, where there are many who take delight in your compositions and in your most lucid learning. Certainly I have no doubt concerning your monks in Spanheim, who were never worthy of you, that, with God, the just judge, taking vengeance, they will receive punishment for their rashness after a short delay of time. There is one thing that afflicts me with sadness in this change of yours: because, on account of the greater distance between the places, I cannot henceforth receive your letters so often.

Our Veldicus in Dirmstein, who is most devoted to you, greatly rejoiced when he heard that you had established yourself in monastic peace; for he feared, as I did, that, attaching yourself to the courts of princes, you would evade the quiet of cloistered life. He especially asks, moreover, that you cause some part of his works, after it has first been corrected by you, to come forth into the light through the printers for the common benefit of readers.
