*1347.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Wighard, which was A.D. 1347, on the day of St. Gereon the martyr, Emperor Louis IV died, and he was buried in the church of Blessed Mary ever Virgin in his city of Munich with pomp and honor. There were those who said that he had died by the poison of a certain duchess of Austria, whom, because of the ugly arrangement of her face, they called Maultasch, that is, “mouth-bag” or “sack”; whether this is true or not, we do not have it sufficiently established.

He left six sons, namely Louis, also called Otto, margrave of Brandenburg, the eldest by birth; Duke Louis, surnamed the Roman because he had been born at Rome; Duke Otto the younger; Duke Stephen; Duke William; and Duke Albert, whom he begot from Margaret, daughter of William, count of Hainaut, Holland, and Zeeland, as was said above in its place. How these six sons, after their father’s death, divided his duchy among themselves, we shall say below in the seventh year of Abbot Wighard. After Emperor Louis died, Henry archbishop of Mainz, although, as we said before, deposed by the pope, Adolf count palatine of the Rhine, Otto also called Louis, margrave of Brandenburg, and the other princes of Bavaria immediately came together; after taking counsel among themselves, they unanimously elected Edward, king of England, to the Roman empire against Charles. They also soon endeavored to summon him by letters and solemn envoys, promising that they would steadfastly provide him counsel and aid until death. Giving thanks to them, he sent back word that he was so occupied in governing the kingdoms of England and France that he could in no way attend to the empire of the Romans.

When these things were known, the aforesaid princes sent envoys with letters to the margrave of Meissen, son-in-law of the deceased Emperor Louis, and called him to the empire. Agreeing to them, he promised that he would come with an army as quickly as possible, although he did not come, having been skillfully forestalled or corrupted by Charles, as we shall say later.

*1348.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Wighard, A.D. 1348, when Charles learned that the margrave of Meissen had been elected against him as king of the Romans, as we said before, he nevertheless [bought him off?] from it for ten thousand pounds of silver. Hearing this, the aforesaid princes, gathered again at Frankfurt, elected Günther, count of Schwarzburg, a Thuringian, as king of the Romans.

In the same year Otto, bishop of Würzburg, died. Albert of Hohenlohe succeeded him and presided for twenty-four years; in the sixth year of his pontificate he surrounded the citizens of Würzburg, rebellious and disobedient to him, with a firm siege for three weeks and brought them back to obedience.

In the same year also William, count palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria, count of Hainaut and Holland, and lord of Frisia, entered the lands of the church of Utrecht with a very great army of his men; miserably devastating everything with sword and fire, he annihilated the whole province. At length he paid the penalty for his cruelty, being made, by God’s vengeance, [insane?].

In the same year also Conrad de Deck was treacherously killed by a certain knight, [Suikerus?] of Gödelfingen, in his own house, which he had in the Bavarian city of Munich, from long-standing hatred.
