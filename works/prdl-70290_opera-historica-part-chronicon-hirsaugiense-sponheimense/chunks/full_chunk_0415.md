Therefore William, Duke of Bavaria and Count Palatine, son of the aforesaid Emperor Louis, having become lord of Holland, ruled those counties for fourteen years, and used this title in his letters: William, by the grace of God Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, Count of Hainaut, Zeeland, Holland, and Lord of Frisia. This William was a proud, restless, warlike man, who inflicted many losses on the church of Utrecht; and after the death of the Emperor he also refused to receive his mother into those lands which she had lent him. Having become [unclear], he received from God, even in this life, the reward of his iniquity. From that time, therefore, down to the year of our Lord Jesus Christ 1425, the Dukes of Bavaria possessed the county of Hainaut, Zeeland, Holland, and Frisia for almost ninety years. When these had elapsed, John, Duke of Burgundy, who on his mother’s side was a son of the house of Bavaria, usurped those counties, as we shall say in its place; his heirs hold peaceful possession of them down to the present day.

*1356.*

In the fifth year of our Abbot William, when Henry of Virneburg, Archbishop of Mainz, was adhering to Louis the Bavarian, the excommunicated Emperor, against the prohibition of the supreme pontiff, and was defending him in his affairs with all his strength, and, though often admonished, would not desist, he was deposed in absentia by sentence of Pope Clement VI in a public consistory at Rome, and was stripped of every pontifical honor. Not accepting the pope’s sentence, and taking as coadjutor of the church of Mainz Cuno of Falkenstein, who afterwards became Archbishop of Trier, he conducted himself in all things as before. But the pope, after Henry had been deposed, promoted Gerlach of Nassau as Archbishop of Mainz; for eight years he did not come into peaceful possession against him and Cuno, his administrator, until Henry himself at last died. While this schism lasted for eight years, the church of Mainz was miserably desolated and suffered infinite evils: monasteries were plundered and destroyed, churches were stripped of their ornaments, and the clergy endured persecutions, fires, and irreparable depredations.

In this same year a dispute over the duchy itself arose between Reynard, Duke of Guelders, and his brother Edward. It lasted sixteen years, with the greatest loss to them and the desolation of the whole land. For as they often fought one another, many thousands of men were killed, villages were left deserted without inhabitants, castles were destroyed, and the land, with the peasants either killed or fled, was left uncultivated. At last, in the year of the Lord 1361, Edward captured Reynard and held the duchy alone, as long as he lived, without anyone’s prohibition, as we shall say below in its proper place.

Also in the same year Pope [Clement] VI, who had long since deposed Louis from the Empire, commanded the princes that, if they did not wish to lose the right of election, they should within a fixed time elect another emperor in place of Louis, the deposed emperor; otherwise he would of necessity make provision for the empire himself. When the princes had heard these things, Baldwin, Archbishop of Trier, Walram, Archbishop of Cologne, Gerlach of Nassau, for whom the pope had provided the archbishopric of Mainz against Henry, John, King of Bohemia, and the Duke of Saxony, meeting at Rhens beside the Rhine, ratified by sentence the deposition of Louis from the Empire. Then, remaining there, in the aforesaid year, the fifth of Abbot William, on the eleventh day of the month of July, which was the day of the translation of Saint Benedict the Abbot, they unanimously elected Charles, who had previously been called Wenceslaus, firstborn son of John, King of Bohemia, as King of the Romans. Leading him with great pomp to Bonn, they caused him to be anointed and crowned by the hands of the Archbishop of Cologne. For they could not come to Aachen, because the people of Aachen [unclear], although the pope immediately confirmed the election. For Adolf, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Margrave of Brandenburg, the Dukes of Bavaria, and almost all the imperial cities followed Louis.

Also in the same year, on the twenty-fifth day of the month of August, war was again waged between the kings of England and France over the duchy of Normandy. In it John, King of Bohemia and Count of Luxembourg, father of Charles, King of the Romans, although struck with blindness, was present in person, and fell after being wounded by an arrow of a certain Englishman. This King John was the son of Emperor Henry VII, of whom we spoke above, and the father of Charles IV. Through him the county of Luxembourg came to the kingdom of Bohemia, namely in the year of the Lord 1310.
