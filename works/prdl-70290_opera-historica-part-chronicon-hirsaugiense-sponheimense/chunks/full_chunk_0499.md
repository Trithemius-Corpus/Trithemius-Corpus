In it also there were more than twenty thousand citizens equipped for war, and an abundance of all things and immense riches. Therefore, when the city had been taken, Duke Charles spared no order, no dignity, no age. He had all the priests taken captive and presented to the bishop, and he even killed some. He consumed all the men, adolescents, and youths by the edge of the sword; the women with their infants, all the widows and virgins, he drowned in the river. O how many thousands of innocents were then killed, who had given cause for this neither by word nor by deed. The walls of the city, all the houses and dwellings, the whole city, the bridges, and all the buildings in it they stripped by fire, carrying away everything they had found: ornaments, books, chalices, monstrances, jewels, and bells. For they left absolutely nothing in the whole city, but, plundering everything, carried off inestimable booty. This miserable destruction of the city of Liege was so cruel that it cannot be sufficiently explained or written by us in any words.

Also in the year noted above, Ruprecht, archbishop of Cologne, oppressed by excessive poverty and want, since he had nothing from which to live, all the things that pertained to the episcopal table having been pawned by his predecessor, asked counsel and help from his canons and feudatories; but there was no one who paid attention, for some had already conceived hatred against him. Hence, compelled by necessity, he called upon his brother [unclear]. Accordingly Friedrich, Count Palatine, his brother, having learned of his necessity, sent him money, grain, and soldiers; with these auxiliaries he was able to recover the things unjustly alienated, and, as we shall say later, he recovered many of them.

*1469.* In this year, which was the fourth of our Otto, Friedrich, Count Palatine of the Rhine, with an army and engines of war, in aid of Ruprecht his brother, descended into the diocese of Cologne, and with a strong hand obtained very many towns and castles that had been alienated from the church of Cologne under title of pledge. These are the towns that he restored to the hands of the archbishop: Bonn, the toll; Neuss, the town; Lechenich, town and castle; Zuelpich, town; Bruhl, castle and toll; the castle of Deutz; Andernach, town; Rolandseck, castle and toll; Gudenberg, castle; Kempen, castle and town; Erpenrod, castle; Kaiserswerth, castle, town, and toll; Neet, castle; Papenberg, castle; Hersberg, castle; and many others. All the aforesaid towns and castles Friedrich the Count Palatine recovered by arms and threats, without money, from the hands of those to whom they had been pawned, and restored them to Ruprecht, archbishop of Cologne, his brother, and to the church of Agrippina. But for his expenses, which he incurred on behalf of the church of Cologne, he received the town and castle of Kaiserswerth, with its toll and all revenues, in pledge.

In the same year, since the notable monastery of our order in Wissembourg was being badly governed by Jakob de Ponte, abbot, [unclear] from the counts Antonium de Liningen, provost, and had come almost to utter poverty, the illustrious and most renowned prince Friedrich, the aforementioned Count Palatine, resolved to bring it back to a better condition and to reform it completely, at the instance, counsel, and direction of Eberhard, abbot of Michelsberg near Bamberg, and Hermann, abbot of the monastery of St. James near Mainz, of our order, visitors of the provincial chapter concerning the reform of Bursfelde, by expelling the unreformed monks and introducing others of the observance. Therefore, together with the aforesaid abbots, who had apostolic authority, consent, and a bull of the pope for the reform, he sent prudent and learned men from among his military councillors, and, with the consent of the citizens of Wissembourg, who had promised entirely to uphold the matter, he reformed the monastery. But behold what the devil did.

During the divine office, while Jodocus de Kalb, doctor of sacred theology and councillor of the prince, was preaching to the people, a tumult arose among the people against those sent by the prince, and the outcry of the stirred-up crowd was raised on high. At length, when the commotion of the populace had been quieted and satisfaction had been offered to the prince, the work begun was completed.
