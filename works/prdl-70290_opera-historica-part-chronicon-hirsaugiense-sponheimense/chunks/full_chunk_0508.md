In this year, on the third day before the Ides of August, Johannes, abbot of Bursfelde, died, the second in the reformation; Theoderic succeeded him, the third in the reformation, and presided for sixteen years.

In the same year, Frederick, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Adolf, Archbishop of Mainz, and Rudolf, Bishop of Würzburg, joined in alliance, brought out an army against those robbers of Rosenberg, Georg, Arnold, and Michael, together with his adherents. Stirred up by diabolical fury, they despised the prince’s commands, plundered all bishops whom they found anywhere, castrated priests by cutting off their genitals, and, carrying out depredations on the public road throughout all the land between Franconia and the Neckar, perpetrated many evils. They also forced the prince’s messengers who had been sent to them, in contempt of them, to eat the letters they had brought. Wherefore the aforesaid princes, moved by this, in the following year strongly besieged the castle of the aforesaid robbers, Bocksberg, for three weeks, battered it with engines, and took it, the robbers having escaped in flight by night with seventy horsemen. These things were done in the following year, namely 1470, on Thursday of the Lord’s Supper. After this they surrounded with a siege the very strong castle Schupf, and took it in eight days, which they utterly [overthrew?] on the octave Sunday of the Resurrection. And thus the rashness of those robbers, humbled and confounded, was thereafter compelled to obey the princes.

But because our present monastery of Sponheim was reformed in the year noted above, and Abbot Otto was deposed, it is worth the labor briefly to learn the manner, time, persons, and authors of its reformation, according to the sequence and order of time, especially wishing to bring it to the knowledge of [posterity?] by the testimony of writings.

*On the Reformation of This Monastery of Sponheim, and the Deposition of the Aforesaid Lord Otto, the Twenty-Third Abbot, and the Other Things Done Here at That Time.*

Therefore, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity noted above, 1469, in the second indiction, when the present monastery, because of the bad rule of the abbots and the dissolute life of the monks, had come to extreme poverty, and was wholly deprived of all its beauty both in spiritual and in temporal matters, the illustrious and most devout prince Frederick, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and Count in Sponheim, son of the late Duke Stephen and brother of Duke Louis the Black, who had his residence in Simmern, grieving over the ruin and desolation of this once distinguished monastery, resolved to subject it to reformation to the honor of God, and, according to his powers, to restore it in both estates to the observance of the Rule. Accordingly, with the consent and good will of the most renowned prince Lord Frederick, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and Prince Elector residing at Heidelberg, concerning whom many things have been said above, he obtained from Adolf, Archbishop of the church of Mainz, two commissioners for this work: namely Hermann of St. James near Mainz, and Conrad of Rodenberg of St. John in the Rheingau, abbots of monasteries of our order, with whose counsel and direction he might bring so necessary and holy a work to the desired end. Therefore the aforesaid Adolf, committing to them his authority in ordinary jurisdiction, to visit and reform the present monastery in spiritual and temporal matters, gave them such a mandate, furnished with his seal.

Adolf, by the grace of God Archbishop of the see of Mainz, Archchancellor through Germany of the Holy Roman Empire, and Prince Elector, to the religious and devout Hermann of St. James outside the walls of our city of Mainz, and Conrad of St. John in the Rheingau, abbots of monasteries of the Order of St. Benedict, our beloved in Christ, greeting always in the Lord. Among the other cares of solicitude incumbent upon our shoulders by the duty of the pastoral office entrusted to us, we strive especially to undertake those by which, according to the statutes of the sacred canons and the decrees of the holy Fathers, we are able fittingly to visit those faithfully subject to our care, to inquire more truly into their morals, state, and conditions, to confirm things well done, correct things badly done, restrain insolence, reform things desolate, and lead those who err back to the path of justice and truth, lest it happen that their blood be required from our hands. Indeed recently it has come to our hearing, not without displeasure of our heart, that the monastery in Sponheim, of our diocese and of the aforesaid order, is in many ways deformed in spiritual and temporal matters, and that its rectors have been marked by negligence in the execution of their offices, and deserve correction; matters which, without the application of due remedy, we ought not to pass over under dissimulation.
