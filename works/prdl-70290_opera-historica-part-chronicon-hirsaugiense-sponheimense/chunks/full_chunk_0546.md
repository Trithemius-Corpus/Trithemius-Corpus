*Chronicle of Trithemius*

...of the monks of our Order, which afterwards was printed [for?] the provincial chapter. *Printed minor works.* Also *Praises of the Carmelite Life*, one book, not yet printed. *In this year of Trithemius. The town of Bingen is burned by fire.*

In the same year also, on the holy day of Pentecost, a great fire arose by accident in the town of Bingen, which suddenly consumed more than two hundred and forty houses around the market, from the upper part of the town as far as the Rhine. The town hall also, together with letters, registers, and certain privileges, along with the chapels of Saint Lawrence and Saint Nicholas, was wholly consumed by fire; and the whole town would have perished had not the people of the Rheingau come to its aid.

In this year died Matthias, the renowned king of the Hungarians, the hammer of the Turks, who, waging many battles for the Christian faith, often triumphed over the forces of the Turks. After his death, when Maximilian, king of the Romans, was preparing to subject Hungary to his dominion, he was anticipated by Vladislaus, son of the king of Poland and king of Bohemia, who affirmed that he was the nearer heir of the kingdom of Hungary; and he obtained it, an agreement having been made between them.

In the same year René, duke of Lorraine, surrounded the city of Metz with a siege, but, not persevering long, he soon withdrew from there after raising the siege, with loss and damage.

*1491.*

In this year, which was the eighth of Trithemius our abbot, in the middle of the night on the third Sunday after the feast of Easter, the town of Montabaur in the diocese of Trier, together with its church, towers, and fortifications, having been set alight by accidental fire, miserably perished in the space of three hours, and many irrecoverable losses were inflicted upon people.

In the same year, when a certain wooden granary in this our monastery, consumed by excessive age, had collapsed, and it was necessary that another be built anew, after much deliberation had first been held, Johannes Trithemius the abbot began to construct anew not a wooden one, because it would last only a short time, but a stone one, which could remain longer. Therefore, in the aforesaid year he constructed from the foundations that great stone granary, which is visible up to the present in the upper part of the monastery, roofed with tiles.

At this very time grain was at a high price in these regions, so that one malder of rye was sold everywhere for two florins in gold coin.

Also in the aforesaid year, on the twenty-fourth day of the month of October, Johannes of Cologne the prior was deposed at his own insistence, and Nicolaus Andreae of Reinig, called of Luxembourg, was appointed the thirty-ninth prior of this monastery; today he is in the eleventh year of his priorate. Among all the monks of this monastery who are alive today, after Abbot Trithemius he easily holds first place in the study and knowledge of the Greek language; for he has perfectly learned both to read and to understand whatever is contained among the Greeks in sacred writings. He also knows perfectly how to write, as well as read, both Hebrew and Greek, and he does not interrupt the study of these literatures, as many do, but always continues it.

*Clingenmünster is transferred from the order.* In the same year the monastery at Clingenmünster, in the diocese of Speyer, was transferred from our order by a dispensation of the Supreme Pontiff obtained through false narratives, although there was no other cause than fear of regular observance. But not long afterward the abbot, leader of the apostates, was removed by a horrible death, just as all who presume such things against God have frequently been accustomed to die.

*The betrothed of the king of the Romans is carried off by the king of the French.* In the same year also Charles, king of the French, against law and equity, for love of the duchy of Brittany, carried off Anne, the only surviving daughter of the duke of Brittany, who had been solemnly betrothed to Maximilian, king of the Romans, and took her as his wife; Margaret, daughter of the aforesaid king of the Romans, whom he had previously solemnly taken as his betrothed and had long kept with him in France while she was a minor, and whom he had ordered everyone to call queen, was repudiated, not without injury. This deed generated the occasion of a great scandal among the people.

*Bingen trades.* Around these times the citizens of the town of Bingen had many dangers and losses, not only from the fire written above, but also from other occasions. For, blinded by excessive greed, in the preceding years they burdened with tolls, exactions, and other unaccustomed charges the market which they had been accustomed to hold on each Wednesday; they harassed with exactions beyond measure the clergy and monks, but also the poor coming from the Palatinate with grain; nor would they acquiesce, either to threats or to pleas, so as to lay aside the unusual burdens. Hence, when the rashness of this presumption, and of many abuses which it is pleasing to pass over, had come by the report of many people to the most just prince Philip, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, after taking the counsel of his men, he transferred the market from Bingen to the village of Münster, which at that same time he had purchased from the Rhine counts... *The market is transferred from Bingen to Münster.*
