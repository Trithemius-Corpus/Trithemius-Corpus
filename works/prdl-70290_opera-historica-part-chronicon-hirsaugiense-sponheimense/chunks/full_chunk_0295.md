He was elected on a Friday and, after the space of a month, confirmed; he received his benediction here in the monastery on the feast of the holy apostles Philip and James. Since either nothing or little concerning his deeds has been committed to writing, we too have been able to insert only a few things in this work of ours and make them known to posterity. We have set down the reason for this openly a little earlier.

In the aforesaid current year of the Lord 1354, which was the first begun year of this Wighard, Salmann, bishop of Worms, died. Theodoric succeeded him, the son of a certain knight, from the family of those who to the present day are called the Bavarians of Boppard, an ancient and formerly privileged family; he presided for nine years and had disputes with the citizens.

In the same year also, on the eighth day before the Kalends of December, Pope Innocent VI, before the third year of his pontificate had yet been completed, at the request of Abbot Wighard confirmed all and individual goods and privileges of this monastery, while residing at Rome at St. Peter’s.

*1355.*

In the second year of Abbot Wighard, which was the year of the Lord 1355, King Charles, having set out for Rome with a great company of princes, nobles, knights, and foot-soldiers, received, together with the queen, the crown of empire with great solemnity and tranquillity. There are those who write that he received the crown of empire from the hand of the cardinal of Ostia, sent for this purpose by Pope Innocent from Avignon.

In the same year, in the month of September, on a certain day around the ninth hour, a great earthquake occurred around Basel and Strasbourg; under its force a great part of the city of Basel was overthrown, and many surrounding buildings and walls and very strong castles in the mountains collapsed, and they wretchedly crushed many people.

Also in the aforesaid year, John, the third of that name, duke of Brabant, when he saw that he was about to die from illness, assumed the monastic habit of the order of St. Benedict; fortified with the saving sacraments, on the sixth day of the month of December he most devoutly rested in the Lord. He was buried in Veleer, a monastery of our order, leaving one only daughter, named Joanna, whom Wenceslaus, count of Luxembourg and brother of Emperor Charles, had taken as wife; with her he contended that the duchy of Brabant had passed into his right. Hence a grave dispute arose between the bishop and Louis, count of Flanders; at length they were reconciled by choosing as arbitrator William, count of Hainaut and Holland, son of the late Emperor Louis.

*1356.*

In the second year of Abbot Wighard, but the year of the Lord 1356, Emperor Charles, having returned from Italy, held a solemn assembly of princes in the city of Metz, where all the princes and nobles of the whole kingdom, even from France, gathered. Charles himself was known and dear to them, since he had long ago devoted himself to letters at Paris, and at one time, with his father the king of Bohemia, was about to fight in aid of the king of the French. Present at this solemn assembly of princes was a certain archbishop, legate of the Apostolic See, for confirming the peace of the whole kingdom; out of reverence for the supreme pontiff, he alone ate at one table with the emperor, while the other princes, the electors, served according to the nature of each one’s office, once, and afterward sat in their own place according to custom. There William, margrave of Julich, with the consent of the princes, was raised by Charles to be duke, and his margraviate of Julich was changed into a duchy with great pomp. Wenceslaus, too, count of Luxembourg, brother of Emperor Charles and son-in-law of John, the last duke of Brabant, whom we said died in the preceding year, was likewise elevated to be duke. This Duke Wenceslaus, son of John king of Bohemia, grandson of Emperor Henry VII, and brother, as we have said, of Charles IV, a man most noble in birth, learned in the education of letters, vigorous in military affairs, hardly second to anyone in life and character, magnificent in justice, clemency, and piety, was, by I know not what divine judgment, always unfortunate; he frequently had the outcome of all his principal actions contrary to his wishes and liable to losses. He obtained the duchy of Brabant with his wife, which he governed together with Luxembourg for almost 28 years; concerning his death we shall write later under the year of the Lord 1383.
