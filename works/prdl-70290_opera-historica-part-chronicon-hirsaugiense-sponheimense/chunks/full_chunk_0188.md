At last, when they could not withstand the force of the soldiers, they submitted themselves and the city to the emperor’s power, taking away no small hope from the Milanese.

In the same year, after the feast of Saint Remigius, the adversaries of the archbishop of Mainz entered a synod with armed force and wished to kill him. But when the counts, together with their men, resisted bravely, they were compelled to depart shamefully. For this reason Arnold himself, archbishop of Mainz, after the feast of All Saints, went to Lombardy, intending to make complaint about the matter to the emperor; and his adversaries, by no means inactive, followed after him.

In the same year Frederick II, archbishop of Cologne, died in Italy; his body was brought back to his homeland and buried in a certain monastery at Aldenburg. When he had been buried, the people of Cologne sent envoys to the emperor in Italy, asking from him Reinhold the chancellor, whom he had chosen as archbishop. The emperor, glad that he had found an occasion to confer honor upon him, consented with grateful mind and granted Reinhold as archbishop to those who asked. Not long afterward the same Reinhold, having received the emperor’s permission, came to Cologne, took possession of the archbishopric and all that belonged to it, and, after arranging affairs briefly, returned again to the emperor in Lombardy with three hundred chosen soldiers.

In the same year, when Pope Adrian was preparing to excommunicate Emperor Frederick because of the discord concerning the marriage, he was overtaken by death and departed this life in the month of October. After his death a great disturbance followed in the Church of God. For the cardinals, disagreeing among themselves, chose two pontiffs for themselves: Roland the chancellor, whom they call Alexander III, and Octavian, whom they named Victor IV. Alexander, the old vicar of Christ, approved in every respect the sentence of his predecessor Peter [unclear], but Victor favored the emperor very greatly. This Pope Alexander confirmed the Carthusian order and gave them certain privileges, receiving that order itself under the protection and defense of the holy Apostolic See.

*1160.*

In the aforesaid year of Abbot Manegold, not yet completed, which was begun as the year 1160 of the Lord’s Nativity on the octave of Epiphany, Emperor Frederick ordered a council to be held at Padua, at which he received Victor, who was present, as pope, but condemned Alexander, who was absent. When the council had been completed, Arnold, archbishop of Mainz, having received permission from the emperor, returned to Mainz. At last, when on the day of the Nativity of blessed John the Baptist he had gone out for devotion’s sake to the monastery of Saint James outside the walls of Mainz, Godfrey, abbot of that place, and Burchard, provost of Saint Peter’s, together with the other associates of the conspiracy, sent out assassins whom they had hired from ambushes, and they cruelly killed the archbishop in that same monastery, a great part of the citizens consenting. When the crime had been committed, the perpetrators of the monstrous offense withdrew with haste to the city; and immediately the counts and ministeriales of the murdered archbishop, together with the butchers, violently drove out the monks, plundered that monastery itself, set it on fire, and utterly overthrew all the dwellings of the monks. But Abbot Godfrey, with the rest of the accomplices of his impiety, had betaken himself into the city, where the authors of the crime, together with the clergy, though compelled by violence, set aside the fidelity which they had given into the emperor’s hands three years earlier and elected as archbishop Rudolph, son of Conrad, duke of Zähringen; and when, by the counsel of his men, he intended to appease the emperor with gifts, he stripped the church of its ornament.

At that time there was in the church of Mainz a most precious cross, which Willigis, archbishop of Mainz, in the time of Emperor Otto III, had ordered to be made for the adornment of the house of the Lord. Its crossbeam was of cypress wood, covered above with gold plates and precious stones. The large image, however, made wholly of the purest gold, weighed six hundred pounds of gold, as the verse carved on it showed, in words of this tenor:

This golden cross holds the following pounds of gold.

Marcelphus the archbishop took one foot of this image and sent it to Rome for the pallium. The other, with the legs, Arnold the above-mentioned archbishop took away, and with it rebelled against Hermann, count palatine.
