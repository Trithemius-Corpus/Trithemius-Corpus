*1508.*

In the second year of our abbot Nicholas, on the twenty-sixth day of February, the illustrious prince Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, Arch-Steward of the Holy Empire and Prince Elector, died and was buried in the church of the Holy Spirit at Heidelberg. He left seven sons: namely Louis, who after his father obtained the principality; Philip, bishop of Freising; Frederick, guardian of the sons of Rupert, his deceased brother, Duke of Bavaria; John, bishop of Regensburg; George, provost of Mainz, later bishop of Speyer; Henry, provost of Aachen; and Wolfgang. He also left surviving him four daughters: namely Elizabeth, who had first married William, landgrave and count of Hesse; when he died shortly afterward without children, she was given in marriage to Philip, Margrave of Baden. The second surviving daughter of the Palatine was Amalia; the third, Magdalena, not yet married; and the fourth, Catherine, a nun of our order in the monastery of Neuburg near Heidelberg, in the diocese of Worms, by the Neckar.

In the same year, a few days after the death of the Palatine, that is, on the sixth feria after the first Sunday of Lent, Albert, Duke of Bavaria, called “of Munich,” also died. His elder son, named William, later, in the fourth year of our abbot Nicholas, received the governance of his father’s duchy by the common consent of the procurators.

In the aforementioned year our abbot Nicholas took down the old wine-press house, which Abbot Conrad had formerly built of fragile wood and which was ruinous from excessive age, and built the new one which is now seen.

In the same year Lord Johannes Trithemius, formerly abbot among us but now abbot at St. James in the suburb of Würzburg, after he had already been summoned for the third time by messengers and letters, and after sufficient travel money had been sent, came to Emperor Maximilian at Mainz and farther on to Cologne. There, having found the emperor, he remained with him for almost three months, and afterward, rewarded by him, returned to his own place. What he did with the emperor, or why he was summoned by him, is not known to me.

In the aforementioned year Jacob, archbishop of Mainz, died; Uriel von Gemmingen, the dean, succeeded him, and in the following year, on Mid-Lent Sunday, when *Laetare* is sung, he was consecrated in the church of Mainz.

In the same year also, in the month of August, Hermann, archbishop of Cologne, died, a prince more munificent to the poor, and especially to cloistered persons, than all men of our age; on them he bestowed many benefactions. He reformed many monasteries in his diocese, both of virgins and of men, and bestowed many great benefactions on all cloistered persons who lived according to the purity of the monastic rule. In the town of his residence, Brühl, which is not many miles from Cologne, he built from the foundations, at his own expense, a new monastery of the Order of Friars Minor of the Observance, which he handsomely supplied with relics of saints, books, and necessary ornaments. He was succeeded in the archiepiscopate of the church of Cologne by the major dean Philip von Oberstein, son of the elder Wirich, who for a long time held the first place in the court of John, archbishop of Trier.

*1509.*

In the third year of our abbot, Emperor Maximilian, summoned into Italy by Pope Julius II, gathered forces and attacked the Venetians, who were not only rebels against the Empire but also disobedient and rebellious toward the Roman Church and Pope Julius himself just mentioned, and for that reason bound by the bond of excommunication and anathema; and he afflicted them with a great calamity. Yet before this fierce and long-lasting war began, [unclear] with the king of the French, Charles [unclear], whom he later called upon for aid against the Venetians, and he did many things with him powerfully and bravely. And so, around the beginning of the month of June in the aforementioned year, with Pope Julius and Louis, king of the French, bringing aid, Emperor Maximilian powerfully attacked the dominion of the Venetians, struck down many thousands of them, and forcefully subjected several of their very strong fortifications to his empire.

In the same year, in the month of May, an assembly of the princes and communities of the cities of the Empire was held at Worms, where Emperor Maximilian, through his orators, sought aid against the Venetians, enemies of the Empire; on that occasion he did not obtain it, although a long consultation was held. Thereafter, in the same year, on the twenty-sixth day of June, Emperor Maximilian again commanded the princes more earnestly by imperial letters to bring aid as quickly as possible against the Venetians, and refuted the reasons alleged against him at Worms.
