*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

In the same year Adelbero, a monk of this community, who some years before had been appointed scholasticus of the monks at St. Alban's in Mainz, by election of the brethren and promotion by the archbishop of Mainz, became abbot of the monastery of St. Ferrutius, which by another name is called Blidenstadt. In this same year Luithelm, monk and scholasticus at St. Aurelius, died, a man most renowned for life and learning, leaving after him many most learned disciples, many of whom became abbots in different monasteries, and some even bishops.

At about these times a monastery of our order in Saxony, called Ringelheim, was founded by Theodoric, count of that place. This count's daughter Mechthild was taken as wife by Emperor Henry I; she bore him three sons and four daughters: Bruno, who was afterwards archbishop of Cologne; Otto, the first emperor; and Henry, duke of Bavaria. Mechthild, the first daughter, was abbess of our order at Quedlinburg. Gerberga, the second daughter, first married Lothar, king of the Franks; after his death she married again, to Giselbert, duke of Lorraine. The third daughter, named Rixa, married Rudolf, duke of Bavaria. The fourth, named Hadwig, married Hugh, count of Paris, who afterwards became king of the Franks.

*922.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Rudolf, King Henry expelled from his borders the Hungarians who were ranging through Saxony to exact tribute, their armies having been cut down. In these times, after Luithelm, Dierhard, a monk, presided over the public school of the monks in this monastery, a man most learned in all literature, both divine and secular. He is said to have written some little works, but they have not come to our knowledge.

In these days there also flourished Gilderic, formerly abbot of the monastery of Saint-Germain of our order, bishop of the church of Auxerre, a man very venerable in morals and learning, who by his labors [unclear] many good things for the church entrusted to him. Rutger also, archbishop of the church of Trier after Rabbod, is held to have been distinguished in these times; among the other monuments of his talent he composed a book of decretals, which, producing it publicly in a synod before his suffragans at Trier, he approved.

Around these times Emperor Henry founded [the church of] Meissen, and placed an episcopal see in it. He honored its bishop with great privileges, and gave him many lands, villages, estates, and castles, so that through him he might more easily be able to resist the Hungarians when they raided the land. Meanwhile also, when he had made [peace?] with the Hungarians for nine years, he saw to it that many cities in Saxony were made very strong with walls and ditches, so that if the enemies broke in again, the Christians might defend themselves by the protection of walls.

*924.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Rudolf, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 924, a certain monk of this community, named Adelhard, died. Once under Abbot Haderad he had been fully instructed in divine and secular writings; but by some occasion or other he fell into such an affliction of the head that after two years he became blind. When the light of sight had been lost, however, the pain of the head also ceased, and apart from blindness the man became very healthy. Thereafter, since, now made blind, he had lost in great part the sweetness of the present life, he made a virtue out of necessity, devoted himself wholly to divine exercises, and strove with all the virtue he could to show himself a faithful worshiper of God. From then on no one ever saw him intent on worldly business, frivolous in conduct, or idle in speech; he was always intent either on prayer or meditation, though more often on contemplation. He is said to have been of such compunction and devotion that whenever anything about the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ was said or read before him, at once dissolved in tears he would cry out with a groan, so that he also stirred others who were present to weeping.

His way of life was so pure and pleasing to God that he deserved to receive the spirit of prophecy. For he often foretold many future things, which the outcome proved true. Indeed, three years before he was to die, he foretold so clearly the [unclear] of the monastery, which followed almost eighty years later, as if he saw it present before his eyes. At his passing, as the monk Meginfrid writes, the sweetest voices of angels singing were heard in the air; lamps were seen at night over his tomb, and certain other signs of sanctity. He died in the aforesaid year, on the seventh day before the Kalends of January, in the twenty-third year from when he had lost his sight, but the seventy-eighth of his age, in the first indiction.
