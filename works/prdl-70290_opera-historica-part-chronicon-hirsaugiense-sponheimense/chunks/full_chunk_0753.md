*Familiar Letters, LIII.*

John Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James at Wuerzburg, sends greetings to Bartholomew Lescanius, priest.

Most loving man, to your letter, in which you give me great thanks for your health, what else can I, or ought I, reply except what John Mesue the physician says in the preface at the beginning of one of his compilations: God alone heals illnesses, and from the soil of frailty, in his abundance, he has brought forth medicine, blessed, glorious, and exalted. Therefore thanks must be given not to me but to God, since in his goodness he not only heals the sick, but, being almighty, even gives life to the dead. I, however, profess myself not a physician but a Christian monk; if his counsels have benefited you in any way, he deservedly must be blessed and praised, from whom we have received all the good that we understand.

Know that the remembrance of me which you testify that you continually keep, when by interceding for my sins you turn to God, is most pleasing to me: namely, that whenever at the altar, about to offer to almighty God the sacrifice of our redemption, you celebrate the remembrance of your friends, you should also say mentally in prayer: I beseech you, holy Lord, almighty Father, eternal God, through the pain of the wounds of Jesus Christ our Lord, remember also your servant, though wicked, John Trithemius the abbot, and whatever he has sinned against you, through the blood of the same your Son mercifully forgive, your Church petitioning through me.

If you keep this remembrance of me diligently, dearest Lescanius, you have given me the greatest possible benefit. I rejoice with you no little that you have found the favor and grace of a good prince; in his service it is fitting for you to live in such a way that you never put off the progress and salvation of your soul for the enticements of the flesh, nor love the advantage and peace of the world so much that you forget the consolation of the heavenly kingdom. As for my return to the March, just as I do not dare to promise it to you friends who ask for it together with the prince, so I do not rashly deny it. For God, whose will and volition it is the salvation of every creature to follow, can do all things. But no determinate knowledge of future things is given to mortals. Farewell, mindful of me. Wuerzburg, the penultimate day of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*LIV.*

To the most reverend father in Christ, lord John Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Wuerzburg, his most dear teacher, brother Theodoric, monk of Fulda, offers reverence and honor, with his most humble commendation of himself.

Reverend father and most loving teacher, on hearing that your paternity had received another abbacy and resigned Spanheim, I was beyond measure troubled in spirit, wavering within myself and in a certain way divided; I became saddened and glad. Saddened indeed for this reason: that the envy of your rivals was permitted to prevail so far that it gave you occasion to leave your monastery, which by your talent, writings, and books you rendered notable, known, famous, and distinguished throughout almost the whole world. For what was the monastery of Spanheim before your reverend paternity was placed over it? It lay hidden in obscurity and darkness, so that for more than ten miles around there was no regard or knowledge of its name. By your studies you made it glorious and known far and wide, as I saw for myself, who served as a disciple under your instruction for more than three years: from the school of Paris in Gaul, from Louvain in Brabant, from Tuebingen in Swabia, and also from Italy, Germany, and other more remote places of the earth, very many most learned men, and princes and bishops too, stirred by the fame of your name, flocked eagerly to Spanheim, not without the advantage and profit of that monastery. All these certainly did not come for this reason, to see the monastery; indeed, what must be held without doubt, they came there because of you and your learning. You also adorned the monastery with new and very beautiful buildings, and with many most precious books; concerning these I am deeply pained, that they must remain in the possession of those unlearned and most ungrateful monks of Spanheim.

But I rejoice and congratulate your paternity no little that you have found a quiet place suited to your studies, although I would rather have advised that you remain with the most illustrious prince in the March, who, as it then seemed to me, was offering you terms not to be despised. Blessed be God, who has granted peace to your paternity and has now left your adversaries in their confusion. They thought that they could bend the mind of your paternity to their side, but they were in no way able to do so.
