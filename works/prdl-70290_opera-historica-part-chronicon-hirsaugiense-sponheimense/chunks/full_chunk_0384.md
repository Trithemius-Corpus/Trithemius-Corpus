Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, confirmed him and had him blessed by his vicar-general in pontificals. Dietlib, ordained abbot, governed this monastery for ten years and six months.

He was a native of the city of Trier, born of honorable parents; having been set to letters in childhood, he advanced not a little in learning as he grew older. When he had become more mature, with progress in his studies, at the age of twenty he was received into the chancery of Henry, bishop of Trier, in which he distinguished himself wisely for some years, and gained by experience much wisdom in conducting curial business. After this, John, Count of Sponheim, having tested the man’s prudence, took him as his chancellor and chief secretary of his affairs, and relied greatly on his counsels as those of a prudent and experienced man. Finally, when he was held by a very severe illness of fevers, he vowed a vow of religion to the Lord; he recovered, and so became a monk under John the Second. When John died three years later, he succeeded in his place by the election of the brothers.

He was a good and pious man, very experienced in business; understanding very well that private property was not permitted to monks, he at once, at the beginning of his rule, cut out this vice by the roots, so that, communal fellowship having been restored, the religion of this monastery seemed in no small part to have been brought back to its former state, although not all other things were reformed to regular purity, because at that time things which we weigh were held in less esteem.

1391. Dietlib the abbot, in the first year of his rule, deposed John, the sixteenth prior of this monastery of Sponheim, because he seemed to be somewhat opposed to the holy reform and to the renunciation of private property. John, deposed from the priorship, bore it patiently; not long surviving, he rested in the Lord in the same year. After him Dietlib the abbot appointed as the seventeenth prior Albert, a monk of holy conduct, whose father was John, knight of Bleinich, and whose mother was Elizabeth, daughter of Henry, knight of Sponheim.

This Albert, who [unclear], was a man sufficiently learned, sufficiently inward in morals and conduct, most devout in prayer and compunction, who never consented to the pursuits of the wicked but always strongly opposed them, although he benefited others little. Once appointed prior, he took care with all zeal always to obey the commands of the rule and of the abbot, to cut out the vice of private property by the roots, and, as far as he could, he constrained the monks by word and example to the observance of the rule. Among others he had as a notable imitator his sister’s son Henry, who later succeeded him in the priorship. He himself presided in the priorship for four years.

In the same year Bohemund, archbishop of Trier, died on the fourth ides of November and was buried in the monastery of Henrad, of the Cistercian order. This Bohemund built from the foundations a new castle at Neumagen near Trittenheim. After him Diether of Nassau, brother of King Adolf, neither elected nor postulated, was substituted, ordained, and sent to Trier as archbishop by Pope Boniface VIII, out of hatred for King Albert; he presided for nearly eight years. He was of the order of Friars Preachers. On all the days of his pontificate he opposed Albert, king of the Romans, but, being far inferior in strength, he could do nothing against him. He did not rule usefully; therefore he stirred up the hatred of all against the people of Trier.

In the same year, on the vigil of Saint James the Apostle, Emicho, bishop of Worms, died and was buried in his greater church of Worms, in the monastic habit, as they say. Eberwin of Cronenberg succeeded him in the episcopate and presided for three years and nine months; at the urging of his citizens he consented that [unclear] from the community should be admitted to the council of the senate. In his time the monastery of nuns of the order of the Friars Preachers was founded at Liebenau outside the walls of Worms, not far from Neuhausen toward the south, by a certain citizen of Worms called Jacob Engelman; he himself confirmed the deed.

1392. In this year, which was the second of Abbot Dietlib, Willichco, brother of the wife of Count Henry of Sponheim, came to the religious life, a noble man, wise and very religious. Hearing the good reputation of Abbot Dietlib, namely that he had taken care to reform this monastery so diligently, he became a monk under him and at length succeeded him in the abbacy; concerning him it will be said below in its place.
