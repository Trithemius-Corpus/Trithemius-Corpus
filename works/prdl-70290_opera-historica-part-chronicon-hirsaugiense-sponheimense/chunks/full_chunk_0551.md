But since no one, however learned, could understand either the principles or the explanations of the things that were promised there, they expressed an uncertain judgment. Some proclaimed Trithemius a divine man, others a most learned one; some, whose understanding was slower and whose will was readier for rashness, asserted that he was a magician and did these things by the ministry of spirits. But how purely natural are all the things that are reported in that same letter he disclosed at the beginning of the book of the *Steganography*. Moved by this novelty, many men from the farthest borders of Gaul came here to see and speak with Trithemius; and when they had seen the books and the things themselves, the report of which they had heard from afar, and when Trithemius had given a very clear demonstration, as was fitting, they confessed that the report had fallen far short of the truth of the facts. Some also, though far distant, sent their messengers with letters to Trithemius, desiring to know the truth of the matter which they had known only by report.

To some of these Trithemius wrote back in such a way that they understood that the operation of his marvels was possible naturally and without superstition, though they did not know the principles of the art itself or the method of working such great marvels through it. Among those, however, who approached Trithemius by letter concerning these matters, Vlascus the Portuguese, certainly a very learned man, dealt with Trithemius in his letter with such subtlety, such learning, and such variety of arguments that no one more acute than he in proposing questions of such matters was ever found by Trithemius.

In the same year, on the ninth day of the month of September, the canons both of the major church and of the other churches of the city of Worms, unanimously, after carrying away their goods, went out from Worms, on account of the wrongs done by the citizens, who rashly violated the privileges of the clergy. Today the third year after their departure is in progress, but nothing has yet been found to have been done about their return into the city. Often the most unconquered Prince Palatine of the Rhine interposed his offices for concord, but because of the obstinacy of both parties he accomplished nothing. They keep very careful watch at the gates, lest any of the canons who had gone out of their own accord return to the city of Worms against their will.

In this same year Maximilian, king of the Romans, after suspending for the time being the war of Guelders, moved his whole army against the Swiss, who, beginning rebellion in the preceding year, had inflicted much damage on the king without his knowing it; and coming in the month of March to their borders, they had several battles with one another, so that more than twelve thousand are reported to have been slain on both sides. There Count Pitsch was killed by the Helvetians, another of Westerburg, together with many noble and valiant men. At length, after many dangers and inconveniences, a concord was issued in the month of September.

In the same year died Wilhelm, landgrave of Hesse, son-in-law of the most illustrious Prince Palatine, without children. His wife returned to her father and laments the hardships of her widowhood down to the present day, since she lost so quickly a husband most loving to her, with no offspring [unclear]. Between Wilhelm, son of his paternal uncle and his successor, and the above-named most illustrious prince Philipp, count palatine, father of the widow left by the deceased prince, a dispute arose by reason of the dowry assigned to the daughter of that Palatine by the deceased before the marriage was undertaken. Hence they held several diets at Mainz, Frankfurt, Worms, and most recently now at Würzburg, but they have not yet, down to the present day, been able to come to agreement.

*1500.*

In the seventeenth year of John Trithemius, our abbot, the following crosses appeared in the linen garments of men, in shirts, in women’s veils, in table linens, not only in those that were in use, but also in those which, shut up in chests, seemed to have no influence of [unclear]. Likewise they often appeared on corporals, altar palls, and albs. They were first seen in Sobernheim and Meisenheim, with the greatest fear and horror of the common people, so suddenly that sometimes, while the *Salve* was being sung in church, thirty people were found to have crosses who had not had them before. Afterwards they appeared in Kreuznach and the villages lying round about, then in Bingen and in Mainz, and consequently in many places of Germany. The crosses were small, of varied and confused color, as if cloth had been smeared with some grease of mixed color; nor could they be erased by any washing, but after the ninth or tenth day they faded away and vanished of themselves. After two years a great plague of men followed these crosses in many places.
