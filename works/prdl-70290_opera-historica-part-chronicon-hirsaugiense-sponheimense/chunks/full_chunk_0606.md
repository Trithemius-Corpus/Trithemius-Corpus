“It is not,” he says, “the part of every man, but only of the wise man, to endure poverty. Let greedy, luxurious, ambitious men, who do not consider that they are mortal, have riches and be possessed by them. Let us follow the ways and life of the lovers of Christ, who admonishes us that, having food and clothing, we should be content. We would deservedly be burdened by want if the rich were either immortal, or if, when dying, they carried away with them the riches they had loved. But now, since they themselves die and, naked, receive burial along with the poor, my poverty does not greatly burden me, since, being about to depart from here, I trust that I can be all the less saddened in proportion as I have possessed in this world nothing transitory that I might love.

But the exercise of the mind toward the understanding of truth, which through the study of the Scriptures is pursued in this life, with ordered delight, toward the final end by studious men and contemners of vanities, brings no bitterness to those about to die, since they understand very well that the perfection of their understanding, which they desire to attain by study, cannot be completed before they are released from the corruption of the flesh. For the human intellect is like an unpolished precious stone: if it is polished it will become capable of light, and will be beautiful, bright, and transparent, in which the beauty of God may shine forth. Yet in this mortal life there cannot be a perfect polishing of the human intellect. Nevertheless it has certain degrees and fixed modes assigned to it by nature, by which, after death in the purgatory of souls, whatever has here through negligence been less applied to its polishing is necessarily added. Therefore our stone is polished here imperfectly, more perfectly in purgatory, but most perfectly in heaven. And so the common study of all ought to be to exercise and polish the intellect, so that a pure and upright understanding of the universal principle, which is God our creator, the never-failing light of the intellect, may arise.

The continual reading of the Scriptures shows us the way of this exercise, if in reading we do not interrupt the practice of prayer, and if in praying the mind is raised to God through the longing of love. Let us look at the ancient wise men of the gentiles, to pass over our own in the meantime, and, comparing their studies with ours, measure both in an equal balance: it will appear clearer than daylight that, in comparison with them, we should be laughed at rather than called studious. Demosthenes, when he had completed seven years of age above a hundred and was now dying, groaned that he would die at the age at which he had first begun to be wise. Socrates, the teacher of Plato, in his ninety-eighth year of age received [unclear] while studying. Cato the Roman, studious at all times, began Greek as an octogenarian. The tongue of Nestor, as Homer bears witness, when he was already aged and decrepit, poured forth speech sweeter than honey in a public assembly.

But let us come to our own men, who at the beginning of Christian philosophy handed down to us the norm of the studies of the saints, so that we may see how little our own estimation of ourselves amounts to. Adamantius Origen, son of Leonides, martyr of Christ, a man most studious of all in the divine Scriptures, is said to have worked out six thousand books in the Greek language, and to have persevered in the study of the Scriptures always, up to extreme old age, poor and humble for the love of Christ. Augustine the African, bishop of Hippo, the most holy founder of your order, a man truly the most learned of all the learned, is found to have written more than one thousand thirty books or treatises. Ambrose, bishop of Milan, is said to have published three hundred treatises. Jerome, the most holy priest of Bethlehem, besides the edition of the New and Old Testament, is read to have published more than six hundred books and treatises.
