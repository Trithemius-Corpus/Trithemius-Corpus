Rather, I yielded of my own free will; but the reason why I wished to do so was furnished by the ingratitude of the monks, about which I remember writing to you long ago from Cologne at very great length. But now I live here much more quietly than at Sponheim, free for myself and for the reading of the Scriptures; poor indeed in possessions, but, as it seems to me, rich in spirit, since I have never loved the vain occupations of the world. Your urging me to come to you, promising me all the necessities of this life as long as I live, is the greatest token of love toward me; for this I give you, as far as I can, immortal thanks, and I shall repay them if ever the opportunity is given me. But to come down to you and remain there thereafter, which you seem especially to desire, you know does not accord with my profession, since I am bound to the monastic religion and sworn to the words of my most holy father Benedict. For it in no way accords with my purpose to leave monastic solitude and to dwell continually in the world with secular men, however good and just they may be. For although perhaps there would be no danger for me to live together with you there, yet it would be scandalous to many in my religion, since just as not all things please all men, so neither are all things expedient for all men. I have therefore resolved to abide in some monastery of regular order and observance, lest I give anyone occasion to think ill of me. Otherwise I would have had not the least among the great princes of the world to provide for me most abundantly in the necessities of the present life, if I had wished to enroll myself among their cares and service. Hence, even to this day, I have known very many who, with a fixed annual stipend proposed, invite me quite richly to their courts. But life is not for fish on dry land, nor is conversation without a cloister safe for a monk. Therefore, lest I make myself a liar, I chose, poor and cast down, to dwell in a monastery, rather than to appear rich and glorious with princes in the world. For what is more fitting for a dead man than to lie hidden in a tomb? And a monk who has once renounced the world: what do you think befits him more than that, despising all the business of the world, quiet in body and tranquil in mind, free for himself and for the divine Scriptures, he should remain in a monastery? For the greatest and principal cause why I resigned the abbacy of Sponheim, full of useless worldly cares, and took up this one of Saint James, than which there is none poorer in the order, was love of solitude, peace, and tranquillity; not so that I might be carried off into idle leisure, but so that I might be able to apply myself more freely to the study of the Scriptures. Nor indeed did my spirit find the envy of rivals so far insuperable that I could not easily have mastered it by the favor and aid of the most serene prince Philip, Count Palatine, if I had wished, especially since all the fathers of the order of the observance desired nothing more than that I should not abandon the abbacy of Sponheim. Hence they sent to me at Budenheim two abbots from the synod which they were holding at Mainz, to persuade me to return to the monastery. But I, moved no less by love of peace and tranquillity than by consideration of the injuries, kept fixed within myself what I had resolved, patiently enduring the occasion given me, by which it might at last, even if late, remove me from the vain and restless occupations of the world. And so, by the bounty of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is the savior of faithful souls, I have attained the peace which I always desired; I now enjoy the peace I longed for; and I shall follow peace as long as the spirit of life remains in me in the flesh. Nor have I thought the poverty of the present place which I have taken up hard to bear, knowing that when the brief span of this life is finished, in the end nothing from all the things possessed in the world will pass with me from here. Therefore, having food and clothing, the Christian is commanded to be content, because those who desire to become rich fall into various temptations of demons. But a monk who loves riches is neither a monk nor a Christian, but rather an apostate and unbeliever, because he both transgresses the Gospel and, as a despiser of his vow and of the monastery, lies to his Lord. But farewell, and also make me more certain of your affairs. From Würzburg, on the twenty-sixth day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.
