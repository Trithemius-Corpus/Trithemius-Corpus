*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

It remains to compare what you have, to complete it, and to enjoy the most honorable men to satiety; and finally see to it that they visit our house. Farewell happily and prosperously, and love us, who love you dearly. From Lubus, on the 27th day of the month of January, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1506.

*LVIII.*

*To the most reverend father in Christ, Theodoric, bishop of Lubeck, Johannes Trithemius sends greeting.*

Iambicus, who had been entertained very honorably among us for almost three months, as the beauty of his face will show, we have permitted to return to you, his lord, now that the duty of his embassy has already been well discharged, most holy prelate. He agreed willingly, although he by no means disliked our lodging, since, having long enjoyed our familiarity, he is now touched by no fear. He does not fear monastic censure, inasmuch as, accustomed to our ways by long habit, he now understands very well that we imitate the counsel of the wise man, whereby we know how to have conversation with each person according to his capacity. For we know, on the testimony of Iamblichus, that neither must one treat with a monk about philosophy, nor among philosophers must one dispute about the religion of cloistered men. We keep each thing in its own place, exercising the monk with the former, the philosopher with the latter. This one thing perhaps could have been troublesome in our hospitality, that in Ursul he remained alone with us, he who had been accustomed to live in our monastery with almost three thousand. For here we are sojourning away from our own people among a few, philosophizing not as we wish, but as we can. Receive your Iambicus, most learned prelate, whom we send to your house, giving thanks for your kindness toward us; and use our service as you please. Farewell, and regard us as commended to you. From Ursul, on the 4th of the month of February, in the year 1506.

*LIX.*

*Johannes Trithemius to Hermann Bu[unclear]schius, poet of Munster, greeting.*

When we were making a journey from the regions beyond the Rhine through the town of Leipzig to the March of Brandenburg, it was by no means clear to us that you were there. For we had heard at Cologne that you were professing your letters at Louvain, and therefore at Leipzig we made no inquiry for you. But now, through the report of Georgius Sibutus the poet, we have learned that you have been provided on the best terms for the youth of Leipzig in more elegant letters. We congratulate you that your affairs have been well established, admonishing you of this one thing: that you must live in such a way that you do not destroy learning by your morals. It does not befit an educated man, even if he is a poet, to be wanton, a follower of drunkenness, womanish, and given to luxury; all of which things quickly destroy a man, as someone sang, saying:

[unclear]

Wine and baths, and love concerned with Venus,
prepare a swifter road to hell.

This also that Maro of yours is said to have warned most beautifully in his little treatise on wine and Venus, where he completes many matters in a few words, saying:

Do not be seized by love of Venus or of wine,
for in one way wine and Venus do harm.
As Venus by force enervates the powers, so an abundance of wine
weakens the steps and enfeebles the feet.
Love compels many men, when overcome, to confess secrets;
mindless drunkenness discloses what is hidden, etc.

When Anacharsis was asked by what means someone might become abstinent, he answered that, if he were sober, he should set before his eyes the shameful movements of drunkards. For he says that the vine bears three grapes: the first of pleasure, the second of [unclear], the third [unclear] a famous saying. The first bowl, he says, pertains to thirst, the second to cheerfulness, the third to pleasure, the fourth to madness. Nothing is more shameful than a drunken man, who has a stench in his mouth, trembling in his body, frenzy and forgetfulness in his mind, who promises stupid things and brings foolish hidden matters out into public; his heart throbs, his face reddens with blood.
