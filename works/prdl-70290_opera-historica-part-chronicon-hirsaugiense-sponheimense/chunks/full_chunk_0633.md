...which, since we often live according to the flesh, may seem contrary. But woe to our prior, swollen with such a vice of ambition; his progress will never be good in the abbacy into which his entrance was perverse. Let him think what he will with his Cynonoti; I cannot but favor the party of the most merciful prince Philip, Count Palatine. For he is a lover of all learned men, gracious, gentle, and always generous to all. By his virtue I could easily ward off the injuries of the Cynonoti, if I did not fear him who said: “Vengeance is mine, and I will repay.” Rather, I shall give place to wrath for a time, until either the rash men repent of their injuries, or we deliberate in due time what it is useful for us to do. For for every business there is a time and an opportunity. And many things that cannot be overcome by strength are conquered by time. As Plato also says:

[Greek verse unclear.]

Time bears all things; long time knows how to change name, and form, and nature, and fortune.

But Sophocles the comic poet also said most truly in the Ajax:

[Greek verse unclear.]

All things grow slack in a long time and are consumed; it brings hidden things into the light and hides manifest things. The day casts down and again raises up all human things.

If the almighty has willed that I be struck by adversity, who am I to contradict him? Or how great am I, that with his help I should not bear it patiently? I myself am prepared, the Lord Jesus knows, and I am not troubled: for the glory of his name, also for my sins, and for love of the heavenly kingdom, not only willingly to give up the abbacy and all favor of this world, but even to subject myself to utter poverty and devote myself to beggary. I have endured undeserved injuries and insults from the prince of the Cynonoti; although I could turn them back upon his own head, I preferred to embrace patience rather than stir up vengeance against the will of God. There is a God in heaven who sees the injuries that I suffer without cause; I do not doubt that he himself will avenge them. For time will bring all things to light, as Menander says:

[Greek verse unclear.]

Time is the judge of merit among men. In time also all things are done and judged. Therefore no counsellor is more useful than time.

For nothing that has an end is long; and compared with eternity, all time is short. I know that I am mortal, and that I must necessarily die soon; and therefore, since one of the two must be, I prefer to suffer injuries rather than inflict them. For St. John Chrysostom says: Whatever we shall have suffered from any human being, either we shall receive remission of all sins, or, if there have not been such great offenses in us, we shall be honored with more splendid crowns.

For thirty years, in peace and tranquillity, I may say usefully, I have devoted myself to Christian philosophy. Day and night I have spent almost continuous study in reading writers, as is known to all who have known me; but how much I have profited, experience will now for the first time show. All the time that I studied has prepared for me the hour of temptation, which imposes the necessary voyage. I must test what sort of skilled sailor I have become, who for so many years have not spared the labors of learning. More than two thousand volumes, not common ones, nor only printed ones, but, as you know and as is evident, very ancient ones, and written by the pen, I have gathered with the greatest diligence and at very heavy expense into the library of Sponheim. In reading and most studiously turning them over for nearly twenty-three years, what I have profited, this time demands that I declare. For, as the wise man has long since usefully said, the learning of a man is discerned through patience. If therefore I come through such great adversities patient, I shall judge that I have learned enough. And indeed the spirit is ready, but the flesh is weak.

Therefore, dearest brother, with what charity I can toward you, I ask that you assist me with your prayers and diligently intercede for me with the mercy of the Almighty, so that through your prayers he may grant me constancy in adversities, may also grant pardon for my sins, and may make me willing to follow his will in all things. I have sent letters to the prior, by which I have summoned him to me at Neumagen; if you wish to write back anything, you can. Farewell, from Neumagen, on the thirteenth day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1505.
