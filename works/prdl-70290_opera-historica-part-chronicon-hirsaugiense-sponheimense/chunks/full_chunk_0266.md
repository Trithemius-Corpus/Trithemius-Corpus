*206. Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

In the same year Albert, king of the Romans, entered the lower parts of Germany with a strong hand, with the mind and intention of making war on John, count of Hainaut, who, as we have said, had occupied Holland. And when he had come to Nijmegen, a town of Guelders, he caused letters such as these to be published throughout the whole region: “Let all men know that Albert, king of the Romans, ever unconquered, has powerfully incorporated into the Roman Empire the vacant principality of Holland, which Emperor Charles, once called the Great, gave as a fief to Count Theoderic.” But when John heard that the king was about to come against him with an army, he gathered a very great multitude of armed men. The king, fearing his boldness and at the same time his strength, entered into peace with him through the mediation of the archbishop of Cologne, and granted him the county of Holland, always to be received as a fief from the Empire.

In the year noted above, while the Jubilee was still continuing at Rome, lord Gotfrid, abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, died in the twenty-ninth year of his rule and the sixty-eighth of his age, a good, mature, prudent man, who governed honorably enough and usefully.

*Concerning Henry the Second, Thirtieth Abbot of This Monastery, Who Presided for Seventeen Years; Concerning Various Deeds in His Time*

When Abbot Gotfrid had died and been buried, the brethren, bereft of a shepherd, assembled together at the time already appointed by the senior monks through the prior. After many counsels and various deliberations, at last they unanimously elected as the thirtieth abbot of this place Henry, the second of that name. Confirmed and ordained, as is customary, by Frederick of Bolanden, bishop of Speyer, he governed this monastery badly for seventeen years, and at last, by command of Emperor Louis IV, was deposed because of the deformity of his life and the squandering of the substance of the monastery, as we shall say in its proper place. He was by nation a Swabian, born of respectable parents; when he was eighteen years old, under Abbot Voland, he became a monk. He was a man given over to quiet idleness, ready and prompt with words for anything whatever, but slow and inept in work and action. Once ordained abbot, he soon began to conduct himself licentiously in all things, and, given over to idleness and sloth, shamefully neglecting both the household property and regular observance, he reduced it almost to nothing. For the temporal condition of this monastery, which he found in good state, he finally left in bad state when he yielded office. He pledged many of the monastery’s goods, sold many, and secretly alienated many without the consent of his subjects; and the price he partly gave to his blood-relatives and worthless persons, and partly consumed uselessly. In morals he was dissolute, wholly secular, since by day and night he was accustomed to have nothing else among his delights than banquets and pleasures. He neither possessed in himself the observance of regular discipline, nor took care to reform it in his subjects. Finally, he was not free from suspicion of a graver crime, because the curtain of vices is accustomed to draw after it a curtain.

*1301*

In the first year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1301, a comet appeared in the sky. Many evils followed, seditions and rumors of wars in various regions.

In the same year, on the fourth day before the Nones of July, William, bishop of Utrecht, was killed in war by his enemies, after he had governed the church of Utrecht for almost five years amid many storms of tribulation and persecution. Guy, canon and treasurer of the church of Liege, succeeded him and presided for sixteen years, a man magnificent in all respects. His natural brothers were Botzard, bishop of Metz; John, bishop of Cambrai; John, count of Holland; and Florentius, prince of Morea.

Also in the same year, on the day of Saint Mary Magdalene, Henry, count of Luxembourg, set up his camp beside the church of the Holy Cross, and laid waste everything round about: hamlets, houses, vineyards, wine-presses, and orchards. He would have advanced further, had not almighty God, through the merits of his saints resting at Trier, sent upon them, on the eleventh night after they had arrived, a spirit of dizziness, so that they struck one another and fled, with no one pursuing.
