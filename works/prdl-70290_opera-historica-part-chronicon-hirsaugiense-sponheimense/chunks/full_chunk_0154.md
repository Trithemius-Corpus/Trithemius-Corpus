*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

He composed a beautiful work on the Song of Songs, divided into four little books, in which he explained all the words quite appropriately as praises of the most blessed Mother of God and Virgin Mary. *On Contempt of the World*, two little books. *On Saint John the Baptist*, one. Also one book of his various revelations, and certain homilies or sermons. The rest of what he wrote has not come to my knowledge.

In the same prescribed year of the Lord, namely 1124, Pope Calixtus II died. Lambert, bishop of Ostia, succeeded him, and, called Honorius II, presided for 5 years and 2 months.

He summoned Pontius, abbot of Cluny of our order, to Rome and ordained him cardinal of the Holy Roman Church, a man learned in every respect and no less [unclear]. The monastery of [Schonau?], at a distance from Bingen on the other side of the Rhine, was founded by a certain noble and wealthy man named Hildelin, who, when the monastery had been completed, became a monk and its first abbot. He also built the adjoining stone monastery of nuns of our order, in which thereafter a great religious community of Christ's virgins flourished.

*1125.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Volmar, King Henry, the fifth but the fourth emperor of that name, died at Utrecht in the week of Pentecost, and, having been brought to Speyer, was buried. He left no children. Then at Mainz, on the third day before the Kalends of September, by the election of the princes, Lothar II, duke of Saxony, was established as king, and at Aachen, on the Ides of September, was crowned, as is customary, by the hands of Frederick, archbishop of Cologne. In the same year, when King Lothar wished to introduce Otto, whom he had appointed duke, to the Bohemians, in the following winter Ulrich, who had occupied those lands, was striving to claim the duchy for himself. But while the soldiers were trying to make a way for themselves through the forest with iron, the enemies suddenly came upon them and killed Otto himself together with many other brave men. Margrave Adalbert was captured. Frederick, duke of Alsace, contriving certain new things against the king, was condemned by the judgment of the princes.

In this same year Arnold, bishop of Speyer, died according to the word of Rupert, abbot of Limburg, of whom we have spoken, namely a few months after he had threatened him with death. Siegfried, succeeding him, presided for 21 years. Hartwig, bishop of Regensburg, also died; in this very year Cuno, abbot of our order from the monastery of Siegburg in the diocese of Cologne, was substituted for him, and presided for 6 years. Henry, duke of Bavaria, also died.

In these times there was a certain heretic named Tanchelm, living in the lower regions, who also denied that the sacrament of the Lord's body and blood was of benefit for salvation to those receiving it. And going about in costly attire and gilded garments, with his hair twisted in a triple cord, he enticed his followers, who followed him armed to the number of about 3,000, by persuasive words and the provisions of banquets, and through them, by committing murders against those resisting him, he gained support. The people of Antwerp and almost all those round about believed him and revered him as a saint and as one sent by God, to such an extent that they even carried off as the greatest relics the water with which he had washed his body, and drank it.

Yet he was of such great incontinence and impurity that he corrupted daughters in the presence of their mothers and betrothed women while their husbands looked on, and asserted that this work was spiritual. At length, after he had disseminated many errors among the people and many murders had been committed against the clergy and the faithful of the Church, while he was sailing in a boat he was struck in the brain by a certain priest inflamed with zeal for the faith, and died. But not even after his death could the error which he had sown be so easily rooted out. For this reason, by the clergy of that place, a church with some revenues was given through the hand of the bishop to Norbert, founder of the Premonstratensian order, for the building of a monastery.
