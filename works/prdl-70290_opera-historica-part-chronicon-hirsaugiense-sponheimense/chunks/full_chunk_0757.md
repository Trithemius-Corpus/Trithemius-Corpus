*576 Familiar Letters*

*LVIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, sends greetings to Conrad Peutinger, doctor of both laws.*

[unclear]

Willibald Pirckheimer, our common friend, wrote to me a few months ago that, at your urging and that of my friends, Royal Majesty had arranged this condition for me: namely, that, under a fixed and sufficient provision for as long as I live, I might henceforth freely philosophize in the city of Augsburg; that a suitable house, too, together with the other necessities of life, would be abundantly provided; and that nothing further remained except that I should merely give my consent in these matters. I give thanks to Caesarean Majesty, and I also have no ordinary thanks for you and the rest of my friends in the community, for such great goodwill of yours toward me. But I can in no way accept the condition that you propose to me, because to desert my place, which the most reverend lord prelate and prince of Würzburg entrusted to me with singular trust and confidence, would be not only most shameful, but also full of the vice of ingratitude. For I would rather lie hidden here in the little monastery, in my accustomed poverty, all the days of my life, than by my departure offend so great a prince or disturb in any small way my patron. Indeed, as far as lies in me, I shall never weaken the confidence that he once seems to have had in my person. Nor does it accord with my purpose, since I am a monk and long ago swore of my own free will to the Rule of the holy father Benedict, to leave the monastery and live in cities, and, after having tasted in some measure the sweetness of the heavenly secret, to be occupied again with the tumults of the world. For it is not fitting, indeed it is not lawful, for men who have once bound themselves to divine services to return to secular actions, nor for those devoted to monasteries to dwell in cities. A fish in the kitchen and a monk in a prince's court seem to be in no way far from the utmost danger, since each is nearest to death: the one, that is, to death of life and flesh; the other, to death of spirit and mind. For just as it seems impossible for a fish brought into the kitchen to be long deferred from death, since it is brought there for this very reason, to be eaten by men, so it is not possible for a monk in a prince's court, or in the turmoil of worldly action, to keep his monastic purpose inviolate, especially if the desire of ambition or avarice draws him outside the monastery. A different judgment is to be made if the consideration of piety compels someone to this very thing. But I, a lover of quiet and peace, who for this reason left the tumultuous abbacy of Sponheim, after the envy of rivals had offered me the occasion, and took up this one, truly rather poor, so that, somewhat free and released from the cares of the world, I might have leisure for myself and for the study of the Scriptures, have already long since determined in the counsel of my mind to hold and continue the pursuit of solitude, and, so far as it can be done without injury to pastoral care, to have no concern with the business of the world. But if Royal Majesty is pleased with my service for the publication of chronicles, I am willing and ready here in my little monastery to do whatever he shall enjoin to be worked out by night, provided only that this labor of mine be in some measure recompensed for the necessity of the place, and that my person, intent on the affairs of others, not be judged burdensome. Yet I thank you again and again for your most friendly affection toward me, and I beg you not to take this opinion of mine amiss. Farewell, healthy and happy, and safe for a long time. From Würzburg, on the sixth day of the month of September, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*LIX.*

*Johannes Trithemius of Saint James at Würzburg sends greetings to Master Libanius Gallus, his most learned teacher, and perpetual charity in the Lord.*

[unclear]

I do not know, Libanius, what evil fortune has brought upon us, or what good it has envied us, so that, although nature and grace together have made us of one mind in mind and spirit, fortune has willed that in body we be distant from one another by so great an interval of places. From this it comes about that we are unable to console one another with mutual words except, for the most part, after several months and years, and sometimes not even that, because of the rare occurrence of those who might carry our letters and bring them back. Recently, at the end of the month of September, I received the letter that, as appeared, you had given in Spain on the twenty-fourth day of the month of June to a certain merchant intending to go to Frankfurt, adorned and at the same time full of many mysteries of our philosophy; and at the fourth reading, often repeated again and again, I was so greatly amazed and rejoiced that I almost seemed to have been made outside myself, and not to be in Francia with myself, but in Spain, or in Majorca, with you. These letters of yours will be for me, as long as I live, an everlasting memorial, and a refuge laid up after our Lord Jesus Christ alone; through them, in adversities, I shall experience constancy, and in doubtful matters, truth—
