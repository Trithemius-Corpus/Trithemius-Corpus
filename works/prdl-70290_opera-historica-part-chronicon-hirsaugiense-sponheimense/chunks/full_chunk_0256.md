*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

*1283.*

When the first year of Abbot Crafto had been completed, in the year of the Lord's Nativity 1283, on the day of St. Potentiana the virgin, the counts of Henneberg led an army against Berthold, bishop of Wurzburg, and burned with fire the very strong town of Schwarzach near the Main. Plundering the monastery of St. Felicity of our order, situated there outside the walls, they carried off everything they had found.

In the same year Pope Martin IV sent into Germany as legate a latere the bishop of Tusculum. Coming to Wurzburg, in the presence of Rudolf, king of the Romans, and of many princes, bishops, abbots, dukes, counts, and nobles, he held a council. There, among other matters, the legate himself in the name of the pope, and the king from the whole people of the empire, began to demand most urgently a contribution of the tenth penny from the clergy, many princes consenting to their view. But Siegfried, archbishop of Cologne, and Henry, archbishop of Trier, strongly opposing the unaccustomed and excessively burdensome exaction, wholly revoked the business.

*1284.*

In the second year of Abbot Crafto, in the year of the Lord's Nativity 1284, Frederick, bishop of Worms, died. Simon of Schoneck succeeded him and presided for eight years, three months, and fourteen days, a just man and of remarkable integrity, who did many good things.

In the same year, on the second day of the month of April, Werner of Falkenstein, archbishop [unclear], held a council; there, among other matters, the legate himself in the name of the pope, and the king from the whole people of the empire, began to demand most urgently a contribution of the tenth penny from the clergy, many princes consenting to their view. But Siegfried, archbishop of Cologne, and Henry, archbishop of Trier, strongly opposing the unaccustomed and excessively burdensome exaction, wholly revoked the business.

In the year of Abbot Crafto [unclear], with two having been elected in disagreement, namely Peter, canon of the greater church of Basel [unclear], bishop of Mainz, buried in the greater church; and the see of Mainz was vacant for almost three [unclear]. In the said church at the Steps, together with one tower adjoining the greater church and the new shops, it was burned.

In the same year Berthold, the forty-second bishop of Wurzburg, died and was buried in the greater church. Mangold of Neuenburg succeeded him and presided for twelve years.

*1285.*

In the third year of Abbot Crafto, in the year of the Lord's Nativity 1285, the church of Blessed Mary ever virgin at Mainz, called "at the Steps," together with one tower adjoining the greater church and with the new [unclear].

In the same year, on the sixth day before the Ides of January, the citizens of Wurzburg expelled from the city all the canons and clergy, stripping them of all their goods.

In this same year Louis, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, son of Otto, died. He was the first of the dukes of Bavaria to obtain the Palatinate, as was said in the preceding sections. Louis was buried in the monastery of Furstenfeld in Bavaria, which he himself had newly built in Bavaria in satisfaction for the wife he had killed. He left two sons: Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine, who had taken as wife Mechthild, daughter of Adolf, king of the Romans, by whom he begot Rudolf, Rupert, and Adolf, concerning whom we shall speak later [unclear] count of Holland, Zeeland, and Hainaut and lord of Frisia; Otto called "the Roman," because he had been born at Rome; and Louis, the other duke of Bavaria. The mother of the aforesaid dukes was Margaret, sister of the king of France and daughter of William, count of Holland; after her father died without sons, she obtained the paternal county of Holland, Hainaut, and Zeeland, and assigned it to her son William, as we shall say in its proper place.

In the same year also Pope Martin IV died at Perugia and was buried in the greater church. At his tomb many sick persons brought there, blind persons led there, and mute and lame persons recovered their former health. Honorius IV, a Roman by origin, formerly called Jacob, cardinal deacon, succeeded him in the pontificate and presided for one year and eleven days, a good and upright man, a true lover of knowledge and honesty.
