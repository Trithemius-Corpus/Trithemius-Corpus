*494. Familiar Letters, LXVII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, sends greeting to his very good brother Johannes Nuntius.*

[unclear]

The prior came to me without letters from anyone, indicating orally that the heads of the factions had met at Bingen on our account on the fourth day of the month of August. He himself also took part in their council, together with his colleague from Bingen, and he reported to us certain things said and at the same time concluded by our rivals in that impious council; the substance of their opinion is comprised in these words. They say that they very greatly desire that we return to the monastery, and that on the part of the Duke and his people there is nothing for us to fear, since he too greatly desires our return, and has often said openly that he has never felt or thought anything against us. But if it does not please us to return to the monastery, the Duke, together with those lying old priests, desires that we resign the abbacy; yet they would rather that we return than that we withdraw. This is the sum of the counsel of the wicked, concerning which Melantius the prior desired our answer, which he might report.

We said that the chapter to be held at Mainz at the beginning of the month of September was drawing near, in which we intend to set forth the case for examination by the fathers, and to hear what they advise. Having learned these things, the prior departed, to report them to the Duke. After his departure, on the very next day Johannes Damius disclosed to us that he had seen, in the prior’s possession, letters addressed to us in the name of our convent; when the prior was asked what they contained, he answered: an invective against the abbot. And Damius said, “Does the convent write against the abbot? O most foolish and senseless men, what are you doing?” And snatching the letters from the prior’s hand, he opened them, read them, and marveled at the prior’s rashness. For they contained certain brothers, fabricated by our prior alone at Mainz with the help of that ruined man Peter Slarion, and falsely written in the name of our convent, while they themselves knew nothing of it. But after Damius had read the note, he tore it up, lest it should fall into our hands.

Moved again by this injury done by our prior, we have resolved with ourselves, brother, and we do not wish you to be at all unaware of it, never to return to Sponheim, but to provide for ourselves elsewhere as best we can. For now the prior’s faithlessness is clearly evident to you, he who up to this point pretended to be faithful to us. We know that he aspires to our abbacy; for that reason he contrives plots against us, with sweet words in his mouth, but he devises deceit in his heart. Therefore we shall yield, not unwillingly but voluntarily; and although we could hold our abbacy even while absent from the monastery, against the will of both the Duke and all of you, nevertheless after a few days we shall freely relinquish it and without any bargain, just as twenty-three years ago we were canonically elected to it without any bargain and without our knowledge. The earthly loss of dignity is easy for us, who know that we are mortal; nor do we weigh what we are called, but rather have judged that attention must be given to what we are.

But we marvel at the ingratitude of the prior, whom we have always exalted with singular goodwill and pursued with many benefits. O prior, most faithless of all monks, not undeservedly to be compared with Judas the betrayer, how impiously you have acted against us, unmindful of all the benefits we have bestowed upon you. Did we not, out of love for you, receive your parents, fugitives from the land of Luxembourg because of war and fire, poor and needy; did we not cherish them, and for twenty-three years now support them from the substance of the monastery, contrary to the opinion of many brothers; and did we not freely give them a dwelling with every kind of liberty in the village of Sponheim? We also arranged that a full prebend, sufficient for both of them, should be served to them daily from the monastery by our servants.

You too we caused to be ordained priest; then we took you on as our chaplain and table-companion; afterward we first appointed you cellarer of the monastery, and when it had become necessary to remove you from that office because of your fickleness, so that no disgrace might arise for you, we raised you to be our prior, though many protested. We never denied you anything you reasonably requested. You also, as you will not be able to deny, we always guarded like the pupil of our eye; and against all the murmurings of the brothers, which your negligence had stirred up, I, too trusting toward you, upheld and defended you for almost fourteen years now. But what kind of return you have made to us in this very tribulation of ours, we do not doubt that almighty God, who knows, will cause you to understand in his own time. We too, not in vengeance, but so that you may recognize that we have understood your ingratitude, shall write you a repayment in due time.

What now, most beloved elder Nutius, does it seem to you? Are we moved justly or unjustly to yield, we who have found such ingratitude and malice in our own man, in whom we used to trust above the rest? He writes eulogies against us under the name of the convent, while all are ignorant of it, he who could never with truth write anything evil about us.
