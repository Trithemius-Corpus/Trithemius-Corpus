In the same year, when king Adolf was weakening the empire, nourishing discords and wars, burdening the people with unaccustomed tributes, fostering robbers and oppressors of the poor, and despising the princes: Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, with certain other princes, called Albert, duke of Austria and count of Habsburg, son of Rudolf the former king of the Romans, from Austria to Mainz, promising that he—Adolf being deposed—would be elected king of the Romans. These [things] known, duke Albert came to the Rhine with a very great army of his [men], whom the princes of Alsace and the aforesaid counts, together with Everhard, count of Württemberg, meeting at Strasbourg with a great army, promised aid against Adolf. The Strasbourg-men, too, receiving four thousand warriors as aid, led [them] to Strasbourg. King Adolf, hearing these [things], soon walled Rouffach with a siege, but, the matter unfinished, [his men] lost, he betook himself into the city of Speyer, awaiting the outcome of the new affair. Meanwhile duke Albert, with a great army of dukes, counts, nobles, and soldiers gathered from Austria, Bohemia, Swabia, and Alsace—accompanied by the bishop of Constance, the bishop of Strasbourg, the provincial count of Alsace, and the aforenamed counts—with none resisting, came to Mainz, where he found Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, Wichbold, elect of Cologne, with certain other princes, assembled. There, therefore, on the vigil of the nativity of St John the Baptist, the princes, king Adolf having been deposed from the kingdom by sentence on account of grave excesses, unanimously elected duke Albert himself as king, and promised him fidelity, obedience, and assistance against whatsoever adversaries. These [things] accomplished at Mainz with great solemnity, Albert the new king, with his whole army, crossed to the town of Alzey, set in mid-journey from Worms to Kreuznach, destroying the whole of it except the castle, and there, tents fixed, settled, about to fight against king Adolf. Meanwhile king Adolf, hearing that he was deprived of the kingdom, and Albert elected by the princes, was dismayed in mind, and, gathering a great army of warriors from the cities faithful to him—Speyer, Worms, Oppenheim, Frankfurt—together with his princes (namely Rudolf, count Palatine and duke of Bavaria, his son-in-law; the count of Isenburg also; the count of Sponheim; the Hairy count; and many others), betook himself to Oppenheim, and for ten days awaited the aids of the friends to whom he had written. But, ten days elapsed, before the promised aids of [his] friends came, impatient of delay, inconsiderately and too precipitately, unequal in strength, he advanced to battle, between the village called Göllheim and the nuns' monastery called Rosenthal, not far from the Worms field; where, battle joined, it was sharply fought, and many fell on either side. King Adolf himself, too, fought most bravely with his own hand; to whom, when victory now seemed to be approaching, his [men] began to flee, and he, deserted in the midst of [his] adversaries, was killed in the same place, and his son captured—the count of Isenburg also, the bravest captain on his side. [Margin: The king is buried at Rosenthal. Albert is made king. The offspring of Albert. The king's six sons.] Otto, count of Oxenstein, supreme captain of Albert, fell there too, with sixty companions and the chief nobles of the land. But the body of the slain king Adolf was buried in the aforesaid monastery of Rosenthal, until the times of Henry, king of Luxembourg, by whose command it was brought with honor to Speyer and placed in the tombs of the kings. There are [those] who write that all those who conspired against king Adolf perished by an evil death, divine grace avenging his injury. For Albert himself, the usurper of the kingdom, was killed by his brother's son, as we shall say in [its] place; the count of Holloch and the count of Oxenstein were likewise killed; the archbishop of Mainz, sitting on his chair, was carried off by an unexpected death; the count of Zweibrücken was drowned in the bank called Glan; Conrad, bishop of Strasbourg, before Freiburg, was slain by a sword by a certain peasant; the count of Leiningen became frenzied and was made wretched by various calamities; and almost all the rest passed from this world by a similar destruction. But Albert, escaping the victor with his princes, sets out for the city of Aachen, and there is crowned king of the Romans by Wichbold, archbishop of Cologne, and reigned 10 years. He had as wife Elizabeth, daughter of Meinhard, duke of Carinthia, of whom he begot six sons and five daughters. The first-born was Rudolf, king of Bohemia. The second, Frederick, duke of Austria, who was elected against Louis the Bavarian, the emperor, as we shall say below. The third was Leopold, duke of Carinthia and Styria. The fourth was Henry the duke, who had as wife Elizabeth, daughter of the count of Virn[eburg]…
