*Sponheim.*

On the feast of St. Martin he received the office of blessing in the church of St. James near Mainz from Berthold, bishop of Pariadensis, vicar in pontificals of the aforesaid lord administrator, while standing by him, in the customary manner and vested in pontificals, were Herman, abbot of the same place, and Anselm, abbot of the monastery of Limburg, of our order, whom he had requested.

*The native origin of Johannes Trithemius.*

This Johannes Trithemius was a Mosellan by country, born in a certain village, Trittenheim, which lies about four miles from Trier, downstream on the left side of the river Moselle. He was born of honorable parents, of moderate fortune, yet free, his father being Johannes of Montegentili and his mother named Elizabeth of Longouicum; the cultivation of vines supplied them with the necessities of life.

When the boy was scarcely one year old, his father died. His mother, out of love for the child, lest the little one be exposed to the injuries of a stepfather, as often happens, remained a widow for seven years. When that time had at last passed, she took a second husband, with whom she also begot several other children; all of them, however, except one son, died in youth. But Johannes, when he was fifteen years old, against the wishes of his parents and all his relatives, touched by love of letters and in a certain way assured by a wonderful vision concerning his future learning, began secretly to learn letters. When this became known to his stepfather, he attacked him with harsh words and harsher blows, and tried with all his strength to draw him away from the literary purpose that he had. Yet the more he afflicted him daily by forbidding him, the more he inflamed him with a more fervent desire for letters. For at night, since by day it was not allowed except on feast-days, and then secretly, when everyone was asleep he would leave the house and go to a certain neighboring friend who had once devoted himself to letters; from him he received the rudiments of elementary instruction in a very short time, so that even the teacher himself, greatly amazed, said that he had never seen so teachable an intellect or a livelier memory in any man. And this prefigured the vision that he had, which, as he revealed it, we have learned to have been as follows.

*He had a vision concerning the knowledge of the scriptures.*

For the space of one year, with continual fasts and prayers, he asked the Lord God with the greatest intentness for two very great things, one of which was knowledge of the scriptures; the other he said he had never disclosed to anyone. Behold, one night a young man in bright clothing seemed to stand by him as he slept, holding two tablets in his hands, one of which was written with letters, the other painted with certain images. He said to him: “Choose from these two tablets whichever one you wish.” At once, burning with love of letters, which at that time he still knew not at all, even while sleeping, he chose the tablet that seemed to be written. The young man who had appeared said to him: “Behold, God has heard your prayers; he will give you both things that you asked for, and indeed more than you could have asked.”

The next day, although he was thinking nothing of the vision, a more wonderful occasion was given to him as he desired the beginnings of letters. For some time, then, in that same village he devoted himself to letters in secret, because he did not dare do so openly for fear of his parents. He learned the alphabet, the Lord’s Prayer with the Angelic Salutation, also the Apostles’ Creed, the Confession at the altar, and the blessing of food and drink perfectly in seven days, and committed them most tenaciously to memory. After this, he also accustomed himself within one month to read books in the German tongue most perfectly by himself.

*With his uncle cooperating, he is permitted to visit the schools.*

When his friends, and especially the priests, saw the young man’s great intellect and love for letters, they persuaded his uncle, Peter of Montegentili, a mature and severe man, to test what such vehement desire for letters in him portended. He consented, and, because he was his uncle, presented himself publicly as the young man’s guardian, despite the stepfather’s protest, and from then on began to take account of his patrimony. But how many blows, how many injuries, how many insults the innocent young man endured from his stepfather; how many showers of tears, in distress, he poured forth before he could escape his yoke, more cruel than any servitude.

At last he escaped the stepfather’s house, as the Israelite multitude escaped Egypt, and for love of letters he made a journey of three days through the wilderness; first at Trier, then in the lower regions, and finally in the town of Heidelberg. And thus, when the three-day journey in a foreign land, as we have said, had been completed, at last he offered himself in this present monastery to the Lord God as a perpetual burnt-offering. Once made a monk, he lived among the brothers without complaint, and preferred the study of letters and the solitude of his cell to all the delights of this world; and he seemed to thirst for nothing in this world more than knowledge of the scriptures.

*The very great library is assembled by Trithemius.*

But the small number of books in this monastery held him back somewhat from his purpose; this, however, as soon as he was ordained abbot, he was eager to remedy. For, as is clear from the inventory of the library, scarcely forty-seven volumes, counting all of them, were found in this monastery at the time of his ordination, of small value and of less use for true learning.
