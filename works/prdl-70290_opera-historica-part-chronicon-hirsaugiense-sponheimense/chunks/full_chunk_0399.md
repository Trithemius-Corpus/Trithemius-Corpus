Given up by the physicians, he turned himself to God, having made a vow of religion on the counsel of his sister, and, to the astonishment of all, miraculously recovered from his illness. Nevertheless, remaining for two years in secular life, he arranged his affairs according to the condition of his purpose, and distributed in alms for the poor a large part of the substance belonging to him. At the urging of the aforesaid sister of his, who venerated Abbot Dietlib and the present monastery with wondrous affection, he came without delay to conversion. Finally, he conferred and gave no small gifts to this monastery.

1310. In this year Henry, king of the Romans, held an assembly of the princes at Speyer, where Elizabeth, daughter of Wenceslaus, the late king of Bohemia, appeared in person, asking King Henry to grant her paternal kingdom to her as a fief. But King Henry, after first taking the mature counsel of his princes, considering that the said daughter of Bohemia was the sole heir of the kingdom, joined her in marriage to his son John, count of Luxembourg, and by imperial authority raised him to be king of Bohemia. Therefore, when the wedding had been celebrated there, he soon sent him into Bohemia, attaching to him Peter, archbishop of Mainz, formerly his physician, with a mighty army. And when John, the new king, came into Bohemia with the queen and the aforesaid archbishop, he soon subjected the whole kingdom of Bohemia to himself and powerfully received it into his dominion. He was crowned by the same Peter, archbishop of Mainz, to whom by ancient right it belonged to anoint and crown the kings of Bohemia, as we have shown far above. And when the coronation had been solemnly performed, at which more than three hundred thousand horsemen and warriors are reported to have been present, the king gave to the aforementioned archbishop of Mainz a golden seat beautifully adorned with gems. The archbishop, taking it with him to Mainz, bestowed it on Saint Martin; it is kept in the greater church down to the present day, and is called by the people the seat of Saint Martin.

In the same year King Henry, with his brother Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, Leopold, duke of Austria, and many other princes, dukes, counts, and nobles, set out with a great army into Italy to receive the crown of the empire. On the way he took by force and destroyed many cities resisting him and in rebellion. And when he came to Rome, he was crowned emperor at Saint John Lateran by cardinals ordained and sent for this by the pope, on the holy day of Pentecost. Fighting with the king of Apulia near the bridge of the Tiber, he defeated the Romans, who were denying him entrance into the city, and killed many of them. And after he had been crowned emperor, he created Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine, and many other princes and nobles knights in the aforesaid church of Saint John. While the emperor was in Italy for two years, his second army, which he had left in Germany specially appointed for this, advanced in battle against the counts of Württemberg, rebels against the empire; from eighty very strongly fortified castles and towns which they held, they vigorously captured seventy-two by arms. For when King Henry was going into Italy, he prepared three armies: the first, gathered from Franks and Bavarians, he sent into Bohemia with his son and with Peter, archbishop of Mainz, as we have already said above; the second against the aforesaid counts of Württemberg; the third he led with him into Italy. The people of Brescia, whom he powerfully defeated by arms, he compelled to obedience to the empire; the Lombards, all the people of Pavia, Turin, Asti, Verona, Piacenza, Genoa, Pisa, Siena, Florence, many inhabitants of other cities, and all who resisted him, he defeated in armed expedition and brought under his power. This emperor Henry was a wise, just, gracious man, vigorous in arms, a most upright worshipper and lover of the Catholic faith, powerful in counsel and spirit, a lover and defender of the clergy; no one like him arose in the meantime.

1311. In this year Willicho, abbot of this monastery, built anew the house of the principal gate of the monastery on the southern side. But after almost 156 years, Johannes of Culenhusen, the first abbot in the reform called Bursfeld, had that gate closed and built a new gate of the monastery on the eastern side. The house of the old gate, constructed by Willicho, he changed for himself into an abbatial dwelling.
