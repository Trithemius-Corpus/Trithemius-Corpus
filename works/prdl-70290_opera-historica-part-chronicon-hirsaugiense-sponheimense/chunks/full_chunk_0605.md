But the victory of virgins is greater than that of angels, and the contest differs in manner, time, and condition. For the angels, living without flesh, by the confirming grace of God became victors once for all by willing rightly; but virgins, living in the flesh beyond the flesh, aided by divine mercy and fighting in a long contest, never triumph securely. And therefore it is more blessed in the flesh to imitate the life of angels than from the flesh to increase the mortal number of pleasure. For an unmarried woman and a virgin, as that holy Christophilus declared, thinks on the things of the Lord, how she may be holy both in body and in spirit, nor has she any place into which she may flow away through vanities, but is always borne toward God with a most pure love, to whom she has wholly vowed herself, cleaving to him by desire the more happily, the more she has made herself a stranger to every consolation of this life. But married women have many and various and useless cares, by which they are oppressed the more unhappily, the less they are accustomed to think on the things that are the Lord’s. We ask you, virgin, most vigilant guardian of the brides of Christ, to apply your mind attentively to the few things we are about to say. Show yourself, as is fitting, the faithful executrix of our commands, which in our last visitation we handed over to you after mature deliberation, especially concerning the enclosure of the monastery, which is to be opened to no one except for a cause of necessity. Likewise let none of the sisters be permitted to approach the speaking-window alone, nor to speak with any person without prudent and suitable witness, whether he be father or brother, mother or sister, or sent by anyone on any occasion whatsoever. Let the dangers of others make us cautious, we who are not ignorant of the cunning of the evil spirit, and have known certain most abominable imitators of him; when these were permitted to speak alone with virgins at the window, they said that they were the brothers and kinsmen of those very women whose sacrilegious corrupters they were found to be a little afterward. We hand over to you both our decree and that of the ordinary, the bishop of Neumagen, by which before all else we forbid you to allow any of the men from Duroburgium either to be seen within the precincts of the monastery or to speak alone at the window with any woman. For you know with what zeal they strive to carry out the works of demons, and how pernicious the speech of such men is to chaste virgins dedicated to God. These are the things which I thought should usefully be made known to you at this time. If you have anything to be conveyed to your people in Cologne, we shall gladly perform the office of carrier. Farewell from Neumagen, 24 June, in the year of Christ 1505.

*XII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot, to Roger Sycamber of Duemeten, a studious and excellent man. Greeting.*

We have written, Roger, to our beloved daughter in Christ, Lady Richmodis, that on the day after tomorrow we shall go down to Cologne, which we also want you to know, excellent Roger, so that if you wish to write anything to our Beifelius, or to Herbenus, or to the rest of our friends, you may conveniently do so through us. Whatever you are going to do, let it be done as quickly as possible, since we shall not remain here longer than two days. Send us something new from your nightly studies, so that we may relieve the tedium of travel by boat with useful occupation. For how troublesome sailing is to us without reading, you, who lived with us in our Spanheim monastery and know our custom from association with us, are not ignorant. For leisure, as Seneca bears witness, without letters is death, and the burial of a living man. Fortune, not industry, gave me the ability to philosophize according to his counsel. “If you wish,” he says, “to be free for the mind, be either poor or like a poor man.” For riches have obstructed many men from philosophizing. Poverty, however, is unencumbered and secure. And so no one can at the same time be free for the studies of letters and for riches; nor does wisdom deign to inhabit a soul which it has seen either infected by desires or entangled in cares for temporal things. Indeed it has happened to me rather often up to now to be entangled in cares, but poverty has never departed from me from the time when I became a monk. For by long familiarity it has become so familiar to me that henceforth it is in a certain way necessary.

By long study I have learned many things, but have I finally been able to grasp this itself, so that I may know the thoughts of a wealthy man?
