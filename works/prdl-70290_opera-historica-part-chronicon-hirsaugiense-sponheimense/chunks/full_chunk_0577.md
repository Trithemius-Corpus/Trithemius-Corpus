*Sponheim.*

*Concerning Nicolaus, twenty-sixth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for twenty years, six months, and twenty-one days, and concerning certain deeds of that time.*

After the voluntary resignation of our abbot, lord Johannes Trithemius, and his transfer to Wurzburg, as was said above, this monastery of ours at Sponheim was vacant for nearly two months. When these had at last passed, two abbots came together with us for the election of a new pastor: namely Thomas at Seligenstadt and Johannes of the Mount of St. John the Baptist in the Rheingau. Since they had been deputed to us as visitors according to the custom of the annual chapter, we gladly and honorably admitted them as directors of the business at hand.

Therefore in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s Nativity 1506, Roman indiction 9, on the fifth day before the Ides of December, that is, on the day after the feast of the Immaculate Conception of blessed Mary ever virgin, in the morning at about the ninth hour, or thereabouts, the prior and brethren of the convent assembled at the chapter-place together with the aforesaid visitors. After the customary prayers and declarations had first been made, they began the business of the election in God’s name by way of a compromise mixed with scrutiny, in the presence of the notary and witnesses. Accordingly, when the votes of each had been examined, as is customary, and set down in writing by the notary, the greater part of the voting brethren directed their votes to Nicolaus of Remich, prior of the convent, and elected him abbot, although the visitors themselves would gladly have set someone from outside over the monastery. But the prior and convent, not thinking it honorable or useful for themselves if they appointed over themselves as abbot some monk from another monastery, because several among them judged that there were suitable men among themselves who certainly both could shrewdly undertake so great a burden and would by no means refuse it, steadfastly agreed that no one other than some excellent man from their own congregation, why should I not say ours, was to be elected. Having so agreed, they prevailed, receiving the aforesaid Nicolaus as abbot. They say the agreement did not sufficiently please the abbots, who would have preferred some one or other of their own. Therefore Nicolaus, elected abbot by his own brethren and confirmed according to custom by Jacob, archbishop of Mainz, after Johannes Trithemius holds the abbacy today, for as long as he shall wish who alone knows and can do all things, God blessed forever. Amen.

His native place was a certain village in the duchy of Luxembourg, which the inhabitants call Remich, not far from the town of [Namercum?]. His father, named Andreas, sought his food by hand and craft, a man of simple and innocent life. He had a wife, Catharina, who was mother to this Nicolaus; but all his brothers died in boyhood. Nicolaus himself, while still a boy, at his parents’ command devoted himself to letters, first in his homeland, then at Trier, and afterward among those Wetterau people who border on the Hessians, whom we commonly call Butzbachers. At that time he made moderate progress, but afterward in the monastery much greater progress.

In the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1481, when he was about eighteen years old, he came to this monastery of Sponheim with two other schoolboys, and was received into the novitiate in regular observance by Johannes of Cologne, prior under the abbot, and after a year had passed was admitted to monastic profession according to the custom of the order. In the year of his probation he had acute fevers, on account of which there had almost been among certain men in the convent a refusal concerning his becoming a monk; and not long after his profession he was ordained subdeacon and deacon at the promotion of Johannes of Cologne, abbot. He lived a quiet life among the brethren without disturbance, and by his conduct never harmed or offended anyone.

When Johannes of Cologne, the abbot, was not long afterward transferred to the monastery of Seligenstadt, Johannes Trithemius succeeded in the abbacy. Because of the gentleness of Nicolaus’s life, he pursued him with the singular goodwill of affection: first he had him ordained priest, then attached him to himself as chaplain and familiar servant, and more frequently stirred him to the study of letters both by counsel and example. At his command Nicolaus became at once a scribe and a reader of Greek, a better Latinist, and in time and with practice an excellent Hebraist. After about four years, Abbot Trithemius, wishing to promote him gradually to higher things, appointed him cellarer, so that he might learn not only internal matters but also, in external matters without which the internal do not subsist, might gain experience with time. But when not yet fully three months had been completed in that office, since he was a lover of quiet and desired more to cultivate inner things than earthly ones, he urgently begged Abbot Trithemius to deign to have him released from so great a labor and burden of office, because he saw that he was by no means adequate for managing affairs and cares. Trithemius granted his petition and recalled him to inner matters. But lest he be thought to have suffered disgrace, he soon appointed him prior, and in that office he indeed remained continuously for fifteen years. In these fifteen years he devoted labor to both Hebrew and Greek letters, with Trithemius as teacher, and became an especially good Hebrew scribe. But how much he advanced in the rest, that is, either in the learning of the Scriptures, or in those things which [unclear].
