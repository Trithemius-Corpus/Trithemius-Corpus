In the same year Henry of Vinstingen, archbishop of Trier, built the castle beside the Moselle that is called Bernkastel; he surrounded the town itself with a wall. Since it is otherwise enclosed by very high mountains on the east, south, and north, and by the river Moselle on the west, it could seem impregnable by the protection of nature.

Also in the same year Hartmann, a youth, the third-born son of Rudolf, king of the Romans, was drowned in the Rhine not far from Basel, and was buried with his mother in the church of Basel itself.

Also in the year noted above, Eberhard, bishop of Worms, who was in Italy, died. His body was brought back to Germany and buried in the monastery of Otterburg, of the Cistercian order. Frederick succeeded him in the episcopate and presided for almost six years.

In the same year, on the day of St. Heribert the bishop, William, count of Jülich, with three sons and noble armed knights, entered Aachen secretly in the evening, about the ninth hour, wishing to take the city and subject it to his own lordship. When this was learned, the citizens ran to arms, attacked him and all his men in the middle of the marketplace, and killed them, so that not one escaped. For the expiation of this deed they were afterward heavily fined. For in the end they were compelled forever to give to the counts of Jülich a third part of the offerings in the church of the blessed Mary ever virgin, whether coming from the devotion of pilgrims or accruing from any source whatever. The counts of Jülich, who are now dukes, also received from that time until the present day the institution of the provost of the church of Aachen. But when William, count of Jülich, had died, Siegfried, archbishop of Cologne, long before an enemy of his, allied with the people of Aachen, entered his land, bereft of its prince, with armed force, destroyed everything with sword and fire, captured towns, occupied fortifications, and reduced the whole county to his power. Against him the duke of Brabant, with the aid of the kinsmen and friends of the slain count, led an army, but accomplished little. At length, when Pope Martin IV interposed his offices, peace was made; the archbishop restored all the lordships, towns, castles, and villages that he had taken away.

Also in the year noted above, after the apostolic see had been vacant for six months, the cardinals at last elected John Gaetano, a Roman by country, of the Orsini family, as supreme pontiff. He was called Nicholas III and presided for three years, eight months, and twenty days.
