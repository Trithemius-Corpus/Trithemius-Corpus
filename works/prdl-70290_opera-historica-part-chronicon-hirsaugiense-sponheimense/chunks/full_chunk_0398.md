*1307.*

In the ninth year of Abbot Dietlib, the clergy of the Church of Trier, on account of the squandering of the goods and revenues of the church, and various other acts contrary to equity, had Diether, their archbishop, summoned to Rome for examination before the Apostolic Pontiff. But before the appointed date arrived, in the same year, on the twenty-third day of the month of November, he ended his last day; he was buried among his Preachers in the city of Trier. Baldwin, son of the count of Luxembourg, brother of Henry the Seventh, emperor, then in the twenty-third year of his age, succeeded him, Pope Clement V dispensing with him concerning his minority. He presided for forty-six years, one month, and eighteen days: a man in every respect magnificent, who also governed his church with sufficient foresight and usefulness, restoring peace, paying debts, and restoring alienated property.

In the same year Pope Clement V, in the Council of Vienne, annulled and destroyed the order of the Templars, which had lasted almost 184 years from its foundation. It was charged against them that in their profession they denied Christ; whether this was true or false is not for us to judge.

*1308.*

In the tenth year of Abbot Dietlib, Albert, king of the Romans, because he seemed excessively avaricious, and because he held the duchy of Swabia, belonging to his brother Rudolf, in the name of guardianship, which he ought not undeservedly to have resigned to John, the son of that same brother, now grown up, and although often appealed to by him paid no attention, was slain by the sword near Schaffhausen; after a year he was buried in the church of Speyer.

In the same year the princes, assembling at Rhens on the twenty-seventh day of the month of November, unanimously elected as king of the Romans Henry, count of Luxembourg, brother of Baldwin, archbishop of Trier. Conducting him to Aachen with great solemnity, they had him consecrated king, and he reigned for five years. He had as wife Margaret, daughter of the duke of Brabant.

In these times Peter, archbishop of Mainz, began to build anew and endow a monastery of the Carthusians outside the walls of Mainz.

In the same year, on the sixth ides of November, there died at Cologne John Scotus, doctor of sacred theology, of Paris, of the Order of Minors; he was buried there in his own convent.

*1309.*

Dietlib, the tenth abbot of this monastery, died on the day of Michael the Archangel, and was buried before the steps of the presbytery, not without many tears of the brethren. For he was a good man, a lover of his brethren, who presided well over the monastery both in spiritual and in temporal matters; because, although he took away the private allowance which his predecessors had granted to the monks, he suitably enlarged the substance of temporal goods. In business he was provident, of perceptive intelligence; his counsel was sometimes used in difficult cases even by the count of Sponheim and neighboring nobles. But although there is no doubt that his deeds, performed praiseworthily, were many, we could not know them all, both because of the negligence of the ancients and because of the distance of time.

*Concerning Wilicho the First, eleventh abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for twenty-seven years, five months, and four days.*

Accordingly, when Dietlib, the tenth abbot of this monastery, had died and been buried on the third day before the kalends of October, in the year previously noted, 1309, in the seventh indiction, the abbacy of this community was vacant. After the grace of the Holy Spirit had been invoked according to custom, they unanimously elected as abbot Wilicho, son of the noble man the count of Westerburg, brother of the countess of Sponheim, a very devout religious man, a conventual monk of this monastery, who almost eight years before, under Dietlib, as we said above, had entered the order. Confirmed and ordained by Peter, archbishop of Mainz, he presided over this monastery with sufficient energy for twenty-seven years, five months, and four days, and did much good for this monastery, both in buildings and in increasing revenues.
