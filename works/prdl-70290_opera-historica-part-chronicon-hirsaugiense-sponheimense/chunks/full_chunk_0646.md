*XLVII.*

*Johannes Trithemius to Johann Bracht, prior of St Matthias at Trier, greetings.*

We have already written to your charity from Cologne Agrippina about the cause of our absence from the monastery; but now we have thought it right to inform you by letter of our stay in the March, likewise from Cologne, but the other one, on the Spree near Berlin in the March itself, so that, knowing our condition, you may by no means be distressed on our account. By the protection of divine mercy we are healthy and unharmed, and we have provided for our affairs in such a way that, if need be, we can live freely outside Spanheim. We have a prince favorable to us in all things; provided for by his munificence, we have want of nothing. We shall not return to the Rhine before Easter, having meanwhile been persuaded by the will of that prince to remain, so that we may bring to its proper end what we have begun for his honor. And in truth, if we did not know that it is contrary to our profession, and by no means fitting for our state, to dwell in the courts of princes, we would never depart from here. For the very best terms are proposed to us, if we should wish to remain wholly with the prince; but lest we do injury to the monastic purpose, it is necessary not to love the courts of princes. Yet we pass through this journey, reasonably marked out, in such a way that we refuse nothing offered to us according to our judgment, until we see what necessity at last indicates should be undertaken. Therefore, God granting, we shall return after Easter to the Rhine; but we shall never enter the monastery unless the rashness of our rivals has first happened to be humbled. But if, with [unclear], they decide that they must persist in their obstinacy, then, with the abbacy of Spanheim voluntarily laid aside, we shall follow God’s ordinance. For we trust in the goodness of almighty God, since to the end he will not despise one whom he himself knows to have been harmed, without fault, by the envy of rivals. Yet it is something we must consider not a little, lest perhaps by the ordinance of the supreme will of God we are called elsewhere, like a useless tree to be transplanted into better soil for the bearing of more abundant fruit. For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor? Therefore we must always fear, and follow the divine counsel in silence. For he who made and created us has care for us; his mercy is more useful to us than all our own counsel. I shall go on, then, gladly and voluntarily following God’s counsel, wherever I must go; and, if it please him, as a useless tree uprooted, I shall be transferred, that I may bear richer fruits of patience. Farewell happily. From Berlin, 20 October, in the year 1505.

*XLVIII.*

*Theodoric, bishop of Lübeck, to Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, greetings.*

That you very greatly desired us to lend you Iamblichus, best father, we learned from the account of our magnificent [unclear] de Lapide, a knight both of the golden order and a candidate of letters. Although we keep him in our house in the place of a most dear and most honorable philosopher, nevertheless we have sent him to you, together with Proclus, Porphyry, Synesius, and the rest of his fellow philosophers gathered together with him, no less willingly than cheerfully, instructing him to go and greet you, our noble guest, in our name. For we believe that such great men of wisdom will be all the more welcome to you, the more you have long enjoyed familiarity with them. Receive, then, best father, these noble messengers of ours, addressing you most pleasantly from the hidden recesses of philosophy, bringing, by heaven, nothing harsh or rough, but savory seasonings from the philosophical academies of the Greeks, skillfully and festively prepared by their hands. When you have enjoyed their familiarity to satiety, see that they return to our house. Farewell, bright cabinet of the scriptures, and love us, who love you dearly. From Lübeck, on Friday, 24 October, in the year of Christians 1505.
