What are our studies, Roger, compared with the lucubrations of those men? They handed over the whole time of their life to letters, always reading or writing something, so that they might raise their understanding to the knowledge of the highest truth and provide useful things for posterity. Finally, what are our lucubrations, by which we are thought to be something above the rest, if they are compared with the little works of the aforesaid fathers, except chaff set beside wheat, certain unripe fruits, for which neither time nor place was present, indeed nor was the seasonable tempering of the air granted, so that they might grow together into the desired food. We write, as Flaccus says, “learned and unlearned, we everywhere write poems”; too desirous of empty esteem, we do not keep back our little works, altogether childish, not for the ninth year, to say nothing of that, but not even for a second year.

Although all these things are so, nevertheless we rightly praise the talents of our own men, since each one does this very thing that he can, out of love of wisdom. For the more assiduous anyone has been in the sacred scriptures, the richer an understanding he receives from them. For beautiful and fruitful is the occupation of the mind of a studious man in the divine scriptures, which illuminates the understanding of the soul reading or writing, and sweetly kindles a man withdrawn from the vanities of the world to the love of God. Hence the divine Ambrose admonishes us, saying: We ought long to wear down and polish the utterances of the heavenly scriptures, turning them over with our whole mind and heart, so that that juice of spiritual food may diffuse itself into all the veins of the soul. And that most holy priest Jerome says: Read the divine scriptures more often; indeed, let sacred reading never be put down from your hands. For if you love knowledge of the scriptures, you will not love vices. For ignorance of the scriptures is ignorance of Christ. For if, according to the Christ-loving Paul, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God, he who does not know the scriptures does not know the power and wisdom of God, which is rightly understood by us only through them.

We are ignorant of many things which would by no means be hidden from us if, after the example of the ancient Fathers, continual reading were familiar to us. Therefore let us do what Democritus advises, Roger, and in time we shall be more learned: [unclear]. If you are, he says, desirous of learning, you will certainly be learning many things. And, wishing to strengthen the memory of the pupil, he finely added: [unclear]. Preserve by meditations those things, he says, which you have learned; but grasp from those who know the things which you have not yet learned. For it is equally shameful for one hearing useful discourse not to learn, and not to accept some good thing given by friends. Farewell. From Neumagen, 24 June, in the year of Christians 1505.

*XIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to his Jodocus Beissel, doctor of both laws, greeting.*

On the first day of this month of July I came to the town of the archbishop of Cologne which they call Bonn, where on the next day, according to the agreement, that most wise and most learned prince Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg, chamberlain and elector of the Roman Empire, met me; summoned by him, I had come down there from the city of Neumagen. Then, at dawn on the sixth of the same month, we came to Cologne, where an assembly of many great princes of Germany is being held, with our most serene king of the Romans, Maximilian, present and hastening an expedition against the Sicambri. When we had come there, on the very next day I thought it appropriate to write to you, so that you would not be ignorant of the movements and terrors of my fortunes, because I count you among all men as a proven friend. For if the possession of no thing is pleasant without a friend, how will communication of sad things not bring relief to one who is saddened? Although adversity, which only the envy of rivals has stirred up against an innocent man, grieves me little, since I gave them no occasion at all.
