*Sponheimense.*

...when he was striving utterly to expel her, she, using prudent counsel, called upon and appointed the aforesaid Lord Ludwig the Palatine as her guardian; and to him she granted in perpetual possession that part which the Count Palatine today has from of old in the county of Sponheim, apart from what was afterward pledged. And thus the Count Palatine of the Rhine was first admitted into this county. This count was a man much given to curiosities, always desirous of novel things, who often spent much money in useless dealings: now on alchemy, now on mathematics, now on these, now on other curious inquiries. For he always had at his court men devoted to such vanities: alchemists, mathematicians, haruspices, exorcists, magicians, predictors of the future, whom he easily believed, and, though very often grossly deceived, nevertheless paid great honor.

In the year noted above, Johannes de Fleckenstein, bishop of Worms, died. He had succeeded Matthew of Cracow, in the sixteenth year of his pontificate, on the [unclear] day of the month of May, and was buried in the major church. Frederick of Domneck succeeded him in the episcopate for nineteen years.

In the same year also Philip, duke of Burgundy, captured that admirable maiden Joan, about whom Master Johannes Gerson wrote several things. Armed and riding among soldiers, she did wonders in war on behalf of the king of the Franks, and predicted future events. He sent her to the king of the English, and he, after she had long been afflicted with many punishments and torments, finally had her burned with fire; this she had predicted would happen to her. This maiden was from Lorraine. When she was fourteen years old she came to Charles, king of the Franks, in the twenty-ninth year of our Abbot Bernhard, who, as we said above, had been driven from his kingdom by the duke of Burgundy, predicting future events to him and promising him the restoration of the kingdom and certain victory against all his adversaries. Although she was a young and tender girl, nevertheless fully armed she mounted a horse with great agility; by her counsel she arranged battle lines, taught ambushes, predicted dangers, held the banner in her hands, and, riding among armed men, always fought first against the adversaries and everywhere did many marvels.

In the year noted above also, the sixth provincial chapter of our order was held at Erfurt, at St Peter’s, on the twenty-first day of the month of April. From it, arising through the rashness of certain subjects, a destructive disaster came upon this monastery. For when the monks refused to undertake the proposed reform, as we said above, they assailed their abbot with slanders and insults. Moved by these things, since he could not avenge himself in another way, without the consent of his convent he alienated from the monastery two courts in Winsheim with all their appurtenances and their revenues, handing them over for nothing to a certain Jacob of Gmund. Through him they came into the hands of Meinhard of Coppenstein, from whom, though they were often demanded back by several abbots with threats and entreaties, they could never be obtained, down to the present day.

*1427.*

In this year, which was the thirty-seventh of our Abbot Bernhard, Emperor Sigismund, with the consent of Pope Martin V, levied an immense amount of money from all the clergy and people of all Germany, intending to fight with it against the schismatic Bohemians, although little or no effect followed. He who had a thousand florins gave two; but he who was wealthy in possession to the value of one hundred florins paid a quarter part of one florin. All the rest, men, women, sons, daughters, hired servants, household members, as many as had completed the fifteenth year of their age, with absolutely no one excepted, each paid thirteen Hallenses. It was indeed a great exaction, but little benefit for the Catholic faith followed from it. Yet there were some among the Germans who refused to submit their necks to this exaction. The summer of this year was exceedingly wet; flooding waters brought much damage upon people, and thereafter a great pestilence followed in all Germany.
