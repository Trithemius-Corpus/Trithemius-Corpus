A man worthy of every honor the monks, utterly [unclear], not only do not revere, but even despise while persecuting him; and him whom, with the highest honor, from the ends of the earth, if opportunity required it, they ought to have called to themselves, they do not shamefully fear to repel from themselves and to cast away. The leaders of the faction, monks utterly ungrateful to God and men, approach Count Frederick: they denounce their abbot Adelbert as idle and slothful, insufficiently [unclear], and falsely invent, in one phrase after another, how useless he is to the monastery. And indeed they easily turn the count’s mind against the holy and innocent man, since he, accustomed to worldly affairs, was thinking of nothing less than the inward quiet of the religious life. The count had also long been not a little hostile to the abbot, because, with the monks themselves also advising it, he had tried rather more freely than was in his mind to require that the transfer of the monastery be strengthened by pontifical authority. Hence he gladly admitted the brothers’ accusations against Abbot Frederick, and would soon have deprived him of the abbatial honor, had he not been for some time prevented by the counsel of his most devout wife. But the cause of accusation brought to him did not seem sufficient, for which an abbot should be deposed: not because he had regard in this to the principle of justice, but lest he be accused among men of fickleness, if, on so slight a complaint of the monks, he had removed from the prelacy a man otherwise of good fame and reputation among all.

Meanwhile, while these things were being done, Abbot Frederick, a lover of holy simplicity, intent as was his custom on heavenly disciplines, wholly ignorant of the machinations being prepared for his destruction, did not turn aside the deceits and frauds of [unclear]. But when the wicked accusers perceived that by this way they could not succeed in utterly casting down the man of the saints, they found a manner of [unclear] of fornication. For, having taken counsel among themselves at the devil’s suggestion, they found a crime. The diseased sheep lay fornication upon their shepherd, so that by injury they might overcome him whom they could not conquer. When this report was brought to the count, he did not examine the case according to the rule of equity, did not have witnesses produced free from suspicion of wickedness, but [unclear] the violence of the man set over them, and therefore admitted as witnesses against the innocent man those whom he had as accusers in a matter not pertaining to him. Therefore the presumptuous judge deposed the holy and innocent man, whom, though accused, he had removed: not confessed, not [unclear] by lawful testimony.
