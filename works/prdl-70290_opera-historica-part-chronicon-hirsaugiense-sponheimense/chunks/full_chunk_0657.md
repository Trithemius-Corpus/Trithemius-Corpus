*Johannes Trithemius.*

... around the feast of Pentecost, the Bavarians, Swabians, and Hessians, conspiring together as one, with certain other princes and cities, ranged everywhere with hostile incursion through all the land of our most serene prince Philip, Count Palatine, both here around the Rhine and in Bavaria, and carried off whatever they found not sufficiently fortified, sparing no place, showing respect to no religion, and reverencing neither sex nor age. They plundered churches, robbed monasteries, carrying away whatever they found: chalices, coverings of altars too, bells, all utensils, both those belonging to divine worship and those belonging to human use. Many villages around us, quite fine and large, with most richly adorned churches, were burned by the enemies and lie utterly consumed by fire. That notable monastery of our order also, Limburg near Durkheim, was reduced to ashes with its church and all its dwellings by the retainers of the Count of Leiningen, and completely destroyed. Those two monasteries around us, one the Porta Mariae of the Wilhelmites, by the [Alexandrines?], the other of St. Disibod, of the Cistercians, by the Palatines, were then stripped of all movable goods found there; they were redeemed with money so that they might not be burned. The farms of our monastery too, almost all of them, lie either burned or otherwise utterly destroyed.

Meanwhile, while these things were being done here around us, I was forced to remain for twenty-two weeks in the nearby Palatine town of Kreuznach, to which I had withdrawn on the vigil of Peter and Paul from fear of the enemies, not without very great loss to the household property. For whatever was in the monastery, lest it fall to the enemies as plunder, had to be carried there. And indeed many things, in the carrying and carrying back, were scattered or even damaged, since none of us could be present who might have applied the necessary care to the goods.

When at last the tumult of the raging people had somehow been calmed, I returned to my monastery, almost empty of grain and necessities, all of which had been consumed by reason of the war. And behold, after a few months had passed, while I was at Budor, certain men from our hooded monks, whom we call donati, led by I know not what spirit, were noted as having conspired against us with the Cynonotes, who were secretly hostile to us on account of the Palatine; they made the Duke of Transylvania hostile to us. He, giving credit to false accusations, had certain persons of our household detained under ecclesiastical immunity, alleging fabricated causes, although everything proceeded from another source.

When I at Budor heard what had been done, moved by the indignity of the matter, from the first day of the month of April in the one thousand five hundred and fifth year of our salvation down to this day I remained outside the monastery, not from fear of the duke or of anyone else whatsoever, but indignant at the memory of the injuries. I was in the March of Brandenburg for nine months with the most serene prince Joachim, elector of the Holy Empire, having for many years been desired and requested by him; and, having now been rewarded by him not inconsiderably, I departed and returned to Budor, and am remaining here until I see what may usefully be done by me.

There are many reasons and causes with me on account of which I shall not readily consent to return to Sponheim. Yet lest I seem to have done anything without consulting the fathers of the order, I shall await their assembly, which will be held, according to custom, at Mainz at the beginning of September. If they are able to provide for my affairs by some useful plan, it will be well done; but if they cannot, or certainly if they neglect to do so, necessity itself will give me counsel, so that I may follow the fortune that presses me. Nor am I the first who, after suffering injury, abandoned the place committed to him, lest he serve the ungrateful, since many before me, not only abbots but also pontiffs, by resigning their dignities and prelacies because of the wickedness of their subjects, have provided for their own safety.

Almighty and most loving God, who feeds the birds of heaven and gives food to beasts of burden in due time, will, I do not doubt, provide for me too the means by which I may live, since I neither desire many things nor covet great things. For having food and clothing, according to the word of St. Christophilus, I have already learned to be content with these, as a certain one of the Greeks said:

[unclear]

I do not seek or wish to be made rich; but let it be mine
to live among modest things, with no evil possessing me.

We desire to know what new thing you have forged; nor do we believe that you grow feeble in idleness, nor that you can spend even one day without continual lucubration. Farewell, mindful of us before God. From Budor, on the last day of the month of July, in the year of the Christians 1506.
