Witnesses, however, who are to be named and produced, if through favor, hatred, love, or fear they withdraw themselves, are to be compelled by a like censure to bear witness to the truth. In testimony of these our letters, to which our seal has been appended. Given in our city of Mainz on the last day of the month of July, in the year of the Lord 1469.

When this commission had been received, the aforesaid abbots took care to bring it as quickly as possible to the knowledge of the aforementioned prince, Frederick. When he had learned these things, he appointed the nineteenth day of the month of August for coming here to the monastery in the morning, secretly or unexpectedly, so that no one among those dwelling around should perceive anything before they arrived. When the appointed day came, which was the Saturday after the feast of the Assumption of the blessed Mary ever virgin, councillors sent by the aforesaid prince, with an armed band, entered this [unclear] monastery in the morning after Prime, taking the greatest care that none of the monks should escape or that anything could be carried away from the monastery.

Meanwhile, after an hour, there also arrived the [unclear] abbots, Hermann of St. James and Conrad of St. John; and as soon as they had been admitted, they entered the chapter-place and caused the abbot and convent to be present. When these had been gathered, in the presence of the prince’s councillors they had the mandate given them by the archbishop of Mainz read before all by a public notary. But when [unclear] had learned that they could not, nor could they in any way undergo the Bursfeld reformation, which was too austere and unbearable to them, the commissioners, after hearing these and many other things that they brought forward against the reformation, not wishing to condemn anyone hastily, admonished them to deliberate better, and thereafter began the office of visitation, making inquiry into both conditions of the monastery.

On the following day, which was the twentieth of the month of August, namely Sunday and the feast of Saint Bernard, abbot, when the inquiry had been completed and the visitors had discovered that the abbot of this place, lord Otto, both because of the weakness of his body and also because of his lesser experience in regular observance, was not suitable or useful for pastoral care under the reformation to be introduced, after pious admonitions they at last persuaded him to yield the abbatial dignity, lest he should in any way wish to impede the holy business of reformation. Therefore he resigned the abbacy in the aforesaid year and on the aforesaid day, before a notary and witnesses, receiving an annual pension for the sustenance of his body. Not long afterward he was made provost of the nuns on Mount St. Rupert near Bingen; there at last he rested in the Lord.

After the resignation had been made, as we have said, on that day and the next the commissioners, together with the duke’s secretaries sent for this purpose, received from the resigning abbot the computation and accounting of all the revenues and debts of this monastery, drawing up an inventory, as is customary, by the hand of the notary. It was found that the monastery had come almost to the utmost poverty. For everything had been pledged: estates and revenues, so that nothing free remained for the sustenance of the monks. The sum of the debts, for which it had been pledged, exceeded the calculation of two thousand two hundred florins, besides very many other burdens.
