The clergy, together with all the people, obeying the apostolic commands, received him with honor and reverence, and peacefully admitted him into possession of the church of Mainz. For he was a learned and magnificent man, worthy of the pontificate itself, who established many things useful for morals in synods, and presided vigorously for fifteen years. Hearing this, Henry, count of Luxembourg, who had sent him to the pope on behalf of his brother Baldwin, was angry, although afterward he confessed that the whole thing had been done by divine ordinance. For through the agency of the same master, Baldwin was afterward made archbishop of Trier, and Henry himself king of the Romans, as we shall say.

*King Albert holds the funeral rites at Speyer.*

In the same year, on the day of the commemoration of Saint Paul the Apostle, Albert, king of the Romans, celebrated with great pomp the obsequies of the recently deceased king of Bohemia in the greater church of Speyer, with many princes, bishops, and abbots standing by. Among them Henry, the aforementioned abbot of this house, was present, not of the lowest reputation. There, during the ringing, the great bell fell, and, breaking two vaults, fell to the pavement and was split into two parts.

In these times also a great discord arose between Diether, archbishop of Trier, and the townsmen of Koblenz, the pontiff oppressing the townsmen with exactions, and they refusing obedience. At last the archbishop, unable to endure their insolence any longer, hired fighters with money, besieged Koblenz, took it, and received its surrender, the townsmen being compelled to obedience. He spent much money on the preparation for this victory; to obtain it he pledged almost all the estates of his diocese.

*1306.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Henry, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1306, on the day of the Purification of Blessed Mary ever Virgin, when the ice and snows had melted, there was a very great inundation of waters around the Rhine and the Main, which brought almost infinite losses upon the people living round about, overthrowing many houses and dwellings, and with incredible fury devastating gardens, vineyards, fields, and meadows. By its excessive force it threw down the bridge over the Main at Frankfurt, partly overturned the walls of Mainz, utterly overthrew two towers in the wall of Cologne, and drowned many people in various places. In these times the Hospitallers of Saint John of Jerusalem, aided by Christian forces, after vigorously besieging the island of Rhodes, together with five others adjacent in the Mediterranean Sea, continuously for four years, finally obtained them; the Turks were killed and expelled, and they have peacefully held it down to the present day, for 196 years.

In this same year the Jews throughout the kingdom of France, after all their goods had been brought into the fisc, were killed; others were driven from the kingdom half-naked.

*1307.*

In the seventh year of Abbot Henry, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1307, indiction 5, when Diether of Nassau, archbishop of Trier, had alienated very much because of the multitude of expenses for the preparations of war, he was denounced by his clergy and summoned to the examination of the Apostolic See in Gaul. But before the date for appearing arrived, he was seized by illness and departed from this life on the twenty-third day of November. He was buried in the church of the Preachers, of whose order he had previously made profession, in the city of Trier. After his death, lord Peter, archbishop of Mainz, had such influence with Pope Clement V and brought it about that Baldwin, brother of Henry, count of Luxembourg, a young man in the year of his age [unclear], to whom two years before he had denied the church of Mainz because of his minority, as we have said, was raised to the archbishopric of Trier. After being ordained, and received honorably as archbishop by all the clergy and people of Trier, he presided for forty-six years, one month, and eighteen days, a man in every respect vigorous and magnificent, who ruled his church excellently and recovered everything alienated.

*1308.*

In the eighth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1308, Albert, king of the Romans, when he had crossed the Rhine by boat with a few men, while the rest of the army waited for the boat on the other side of the bank, as is customary, John, his nephew through his brother, approached with the associates of his conspiracy and killed him when he feared no such thing; slipping away in flight, he escaped. The cause was this: Rudolf, duke of Swabia, brother of King Albert, when about to die, entrusted the aforesaid John, his son, still a boy, together with his whole duchy, to his guardianship.
