*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

...to be sent to Libanius Gallus in France; for [he has written?] to me in the meantime, and makes no mention of them, with the result that I doubt whether they have come into his hands. Johannes Palconydorus, your fellow Carmelite, when he last left me at Sponheim, promised that he would return in a short time; behold, four years have now passed, during which I have been able to hear or learn nothing of him anywhere. If you know where he is, or whether he is alive or dead, I ask you to inform me as soon as possible.

I, sitting here in a place of solitude and quiet, victory over my enemies having been granted to me, devote myself to sacred letters, and I find true what divine Scripture promises: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth,” [unclear]. Pray humbly to God for me, who raises the poor man from want, that he may deign to keep me in his will. For I know that the time of my pilgrimage on earth is short. As Seneca says, what we live is a point, or rather less than a point. And this very least thing nature has divided by a certain appearance of a longer span. For she wished one part of this to be infancy, another childhood, another youth, another a kind of decline from that into old age; and this, heavy with its own inconveniences, she does not allow to be quiet, so that a certain wise man in Ecclesiasticus said with just consideration: “Great occupation has been created for all men, a heavy yoke upon the sons of Adam, from the day of their coming forth from their mother’s womb until the day of their burial in the earth, the mother of all.”

For all things that are from the earth shall be turned back into the earth. And therefore man has been made like vanity, since his days pass like a shadow. Behold, the brief years pass away; we are tossed by storms of vicissitudes. For what are we wretched mortals, if not wind, a shadow, we who live in turmoil and, falling in a moment, appear no more? As Sophocles in the Ajax introduces the raging Odysseus speaking:

[Greek unclear]; that is:

For I see that we, being nothing, are nothing other than
images, all of us who live, or a light shadow.

Hence Homer, prince of poets, contemplating the misery of man, groans and says in the Iliad, [unclear], as you yourself know:

[Greek unclear]

For certainly there is nothing more wretched than man,
of all things that breathe and creep upon the earth.

We are to be compared with the leaves of trees, as the same Homer says in the sixth book, since just as leaves fall from the trees when winter hastens on, and others grow up in the season of spring, so of the race of men one generation is born, and another dies. A leaf which has once fallen does not rise again to the tree; after a man has once died, he will by no means return thereafter to this life. Ah, gods above, how much blind night mortal hearts have! We mortals live and do not consider that we are going to die. [unclear], let us rejoice, we embrace perishable things as though we were going to live forever; when we least expect it we are snatched away from here as if we had never been born. O Kymolanus, let us think on our last things while it helps to have thought of them, lest too late we lament the sloth of life.

The Greek grammar of Lascaris, which I lent you at Cologne, is yours, since it is not necessary to me. For I have another like it, recently printed at Venice. I know no one here devoted to Greek letters except the one Engelhard Funck, dean of our monastery, a learned man, practiced in verse as well as in prose. Farewell, mindful of us before God. From Würzburg, on the sixteenth day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*XLV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James in the suburb of Würzburg, to Christian Masseus, priest of Ghent, residing in the monastery of Saint Jerome, his very dear friend, greeting.*

How great is the desire with which I long to know what you are doing, how you are, what new thing you are writing, or whether you have completed your chronicle, I cannot express in letters, nor, since we are separated far from one another, make known to you in words. Most of all, however, I would wish sometime to see your lucubrations on the reckoning of times, which you long ago promised to transcribe for me. Five years have now flowed by from that time when you began to write the Chronicle...
