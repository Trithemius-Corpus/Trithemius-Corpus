I ask that hereafter you [produce?] such little works, the working-out of which may sweetly increase the love of God in you, and lead the reader’s mind to contempt for worldly things. Let childish games now give way: poems of poets do not befit an old man; you must proceed in prose, in keeping with the honor of old age. Let immature youth play with tortuous little verses; we love the plain road. But if you like to write verse, I say again that an old man ought to compose no poems except penitential ones, perhaps epitaphs for the dead, unless he is such a paraphrast as has always had practice in verse and never in prose, so that, with Prudentius, Sedulius, Juvencus, and their like, having turned to penitence, he may apply the weapons of worldly wisdom to the praises of divine Scripture. We read no poems of the old holy doctors, Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory, Origen, John Chrysostom, Basil, Cyril, and very many others, except hymns composed to the praise of God and a few epitaphs for the dead, although we do not doubt that they possessed the art. For, wishing by their lucubrations to benefit the salvation of souls, they preferred to write in prose rather than in verse, because it is difficult, in all the things that usefully present themselves to be written, to observe the law of meters; and this kind of teaching would seem more fitting for young men than for old men, and rarely does it happen that anyone is found perfect in both. You ask that I write out for you an account of all my lucubrations, their titles and subjects, especially of those which I composed after the publication of the book on ecclesiastical writers. For you say that you have a most honorable reason for seeking an account of them. I shall satisfy your desire as soon as I have leisure, which could not now be done, since the messenger was hurrying and I was occupied with other matters. I do not doubt that Herbenus is most loving toward me; I love him no less than I am loved by him. Greet in my name, whenever opportunity is given, our Herbenus, our common friend and most upright fellow-philosopher, who is worthy of the friendship of all good men, being distinguished no less for holiness of life than for learning in letters. Farewell, sound in mind and body, mindful of me before God. From my monastery of St. James at Würzburg, on the 12th day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1507.

*XLI.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of St. James at Würzburg, to Wilhelm Veldicus Monapius, parish priest in Dirmstein, theologian and mathematician, our very dear friend: greetings.*

My friend, I received on the first day of the month of August the letter that you sent to me on the fifth day of the month of July, together with Roger’s, and I have resolved to answer them in order if I can. And first indeed I give you great thanks for your goodwill toward me, by which you rejoice in my tranquillity, and are glad that I have found a place suitable for philosophizing with freedom. I myself indeed rejoice not a little. Greet in my name, whenever opportunity is given, our Herbenus, our common friend and most upright fellow-philosopher, who is worthy of the friendship of all good men, being distinguished [unclear] for life than for learning in letters. Farewell, sound in mind and body, mindful of me before God. From my monastery of St. James at Würzburg, on the 12th day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1509.
