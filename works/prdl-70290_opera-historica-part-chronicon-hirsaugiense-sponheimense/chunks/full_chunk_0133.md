But he himself, a quiet man, faint-hearted, mild, and gentle, not sufficient to bear the burdens of secular business, withdrew to inward things and devoted his attention to prayer, reading, meditation, and the contemplation of spiritual exercises. He was elected, indeed, because of fear of Gebhard, the aforementioned bishop of Speyer, who was trying to retain that abbey for himself: so that, if he attempted to begin any injury or violence against the monastery and its monks, he would be restrained by the authority of Bruno, the full brother of that abbot, who was the most powerful among all the Swabians. He also had very many other men, distinguished by riches, power, and nobility, joined to him by blood and friendship, who by their power would easily have hindered all the bishop’s attempts. But when the bishop saw that the brethren had ordained another abbot for themselves, he indeed took it badly; nevertheless he attempted no evil against them. For he was so involved and occupied in the cares and business of the church of Speyer that he was allowed little time to think about others’ affairs. Therefore Bruno, confirmed in the abbacy, entrusted the ordering of secular business to suitable brethren; and he himself, attending with the others to inward matters, showed his life to his subjects as an example of good works. Although he was noble and weak by nature, nevertheless he was never costly or singular even in his food.

*1106.* While the first year of his rule was still running, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1106, Henry the younger king, son of Emperor Henry IV, announced a meeting of princes at Mainz at Christmas; and there was so great a gathering of bishops, abbots, and princes as had not been seen for many cycles of years. There the legates of the apostolic see arrived, the bishops of Albano and Constance, testifying that the sentence of anathema had been thundered against Henry the elder, the emperor, by so many supreme pontiffs and other bishops, and proclaiming that he had been and was cut off from the communion of all the faithful for many years [unclear]. He resigned the scepter, orb, and crown before the princes and legates, and promised thereafter to take counsel for his soul. In this order Henry, the seventh of this name, after his father and then by all the princes of Germany, was now elected king a second time; and, confirmed as Catholic by the apostolic legates through the laying-on of hands, after receiving the oaths according to the custom of the time both from the bishops and from the laity, he began to reign, in the year, as has been said, of the Lord’s Nativity 1106. And from then he ruled for 20 years.

In the same year Henry the elder, Emperor Henry IV, father of Henry V, died at Liège, in the fiftieth year of his reign and the twenty-third of his empire; and he was first buried in a certain deserted church, until, through the intervention of his son, after absolution by the pontiff of the apostolic see, he was carried to Speyer and entombed. But, to comprise in a brief summary the evil history of that man, let us insert what the ancients wrote about him. That this Henry was a man [unclear], and by just judgment cast out from the Church, was truly evident to all in his time. For he sold all spiritual things; he was disobedient to the apostolic see; and, after the vicar of Christ, Pope Gregory VII, had been put to flight, he set up the idol Wibert of Ravenna in that very see. Against his lawful wife, a most honorable matron, he committed unheard-of crimes; and, renewing the Nicolaitan heresy, he prostituted her against her will to be violated by many by force. For these and many other crimes, excommunicated by many Roman and other pontiffs, he [unclear] the curse of the apostolic see, spurned it, and held it in contempt. From this there followed a very great persecution in the Church of God, through envy and discord, while one persecuted another even to death; and there was such a pernicious schism in the Church of God, with some adhering to the pope, others to the emperor, that apostolic censure was held for nothing, and no one cared about the sentence of excommunication itself. And although in all these things the emperor was most obstinate, so that he would not by any reasoning recognize the authority of the apostolic see, [unclear] when at some time he was sitting for the necessities of nature, certain men wishing to pierce him through were captured as assassins. Many princes also, who committed many evils against him and inflicted great contempt upon him, as soon as they had prostrated themselves before him and sought pardon, he mercifully forgave everything. Toward the poor he was very merciful and bestowed much alms upon them, and he had more compassion than can be said for all the afflicted.
