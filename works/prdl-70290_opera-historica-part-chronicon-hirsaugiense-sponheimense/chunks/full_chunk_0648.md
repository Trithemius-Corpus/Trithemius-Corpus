Consider, finally, most revered father, your only brother, Master Jacob Trithemius, a very dear friend to me, lest, laboring in vain among the Limburgers, he suffer the loss of a noble talent. For he will be able to obtain no profit from those men who, turning over stones and logs, have no commerce at all with the study of the Scriptures. He is a man slight by nature, born for philosophy, not for the labors of peasants; if he hopes that he will attain anything great, among those men he will feel himself most certainly led astray. Be dutiful toward your own, you who have always been most merciful toward strangers. For since you are known and familiar to so many and such great princes, it will not be difficult for you to obtain some ecclesiastical office for your brother, from which he may lead an honorable life with letters. You who have so often interceded for strangers and been heard will suffer no refusal when intervening for your own. May your dignity prosper through many ages. From Speyer, on the Ides of November, in the year of salvation 1505.

*52. John Trithemius to Eucharius Heimerius of Neometa, greetings.*

You advise well, Eucharius, that we should return more quickly to our own affairs, as though there were not a fatherland everywhere for us, whom the Christian religion commands to leave father and mother for the love of God, and the monastic institution enjoins the renunciation of all earthly things. For we follow the prince, compelled by a certain necessity, not as courtiers, but rather as men needing counsel and assistance, since, being abandoned by those necessary to us, we fear graver dangers. It is hardly unknown to you what an outrageous insult we have endured from the Cynonoti, under whose rule we shall not easily consent to serve for long. With them certain of our own, filled with a spirit of faithlessness and conspiring against us, added the poison of malice; going about, they increased the envy of many rivals against us. It therefore pleased us to yield to evil for a time, until our own men at last learn by experience what good our presence hitherto brought them. And what was not sufficiently discussed when we left our fatherland? Those who were the authors of such great evils against us, those who from among our own were said to have conspired with our rivals, all cried out that they were innocent. Overcome by the counsel and assent of my most illustrious prince Philip, Count Palatine and of Brandenburg, we consented to leave our fatherland, by God's bounty to return to the Rhine at the feast of Easter. Meanwhile, if our rivals repent of the rashness committed against us, it will be well done and we shall return to the monastery; but if they persist in their stubbornness, if they do not acknowledge themselves for their injuries, if our brothers have dealt with their leader no differently than hitherto, we shall adhere to our own counsel, nor shall we humble ourselves before our enemies, nor shall we return to the monastery, but we shall provide for ourselves another dwelling where, with peace and tranquility, we may serve almighty God in the order. For, having a just cause of indignation at the injuries, we cannot easily forget them, nor henceforth do we ever trust those men who, though harmed by us in nothing, presumed to carry out so great an insult against us. Let it suffice our rivals that, having patience for the love of Christ, we do not seek vengeance, although we could avenge ourselves; we pass over insults in silence. They thought us so bound in mind to one place that we would necessarily not care about what they did to our contempt, as though outside Sponheim there were no place for us to remain. We trust in the Lord Jesus, who has never abandoned anyone crying out to him in tribulation, that he will have mercy on us and will prepare a place for us where, with greater peace and tranquility, we may devote ourselves to him than we ever could at Sponheim. The courts of princes do not delight us, as you suppose, nor have we resolved to adhere to an earthly prince unless it is necessary, although there are not lacking several who would admit us with great desire to be supported permanently. The continual study of the Scriptures has given us this: that we fix our mind not on place, but on truth. What you advised about your brother we received gladly; perhaps for him a richer lot will arise in time. Farewell; pray for us to God, who alone can do all things. From Berlin, on the eighth day of the month of January, in the year of the Lord 1506.
