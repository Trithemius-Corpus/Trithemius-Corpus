But if we wish to describe a master of the seven liberal arts, those things which we said pertain to a priest will not suffice. For the method of a master teaching in the schools is one thing, and that of a priest preaching in the Church is another. For the latter, indeed, a use of the sciences drawn from an art in a certain general way is sufficient; but upon the former the necessary office of teaching enjoins that even the particulars of the individual arts be examined particularly in their special character, so that he may know not only how to use the sciences he has acquired, but also how to give rules and precepts to his pupils for acquiring them. But you, who, after receiving the mastership and leaving the schools of the artists, have betaken yourself to Christ’s school, having been made a priest and minister in the Church of God, retaining only the necessary use of secular letters, turn yourself henceforth wholly to the study of the divine Scriptures, in which true saving knowledge is contained, and heavenly wisdom sweetly illumines the mind. For all the knowledge of this world is vain if it has not been turned to the worship of God. But sacred Scripture, which we rightly call divine, far exceeds all the knowledge and teaching of this world, since it proclaims true things without ambiguity, calls the mind of the gentle reader from earthly things to heavenly ones, and, for the love of God, renders it humble in prosperity and strong and constant in adversities. This is the force of the sacred river which makes glad the city of God, at once shallow and deep, in which both the simple and little lamb may walk and the greatest elephant may swim. Wondrous is that river, in which the simple Christian, even without literature, may, so to speak, pass securely to salvation with dry feet; and the great and subtly learned man can swim by being soberly wise, and, if he searches too deeply, be submerged. For it has, in public, that from which it nourishes little ones, and it keeps hidden that by which it leads the minds of the lofty into wonder. For in one and the same utterance, while it narrates history, it unfolds manifold mystery; on the surface it sweetly refreshes the simple, and in the secrets of mystery it sublimely exercises the wise. In what is open it is drink, because it needs no breaking; in the more obscure parts it is food, because it cannot be swallowed without the breaking of exposition. Therefore Saint Ambrose instructs us, saying: We ought long to rub and polish the utterances of the heavenly Scriptures, turning them over with our whole mind and heart, so that that spiritual juice may spread into all the veins of the soul. Rich and most fertile is the field of the divine Scriptures; it has within itself all delights and brings forth every sweetness. And just as manna tasted in the mouth according to the will of each person, so the divine word will furnish in your heart every flavor you wish. Nothing can be better, sweeter, or more pleasant for a Christian priest than if at all times he occupies himself with the sacred Scriptures with the desire of his heart, and keeps himself clean from this wicked age. For blessed Jerome says: Love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh. Therefore let the divine Scriptures always be in your hands, and let them be turned over in your heart by continual meditation; and do not think it sufficient to keep the commandments of God in memory and to forget them in works, but read them often for this reason, that you may do what you have learned. For it is not the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of God’s commandments who receive from him the crown of righteousness. Live in such a way that you may deserve to attain the wisdom, the knowledge of divine and human things, necessary for you, because there is no more pitiable poverty, or more shameful want, than for one established in the priestly order to lack wisdom, and for him who is commanded by office to teach others to be a foolish man and without learning. Farewell, and pray to God for us. From Würzburg, on the 24th day of the month of June, in the year of Christ 1506.

*II.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg, to the prior and convent at Sponheim: to have continual fear of the divine name.*

Almighty God, whose power is eternal and whose will is never unjust, holy and just in all his works, who permits nothing without cause. He himself, just, holy, and merciful, has made all things in true judgment, since he is good and almighty; he does absolutely no injury to anyone, he who keeps truth forever and ever, who executes judgment for those suffering injury, who gives food to the hungry. The Lord raises up those who have been cast down; the Lord loves the righteous. The Lord kills and makes alive; he brings down to the lower world and brings back. The Lord makes poor and enriches; he humbles and exalts. He raises the needy from the dust, and from the dung lifts up the poor. He will preserve the feet of his saints, and the impious shall be silent in darkness. If it had not been that the Lord was among us, let Abbot Trithemius now say, if it had not been that the Lord was among us, when wicked men rose up against us, perhaps they would have swallowed us alive.
