*Johannes Trithemius.*

...they had been afflicted in many ways by the Egyptians with mud and brick. The apostles of the Lord also, beaten with rods by the Jews, cast into prison for the name of Jesus, and having suffered many insults, afterwards passed over to salvation. By their example you too, indeed, suffered injury and insult from your monks, lest, when you were to be transferred elsewhere by the will of God, you should seem to have abandoned the wicked without cause. The monks of Sponheim, strengthened by the counsel and assistance of depraved men, full of the poison of malice, were trying to bring your holy name into contempt and reproach, but they had no power at all, since, by the justice of God taking vengeance upon them, they are now all terrified and confounded, falling into the pit which they made. But you, most learned teacher, remain glorious in the esteem of all good men, and your name is celebrated more and more from day to day. Farewell, most reverend father, and hold me, your disciple, commended. Given from Trittenheim on the eighteenth day of the month of April, which was the second Sunday after the feast of Easter, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1507.

*XVII.*

*To the Reverend Father Lord Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of the monastery of St. James at Wurzburg, his most sweet teacher. Johannes Gotfridi, useless pastor of the church in Mandal near Kreuznach, sends greeting.*

Most loving teacher, by your letters, at once most sweet and exceedingly bitter, I was so overcome, with little eyes weeping unceasingly from bitterness, and so disturbed over your resignation, that the bitterness of the death of all my parents and friends could never have saddened me so much. For I alone am not now saddened by your perpetual absence, but all the priests, masters, and learned men round about lament your departure most bitterly. For to whomever I showed your letters, and I showed them to many, as soon as they had begun to read them they were compelled by tears to interrupt their reading. Now it is said with one voice by all: Alas, that so great a man, so learned, so experienced, and celebrated in every land, one whose like Germany does not have, excelling in wisdom and religion, compelled by the envy of monks to abandon his monastery and his country alike, has transferred himself to foreign nations.

That most unjust chancellor of Duke Johannes, made your slanderer and enemy unjustly through I know not what mules, unless through your monks and subjects, at the instigation of the abbot of St. James at Mainz and of your brothers, made the most pious prince offended with you. For love of you I did not blush recently to reproach him for this to his face before many people, at the time when your successor was being elected in Sponheim. Answering in fury, he said: Who drove out your abbot Trithemius, whom you commend so highly? Or who harmed him, saddened him, or offended him? Why did he not remain in his monastery? Or why did he not return, since there is no one who would have prevented him? Why did he not acknowledge Duke Johannes as equally his lord and the protector of the monastery, just like his Palatine, whom he always preferred to all? That useless beast was saying many other things too, by which he publicly declared his envy against you, the best of men.

It was the day of the month of December when the election was being celebrated, and the monastery was filled with knights, so that the fathers of the order, together with the monks, could scarcely have a suitable place for electing. O most sweet teacher, how bitter a spectacle it was to my heart to see, in the place of you, my most loving father and the best and most upright of men, another man raised up, and one unworthy, unlearned, inexperienced, [unclear], who in fact betrayed you like Judas and, under the appearance of humility, was always secretly opposed to you. I was present at the election, as you know; I heard and saw everything that was said and done, and, when I called you back to memory, all my inward parts trembled, and amid the song of gladness my little eyes poured out tears without interruption.

Do not suppose, most learned teacher, nor take it that I write these things from zeal for vanity or with the intention of flattering, since not I alone, but the whole neighborhood, of laymen as much as of priests, is affected by your most grievous departure with sorrow of mind and body. And not undeservedly. For you were the glory of the whole country, the light of Christians, the teacher and mirror of cloistered and religious men, the defender, instructor, and advocate of rural priests, also the father of the poor and the physician of the sick. We all used your prudence and learning according to necessity at will, and there was no hesitation that was not removed by your counsel. For you were a most ready comforter to all who fled to you, and whatever could be requested from you lay open without envy to the poor as to the rich. O how often, when I was troubled now by patrons, now indeed by peasants, did I flee to your wisdom as to an oracle, seek counsel, implore aid, and, always protected by your defense, come out victorious in all things. Never from you without...

*Trithemius’ kindness.*
