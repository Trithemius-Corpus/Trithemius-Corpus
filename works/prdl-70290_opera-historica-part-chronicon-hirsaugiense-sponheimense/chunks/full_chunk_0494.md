At length judgment was pronounced by the judges of the see of Mainz for that cleric against the abbot; this deed brought great loss upon this monastery.

In the same year also Louis, Duke of Bavaria, surnamed the Rich, son of Henry and father of Duke George, gathered a great army of Bavarians, Bohemians, and Austrians, and besieged the city of Eichstatt; but, when an agreement intervened, he withdrew. From there he led his army against the Margrave of Brandenburg, and took two of his castles, Landeck and Stauff, and utterly destroyed them. Albert the Margrave came out against him with a great army, having with him William, Duke of Saxony; but they did nothing worthy of memory. But an agreement was made in such a way that the Margrave should surrender into Duke Louis’s hands certain letters and privileges given against him by the emperor, and should receive his fiefs from the church of Wurzburg, which before he had refused to do, as Burgrave of Nuremberg; and they departed from one another. Afterwards, on a certain day appointed for this at Nuremberg, the Margrave, as he had promised, delivered the emperor’s letters to Duke Louis in the public town hall, and he at once tore them up in the sight of all. When the emperor heard this he took it very ill, and wished Duke Louis to have incurred the crime of lese-majesty; and for that reason he protested against the agreement made between them, and many evils followed from it.

*1459.*

In the fourteenth year of our abbot Conrad, on the day before the Nones of May, Theoderic Schenk of Erbach, Archbishop of Mainz, died. Diether of Isenburg, elected by the chapter on the seventeenth day of September, [unclear] on the Kalends of July, was elector for almost five years. Adhering to the Margrave of Baden and Louis the Black, duke, with the other accomplices, against Frederick, Count Palatine, guardian of Philip, he damaged his church in many ways, and exposed the whole diocese to burning, plunder, and devastation by enemies and friends.

In the same year, on the second day of the month of September, Siegfried, Bishop of Speyer, died. John Nix of Hoheneck, called Ensberg, elected by the chapter on the eighteenth day of September, succeeded him and presided for five years. Adhering to the Margrave of Baden and Louis the Black, duke, with the other accomplices, against Frederick, Count Palatine, guardian of Philip, he damaged his church in many ways, and exposed the whole diocese to burning, plunder, and devastation by enemies and friends.

In the year noted above, too, the sixteenth provincial chapter of our order was celebrated after the feast of the Lord’s Resurrection, on the second Sunday; at it presided George of St. Giles there, Berthold of St. Giles in Brunswick, Eberhard of Mount St. James near Mainz, and Henry in Ettenheim, most religious abbots of monasteries of our order.

In the same year Emperor Frederick commanded all the cities of the empire to bring aid with all their strength to Albert, Margrave of Brandenburg, against the aforesaid Louis, Duke of Bavaria, guilty, as he himself maintained, of lese-majesty. All in Swabia did this, except the Nurembergers. Therefore Duke Louis, moved by this, gathered a great army of Bavarians, Bohemians, and Austrians, entered the Margrave’s land, took many villages, castles, and towns, practiced burning and plunder, and inflicted boundless losses on him, with no one able to resist. But afterwards the Margrave, having called together the cities of the empire, while Louis was in Bohemia for I know not what reason, entered his lands in like warfare, devastated, plundered, and burned them. Finally agreement was again made through the king of Bohemia, on condition that what had been taken should be restored.

*1460.*

In the fifteenth year of our abbot Conrad, Albert, Duke of Bavaria, son of the late Duke Ernest, died, leaving five sons: John, who died without children; Sigismund; Christopher; Albert; Wolfgang; and four daughters: Elizabeth, Duchess of Saxony; Margaret, Countess of Mantua; and Anna, a nun, enclosed for the love of Christ in the monastery at Munich. Moreover Albert kept his aforesaid brother Christopher in chains for some time; the history of this is known to all.

In the same year Albert the Margrave, Diether, Archbishop of Mainz, Louis the Black, duke, and Emich, Count of Leiningen, with their adherents, conspiring together against the great Count Frederick, became his public enemies and defiers; with them was Count Ulrich of Wurttemberg, who had previously taken Philip’s mother as his wife. Therefore Duke Frederick, compelled by necessity, gathered an army to avenge the injuries, and first indeed [took] Schavenberg, a castle in Strata Montensis, commonly [unclear] among the abbots of this place [Gelerich?].
