*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

... from the burial. The prior and convent, lest they wander any longer like sheep without a shepherd, unanimously appointed the third day thereafter, that is, 23 January, for the election of a new abbot. When that day arrived, and those absent who ought to be present at an election of this kind had meanwhile been summoned, all assembled in the chapter-place in the morning; and, after the grace of the Holy Spirit had been invoked in the customary manner, after long and mature discussions held among themselves, they at last elected as the twenty-second abbot of this monastery Lutfrid, a monk of this convent, a gentle and religious man. After he had been confirmed by the Ordinary, the bishop of the church of Speyer, he received the office of benediction from Guido, cardinal of the holy Roman Church, bishop of Palestrina and legate of the Apostolic See throughout Germany. He presided over this monastery for eleven years and five days. For he refused to be blessed by his own bishop of Speyer, who favored King Philip against the mandate of the supreme pontiff; and for that reason, in accordance with the privilege long before granted by Pope Urban under Abbot Gebhard, [unclear].

[unclear] he was accustomed to be reproached; but, from earliest age devoting himself to peace and silence, even while still a boy he already seemed to live as one who had become a monk. When he had grown older, he resolved to renounce worldly affairs utterly for the love of Christ, and to serve the almighty Lord in tranquillity of mind and body. He therefore came to Hirsau, and under Abbot Conrad became a monk. He lived among the brethren without complaint and, after being tested for a good many years in certain lesser offices, at last, after the death of Marquard, as we have said, he was ordained abbot.

*His family and origin.*

He was born in East Francia, begotten of free and honorable parents. As a young man he was set to letters and made moderate progress in study. For he did not immerse himself, as many do, in the games of the people and temporal affairs, but rather entrusted himself to necessity. [unclear]

At about the same time at which his election was held at Hirsau, namely in the year of the Lord 1205, Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, and the bishop of Cambrai, appointed commissioners and judges by the Apostolic See, came to Cologne. In the greater church of St. Peter, before the clergy and the whole people, they publicly announced that Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, had been excommunicated by Pope Innocent, and they ordered that, in all the conventual and parish churches of the same city, on each Sunday and feast day, he should be declared excommunicate. They also had this in their mandate: that if, within a month after [unclear], he persisted in defying the censures of the supreme pontiff, the aforesaid commissioners should again come to Cologne. In the greater church, before the clergy and people, in the presence of King Otto, on the thirteenth day before the Kalends of July, by definitive sentence and apostolic authority they deposed the same Adolf from every office and archiepiscopal dignity, and by the same authority ordered the canons to elect another bishop. Obeying the apostolic commands, they elected Bruno of Sayn, provost of Bonn, as archbishop; and countless evils followed.

For Adolf, going to King Philip, on account of whose adherence he had been deposed, while Philip was holding court with the princes at Speyer, explained what he was suffering and sought counsel and help. Therefore, by the common decree of the princes, the king gathered a great army against Bruno and the people of Cologne; and after the Nativity of Blessed Mary ever Virgin he crossed the Moselle. On the feast of St. Michael the Archangel he encircled Cologne with a siege; when this was finally abandoned, he besieged and captured Neuss, which he gave to the deposed Adolf. When these things had been done, by burning, plundering, and miserably afflicting it everywhere, he devastated the whole province and captured many fortifications. He also built in his time a handsome and very strong castle called Landskron, to subdue the people of Cologne.

Otto of Ladenburg succeeded him in the episcopate and presided for sixteen years and five months, a magnanimous prince, feared for wisdom and dignity.
