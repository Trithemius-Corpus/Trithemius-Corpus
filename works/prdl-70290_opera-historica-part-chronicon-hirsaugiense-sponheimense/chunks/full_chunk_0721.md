*XXVIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, to Friedrich von Redwitz, canon and cantor of the church of Bamberg and provost in [Oringau?], his friend, greeting.*

Most distinguished sir, as to the fact that I return a reply rather late to your most elegant letter, do not think it was done through forgetfulness of you, but because of my usual literary occupations. That you have admitted me among your friends I both greatly congratulate myself upon and render you immortal thanks. For what sweeter thing could have happened to me, a man who is a stranger and established in a foreign land, than to obtain the friendship of a most excellent and most learned man? Not only nobility of birth and integrity of morals have made him worthy of regard, but also an education in every kind of letters, adorning him with all virtues, has deservedly rendered him glorious.

It delights me all the more to have entered into friendship with you, O most reverend father, because, according to the opinion of divine Jerome, I understand this bond of ours to be true: one joined by the glue of Christ, brought about not by advantage in household affairs, not merely by the presence of bodies, not by crafty and fawning flattery, but by the fear of God and the study of the divine Scriptures. This is perfect friendship, by which a friend is loved in God, and, through the love of Christ, there is one mind in two bodies, for no other reason than the likeness of studies by which both friends strive together toward the summit of the highest and unchangeable good.

Therefore true friendship cannot exist among evil and reprobate men, since, as Aristotle says, perfect friendship exists only with respect to one thing, because what is perfect cannot be directed toward many things but toward one. But the evil and reprobate always live in disturbance, never peaceable, never quiet, never ceasing to foam forth wickedness; they are carried away to many things, unity being neglected, and for this reason their associations should be called not friendships but rather conspiracies. For friendship is an equality of minds, standing firm in understanding and reason; wherefore it has never happened that there is any friendship among the wicked, who live by appetite and not by reason. The aforesaid philosopher expressed this very thing sufficiently in the Ethics, saying that only the friendship of good and honorable men is unchangeable.

No equality of minds, however, can be more constant than that which is born from divine love, by which the one, true, and only unchangeable good is known through faith with pure devotion, and is loved with fervent desire by friends in equal consent. Concerning this very bond the wise man says: “There is no comparison with a faithful friend,” because “a faithful friend is a strong protection; he who has found him has found a precious treasure.” Hence I rightly consider myself blessed, since I have found you a true friend, namely one who loves nothing else in me than the glue of Christian charity, which is the love of heavenly wisdom and the study of the divine Scriptures.

Who does not know that the enjoyment of eternal happiness, to which all our studies must be ordered, consists in the knowledge and love of the highest and constant principle of all things, which in our custom we call God; and that in the kingdom of blessedness that very enjoyment is common to all the good, and that by adherence to that good the perfect friendship of all the elect of God is confirmed, never to be dissolved by any intervening division? For the friendship of all who are united to their principle through the grace of enjoyment is therefore indissoluble and firm by an everlasting covenant, because they will one thing, love one thing, and consist in the enjoyment of one thing.

Therefore we, who have established the foundation of our friendship in Christian love to be made firm, approach that heavenly bond all the more nearly, the more we shall be in agreement in study and will upon the way that leads to it. There is, moreover, a threefold road to that friendship of eternal enjoyment, which directs those walking upon it into one way. Knowledge of divine goodness is acquired by the study of the sacred Scriptures; the love of heavenly desire is nourished by the exercise of virtues; and the worship of God is declared by the observance of the commandments.

Knowledge, therefore, causes love, because no one rightly loves what he does not know; love, moreover, demands worship, as Gregory says: “The love of God is never idle. For it works great things if it exists; but if it refuses to work, it is not love,” since the showing of work is a true sign of love. This is the threefold cord of true Christian friendship, which is not easily broken, because whoever walks in agreement with a faithful friend upon this road will easily overcome his adversaries: the flesh, the world, and the devil.
