1263.

In this year, on the day after the Epiphany of the Lord, in the time of Richard, king of the Romans, an agreement was made on Mount St. James between the aforesaid Philip of Hohenfels and the prelates of the church of Mainz, and was confirmed under penalties of proscription and other terrible censures, as is clear in the letters concerning it, which we have omitted because of their excessive length.

In these times also there was a long and very serious dissension between the archbishop and the citizens of Cologne, the former wishing to draw the liberties of the citizens to himself, while the latter fought most vigorously for liberty. At last the citizens, capturing their archbishop Engelbert and Theoderic of Falckenburg, his brother, compelled them to make peace.

1264.

Nicholas, the thirteenth prior of this monastery, died this year on the Kalends of January, a good man and a lover of regular discipline. After him a certain monk, named Otwinus, a wise and very notable man, was appointed the fourteenth prior. He presided in the office of prior for twelve years, and would gladly have reformed the collapsed monastic discipline, but because of the malice of the times he was able to make little progress.

In the same year the Hermit Friars of the order of St. Augustine, entering Worms, began to build a monastery, and, having completed it, they inhabit it down to this day.

*Concerning Peter, the eighth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for twenty-five years and three months, and concerning the various deeds of that time.*

After Abbot John had died and been buried, when the brethren of this monastery who were then present had assembled to elect a new pastor for the widowed church, a grave disturbance followed. For two men were elected through dissension. Peter, who was cellarer, concerning whom we spoke above, had one part of the convent for himself; and William, a monk who held the office of cantor, obtained the other part of the electors for himself.

And when a serious contention arose between the parties of this one monastery, and each party tried to defend its own elected man, Peter, who was a crafty man, using the former authority of his office, [unclear], surrounded by a band of his household supporters, powerfully invaded the abbacy and [unclear] himself in all things. Yet afterward he resigned the whole case into the hands of Werner, archbishop of Mainz. This contention lasted for the space of a year, not without damage to the temporal goods of the monastery and great danger to souls. Count Simon at first indeed supported the party of William and implored [unclear]. But at last Werner, archbishop of Mainz, summoned both elected men to him, and, carefully examining the manner and form of the election, and likewise the zeal of the electors, repudiated William and confirmed Peter as abbot of this monastery.

After he had devoted himself for some time to letters, he came to the monastery and under Abbot John became a monk; by him afterward he was appointed prior, as we have said, and then cellarer, and finally abbot.

*The church in Gentzingen is alienated.*

This Peter, in the first year of his confirmation, immediately gave the parish church in Gentzingen in the district of the Nahegau, which from the donation of the late noble man Udo our monastery had possessed peacefully for 138 years, to the chapter of the greater church of Mainz with all its appurtenances, as is clear in these letters:
