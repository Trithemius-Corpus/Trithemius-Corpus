*Familiar Letters*

... you will have [them] printed by the printers, of whom there are many in Nuremberg near you. If with kindly favor you are willing to meet my wishes, I shall see to it shortly that I send you two or three of my works. I recently wrote a certain prognostication, full of jokes and witticisms, which pleases many; but since your censorious severity disdains all jokes, I did not judge it proper to send these things to you, who, by the maturity of your life and words, are wont to be exceedingly formidable to trifles. I have also written to Jodocus Rubecacensis, a theologian most devoted to you in the church of Neometa, a single little work of familiar poems, which we both ask to have printed. I have written four volumes of my lucubrations, as you know, in each of which are contained thirty-four little works; if you order these to be given to the printers for printing, I shall send them all to you, when you have appointed for me a definite messenger to carry them. Our Johannes Canter, the Frisian physician, our common friend, has paid the debt of the flesh; remember him before God. Farewell, and pray for me. From Hegen, on the first day of the month of December, in the year of the Christians 1506.

*XII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, but formerly of Sponheim, sends greetings to Roger Sycamber, his most steadfast friend.*

I do not doubt that you rejoice together with me, if I myself rejoice. But if I myself do not rejoice, then you too will not rightly rejoice with me when I am not rejoicing. I do rejoice, however, that I have overcome the envy of my rivals, and at last, with the help of the Lord Jesus Christ, have escaped the company of my most ungrateful monks, whose ways, opposed to my purpose, I was forced to bear for twenty-three years not without bitterness. I have escaped wretched, ungrateful, and most foolish men, and have come to a place, as I hope, sufficiently quiet, in which I can both attend to my own salvation and continue the holy studies of the Scriptures. Yet I grieve that good and innocent men have had to depart because of the wickedness of the faithless, although they too at that time can provide for themselves safely enough elsewhere, if they are burdened by the company of the depraved. You feared that I would thrust myself into the courts of princes, because there were many who delighted in my service. You were not alone in fearing this. But in all my adversities I resolved this in my heart, with God showing mercy: by no means to abandon the way of the Lord, but to keep inviolate until death the profession of holy religion. Hence, let this be said in your ear without boasting: the more my rivals raged against me, the more I felt the grace of God present to aid me; by it it came about that, although with the help of many nobles and princes I could quite worthily have avenged the injuries done to me, I wholly neglected every vengeance. For unjust tongues, depraved by their habit of lying, moved against me, inventing such things as had never been thought by us. But in truth the principal cause of the whole persecution against us was that, being well disposed toward the most serene Prince Palatine, I was cherished by him with singular clemency. For this is plainly enough known to very many. It was notorious that Duke [Cyno-?] did not favor Palatine affairs; this was clearer than light at the time when, in the past war, the provincial Count of the Hessians devastated Naganium with plunder and fire. Therefore, out of hatred for the Palatine, not only the [Cynonori?] persecuted me, but also certain men from among our own, such as the abbots of the Mount of St. James at Mainz and of the Mount of St. John at Rheingau, who, together with their monks, for many years back have never ceased to assail with curses a most gentle prince, although he has done them no harm. They also always envied me both my name among students and my good reputation, bearing it with a most unjust spirit whenever I had been honored by any of the princes. When, in the year 1503, the princes, electors of the Empire, had assembled at Frankfurt in the month of October for certain affairs of the realm, it happened that I too, having been summoned, was present at the court of the most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg. There were present Raymond, cardinal priest of Santa Maria Nuova, at that time legate de latere of the apostolic see, and the archbishops Hermann of Cologne, Berthold of Mainz, and Jacob of Trier. I was a continual table-companion of that same Margrave Joachim; I was called to table four times by the cardinal and twice by the archbishop of Cologne, and on my departure was presented by them with very beautiful silver vessels and held in great familiarity. Hearing these things, the aforesaid abbots and monks were torn in their hearts by envy, and since they could do nothing else, they began to persecute me with slanders...
