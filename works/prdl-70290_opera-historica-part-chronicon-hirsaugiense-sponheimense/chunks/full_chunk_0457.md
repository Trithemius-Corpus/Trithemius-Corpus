*1408.*

In the seventeenth year of Abbot Bernhard, a great dissension arose between John, Duke of Bavaria, elect of the church of Liege (who for almost sixteen years had sat as bishop and had not received sacred orders, contrary to his oath), and the citizens of Liege with those adhering to them. This brought a disastrous defeat upon the people of Liege, whom the aforesaid John overcame in war. For the people of Liege besieged John the elect himself in Maastricht, where they practiced such cruelty that, when women fled from the said city because of want, they cut off the [unclear] garments around their private parts. And although William, Duke of Bavaria and Count of Hainaut, Zeeland, and Holland, brother of John the elect, and also John, Duke of Burgundy, the son of their sister, at first wished to give him no assistance, nevertheless at last, seeing him placed in such great necessity, they came to his aid with one hundred thousand men equipped for war, while he himself, besieged by the people of Liege in the city, did not know it. When the aforesaid dukes came with their armies to the city of Maastricht, they fell unexpectedly upon the people of Liege on the twenty-third day of the month of September, killed more than thirty-five thousand of them, only a few escaping; on their own side scarcely ten died. And thus John the elect obtained the whole diocese.

*1409.*

In the eighteenth year of our abbot Bernhard, the aforesaid William died, Duke of Bavaria and Count Palatine of the Rhine, Count of Holland and Zeeland, lord of Frisia, son of Duke Albert, nephew of Emperor Louis IV, leaving one daughter, named Jacqueline. She first married the Dauphin of France, afterwards the Duke of Brabant, and finally a certain nobleman of the French, all of whom died without children. This daughter Jacqueline, after her father’s death, was striving to obtain by right the inheritance of that county; against her the aforesaid John, elect of Liege, her father’s brother, obtained it by violence, and as long as he lived he retained Hainaut, Holland, Zeeland, and part of Frisia under his lordship, while the aforesaid daughter of his brother protested. At length, wishing to have an heir after his death, he abandoned the bishopric of Liege and, with the dispensation of the Council of Constance, took a wife, but he did not receive children from her.

In the same year, on the twenty-first day of the month of May, died Blanche, daughter of Henry, king of England, wife of Louis, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, son of Rupert, king of the Romans, who was called the Duke with the Beard, because he afterwards did not consent to have shaved off the beard that he had grown when he set out for the Holy Land. After her he took as his second wife Matilda, daughter of the Duke of Savoy, a woman of great piety and devotion, by whom he begot three sons: Louis, Frederick, and Rupert. After his death, she married Albert, Duke of Austria, brother of Emperor Frederick.

*1410.*

In the nineteenth year of our abbot Bernhard, Rupert, king of the Romans, Duke of Bavaria, and Count Palatine of the Rhine, died at Oppenheim on the twelfth day of the month of May, and was buried at Heidelberg in the middle of the choir of the church of the Holy Spirit, with a mausoleum raised high. This Rupert, before he was elected king of the Romans, first founded, built up, and endowed the University of Heidelberg in the year of the Lord 1386, which Pope Urban VI confirmed and honored with various privileges. He also built in the same town a notable church in honor of the Holy Spirit, in which he established many sufficiently rich and good prebends of canons and vicars.

After the death of King Rupert, the prince-electors, coming together, unanimously elected as king of the Romans Sigismund, son of Emperor Charles IV, brother of Wenceslaus, once deposed king, king of Bohemia and Hungary, a strong, vigorous, wise, and most Christian man, who ruled for twenty-seven years and vigorously did and accomplished many things for the benefit of the Catholic Church.

*1411.*

In this year, which was the twentieth of our abbot Bernhard, there were serious dissensions and discord between Louis the Palatine, son of the late Rupert, king of the Romans, and John, Count of Sponheim, over certain boundaries and liberties in the counties, especially in Kreuznach. In these matters Count John obtained victory by the sentence of the judges against the aforesaid Louis the Palatine. In these times the condition of this monastery in temporal matters was very poor; the monks, casting aside the observance of discipline, lived in great deformity of life.
