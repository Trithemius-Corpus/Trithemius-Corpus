Why, Lord God, who judge justly, did you will this to happen in this way? Surely you did not will the impious to prevail and consume the just? Far be it. We see your judgments, Lord; we do not know their causes, nor does it belong to us to examine the causes of your judgments, which we may without sin not know. Rather, to your most holy name we give such thanks as we can, you who allowed that monastery to fall for causes [unclear], so that it might rise again at once into a better state and condition. For if we wish to compare the monastery of Hirsau which we see today, with its persons, goods, substance, and buildings, with the old one whose miserable collapse we lament, we shall find no comparison of the past with the present. For present-day Hirsau surpasses and exceeds that old monastery with all its conditions as much as it seems later in time. But, pursuing the history in due order, let us faithfully and briefly set forth the deed as it has been transmitted to us by the testimony of old writings. For even if it may contribute little to the edification of readers, nevertheless knowledge of it will be a not useless warning to all who pass their life in cloistered conversation under a similar lot, by which they may learn that in elections of abbots they should seek not themselves, not their own advantage, but the honor of God alone.

*Concerning Conrad, the tenth abbot of this monastery, who presided for about twelve years, and concerning the very bad dissension which occurred in the election.*

When, indeed, the venerable abbot Hardfrid had died, with sixty monks, as we have said, twelve survivors remained from the convent. After almost three months they came together bodily in one place to elect an abbot; yet in mind, as will soon appear, they were far separated from one another. For by choosing two men in dissension, Conrad, who received the gift of confirmation from the bishop, and the fugitive Eberhard, who despoiled the monastery, they, alas, furnished the seedbed and material for the destruction of this place to the impious. One party indeed adhered to Conrad as legitimately and canonically elected; the other proclaimed that Eberhard was the true and legitimate pastor according to the first sanctions. Nevertheless Conrad prevailed by right and, as elected by the sounder part of the brethren, received ordination from the pontiff. Meanwhile Conrad was concerned about the gift of blessing; Eberhard, after carrying off all the treasures, chalices, ornaments, and whatever he could steal, hastened with two monks of his party to the count, who was otherwise already gravely hostile to the monastery, to seek aid. The count received him not only willingly but even very gratefully, rejoicing, as afterwards clearly appeared, that an opportunity had been given him by which at last he might satisfy the mind that had long gaped after the monastery’s goods. Immediately the robber savagely invaded the possessions of the monastery, long before offered by Christ’s faithful to God and Saint Aurelius; he tore them away and granted them for distribution as pay to his soldiers, as if in repayment for service rendered. He invaded villages, courts, fields, houses, and whatever he had learned belonged to the monastery’s right; and, under the pretext of the just defense of Eberhard, the abbot, as he himself said, he gradually confiscated the monastery’s property for his own uses. Whom could the monks invoke as protector, having no defender in all these matters, when all either feared the count’s power or were led by greed for the monastery’s spoils? Moreover Eberhard, given over to a reprobate mind and alienated in mind, incited the soldiers to plunder, saying that he was abbot and that it was lawful for him to permit the goods of his monastery to whomever he wished. Meanwhile Abbot Conrad and the monks consenting to his party were oppressed by insults and injuries; they hastened to the bishop, asking aid against the attacks of tyrants. But it is agreed that nothing was done at that time for the restoration of what had been taken away; rather, the violence grew greater day by day, since the count now publicly declared that it was lawful for him to reclaim what had been offered by his ancestors. Every occasion seemed blessed to him by which he might satisfy the avarice into which he had gravely fallen from the spoils of the monks. And so Conrad, now a poor and miserable abbot, with a few monks, and not all of them faithful, remained in the monastery, struck by the greatest fear and dread, scarcely having the means to feed and clothe himself and his own.

He was elected in the year of the Lord’s nativity 989, in the second indiction, on the twelfth day before the Kalends of March, and he presided over the monastery in many tribulations and miseries for almost twelve years. He was by nation a Frank, born of humble stock, and for that reason less fit to resist such great evils and so many tyrants. He was a good and simple man, born more for handling the mysteries of religion than for governing a monastery prudently and usefully amid such great disturbances. Moreover that most wicked apostate Eberhard, when he was threatening that he would soon invade the monastery with a strong hand and cast Abbot Conrad into prison, was overtaken by death.
