*XXXVIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St James at Würzburg, to Jakob Wimpfeling of Sélestat, his singular and dearest friend, greeting.*

[unclear] letters from Strasbourg, which you sent to me on the twelfth day of the month of July, I received on the twenty-second of the same month. But those which you write that you had given before have not yet come to my knowledge. Lord Gaspar, bishop of Bethlehem, vicar in pontifical matters of our most reverend general prelate, brought me the little book destined for me by you, ascribed to the holy abbot Bernard, on the seven penitential Psalms. The man who delivered to me your aforesaid letter at the same time handed over in your name two little books lately printed at Strasbourg, one by William, bishop of Paris, on the holding and plurality of benefices; the other by Albert the Great, bishop of Regensburg, on the fervor of charity. Do not doubt that I received them with a most grateful mind.

With the afflictions that you suffer I sympathize from a most faithful heart; and if you think that I can do anything for your peace, let me know, and at once you will find me ready for everything. Trust in the Lord Jesus, and walk firmly in the way of truth, and truth itself will set you free. This one thing only I warn you: do not hereafter be over-ready to occupy yourself with the business and affairs of cloistered men, because what is outside you and the condition of your state is no concern of yours. For what is it to you whether Augustine was cowled or toga-clad?

You wrote in that battle of yours concerning integrity that Bede was not a monk, and likewise very many others whom I counted among monks in the book on ecclesiastical writers; and, unless you were yielding to me as a friend, you promise that you would easily prove it. Let us engage with one another, if you please: those whom I wrote were monks, and especially Bede, you will by no means show not to have been monks. You seem to me not to have surveyed sufficiently the histories of past time, since you do not know that Bede was a monk. But I have thought that you should be spared, since I understand that you are afflicted more than enough by the vexations of the hermit brothers of St Augustine.

You wish to know from me whether there could be a place for you with my successor in Sponheim, and who he is, since you wish utterly to flee the company of worldly men. Indeed in the abbacy of Sponheim the prior whom you know succeeded me, no small part, although hidden, of my persecution. But I fear that you will not have a place to stay there, because the prince of the [Cynonoti?] is little inclined toward men devoted to letters; you will experience this if you go there. I do not utterly reject your desire to live with me, but I advise this first: that after having examined the people and the place you yourself should come, and if the matter is to your mind, we shall discuss by counsel on what condition you may attain the result. For whatever I can do, I shall do most willingly out of love for you. I have a poor and small little monastery, but quiet and sufficiently suited for one philosophizing; only the lack of books troubles me, in which, as you know, at Sponheim I was very rich.

Farewell, my Jakob, and persuade yourself that I am with you, I who shall never desert you. From my monastery near Würzburg, on the twenty-seventh day of the month of July, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*XXXIX.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, to his most loving confrater Simon, abbot of the monastery of Laach, greeting.*

[unclear] What is this, dearest confrater and friend, that you marvel that I resigned the abbacy of Sponheim, in which you say I was famous and distinguished, and blessed with many friends? Marvel rather that for so long a time I was able to endure the deceits and insolences of the monks, and to overlook, as if I did not know them, the plots of the [Cynonoti?]. For the twenty-fourth year I presided over the monastery of Sponheim amid many labors and no small poverty; and when, with buildings, books, ornaments, and many necessary things, as you yourself saw, I had at last adorned it beautifully, and had brought it back to a good condition of temporal affairs, when the war of the empire came upon us against the most illustrious prince Philip, count palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria, and prince-elector, under whose protection the monastery is known to be established, it sustained a grave loss of its possessions and goods from the enemies.
