Take care to resist, with continual battle declared, the three especially deadly adversaries of priests, by whom many perish today, as manfully as you can; when these have been overcome, you will easily vanquish the whole crowd of evils attacking you. The three vices of priests, by which very many are swept away and slide down to hell, are avarice, pride, and lust, [unclear Greek]. Happy is that priest who in this time has kept himself unconquered by these demons, and has not walked in the public road of priests. We have written to our common friend Beiselius about the things that are happening around us; so that you may not be ignorant of them, we send you enclosed a copy of the letter which we gave to him. We ask, moreover, that you send back to us our translation of the Mystical Theology of Dionysius, together with the Greek copy, which we lent you two years ago when you were with us at Spanheim. Farewell. From Cologne, on the eighth day of July, 1505.

*XV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, sends greetings to Johann Capellerius, mathematician.*

On the sixth day of this month I came to Cologne with the most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, which I did not wish to remain hidden from you any longer, because you have always kept inviolate the rights of our old friendship. We ask, therefore, that you come to us as soon as possible, accompanied by the Muses and the lyre, since the place is suitable for us, and there is time very abundantly sufficient for philosophizing and pursuing our plan. Do not wait for the sextile of melancholizing Saturn, which on the day when we entered Agrippina did not pass by very auspiciously, a swelling of the left foot having been inflicted upon me. How long we are to remain here is uncertain to us. Therefore cut short delays, and come as quickly as possible, because much discourse that must be brought forward presents itself to us for you. Farewell. At Cologne, from our lodging, which is in the street that they call *potoplaetae*, because it truly abounds in drinkers. Looking for the Margrave’s insignia at the doors, you will by no means go astray. On the eighth day of the month of July, in the year of the Christians 1505.

*XVI.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, sends greetings to Jakob Kymolanus, of the Carmelite order, his friend.*

When a few days ago I had come by boat to Cologne, I heard that you were there in the convent of your order; and today our Jakob of Maaseik, doctor of medicine, invited by us to luncheon, made me certain of this, though I had been somewhat doubtful. Soon, when the dishes had been removed from the table, I asked for pen and paper, and wrote you the letter which I am sending, unable any longer to defer my longing for you. And so we wish, and earnestly beg, that tomorrow morning you come to us, to dine with us and to grant the whole day to our disputations. There will be no one present to hinder us; for I am alone with my attendants and with one necessary mathematician of the Margrave of Brandenburg, a man indeed exceedingly courteous. Let nothing delay you from coming. You will recognize the lodging by the report of this servant of mine. Farewell. From the very house of our residence in Cologne, today, the ninth day of the month of July, in the year of the Christians 1505.

*XVII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, sends greeting to the excellent father and friend Ger[ardus?] Laco of Breitbach, Abbot of the monastery of Deutz.*

What adverse things fortune, not to say the perverse rashness of men, has fashioned for me cannot be wholly hidden from you. For the report has been spread through all the land that Abbot Trithemius is living outside his monastery, although the cause is not equally known to all; hence many circulate various rumors among the common people that are by no means true. Since, therefore, we are situated not far from one another, you indeed in Deutz in that most holy monastery of yours, but I at Cologne in a lodging arranged for me by the prince on whose account I came, in order that I may make you more certain concerning our affairs, come to me tomorrow, to dine and feast on noble fare from the table of the most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, by whose munificence we are copiously provided. I, for my part, am prevented from coming to you for two reasons: one on account of the aforesaid prince, whom I cannot leave; the other because of the swelling of my left leg and foot, which forbids me every ability to go about.
