With these letters and other [unclear] Trithemius sent a certain learned man to Frankfurt (it was in Advent), to investigate subtly among the preachers who that brother Pensans-manus was. When he had come there, using diligence, he discovered that it was the aforesaid lector Wigand Cauponis. When he reported this to Trithemius, a great literary war arose between them, which lasted between them for almost two years. For soon against Wigand several very learned men, defenders of Marian purity, wrote in verse and prose, defending Trithemius’s book and his opinion concerning her most pure conception. At length, after two years had passed, concord was made between Trithemius and Wigand through the rector of the University of Cologne, Ulrich Kreityvys of Esslingen, most famous doctor of sacred theology, canon of the greater church of Cologne and councillor of the archbishop, and through Tomus de Scotia and other doctors of sacred theology, so that Wigand abjured his opinion contrary to the most pure conception of Mary and to Trithemius’s little work, and was compelled by Trithemius himself to seek pardon for his rashness; and, injuries being forgiven on both sides, neither would henceforth write invective against the other. Nevertheless the preachers afterwards in the Roman curia under Pope Alexander VI attempted, or treacherously strove, to obtain something against Trithemius, but they made no progress. For Abbot Trithemius had most firmly adhering to him the University of Paris, that of Cologne, that of Tübingen, the whole order of the Carmelites, the order of Minors, the sacrosanct Roman Church, the greater [unclear] of the cardinals, archbishops, bishops, and many princes, and almost all the clergy in Germany, and innumerable learned and erudite men.

At the same time he also wrote: *Soliloquies of a Man to His Soul*, one book. *On the Origin and Progress of the Order of Carmelites*, two books. He also collected, by abbreviating them into one volume, the statutes of the provincial chapter of our order. He also elucidated Greek questions to Rutger Sicambrus on the discordances of the Gospels. Likewise he translated from Greek into Latin a book of Abbot Maximus on the incarnation of the Lord, and certain letters of the Third Synod, likewise of Saint Cyril, archbishop of Alexandria.

Also in the year noted above, in the month of July, Maximilian, king of the Romans, entered Gelderland with a strong army against Duke Charles, who, having heard of the king’s arrival, had fled to Lorraine. Many of the king’s hired foot-soldiers did evil things in the land, with plunder, sword, and fire, sparing no one. For when the men fled together to the city, poor women remained in the little villages; from them those plunderers, not to call them most cruel murderers, wishing to extort money, committed unheard-of crimes with threats, tortures, and killing. From among these, so that the rest may be imagined, let us report one. A certain peasant, hearing of the king’s arrival, betook himself to Nijmegen, the principal town of Gelderland, leaving his wife in the village with the little children. The most wicked bloodsuckers, thirsting more for booty than for the king’s victory, pressed the woman with threats and terrors to disclose her husband’s money. But when they could extort nothing from her by threats, [unclear] that he might by pity compel her to their wish, so that he might be roasted alive in the sight of his mother. But since the mother did not have the money with which to ransom her son, the most cruel robbers, while she looked on and groaned, roasted the boy until he died. They committed many other deeds horrible to tell against the poor. King Maximilian, however, not knowing such unspeakable crimes of his men, left Gelderland without obtaining victory, with loss and grave damage to his affairs.
