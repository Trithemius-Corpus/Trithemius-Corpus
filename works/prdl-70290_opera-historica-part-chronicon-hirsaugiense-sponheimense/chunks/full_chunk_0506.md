When they first arrived they were received gladly, and were appointed by the abbot as masters to instruct the monks of this convent. But, harassed by the excessive importunity and malice of the monks, especially the young ones, mocked, and sometimes even subjected to blows, when they saw that they could make no progress, they returned to Cologne, whence they had come.

In the same year, on the nineteenth day of the month of April, which was the third Sunday after the feast of the Lord's Resurrection, the nineteenth provincial chapter of our order was held at Monks' Mount near Bamberg. Presiding over it were Johannes of Bursfelde, Melchior of Augsburg, Heinrich of St. Michael's at Hildesheim, and Johannes of Holy Cross in Werdea, abbots of very distinguished and celebrated monasteries of our order.

In this year, on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of April, died Andreas of Lower Utrecht, a monk of this place. He left certain little works of his talent, namely: *Soliloquies of Man to God*, one book; *On the Progress of the Virtues*, two books; *On Abstinence from Meat*, one book; *On the Use of Flocci in Our Order*, one book; also various sermons, poems, orations, and many letters to different persons, and certain other things, of which some are still extant, and some have been destroyed through negligence.

In these times there came from Basel a certain poor man, having with him his [unclear] son, named Theoderic, six years and two months of age, who in words was so mature and composed, as if he had attained his fortieth year in conduct, that he seemed very like a most eloquent orator; and he could excuse his own childhood and ignorance in both languages so beautifully, and speak about any subjects whatsoever, that he turned everyone to amazement.

In the aforementioned year, in the month of June, died Philip, duke of Burgundy, who accomplished many things vigorously in his life; [he left] a single heir, Charles, duke of Burgundy, who himself also vigorously performed many things. 1468.

In the second year of Otto our abbot, Charles, duke of Burgundy, led a strong army against the people of Liege.

In the aforementioned year Adolf, archbishop of Mainz, by his ordinary authority, brought forth [unclear] and, after [unclear], graciously admitted the privileges also, however granted from the apostolic see, to the aforesaid union and chapter.

In the same year Matthias, bishop of Speyer, on the third day of the month of January, incorporated the church of St. German outside the walls of the city of Speyer, in the diocese of Mainz, into the church of St. Maurice within the walls, because of the previous desolation of the priests' houses of St. German by Frederick, count palatine, against Johann Nix, bishop.

In the same year Charles, duke of Burgundy, with a very great army of armed men, advanced into Gaul against Louis, king of France, and remained there for several days, and throughout the whole kingdom inflicted much damage by fire and plundering. Meanwhile, while these things were being done in France, the factious, inconstant people of Liege, at all times ready for sedition, prepared themselves again for rebellion, and began in their accustomed manner to rage against their bishop. When this became known to the bishop himself, coming from France with seven thousand Burgundians in great array, he resolved to besiege Liege with a strong hand. When he had come to the city called Tongeren, and its provost had made this known to the Liegeois, they entered the city by night with two thousand men, seized the bishop, killed many nobles and counts, while many others slipped away in flight, and led him captive to Liege. Meanwhile, however, agreement was made between the duke and the king of France. Therefore, when report of the deed came to Duke Charles, moved by excessive fury and having a very great fighting army from Picardy and Burgundy, forty thousand armed men, of whom he lost almost two thousand in the siege itself, after many labors he at last took the city and exercised unheard-of cruelty against the citizens. The city was very beautiful in its buildings; in it there were thirty-two parish churches, but collegiate churches [unclear].
