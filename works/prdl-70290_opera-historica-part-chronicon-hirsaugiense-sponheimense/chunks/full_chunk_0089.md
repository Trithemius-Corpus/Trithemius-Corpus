*72. Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

*1071.*

In the second year of Abbot William, King Henry took the duchy of Bavaria away from Otto, which his mother the empress had previously given him; in his place he appointed Welf, a vigorous and warlike man, a Swabian by nation. Thereupon Duke Otto, fleeing to the Saxons, was made commander of the war against the king. In the following year many princes conspired against King Henry and the Saxons, and with a most bitter spirit toward him they waged many battles against him.

At this time Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, went to Galicia, to St. James, for the sake of prayer; and on his return, turning aside to Cluny, he resolved to remain there, in contempt of the world. On the Sunday which preceded the feast of St. Michael the Archangel, he again publicly professed regular obedience before all, into the hand of the abbot. But when hirelings tried to purchase the bishopric of Mainz from the king for money, he was compelled by the obedience which he had promised the abbot under the Rule of St. Benedict to return to his see; and on the eighth day before the Ides of December he returned to Mainz, and governed his see for another twelve years. Thus Archbishop Siegfried founded in Thuringia a monastery of our order, which is called Hasungen, in which he too was buried.

The first abbot of that same church was Giselbert, a monk of this monastery of Hirsau, whom Abbot William, at the urging of Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, sent to that place with the whole necessary convent. This Giselbert was a venerable abbot, a man learned in good letters and conspicuous for integrity of morals, a very great lover of regular discipline, who in a short time gathered many monks into the aforesaid monastery to serve the Lord.

But since they were in no way willing afterward to consent to King Henry, excommunicated by the supreme pontiff Gregory VII, they were all expelled from the monastery together with the abbot. More than seventy in number, they came to Hirsau and were honorably received by the holy Abbot William; he kept them with him for some time, and afterward assigned to them the cell of St. Gregory for a dwelling, as will be said more fully in its proper place. The same archbishop also established the cloister of Blessed Mary ever Virgin ad Gradus, in the city of Mainz, which he endowed with many estates and ornaments.

*1072.*

In the fourth year of Abbot William, which was the year 1073 of the Lord’s Nativity, when Pope [unclear] had died, [unclear] archdeacon [unclear], and he presided for twelve years; he was called Gregory, the seventh of that name, a man certainly of much learning and great piety, prudence, justice, constancy, and religion, who both did many good things for the defense of ecclesiastical liberty and endured adversities.

He had first been a monk of Cluny and was ordained by Pope Leo IX as archdeacon of the holy Roman church at the monastery of St. Paul. In this year, after Alexander, he was created supreme pontiff by the consent of all. Holding a synod at Rome, he decreed, under the great penalty of excommunication, that all priests and deacons should as quickly as possible cast off from themselves concubines and all women, except those whom the rule and the holy synod admitted, and should live chastely according to the canons. Finally he forbade laymen to presume to hear the masses of priests who had concubines. In the same synod he also excommunicated all who sold or bought bishoprics, abbeys, provostships, deaneries, canonries, prebends, priesthoods, diaconates, subdiaconates, tithes, and the other ecclesiastical offices.

After the crimes and evils perpetrated, when [the king], often summoned to Rome by messengers and letters for synodal answers, scorned to come, he excommunicated him; whence evils were multiplied in the kingdom and the Church.

Very clear and celebrated in these times throughout Germany and Gaul was the memory of St. Sebald in Nuremberg and of St. Heimerad in Hasungen; and they were visited daily by a great concourse of peoples because of the healings which were very often divinely granted there to the sick. The latter of these, namely Heimerad, was a priest of most holy life, who died in the year of the Lord 109[unclear], in the indiction [unclear], on the twenty-fourth day before the Kalends of July. Over his tomb Archbishop Siegfried built a monastery of our order, as we have said; its patron is St. Heimerad, who shone there with many miracles.

*1075.*

In the sixth year of Abbot William, King Henry attacked the Saxons in war, and after many had been killed on both sides he at last obtained the victory.

In the same year Henry, bishop of Speyer, died; Hutzmann succeeded him.

*1081.*

*Duke Otto of Bavaria is deposed. Archbishop Siegfried becomes a monk of Cluny. The monastery of Hasungen is built. Giselbert, abbot from our nation, is sent to Hasungen. Hildebrand from abbot becomes Pope Gregory. Concubinary clerics are excommunicated. Simoniacs also are excommunicated. The pope excommunicates the king. St. Heimerad, etc. The king wars against the Saxons. St. Sebald in Nuremberg, famous for miracles.*
