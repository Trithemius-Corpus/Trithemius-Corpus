*Johannes Trithemius.*

[unclear] I lived content. I despised several rich and wealthy abbeys that were offered to me, knowing that in great abundance of things there are many disturbances, and that nothing is so contrary to my purpose as to be immersed in temporal affairs and to have no quiet left for the pursuits of salvation. For I know who said: “Wisdom fills a tranquil mind, free from cares, and will be the guest of a peaceful breast.” I was therefore content with familiar poverty, and, having despised the pomps and riches of the world, I did not refuse to lie hidden in the little hut at Sponheim. Twelve years ago he had infected the hearts of certain brothers, who favored the party of the most serene prince, the Count Palatine, badly; by their hidden plotting the envy of wicked men at last rose up against us. The favor of the Palatine prince toward me increased the envy of the Cynonoti; the consideration of unequal power furnished the cause of their hidden hatred. They saw me very often admitted among his intimates and honored by the munificence of divine Jove, and they wasted away, dreaming of gains greater than there were. As long as the strong lion stood in his flower, the little dogs dared nothing, but they waited for a time when they could exercise their malice upon the innocent man. That day desired by wicked blasphemers came, on which impious robbers, disturbing the whole principality of the Palatine, laid it waste with plunder and fire, sparing neither monasteries nor the churches of God, and showed themselves not Christians but rather faithless Turks. Meanwhile the most valiant lion, with the eagle pursuing him, gravely worn down by the forces of the whole empire, began to suffer no small loss, so that the faith of many might be tested. Many thought the lion prostrate, [unclear], and suddenly extinguished; and those who earlier had seemed friends through fear, not love, abandoned the prince when he was set in adversity and sided with his adversaries, according to that common proverb among the Greeks: [unclear]. Thus we all fell with him. From this envy took the boldness of rashness, and attacked me, poor and harmless Trithemius, having found the long-desired opportunity by which it might boast itself powerful among gnats. For while I was staying at [Budoras?] with the most sacred prince, the chief followers of the Cynonoti, at the urging of certain of my brothers who had been caught in crime, entered the monastery, seized certain members of the household who had observed what was done, stripped them of all their possessions under ecclesiastical immunity, broke down the doors, and threw them into chains. When this was reported to me at [Budorim?], it displeased me not a little, because I considered that it had been done out of hatred for me, fearing what happened: that it could in some respect injure the purity of my name among those ignorant of the truth. I therefore determined within myself not to return to the monastery before I had avenged by justice the cause of the injury committed. I remained away eighteen months, moved, as it seemed to me, by reason in recollection of the deed; and the longer it went on, the greater a nausea at returning began to come over me. Indeed the ingratitude of my brothers moved me not a little to yield; I had often experienced it gravely, but, with shame forbidding, I did not wish to bring it to light, judging it better that I should suffer injury than that, even with the truth, I should defame a multitude. Let the occasions of my departure remain entrusted to God and, so far as concerns me, covered in silence; knowledge of them could have brought scandal to hearers rather than edification. Believe me, my friend, I should wish you to know that, unless the injuries had been grave and the cause of my departure exceedingly reasonable, I would not have resigned my monastery, over which I presided for twenty-three years with the greatest solicitude, nor the most precious library. God permits nothing without cause. Who knows whether he wished me to be transferred to another place? I know indeed that not a few wonder that I gave up the abbey of Sponheim, which I made most beautiful with books and buildings, to such an extent that in all Germany there is nowhere found a library in which there are so many precious and very rare volumes, both new and old, in every science of the scriptures, written not only in Latin but also in Hebrew and Greek, in a very ancient hand. For, as you saw, I brought together more than two thousand volumes, rare and very ancient, from diverse kingdoms of the world; all these, together with the buildings and various possessions, I gave up for the love of peace. If any grief over their loss had begun to strike my soul, I formed for myself the likeness of death, by which I persuaded myself not only to forget the books, without which at one time I could not live, but even, so to speak, to hold myself in contempt. I confess that once I was held by a great love of the library, and I preferred my books to all the riches of the world; but after I perceived that the change of my affairs was at hand, I despised as dung all the things I had previously loved, and I commanded my soul henceforth to believe nothing its own except itself, and to learn, while living in the flesh, much more not to love those things which in death it would necessarily have to leave behind.
