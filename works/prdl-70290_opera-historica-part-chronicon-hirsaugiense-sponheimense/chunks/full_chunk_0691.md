*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

... I am burdensome to none, troublesome to none, but cherish all with fatherly affection, treat all charitably, and love them in true affection, so suddenly and without cause to be abandoned by his own sons, whom he gathered, cherished, and nourished in the Lord? O prior, not undeservedly to be compared with Judas the betrayer, how treacherous a vicar you have shown yourself to us, you who, in order to obtain our abbey, have plotted against us with manifest treachery. Remember, even now at last, most ungrateful of all men, how greatly we always took care, with the zeal of humanity, to do good not only to you, unworthy indeed and never deserving it, but also bestowed our benefits on your parents with the greatest piety. For you know, faithless man, and cannot be ignorant, that when war was laying waste the duchy of Luxembourg, they were compelled by want to leave their homeland naked and beggars; finding no one to receive them, they came to you, who were then living with us as a novice at Spanheim, and at the gate of the valley of men they lived for the space of [unclear] year in great poverty. At last, when we were called to the abbacy, moved with compassion, and with you also urging it, we summoned them to the village of Spanheim; we granted them an excellent dwelling free of charge, and from the monastery we assigned them each day a sufficient prebend in food and drink, for God’s sake and out of love for you, which today, into the fourteenth year, just as we established it for them from the beginning out of love, [unclear] cannot be ignorant.

Finally we made their children free from every servitude, exaction, and burden of villages and princes; we gave them clothing; in every necessity we always provided for them abundantly down to the present year. You too, first from our domestic chaplain, we appointed to the ecclesiastical office of our monastery, having singular confidence in you. But when after a few months, because of your inexperience and much negligence, we were compelled to remove you from that very office of cellarer, lest you incur any stain of unfavorable opinion, we appointed you cloister prior, against the will of many. We have also upheld you in that same office now for sixteen years against the murmurings of all the brothers; we have always defended and excused you. We are ashamed to recall our kindness toward you, which we confess was liable rather to negligence and remissness than to virtue. You never conducted yourself in the monastery as a worthy prior, because you neither greatly cared for regular observance nor loved zeal for the inner life. When we were often occupied in visitation of the order, you, without cause, without necessity, and without our permission, which was by no means lawful for you, used to run off to the convents of the nuns of Saint Catherine, Saint Rupert, and Ibingen, seeking [unclear] comforts, and were accustomed to wander to villages and towns within a circuit of three miles, contrary to the rule of monastic institution, while you begged those whom you restrained not to report your wandering to us. But when we did at times learn what had been done, burning with excessive charity toward you, we either entirely dissembled for the sake of peace, or corrected it less adequately than we should have. We always bore toward you a most pious mind; but you, imitating Judas the betrayer, have repaid us evil for our goodness toward you. But because, though we easily could have done so through you and through others, we did not desire to avenge the insult done to us, we passed over the rest of the crimes of your malice, not doubting that you would fall into the pit which you made, by the just judgment of God raging against you, you who have faithlessly, without cause, and unjustly persecuted us.

Yet we fear, not without a foreboding of future things, which the good brothers will learn by experience too late, how useful and pious a pastor they lost in us, by their own will rather than ours; whether they who thought to come to our aid in time of necessity, out of love or out of hatred of rivals or of us, themselves despised it. They very often asked that we return to the monastery; but that they never did this with a whole heart toward us has been made persuasive to us by two considerations. The first of these is that they could never be induced even lightly to attempt to soften the mind of the prince of the [Cynonoti?], whom certain men by their lie had rendered hostile to us, fearing to be accused of duplicity if they later praised again one whom they had previously blamed. The other testimony of their faithlessness is sufficiently manifest, since those who previously were always insisting that we return, changed, or rather abominable, among the [Pingiones?] in the council of the malicious, asked that I yield the office of pastoral care. But we judged that we should provide for our affairs according to our own decision, not theirs; and we yielded when it pleased us, not when they themselves wished. We grieve that the prince, otherwise humane, gentle, and to that point a lover of uprightness, on this occasion proved too credulous toward the accusations of the depraved, although we never contradicted his command even by a word, to the detriment [unclear]. We confess that we have always favored the just party of the Palatine, because we recognized that prince as most Christian, devoted to God, always kind to good and learned men, gentle and most pious; his many benefits toward us would deservedly charge me with ingratitude if we felt otherwise.
