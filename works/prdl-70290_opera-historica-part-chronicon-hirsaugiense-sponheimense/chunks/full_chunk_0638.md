...to which we all hasten; but Christ Jesus is the humble way by which one goes into heaven. Therefore whoever desires to ascend the lofty mountain of eternal happiness must not neglect the way of humility. And so, however much in this world we have advanced either by learning or by living well, we ought to think more about what is lacking in us than about what is present in us. For we know more things not than we have learned, and we have neglected more than we have done good things.

Considering this faithfully, blessed Augustine said in a certain passage: “More praiseworthy is the mind to which its own weakness is known than one which, without regard to that, would examine the walls of the world, the paths of the stars, the foundations of the earth, and the heights of the heavens.” And holy Jerome, in a certain letter: “This,” he says, “is the sole perfection in all things: the true knowledge of one’s own imperfection, because a great ascent to God is the judgment of one’s own weakness.” The sacred virtue humility is the foundation and guardian of all virtues.

Let all the proud therefore blush, who grant a place to pride in their hearts, since they know that only through humility is there a path of the mind into heaven. Let those who reckon themselves great among the wise men of this world hear that Socrates first obtained the palm of wisdom from Apollo when, after the studies of many years, he at last professed that he knew this: that he did not know. For we are called Christians in vain if we have not followed Christ, who for this reason called himself the way in the Gospel, so that the conduct of the master might become the disciple’s rule of living, and the Christian might imitate that humility which Christ always displayed.

Humility is the stable foundation of the virtues; if it is omitted, what will the gathering of the other virtues be called except a kind of ruin? For the first thing for a man pursuing wisdom is to consider what he himself is, what is within him, what is outside; likewise what is above him, what is against him, what is before him, what is after him. This contemplation bears a fourfold fruit: low esteem of oneself, charity toward one’s neighbor, contempt of the world, love of God. And this is the way to true wisdom, since without humility all human learning is useless, and with pride it is altogether contemptible.

Therefore pride must above all be guarded against by all who strive toward wisdom through the study of the Scriptures, since just as no wise man is proud, so one who is proud will never arrive at wisdom. For pride is the beginning of every sin among angels and among men. Envy does not produce pride, but rather pride makes envy. For only love of excellence envies. But love of excellence is called pride; since it is an evil in men, it does not please God, as sacred Scripture says: “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” Therefore we must fear lest, growing proud, we fall, with God permitting it, even on a slight occasion, as Sophocles says:

The other vices have force only in evil works, but pride must especially be guarded against in good works. Since pride is hateful before God and men, you do rightly, my friend, by humbling yourself before both, so that you may obtain grace.

Narcissus, too curious in investigating rather hidden matters, I have urged, both now and often before, to abandon vain studies; dreaming of some great thing or other, he promises himself that mountains of gold will shortly arise for him. Let us allow him to go on, blind, along the road he has begun, until he understands that he has been cheated of his desire, just as all the others before him who labored under the same foolishness. He has begun to waver somewhat on hearing from us of the danger and difficulty of those matters which he pursues without a teacher. But from him you will receive the answer to your petitions. Farewell in health. From Neumagen, 24 August, in the year 1505.
