*1501.*

In the eighteenth year of John Trithemius, our abbot, the thirty-first provincial chapter of our order was held on the third Sunday after the feast of Easter in the monastery of Fulda. Presiding at it were the aforesaid John Trithemius, our abbot, who delivered the address, and John of St Aegidius in Nuremberg, John in Huysburg, and John in Elchingen, [abbots?] of monasteries of the order. Nothing new was decreed, but many matters were nevertheless treated in orderly fashion.

In the same year Christopher, margrave of Baden, was here [unclear], having sent his cavalry ahead to Winterburg so that they might not burden us by their number. He came to see the library and certain secret things, on account of which in the preceding year he had summoned Trithemius to his castle of Lutzenburg, and took them into his notice and knowledge.

In the same year also, on the day of the Conversion of St Paul, there died a most Christian and truly most devout woman, Lady Margaret, wife of the most illustrious prince Philip, count palatine and duke of Bavaria. She was buried at Heidelberg in the choir of the church of the Holy Spirit; her thirtieth-day observance the prince celebrated solemnly in Lent.

In the year also noted above, the theological faculty of the University of Mainz, stirred by the example of those of Paris and Cologne, made a statute on the nineteenth day of the month of October on behalf of the truth of the faith and in defense of the most pure conception of the most blessed Mary ever virgin, determining that she was always preserved, exempt, and immune from every stain of original sin, and judging the contrary assertion impious and erroneous. And it nevertheless decreed that henceforth no one should be promoted in theology unless he swore that he would never in any respect favor the contrary opinion.

In the same year, after the death of a certain tyrant who had wickedly oppressed the poor in Moravia, when his son, succeeding to the paternal inheritance, inhabited a certain castle of his, named Giemps, with his wife and daughter, behold, one day, as he was sitting at table, two evil spirits in human form came to the gate of the castle, asking to be admitted. The porter said to them: “Who are you, or where are you from? For I must report this to my lord.” They answered him and said: “It concerns neither you nor your lord who we are or where we are from; but admit us as quickly as possible, otherwise we shall see to it that an entrance lies open for us.” As the porter ran to announce this to his lord, when he had entered the place where he was sitting, he saw those two men whom he had left outside the gate standing before his lord, and from fear he could say nothing. But they, after many threatening and terrifying words and horrible visions, to be brief in the telling, compelled him with all his household to leave the castle. After their departure, a great multitude of demons gathered and began to inhabit that castle; howling day and night and clapping, they do astonishing things.

In the year noted above, on the day of the Epiphany of the Lord during mass, a certain man of the knightly class, named John of Eltz, came with certain other knights, who complained that their rights and liberties had been violated by the archbishop of Trier. With ships and cavalry, by the Rhine and by land, he seized Boppard in advance, and at once placed his own guards on the walls, held the towers and gates, and took possession of the town without anyone resisting, all those who had been on the archbishop’s side having been cast out. What will follow, we await.

In these times heretics multiplied in Bohemia, who are commonly called Diggers, because they meet at night in hidden ditches and caves, practicing obscenity without distinction of persons, in the manner of beasts. They despise the Church of God and its ministers, mock the sacraments, and teem with endless errors. This most abominable kind of people is growing and increasing astonishingly every day, to such an extent that in the year noted above their number was found to be more than nineteen thousand. But very many nobles and powerful men in the kingdom of Bohemia have turned aside to those most foul heretics; among them one, named Christopher, a noble and wealthy man, distributed forty thousand florins among the same heretics as alms, and became one of them.

In these times in Bohemia there was a certain nobleman, Lawrence Glatz of Rotchausen, a good Christian in faith and conduct, who had bought a certain town called Gurricke. In it he found that almost all the townspeople were heretics of the aforesaid sect. When he had set before them the law that they should either renounce their error or, leaving all their goods in rightful condition, depart from the town, all with one [unclear].
