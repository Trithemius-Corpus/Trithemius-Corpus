*Familiar Letters*

...you have begun; if you have not yet completed it, the work is indeed either very great, or you are the slowest compiler of all. I greatly fear, however, that what we know happens today to many cloistered men placed under obedience to prelates may happen to you: men endowed with very noble talents are compelled to take up the base burdens of worldly business, and those whose mind is most fit for the study of letters are forbidden to handle a book with their hands.

I sent you recently, as you requested, the names of the twelve months by which they are called among the Greeks and likewise among the Hebrews, written in their own letters; nor do I sufficiently understand how they are useful to you in the publication of chronicles. But the Greek epigrams which you say are preserved among you, inscribed on a golden cross, have caused me no small wonder: how they came from Constantinople to Ghent. For the inscription states that the cross itself was made by order and at the expense of the emperor of Constantinople. My wonder ceased at once when it came to mind that Constantinople was once captured and despoiled in the time of Emperor Henry IV by Godfrey, count of Bouillon, who, because of the magnificent deeds he performed in the land of Lorraine, deserved to be distinguished with the title of duke and to be raised up as king of Jerusalem. For he, intending to lead an army through Hungary and Bulgaria into Palestine, provoked by the injuries of Emperor Alexius, captured Byzantium; and he gave the spoils to the soldiers, many of whom afterward returned to Germany and brought to Latin churches relics, crosses, and golden and silver tablets taken from the Greeks.

Remember, Christianus, in the publication of your chronicles, that almost all chronographers have gravely erred in the reckoning of the years of the Lord, omitting twenty-four years which passed under the consuls without being correctly reckoned; you will find this subtly calculated from Marianus Scotus the monk and Augustine Wesgallus, theologian of Louvain. I wrote to you more fully about this very matter three years ago from Spanheim through John Steinen Moelen, priest of Mechelen. Meanwhile we too have written a chronicle, of no small size, of the monastery of Spanheim, over which we then presided, beginning from its first foundation, which took place in the Christian year 1040, down to the year 1502; in it we led the sequence of the narrative through the succession of all the abbots down to the nineteenth year of our prelacy, inserting many matters done in Germany which seemed to us worthy of memory.

Please hasten at last to put the final hand to the work, so that at some time we may deserve to see it. As soon as possible make us more certain about the things that are going on around you. Concerning ourselves, we inform you that for love of peace we have resigned our abbey of Spanheim, and have taken up the one in which we now live, St. James at Würzburg, certain rivals giving us the occasion, men to whom our sacred studies were by no means pleasing. Farewell, and pray to God for us. From Würzburg, on the sixteenth day of August, in the Christian year 1507.

*XLVII.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, to Johannes Damius Curtensis: greetings and sincere charity in the Lord.*

Brother Wilhelm Orphanus, the bearer of the present letter, nephew of Abbot Macharius of Limburg, has reported to us a tale of great rashness, which, if it is true, makes us wonder greatly. For he says that the abbot of Bursfelde, together with I know not what other man, recently performed the office of visitation at Spanheim, and, among the other commands of his authority, ordered Abbot Nicholas, our successor, under certain penalties, to take care to alienate, dispose of, and sell from the monastery all volumes written in the Greek language, both manuscript and printed, which we, as you know, gathered from Greece and Italy with great labor and heavy expenses.

If these things are true, for they cannot be hidden from you, we most earnestly ask that you go as quickly as possible to the monastery of Spanheim, and [unclear] those Greek volumes together with the Hebrew ones, which were bestowed on us by the generosity of princes, [unclear] to gather enough money for us, by which we may redeem the books put up for sale. Yet we cannot rashly give credence to what has been said. What reason could have moved that good abbot to order Greek books to be sold, in which nothing evil is contained? Or perhaps does he labor under hatred of us, or, led by a zeal of bitterness, does he think that by this the memory of Trithemius must be wiped from the earth, as the Romans are read once to have done to the blessed Pope Gregory I, who after his death consigned to the flames all the books composed by him that they could find?
