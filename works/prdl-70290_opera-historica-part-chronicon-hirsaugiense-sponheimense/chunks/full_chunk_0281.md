In the ninth year of Abbot Sigismund, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1326, Louis IV, emperor, raised Reinhard, count of Geldern, to duke; he had kept his father, of the same name, captive in custody for almost eight years until his death. And from then onward the princes of the land of Geldern began to be dukes, retaining the title of the county of Zutphen down to the present day.

And in this same year, on the seventh day before the Ides of October, his father died in captivity. When Frederick, bishop of Utrecht, had died, two men were named amid disagreement: James, dean of the greater church, was elected by the greater part of the chapter; James of [Suden?], bishop, was postulated by the smaller part at the urging of William, count of Holland.

But the former, James the dean, prevailed and was confirmed as bishop of Utrecht. When he died after a short time, the canons of the greater church unanimously elected John of Bruchorst, provost of the church of Saint Saviour in Utrecht, an excellent man, who at once took up the rule and administration of the whole episcopate as the true pontiff canonically elected. Meanwhile John of Diest, provost of Cambrai, came from Avignon, having been appointed and consecrated bishop of the church of Utrecht by Pope John XXII at the urging of the duke of Brabant, Reinhard duke of Geldern, and William count of Holland. Entering Utrecht with armed force and great display, after expelling John of Bruchorst, the elected bishop, he assumed the pontificate, which he governed for almost fourteen years.

In the aforesaid year also a certain Jew, receiving baptism in Hainaut, was lifted from the sacred font by William, count of Holland and Hainaut. Passing and living among Christians, he pretended to be devout, though filled with Jewish malice. For on one occasion, passing alone in the church of a certain monastery, he unworthily beheld a most beautifully painted image of blessed Mary ever virgin.

At once filled with his old hatred and inborn fury, he drew a dagger and, bursting out in words of blasphemy against the holy Mother of God, struck the bright face of the image and foully wounded it. And behold, immediately abundant blood, issuing from the wounded image, began to flow around. Seeing this, the wretch secretly fled from the church. But the blood, preserved by the faithful, was a remedy for many illnesses.

But after the perpetrator of so great a crime had lain hidden for some time and could not be discovered by anyone, the most blessed Mother of God, Mary ever-virgin and ever undefiled, appearing in dreams to a certain blacksmith as if pierced with wounds, showed him the perpetrator of the crime and encouraged him to a duel against the faithless man for the declaration of the truth. The smith came to the judge and accused the Jew of the crime; when sufficient testimonies for proof of the charged crime were not [unclear], or that he would deign to entrust the office of imperial consecration to one of the cardinals, as Pope Clement V had done with Henry VII, saying that it was neither suitable for him nor according to the custom of the Roman Empire to be blessed and crowned anywhere else. But the pope refused both, ordering him to come to him at Avignon.
