But if I am enrolled in the company of you and yours, I confidently affirm that it has gone very well with Gerbel, since you will judge, not without care, that I am not mortal but endowed with immortality. As for what remains, most reverend father: whatever it is that is now being most elegantly fashioned and overlaid beneath your hammer, do, I pray, let me know, who am yours and consecrate my whole self to your school; or write out what you greatly desire this new follower and soldier of yours to know. For I eagerly await your letters, which, assuredly with that veneration with which I am accustomed to receive the writings of the most learned, I shall kissingly adorn with everlasting cedar, perpetual ivy, and eternal honor. Farewell, most reverend father, and deign to number me among your own. From Cologne, the fourth day before the Ides of June, in the year of the Lord 1507.

*XXXII. Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, formerly of Sponheim, sends greeting to Nicholas Gerbel of Pforzheim.*

Long ago, when I received your letter, excellent Gerbel, I resolved in my mind to reply at the same time to your wishes, as was proper, so that you might not be disappointed in the outcome of your confidence in me, and so that our friendship might become the more pleasing the sooner it had grown together. But because I delayed for a while, you should not therefore think that you have been either neglected or despised by me; and you will of your own accord pardon the delay, if you weigh exactly the inextricable occupations of servants of the sanctuary. It was also necessary that a volunteer letter-carrier be at hand to carry our little letter to you, lest, if entrusted to rascals, it perish midway on the journey. You ask first of all that we enroll you among our friends, requesting a thing that is certainly not difficult, since it has always been most pleasant for us to have all students of good letters among our brothers and dearest friends. But men of darkness and the greediest brooding spirits of riches we are never accustomed to admit into our friendship, because they oppose good studies, and because it is always their habit to snarl against learned and educated men. You, however, studious Gerbel, whom your most elegant letter declares learned and thoroughly virtuous, we willingly receive with open hands into our friendship, though we are quite middling, and we promise faith and charity in unshaken constancy. Although, in your estimation of us, deceived by report, you have erred greatly, if I may say so with your leave, since your praises far exceed our merits, nevertheless we accept the integrity of your mind toward us with the benevolence that is fitting. For I know that you do not imitate those utterly vain flatterers, who, when they seem to praise someone, deceive him, lying with fabricated proclamations, because they pursue not the man himself but gifts and rewards. You, however, have praised from love; trusting in deceptive report, whose judgments are blind, yet any intention of lying or will to deceive was far from your mind. But consider whether I am a truthful paraphrast of your letter, so that you may understand that I have grasped the meaning of your right opinion. Let the testimonies of truth be unstained, because you have conceived in your mind a matter sufficiently plain and manifest: that the man who denies that the friendship of the most illustrious men in the literary arena is immortal should be considered out of his mind rather than of sound head. For all the glorious deeds of former men would long since have perished with their authors, if they had not been endowed with immortality by the monuments of writing friends. Therefore antiquity judged those men fortunate who, doing things worthy of remembrance by talent, wisdom, or courage, had fallen into those times in which there was an abundance of the most learned and most illustrious men, by whose urgent friendship whatever seemed worthy of praise received immortality through the support of letters. Rightly, therefore, you promise yourself immortality from the friendship of illustrious men, men most renowned in the matter of letters; for just as the stars of heaven, joined by friendship with glittering rays, so too those illumined by the light of immortality often remain glorious among posterity. All learned men write good and honorable things to their friends, and they are accustomed to make their name and fame immortal no less than themselves among all posterity. But no one has ever endowed with immortality in writings an enemy or a man lurking in obscurity. I, however, my Gerbel, although I know that I am neither learned nor a giver of immortality, but only moderately studious, nevertheless shall take care, as far as my powers allow, that you have not entered into friendship with us—

*It is agreed that the friendship of learned and distinguished men gives immortality.*
