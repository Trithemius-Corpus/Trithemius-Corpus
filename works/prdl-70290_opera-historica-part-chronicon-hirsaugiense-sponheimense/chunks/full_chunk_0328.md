All the time in which he was prior, namely for thirteen years, he spent either in the Scriptures as much as he could, or in prayers and the worship of divine praise. He always fled idleness with the greatest care, knowing what is written: “For in desires is every [unclear],” etc. Therefore he was accustomed to occupy himself usefully at all times, lest through idleness an entrance should lie open to vices. He was, however, as we have said, very learned in the writings of both branches of knowledge; always aided by his natural ability, with continual practice he became more learned every day, and by his own example and instruction he also stirred the brothers who seemed more fit for learning to love and study letters. When he had been ordained abbot, since he was now compelled to have regard for temporal as well as spiritual affairs, he showed himself so diligent and attentive in both that one would have sworn nature had fitted him for either.

In this same year in which Abbot Rupert was elected, serious disputes arose between the bishop of Worms and his citizens over various matters and ecclesiastical liberties. Emicho, count of Leiningen, Werner of Bolanden, with their followers, involved themselves in these; the count of Sponheim lent them his aid.

*A.D. 1200.*

Conrad, archbishop of Mainz, entered Hungary to restore peace between the two brothers Emeric and Andrew, who were contending for the kingdom. When agreement had been made between them and he was preparing to return, in the regions of Austria, in the territory of Passau, he paid the debt of the flesh. After his funeral rites had been celebrated, Wolfger, bishop of that place, honorably conveyed his body to Mainz and had it buried there.

Meanwhile a serious dispute arose in Mainz. Some elected Lupold, bishop of Worms, as archbishop; others preferred Siegfried, provost of the greater church. Leaving the city, they came to Bingen, and there confirmed the election which they had begun concerning Provost Siegfried. Hearing this, Lupold came with a strong hand, with the favor of King Philip, and drove Siegfried and his electors out of Bingen. But Siegfried, seeing that his men were making less progress, betook himself with his followers to King Otto. He was kindly received by him, and received the regalia from him. Relying on his authority, he returned to Bingen with the forces of an army; Lupold barely escaped by flight, and he captured many from his side along with the town. And so Siegfried, confirmed in the see of Mainz, presided as archbishop for almost fifteen years. This Archbishop Siegfried, in the first year of his pontificate, ordained our Abbot Rupert, of whom we are speaking, at Mainz in the church of Saint Alban, on the third Ides of November, which was the day of Saint Martin, our patron. Elsewhere I found that he received the gift of benediction from Guido, cardinal of Palestrina, legate of the apostolic see, at Saint Pantaleon in Cologne; I believe this to be truer, especially since Siegfried did not yet have the pallium.

In this same year many princes, bishops, and abbots, with an infinite multitude, signed with the cross, set out for the Holy Land in the service of the Lord’s Sepulchre. Among them was Berthold, count of Neuenburg in Breisgau, who, having received money, handed over his county to the church of Strasbourg as a perpetual possession, with all its appurtenances. He himself, with his son, set out for Jerusalem, and there persevered in the service of the Lord’s Sepulchre until death. Stirred by his example, many noble and powerful men, selling their estates, dedicated themselves with their wives and children to the perpetual service of the Holy Sepulchre. Lutold, bishop of Basel, likewise set out.

*A.D. 1202.*

Adelbert, count of Sponheim, set out for the Holy Land with many others, and for two years served Christ there as a soldier. In the same year Baldwin, count of Flanders, made his way to Jerusalem with great equipment. Rupert also, abbot of the monastery, since he was a wise and most prudent man, was appointed governor of the county of Sponheim by Count Adelbert, along with certain others, and governed the province quite prudently until his return.

Abbot Rupert made Peter, a monk of Bingen, whom three years before he had ordained prior in his place, after he had been deposed, I do not know for what reason, provost of the nuns on Mount Saint Rupert, at the request of Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, of the mistress, and of the whole convent of that monastery.
