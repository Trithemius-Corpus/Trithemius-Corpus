There is nothing among demons, as Chrysostom says, so pleasing and friendly as drunkenness and lust, which is the mother of all vices, and nothing so contrary to them as sobriety, which is the mother of all virtues. What else shall we think drunkenness to be than a man’s voluntary madness, which overturns the mind, removes memory, brings on forgetfulness, weakens the body, and kills the soul? No one given over to wine and drunkenness will ever be able to be a good man, nor wise, nor learned, nor faithful, nor useful to anyone, nor worthy of any honor or remembrance. Therefore you, Buschius, flee all plagues of the soul, especially wine and sexual pleasure; by their excessive use all virtue is enfeebled, the mind is blinded, and one’s reputation is exposed to reproach before all. Spare your eyes, which, struck by both evils through your own excessive willing, are now almost destroyed by discharge and redness. Spare your property and substance, which you acquire laboriously by teaching, lest, when you have become older, you be forced to beg. Remember that, as the poet says: “By wine beauty perishes; by wine age is corrupted. Through wine often the mistress does not know her own man.” And what Aristotle said: that one who is sober and watchful cannot easily be attacked; but one who is drunk and sleeping, like a dead man, is easily captured. We beg that you both be sparing with our admonitions and acquiesce in them; for you are not unaware that we love you, and we know that you need a bridle. Around the feast of the Lord’s Ascension, when we are about to return to the Rhine, by the Lord’s bounty we shall meet with you, to confer with you more fully on those matters that are in our mind. Meanwhile, farewell in health.

From Berlin, the first day of March, in the year 1506.

*LX.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Conrad, venerable Abbot of the monastery of Saints Ulrich and Afra in Augsburg, greeting.*

After the most turbulent Palatine war had arisen, the envy of certain men against me on account of my prince himself, when I was with him at Heidelberg on the sixth day of the month of April, made it seem good to me to withdraw for a time from my monastery, lest something worse should follow. Meanwhile our king Maximilian summoned a meeting of the princes at Cologne Agrippina for the beginning of the month of July; and I too, called by certain princes, went down by boat. At last, after thirty days, when the council had been dissolved and the princes were returning to their own places, I, having often and much been asked by the most serene prince-elector Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, with the consent of my prince, the elder Palatine, entered the March, having meanwhile entrusted the government of the monastery of Sponheim to my Prior. And now indeed I am here, having the intention, after the feast of the Lord’s Resurrection, of returning to my own affairs and, according to the ordering of matters arranged, as I shall find them, either to change or to continue my plan. How gladly I would have come to Augsburg for the provincial meeting of our order, if the Prince had agreed that I should leave here before Easter, and if I hoped to find good counsel for my affairs among the fathers. But because it is necessary to comply with so great a prince, I have thought that my place should be entrusted to your paternity, so that, according to the tenor of the procuration which in the preceding year, when the provincial meeting of the fathers was being begun because of the war, I had sent to you with the money, you may act, and appear before the presidents on my behalf, and in my name carry out each matter according to the custom of the order, and render my necessary absence excused. That you would be willing to do this, and would do it, in view of the mutual friendship between us, I ask you again and again. If you wish me to know anything of your thoughts, write back, and hand your letter itself to this messenger of my prince, since, setting out for Salzburg, after completing his embassy he is to return to us through Augsburg. Remember that the chirograph of our account for the past presidency is to be delivered to the abbot of Nuremberg. Farewell. From Berlin, 24 March, in the year 1506.

*LXI.*

*Johannes Trithemius to Georg Sibutus, poet laureate, greeting.*

We have heard that you have entered into a contest with Hermann Trebelius; we would judge that you had done better not to do this. It is not fitting for your age to provoke old men with writings, and, as someone says, to wage war over goat’s wool. It is more becoming for you, a young man, to listen to your elders and learn from them, not to despise or envy your betters, even if you are wiser.
