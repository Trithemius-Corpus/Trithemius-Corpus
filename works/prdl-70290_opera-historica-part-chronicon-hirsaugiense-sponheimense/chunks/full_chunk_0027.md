In the same year Farabert, abbot of Prum, with the consent of the king of the Franks, voluntarily resigned his abbacy; Regino, a monk of the same monastery, succeeded him, a man most celebrated for his life and learning, who, among other little works of his talent, wrote a Chronicle of past time, addressed to Adelbero, archbishop of Trier, beginning from the origin of the world down to his own times, a work indeed most celebrated and most worthy to be read by learned men.

Around these times there flourished at Mainz, in the monastery of Saint Alban, Rupert, a monk most learned in every respect, skilled in Greek and Latin, who, publicly presiding over the school of monks in the same place, left to posterity many monuments of his talent. His Homilies and Sermons, not without elegance, are extant; he also described in verse the life and passion of Saint Alban. He composed some works on musical proportion. Sebulco also, abbot of Saint-Denis, a noble and powerful man, is held in esteem among the Gauls; even kings feared him because of his admirable wisdom and prudence.

When he was pressing the assault upon a certain castle situated in Aquitaine with too much ardor, he was struck by the blow of a stone and died in the following year. Around these times Liutprandus, deacon of Ticinum, flourished, a man skilled in Greek and Latin and not at all poorly learned in secular letters; among other little works of his talent he published a history of the deeds of the Berengars, which he called by the Greek word *Antapodosis*, that is, “retribution.” For in writing he repaid Berengar, the tyrant raging in Italy against the empire, because, driven out by him, he had been compelled to go into exile. He began this history of his at Frankfurt, not far from Mainz, and completed it in Greece.

In these times also there flourished in Burgundy Berno, formerly a count, founder and first abbot of the monastery of Gigny, a man venerable for holiness and religion, who also, by the gift and at the expense of his grandmother the countess, built the monastery of Cluny, over which he set as first abbot Saint Odo, once a musician of Tours, a learned and most holy man, who wrote several distinguished little works of his talent, of which I have already made mention in the second book on the illustrious men of our order. Paschasius Ratbertus also, abbot of Corbie, is held to have been famous in these days; he wrote to Abbot Placidus a little book, not to be despised, on the sacrament of the altar; also the life of Saint Adalhard; and also a very beautiful work on the Lamentations of the prophet Jeremiah. Since he was not ignorant of the Greek language [unclear] of Bremen flourished in these times and governed his church energetically for twenty years, a learned and most holy man, whose feast is celebrated on the Ides of May.

In these times, under Abbot Haderad and the scholasticus Luthelm, many studious and most learned monks flourished in this monastery. Of these, one named Sigismund, elected bishop of Halberstadt, presided energetically by word and example over the people entrusted to him for almost thirty years, and lies buried in the same church. Cuntzigo also, a very venerable monk, in the reign of Arnulf was made abbot of the monastery of Saint Nazarius at Lorsch, in the diocese of Worms; yet, scarcely surviving one year, he died and was buried in the same monastery.

There was also among them another monk named Herdericus, a man most learned in all literature, both secular and divine, who wrote many and various works, especially on music, and very beautiful chants in honor of the blessed Mary and of various saints. He also wrote many poems of different kinds; of these I have seen nothing except these two little verses, which Meginfrid attributed to him in the Chronicle, to be said over the blessing of food and drink:

May the right hand of Christ bless the gifts set before us;
May the kindly right hand of God bless our drink.
