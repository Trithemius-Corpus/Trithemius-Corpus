*An Excellent Chronicle of the Monastery of Hirsau, of the Order of St. Benedict, published by Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim.*

The ancients applied incredible diligence to the writing of histories, believing that the commonwealth could be adorned by them no less than defended by arms. For history is, as it were, a preserver of memory, an example of virtue, the companion of prudence; and by itself alone it renders immortal, with a certain everlasting renown, the fame of good men who strove to surpass others in wisdom, holiness, and courage.

Who would make known today to those who do not know it the origin of the world, the reckoning of the years, and the things done in the meantime? Who would persuade unbelievers of the incarnation, the birth from the Virgin, the teaching, miracles, passion, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ, our Savior and the Son of the eternal Father? Or who would disclose to the ignorant, in writing, the sufferings of the martyrs, the lives of the saints, the destruction of the Trojans, the wide empire of the Romans throughout the world, the various deeds of kings and princes, and the other matters of antiquity worthy of remembrance that have become known to us; or even, if the possibility were granted to narrators, who would believe them, if no writers of histories had existed before us? History alone, therefore, is that by which it comes about that we, who are separated from the ancients by the interval of a very long time, seem to have been present at their deeds and to have lived in the same times. Indeed, history bestows immortality among mortals upon human beings; it adorns and preserves the commonwealth; by seasoned prudence it keeps it free from many dangers; it instructs faith, raises up hope, sets before us the reward of eternity, and adds strength to laws; since it has shown that everything which sense either desires or fears is nothing other than a dream, it cares for the immortality of the soul and renders men eager for the contemplation of divine things.

*The difficulty of writing human history.*

How great a benefit comes to us from the reading of histories no one who has even moderate understanding can be ignorant. Knowledge of the deeds of the ancients, if they were good, teaches and instructs us toward the imitation of virtues and toward the wisdom and courage of heroes. But if they were evil or wrongly done, then by warning us not to become imitators of the depraved it makes us cautious and prudent. For history, the living model of the dead and the inextinguishable light of mortals, sets before us the merits, virtues, and praises of good men in such a way that, stirred by their glory, fame, and immortality, [unclear] to be what they themselves were, so that we too may leave to posterity an example of virtue and holiness such as we received from those who preceded us in time and in virtue.

But when it shows readers the impious deeds, disgraces, and deserved punishments of the wicked, should we suppose that these things were written as though proposed to us for imitation? Rather, we should believe that we are being warned, lest, by committing similar acts, we incur like punishments to those that they paid. Add that prudence can be grasped by us only through experience of many things; and to this, since art is long and life is short, no easier access lies open to anyone than to the man who, from love of truth, runs through whatever histories of past time he has found with diligent inquiry of mind.

Since this is so, I should believe, most excellent Blasius, distinguished Abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, that you were moved not by a zeal for vanity but rather by the divine Spirit: you who do not cease to urge me, by so many importunate prayers, indeed by so many magnificent benefactions, that I should be willing to write, for the knowledge of posterity, the history of that same monastery over which, by God’s favor, you preside, from the notes and records offered to me by you and from the various chronicles of our men, of which there is a great abundance among us.
