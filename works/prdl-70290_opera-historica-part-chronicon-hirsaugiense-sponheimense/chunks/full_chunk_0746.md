*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

... that the abbot of Bursfeld, bound by devotion to Christ, should think this, he who knows that we have deserved better in the reform of the order, although our concern for having remembrance among posterity is either none or small. Rather, we believe that he was moved by pious consideration: since he found no one at Spanheim skilled in Greek letters, he therefore judged the books almost useless to the place and ordered them to be sold, and the price turned to the necessary uses of the brethren.

Although that sale does not please me, because I would very much wish all the books, acquired by me at great cost, to remain in their own place for the perpetual adornment and honor of the monastery of Spanheim. For you know that we have spent more than fifteen hundred gold ducats on books in the twenty-four years of our prelacy, and have gathered a great quantity and multitude of them together. Indeed, three years ago we counted two thousand volumes in the library of the monastery of Spanheim, when Henricus de Bunau, golden knight and most learned orator of the dukes of Saxony, was with us; none of these was in the place at the time of our entry into the abbacy. This, brother, you can by no means be ignorant of, since you entered the monasteries with me and for a long time were my servant as chamberlain.

In every faculty of learning we gathered many volumes, not only printed ones, but also several written on parchment, precious and rare, so that I would dare confidently to say that in all Germany there is no similar library in which so many rare and very ancient volumes are found, in every variety of writings, both divine and secular, and also in diverse languages: Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Chaldaean, Arabic, Indian, Ruthenian, Tartar. I deliberately pass over the rest, which use our characters, such as Italian, French, Bohemian, and the like. For I know that the number of Greek volumes, manuscript and printed together, exceeds a hundred; concerning these we most earnestly ask you to take care. And if they are for sale, let them be acquired for us. For we shall give the price of a fair valuation, which no one else will give, since today we know no one in the whole province of the Rhineland learned and devoted to Greek letters. But if the books are not for sale, we are very well content that they remain in their own place. Come, we pray that we may find your diligence in harmony with our desire. Farewell, remembering us before God. From Würzburg, on the twentieth day of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*XLVIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, to Johannes Virdung of Hassfurt, most learned mathematician, greetings.*

Henricus Gronigerus, secretary of our most reverend prelate, delivered to me your letter, from which I plainly learned that my petitionary cause before the most illustrious prince Philipp, Count Palatine, had been advanced by you. For this reason I give you the greatest thanks, and I shall repay them according to my powers whenever the opportunity is given me. Concerning the prince’s books, however, I shall act according to your advice, although I fear lest delay bring danger, since men are for the most part lacking in vigilance and apply little care even to arduous matters.

The book of yours which you lent me I entrusted to the hands of a scribe; as soon as it has been copied, it will be returned to you without delay. But my books of Berengarius, which I had left with you at [Büdoris?] to be copied, when you have finished them, order them to return to me.

That man about whom you wrote to me, Georgius Sabellicus, who dared to call himself prince of necromancers, is a vagrant, a chatterer, and a [unclear], worthy to be chastised with lashes, lest he henceforth rashly dare publicly to profess such unspeakable things contrary to holy Church. For what else are the titles he assumes for himself but the most foolish and insane signs of a mind by which he shows himself a fool, not a philosopher? For thus he fashioned for himself a title suited to him: Master Georgius Sabellicus, Faustus the younger, fountain of necromancers, astrologer, second magus, chiromancer, pyromancer, second in the hydra art. See the foolish rashness of the man, with what madness he is carried away, so that he presumes to profess himself the fountain of necromancy, when truly, ignorant of all good letters, he ought rather to have called himself a fool than master.

But his wickedness is not hidden from me. When in the previous year I was returning from the March of Brandenburg, I found this very man at the town of Gelnhausen; about him many frivolous things were said to me at the inn, promised by him with no small rashness. As soon as he heard that I was present, he fled from the inn, and could be persuaded by no one to present himself before my sight. The title of his folly, such as he gave to you...
