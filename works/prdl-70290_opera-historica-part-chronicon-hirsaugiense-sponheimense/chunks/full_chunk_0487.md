In the same year also, the bastard son of Philip, duke of Burgundy, desiring to gain possession of the lordship of Trier, secretly met with two of the citizens, men of no low rank, one of whom, born of a knightly family, was called Wigand Schenck, the other Johann Longus; and, by promising them a great sum of money, he encouraged them to betray the city. But while they were taking counsel about the manner of this betrayal, in a certain inn in the town of Luxembourg, it happened by divine will that a maidservant of the house heard their discussion. Through her the men of Trier were warned, and they put the traitors to torture; after they confessed, they cut off their heads and divided them into four parts. The history of this deed was afterwards sung for a long time by boys in a vernacular song, which has now almost passed from human memory; in it Bulmann, master of the citizens, and another called Master Michael, who was the first to lay hands on the traitors to seize them, are introduced.

In this year Conrad the abbot sought back from Meinhard of Coppenstein those farms at Winsheim which Abbot Bernhard had once, as we said above, in the year ’36, alienated without the consent of his brethren. He summoned him before the advocates of the princes at Kreuznach, Frederick of Lerenstein and Johann of Wachenheim, producing the testimony of certain persons who had heard the revocation made by Abbot Bernhard, now dead, concerning the unjust alienation of those farms. Therefore the advocates, after hearing the parties, entrusted it to the abbot that he should present his petition reduced to writing on another occasion, and that Meinhard likewise should reply in writing; when this had been done, they said that they wished to decree what was just. The abbot did what he was ordered and presented his petition against Meinhard to the advocates in writing. But the advocates, whether entangled in their excessive cares or too much inclined to the opposing party, as was then believed by many, after receiving the request of the abbot and convent, deferred sentence, keeping the abbot’s mind in suspense with fair words from day to day, alleging now this and now that as an impediment. Thus the business remains pending up to the present.

In the same year, after a disagreement had long arisen between Wilhelm, duke of Saxony, and Philip, duke of Burgundy, over the duchy of Luxembourg, the one alleging hereditary right and the other a pledge-right, an end was at last put to the disagreement. Since the noble inhabitants of the land of Luxembourg had begun to hold their lady, the duchess of Bavaria, in contempt, Frederick, king of the Romans, had delivered the duchy itself to the aforesaid duke of Saxony, Wilhelm, for a certain sum of money, and sent there with an army a certain count of Gleichen, who was to undertake to govern the duchy in his name. When the aforesaid count of Gleichen came to Luxembourg, he obtained the greater part of that land; after placing soldiers throughout the region, he himself stayed with some men in the town of Luxembourg. Meanwhile, since the lady of the land had the Saxons as enemies, she had Philip, duke of Burgundy, secretly summoned, promising that she would deliver all her right into his hands.
