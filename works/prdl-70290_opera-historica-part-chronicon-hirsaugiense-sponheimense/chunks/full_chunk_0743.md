The second is the undertaking of very great labor and the repayment of a small reward. For, as you have seen, the work is an arduous one of the keenest speculation, which cannot be completed without the greatest labor. And what recompense is there for so much labor, except perhaps a small weight of gold, which is transitory, and for that reason should rather be despised by a Christian monk? I have never had the mind to labor for temporal reward, since I see myself called into the vineyard of the eternal King. The third cause is the judgment of the unlearned common people, by which they ascribe everything they do not understand to evil arts. I have written nothing so far at which anyone could justly marvel; I have done nothing astonishing. Yet I suffer the opinion of the crowd, since many think me a magician, asserting that I have raised the dead, summoned demons from below, foretold future things, and brought back thieves by incantations and bound robbers. All these things are invented and false; I have never done them, nor indeed even thought them. I admit that I have read very many books of magicians, not so that I might imitate them, but so that by refuting them I might one day confound their most wicked superstitions, which, with the Lord Jesus Christ granting it, I trust I shall do shortly. What do you think the foolish rashness of unlearned men in the future will think of me, when I bring the Steganography into the light, which has invented such lies about me when it has as yet seen nothing of the secret? Although I do not greatly value these chatterings of the inexperienced, being content with the testimony of conscience, nevertheless I do not intend, so far as it is possible, to give occasion for evil suspicion. Farewell, healthy and happy, firm and constant in the strength of our old friendship. From Würzburg, on the 16th day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*XLIIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of St James at Würzburg, sends greetings to Jacob Kymolanus, theologian and mathematician of the Carmelite order.*

Kymolanus, I received on the last day of the month of July, through a certain merchant, your letter which you wrote to me from Ghent on the first day of May; in it you show yourself, as is your custom, very eager for my affairs, for which I give you immortal thanks. I therefore wish you to know that, by the mercy of Christ Jesus, I am safe and well; that, as you write you have heard by the report of many, I have resigned the abbey of Sponheim; and that by my own willing choice I have accepted the one in which I now dwell, poor as it is. You heard from me the reasons for this exchange when two years ago we were together in Cologne. The precious library of two thousand volumes, which you once saw, I left entire in Sponheim, because it had been acquired from the substance of the monastery. For nothing is my own, since I profess myself a monk according to the rule of our most holy father Benedict. I did, however, bring with me to Würzburg certain little books on the mystical secrets of nature, whose reading is not suitable for everyone, which never belonged to Sponheim. The Greek volumes, too, and the hundred Hebrew ones, apart from that small printed Bible, I left in the monastery together with the rest. Afterwards, however, I acquired for myself volumes in both languages with money given to me by the Margrave of Brandenburg; if I need any more, I shall provide for myself in time. The prior succeeded me in the Abbey of Sponheim, certainly a man whom I knew inwardly in [unclear], who does not greatly value the study of the Scriptures; from him, if I wished, I could easily buy back the Greek books at a low price. But it does not please me to take away from the monastery of Sponheim its adornment, which I acquired at a sufficiently great price. My poverty is sufficient for me, since I have learned to be content with few things, for the time now seems to me to be at hand when multitude ought to be reduced to unity. I have read many things almost unbelievable; it is enough: let works sometime follow, as time demands. What need is there to run through many things by reading, when a few things can suffice for necessary learning? Today the world abounds in volumes, and so many books come daily into the light that there is no one who can read them all. For the art which they call printing, invented in my infancy at Mainz, the metropolis of the Franks, brings forth into the light every day almost infinite volumes of old and new writers. Johannes Amerbach, a citizen of Basel, a learned and wholly upright man, last year printed all the books of lord Augustine, as many as he could find, in fifteen volumes, with sufficient correction. With similar diligence he struck off in type all the works of Saints Jerome and Ambrose, and he is also going to print anew the little works of the divine Pope Gregory, some of which indeed have never been printed.
