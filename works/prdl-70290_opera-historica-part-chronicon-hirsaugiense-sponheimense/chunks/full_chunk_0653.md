*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

And the potter hates the potter, and the craftsman the craftsman,
and the poor man envies the poor man, and the poet the poet.

Envy, moreover, is the worst evil, harming no one more than its own author: the daughter of pride, a death-bearing poison of the mind, by which the devil, most guilty, overthrew the first man. But consider what Scripture says: “The little one,” it says, “envy kills.” Saint Pope Gregory, in explaining this, says: “We cannot envy any except those whom we think better than ourselves in some respect. Therefore he is little who is killed by envy, because unless he himself were inferior, he would not grieve over another’s good.”

No loss is more monstrous than envy, which rightly must be called the abyss of erroneous agitation, the hell of the human mind, the spur of contention, the sting of corruption, the entrance of diabolical obstinacy. Envy bears more heavily upon its own author, the mind from which it takes its origin; like a viper it kills its parent, and through it, by the devil’s deceit, death first entered the world.

Hence Solomon, instructing a well-disposed hearer, says in Proverbs: “Do not eat with an envious man, nor desire his foods.” For envy devours all things with a pestilential heat: it troubles the soul, consumes the understanding, burns the breast, fastens upon the mind, and feeds upon the human heart like a kind of plague. Envy is an inextinguishable fire. For as a moth eats a garment, so envy consumes the one who is jealous. Seneca advises that one should beware a friend’s envy more than enemies’ treacheries. If you have not envied, you will be greater; for he who envies is lesser. The envious man makes another’s good his own evil; that evil is far from the learned, wise, and good man.

Misery alone lacks envy. Therefore it is better to suffer envy than to practice it. If you wish to be free from envy, do not hunt after vain glory by your night studies, but imitate your Tibullus, saying in the fourth book:

“There is no need of envy; let the glory of the crowd be far away.
Let him who is wise rejoice in silence by himself.”

Show yourself modest in all things; you will be more gloriously without envy. Modesty, moreover, is the virtue that moderates the passions of the soul, rendering a man pleasing and estimable to all who behold him, so that he utters words examined by the balance of justice, and there is seriousness in his meaning, weight and measure in his words.

It does not befit men devoted to the study of letters to be inflamed with contentions, to neglect more useful matters and wage war over useless speech; by this the minds of teachers are torn away from the state of rectitude, and the hearts of hearers, clouded over by the mist of vanity, are prevented from reaching the hidden things of wisdom. Everyone willingly admires, praises, and widely receives the teaching of that man in whose speech modesty shines together with gravity, where no contention of perverse rivalry is seen, no arrogance of pride, but calmness of voice, mind, and mouth is displayed with sweetness, and humility, the friend of wisdom, shines forth.

For no wise man can be proud and arrogant; but he who is exalted and proud is not wise. The condition of our mortality is the greatest cause for humility for us; for pride we have absolutely no cause. For what are we, O poet, except human beings, necessarily destined to die shortly? We are like a shadow in the wind, as Sophocles says in the Ajax:

[unclear Greek verse]

For I see that we are nothing other than images, all of us who live, or a light shadow.

“It is a point,” says Seneca, “that we live; indeed less than a point.” But nature has divided this smallest thing by a certain appearance of a longer span: it has made one part of it infancy, another childhood, another adolescence, another a certain inclination from adolescence into old age, and from old age it has established a certain return to childhood, through which all things are enclosed by death.

What else is man than foul seed, the juice of dung, food? After being a man we shall be a rotten corpse; from the corpse, worms; after the worm, the worst stench, the greatest horror. And thus every man is turned into this form. For as Solomon said in Ecclesiastes: “Just as man came forth naked from his mother’s womb, so naked he will return to the earth; he will take nothing with him from all his labor.” Truly pitiable is the weakness of man: as he came, so he will return. What therefore will it profit him that he labored for the wind? All the days of his life he eats in darkness, amid many cares, hardship, and sadness.

“O deceptive hope of man,” Cicero cries, “and fragile fortune, and our empty conceptions, which are often broken and collapse within a moderate space, and are overwhelmed in the very course of their progress before they have been able to sight the harbor.” Therefore always think upon your end, [Sibute?], and know yourself to be mortal; never in your mind touch pride, the mother of all evils.
