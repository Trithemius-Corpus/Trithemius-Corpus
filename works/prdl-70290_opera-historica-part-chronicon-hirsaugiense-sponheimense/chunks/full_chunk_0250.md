*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

Elected, moreover, and confirmed by Henry of Liningen, bishop of Speyer, and solemnly ordained according to praiseworthy custom, he presided over this monastery of Hirsau as the twenty-sixth in the order of abbots for 10 years, 3 months, and 4 days. He was Bavarian by nation; having become a monk under Eberhard, the twenty-third abbot of this monastery, and then having been appointed prior under Volpoldus, his predecessor, he continued the weak discipline that he found. At length, when ordained abbot, he cared little for the integrity of regular observance, but rather pursued external things, the pomp of power and the vanity of glory. Among the abbots of his time he was not of the lowest reputation, because of a very celebrated fame acquired by many labors [unclear] and abstinences. For the observance of the order of our most holy father Benedict had, in the time of this abbot, failed to such an extent in almost all the monasteries of the German nation that no trace of the former institution was discerned in their morals. Everything was corrupted, everything collapsed; the monks together with the abbots fell headlong into the abyss of vices. No one cared about this deformation of our order; no one seemed to have grieved over it: not the king, not the pope, not the princes; indeed no prelate at all gave any thought to the reform of the monks. For the transgressions of the monks were so public that they were thought lawful. Hence it came about that the deeds and actions of the abbots of this monastery, from this point down to Abbot Frederick, the thirty-seventh, have been transmitted to our memory in very small number, because of the obscurity of the times and the negligence of ignorant monks.

In the year noted above, in which John was elected abbot, Iringus, bishop of Wurzburg, and the clergy suffered many injuries from the citizens; for these, at length, the citizens themselves were compelled to make satisfaction in two hundred and two thousand marks of silver and to return to obedience to the bishop.

*1266.*

In the first year of Abbot John, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 1266, the aforesaid Iringus of Reinstein died, the forty-first bishop of Wurzburg, a steadfast, warlike, and magnanimous man. Berthold of Sternberg succeeded him and presided for almost 18 years.

In the same year Charles, appointed king of Sicily by Pope Urban, cruelly killed Manfred, king of Swabia, duke, son of Frederick II the emperor, after he had been defeated not far from Benevento. After his death Conrad, whom the Italians called Conradin, grandson of the former Emperor Frederick through his son Conrad, a very handsome young man and the last duke of Swabia, sought the kingdom of Apulia by right of his paternal inheritance; but, having been unjustly repelled by Pope Clement IV and Charles, count of Provence, of whom we spoke above, he did not attain the kingdom. Indeed, after two years he was miserably killed by that same Charles.

For when the rulers of that city seemed to burden the citizens with unusual levies and exactions, the citizens, following the counsel of wicked men, rashly opposed the council; and after a very grave sedition had been stirred up, rushing to arms, and attacking one another in a mutual gathering within the city itself, they wounded one another with weapons, with not a few killed on both sides.

*1268.*

In the third year of Abbot John, in the year of the Lord's nativity 1268, Conrad, grandson of Emperor Frederick through his son Conrad, the last duke of Swabia, having gathered an army of helpers from Germany and from wherever he could, determined to enter Apulia against Charles and to recover his paternal kingdom. Charles defeated him in the borders of the Alban territory, captured him, and cruelly killed the most noble youth. In him the genealogy of the illustrious Swabians came to an end; under it the Roman empire, resting securely for more than one hundred and twenty years, had received its growth.

In the same year, on the day before the Nones of June, Henry, bishop of Utrecht, died in the twenty-fifth year of his pontificate; John of Nassau succeeded him. In these times knights fighting with one another at night in the regions of Holland are said to have appeared in the air with arms. A great calamity of that people followed this prodigy. For the untamed common people rose up against the princes and nobles of the land and, after forming a conspiracy, resolved by unanimous consent to extinguish them all and utterly destroy them, and to claim every kind of liberty for themselves
