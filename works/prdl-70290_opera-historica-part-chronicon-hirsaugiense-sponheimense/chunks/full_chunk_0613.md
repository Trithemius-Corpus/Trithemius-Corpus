Accordingly, after some passages, he warns that, if riches are ours, they must by no means be spared, since we may neither carry them away with us nor bring them into Pluto’s darkness, saying beautifully as follows:

[unclear]

Among the ancients who took up the precepts of philosophy, it was above all necessary to despise riches as certain impediments to wisdom, for they understood with sufficient foresight that a mind occupied with earthly things could in no way be free for divine teachings. And why do I speak of men of the world, who did not find the path of true philosophy by which one comes to eternal happiness, when the chief apostles of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, and their disciples and followers, in order to attend more freely to heavenly things, despised all the perishable riches of the world through love of things above? For rightly a certain imitator of the saints, calling us to true wisdom, sang thus:

Wisdom fills a tranquil mind, empty of cares;
she will be the guest of a peaceful breast.

And the Holy of Holies himself says through a certain prophet of his oracle: Upon whom shall the Spirit rest, if not upon the humble, the quiet, and the one who trembles at my words? How many today do we see among Christians, arrogating to themselves the name of the more sacred philosophy, who are covetous, greedy, luxurious, unclean, and given over to every animal passion; who think themselves to be what they are not, because they have made themselves such as they ought not to have been. Of them the holy apostle of Christ says: Know this, that in the last days perilous times will come. For men will be lovers of themselves, lovers of money, boastful, proud, blasphemers, disobedient to parents, ungrateful, unholy, without natural affection, implacable, slanderers, incontinent, fierce, without love of good, traitors, rash, puffed up, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God, having a form of godliness but denying its power; and from such turn away.

How the greater part of Christians today has everywhere given free rein to the passions just listed, none of us who has lived even moderately among men is ignorant. Most of all we see that avarice, which is slavery to idols, has infected almost the whole human race, and, if it is right to say so, even those who, by the preeminence of their dignity, ought above others to be despisers of riches. Hence very few today betake themselves either to the priesthood for love of God or to the study of letters from desire for wisdom; rather, what is greatly to be lamented, in both cases temporal advantage is sought by many, and worldly honors and riches are thought of more than reverence for God, so that the prophecy seems to be fulfilled to the letter in which it was said: And it shall be, as with the people, so with the priest. From this arises contempt for the priesthood; the word of the Lord is blasphemed all day long. If even the life of a priest is despised, what wonder if his teaching is despised by the ignorant people? To such a man the holy apostle of Christ, rightly reproaching his evil life, is shown to have said: You who teach others, do you not teach yourself?

But our Lord Jesus Christ prescribed for us a model of teaching, concerning whom the holy evangelist Luke reports at the beginning [of the Acts?] that, after giving commandments through the Holy Spirit to the apostles whom he had chosen, he was taken up. Hence Saint Ambrose says: Life must be sought before teaching. A good life without teaching has grace; teaching without life does not have integrity. For to teach and not act, as Chrysostom bears witness, not only contributes nothing, but brings very much harm. For great condemnation belongs to one who indeed composes his discourse, but shamefully neglects his life in deed. Therefore, before all else, let a holy life confirm teaching, lest that saying written in the sacred Gospel be said to anyone: Hypocrite, first cast the beam out of your eye; then you will be able to see the speck sticking in your brother’s eye.

But we, O Jacob, leaving aside the multitude of those who do not philosophize rightly, should direct our studies to true philosophy, which consists in the knowledge and love of the highest truth. For the most holy attainment of this we need neither gold nor money, but only the tranquility of a purer mind, together with desire for divine love, observance of the commandments of God, in all gentleness, patience, and long-suffering. Farewell, and do not neglect to visit us more often, lest we be consumed by idleness. At Cologne, from our lodging, on the twelfth day of July, in the Christian year 1505.

*XIX.*

*John Trithemius, abbot, to John [Capellius?], [unclear].*
