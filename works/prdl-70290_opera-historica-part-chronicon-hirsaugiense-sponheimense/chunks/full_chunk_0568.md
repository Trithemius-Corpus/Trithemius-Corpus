*Chronicle of Trithemius*

After [his?] death Pius III was appointed pope; when he died within the space of one month, Julius II was ordained by the consent of all.

In the year also noted above, namely on the third day of the month of September, the annual chapter of our order was held at Mainz in the monastery of St. James; and Johannes Trithemius was present and delivered an oration to the fathers.

In these same days, little by little, the preliminaries of wars began to boil up against Philip, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria; from these afterwards countless evils followed, both hereabouts and in Bavaria.

In the prescribed year of the Christians, in the month of October, the prince-electors assembled at Frankfurt, an imperial town: Berthold, archbishop of Mainz; Hermann, archbishop of Cologne; Jacob, archbishop of Trier; Philip, count palatine of the Rhine; and Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg; and, in the name of Frederick, duke of Saxony, the orator Heinrich von Bunau, knight. They treated certain affairs of the empire in common. The most reverend legate of the Apostolic See was present, Reymund, cardinal priest of Santa Maria Nova, bishop of Gurk, together with the orators of Maximilian, king of the Romans, ever august. Johannes Trithemius, our abbot, was present, summoned by letters and messengers by the most serene prince Joachim, the aforesaid margrave, who had already for a long time burned with desire to see and hear Trithemius. He remained with him for ten continuous days, while, to the best of his ability, he gave an account to the prince of the things that the prince desired to hear from him. While our abbot was staying at Frankfurt, the apostolic legate was invited to the margrave's table; Trithemius himself was invited, and when at last he was about to depart, our magnificent abbot was presented with gifts by both. In taking leave of the abbot, the legate used the words of the most blessed Antony, saying: “O how late you are known, how soon you depart.” The abbot was called four times to the cardinal's table; the cardinal promised him great things if he was willing to go with him to Rome. The same legate, by apostolic authority, confirmed the Office, the Rosary, and the treatise of prayers in praise of St. Anne published by Abbot Trithemius, granting eighty days of indulgences to each and all, as often as they should pray that Office, or the prayer, or his Rosary. Likewise, to those visiting with devotion the altar of most holy Anne, mother of Mary, which the same abbot Trithemius founded in the abbatial house, on the feast day of St. Anne, that same patroness, and also on all feasts of the blessed Mary ever virgin, of Andrew the Apostle, of Mary Magdalene, on the day of the dedication of that altar, which is the fourth day before the kalends of August, on the day of Easter, Ascension, Pentecost, Corpus Christi, Martin, Benedict, the Ten Thousand Martyrs, All Saints, the Epiphany of the Lord, all the Apostles, and St. Joseph, foster-father of our savior the Lord Jesus Christ, as often as they should do so, he granted eighty days of indulgences, to last in perpetuity.

1504.

In the twenty-first year of our abbot Trithemius, and the forty-third of his age, a most dreadful war arose between Emperor Maximilian and Philip, count palatine of the Rhine; its cause and beginning were as follows. When George, duke of Bavaria, had died, who had received from his father the name “the Rich” together with abundance of goods, Rupert, his son-in-law, duke, son of the aforementioned count Philip, palatine of the Rhine, said that the duchy of Bavaria and all the riches of his father-in-law belonged to him by right. He had George’s only daughter as wife, by whom he had already received legitimate children as successors. But Albert, duke of Munich Bavaria, was forearmed by certain agreements and letters formerly made between him and Duke George himself, sealed by both parties, by which it was provided that daughters should not succeed in the duchy, but that if one of them died earlier without male seed, the other should obtain his principality. Though warned in every way, Rupert refused to accept this, but persisted in his own opinion against the imperial decree. Therefore the most gentle emperor Maximilian, moved by zeal for justice, after he saw that he accomplished nothing by exhortation, subjected Duke Rupert, as a rebel, contumacious and disobedient to the constitutions, to the imperial ban by sentence, and punished all who adhered to him with the same penalty.

What meanwhile was Rupert’s father Philip to do, pressed too hard from both sides? “For if I abandon my son,” he said, “I shall violate the right of fatherhood; but if I show myself a father to my son, I know that Caesar and the whole empire will wage war against me.” At last the love of fatherhood overcame the advantage of his own utility; an army was sent to aid his son, and he made himself subject to the incursion of all the princes—
