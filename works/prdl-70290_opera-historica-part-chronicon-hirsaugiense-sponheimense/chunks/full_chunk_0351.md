Hence the nuns of the aforesaid monastery of St. Rupert, made bolder by such a contrivance or occasion, obtained from Sifrid, archbishop of Mainz, that their aforesaid monastery should be entrusted for all future times to the abbot of this monastery, as it had been entrusted from the beginning of the foundation of the abbey of St. Disibod. But because religious discipline had grown exceedingly lukewarm in that monastery of Disibod, they could easily obtain from the archbishop what they asked for the salvation of souls. For in this our monastery at that time there was still a praiseworthy observance of discipline. Therefore lord Sifrid, the archbishop, entrusted the aforesaid monastery of the nuns of the monastery of St. Rupert to Iuano, abbot of this monastery of Sponheim; and it remained so entrusted for many years, until at last regular discipline in both places almost wholly perished, and the nuns, seeking liberty, paid no attention thereafter.

*1225.*

*Sifrid the archbishop dies, etc. [unclear] Sifrid [unclear]. Engelbert, archbishop of Cologne, is killed by his nephew.*

Sifrid the third succeeded and presided for twenty-four years, a man of great deeds and of such authority and constancy that he fearlessly excommunicated and opposed Emperor Frederick, by command of the lord pope.

In the same year, on the eighth ides of November, Engelbert, archbishop of Cologne, when on the previous day he had dedicated a church in a certain village, was cruelly killed as the day was already drawing toward evening by Frederick, count of Eisenberg, his brother's son, pierced through with thirty-seven wounds or blows because of [unclear]. Buried in the church of Cologne, he began to shine with countless miracles. But Frederick the count, the impious parricide, with his accomplices numbering twenty-five, soon betook himself to the castle of Eisenberg; but, not trusting the fortification, he fled from there and, lying hidden as an exile for a whole year, excommunicated by Conrad, bishop of Porto and legate of the apostolic see, at last, when the year had passed, was found and captured by a certain knight. He was sold for one hundred and two thousand marks to Henry, the archbishop who had succeeded Engelbert; bound, he was led outside the walls of Cologne on the eighteenth calends of December and broken on the wheel, while his mother stood by beneath the wheel, crying: “Alas for me, these are my entrails to be devoured by crows, just as I saw in a dream when I was still carrying you, my son, in the womb.”

But when Frederick had thus been broken on the wheel, the signs at Engelbert's tomb soon ceased to occur.

*1226.*

John, a monk of this monastery, who had been prior before Reginfrid, a shrewd, religious, and very devout man, after the death of Peter, provost of St. Rupert's at Bingen, was appointed provost there by Abbot Iuano. He always kept the observance of regular discipline inviolate, both in himself and in others, to the utmost of his ability.

In these times Henry, bishop of Worms, son of the count of Saarbrücken, founded a monastery of nuns at Kirschgarten, outside the walls of the city of Worms, and placed there nuns of the Cistercian order, who inhabited that place for almost [unclear] years under the same religious rule, until the times of Bishop Frederick, who in the year 1443, after the nuns had been expelled, introduced reformed canons regular of good observance. The aforesaid Bishop Henry was in dispute with the citizens of Worms over ecclesiastical liberty. When they would not obey his command, he placed them under interdict. When he made no progress by this, he ordered all the clergy to depart except the parish priests, to whom the care of souls had been entrusted; nevertheless they did not dare to give the body of Christ to the sick unless they promised that they would obey the bishop if they recovered, nor were the bodies of the dead permitted to be buried in holy places. And when these things had lasted for the space of a year, certain of the citizens, affected by weariness of them, turned to the bishop's side and stirred up partisanship in the city. Hence the council, compelled by necessity lest it suffer worse things, entered into peace and concord with the bishop, and they were absolved.

*1227.*

In this year, which was the fourteenth of Abbot Iuano, Marquard, a monk of this monastery, son of the count of Sponheim, died in the thirteenth year of his conversion, a good man of singular devotion.

In the same year Pope Gregory IX excommunicated Frederick the second, emperor, because, contrary to his promise, he neglected to cross over for the recovery of the Holy Land; and the whole army gathered for this expedition from various parts of Europe was dispersed, whence many evils followed.

Also in the same year, on the third ides of September, Louis the Pious, the fifth landgrave of the Thuringians, the first margrave of Meissen, husband of St. Elizabeth, died in Sicily, in a certain city called Otranto. His bones were brought back to Thuringia and buried in the monastery of Reinhardsbrunn of our order.
