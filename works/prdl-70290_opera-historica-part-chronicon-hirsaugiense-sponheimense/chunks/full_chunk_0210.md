In the same year also Luthold, bishop of Basel, Baldwin, count of Flanders, Adelbert, count of Spanheim, and many other princes, proceeding through Greece with a great army toward the Holy Land, took Constantinople and made Alexius, son of Isaac, formerly emperor of Constantinople, emperor. When he was soon killed by the Greeks, Baldwin, the aforesaid count of Flanders, received the empire. Through his agency the union of the Eastern and Roman Church was confirmed. He ruled over the Greeks for almost seven years.

In the same year also Conrad, count of Zollern and burgrave of Nuremberg, established in the presence of King Otto, freely and willingly resigned forever to Conrad, bishop of Speyer, and to his successors, the castle of Rietburg, which he had received in fief from the church of Speyer.

*1201.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Marquard, namely in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1201, King Philip, gathering an army again, crossed the Moselle, besieged Cologne, took the archbishop, and thus compelled the city and the whole province to surrender. Afterwards, coming to Aachen with many princes, he placed himself, together with the queen, on the seat of the kingdom.

In the same year, after King Philip had prosperously returned to the upper parts, King Otto, having gathered an army of his men, ascending again to the upper parts, reached as far as Wissembourg; but, unable to advance farther, he returned to the lower regions of the land.

In the same year also, around the feast of the apostles Peter and Paul, Guido, cardinal bishop of Palestrina, came to Cologne and declared King Philip and all those who agreed with him excommunicated, commanding all men by apostolic authority to accept Otto as king and to abandon Philip, under penalty of excommunication. There then for the first time Siegfried, elect of Mainz, was ordained priest and archbishop by the hands of that cardinal; and, having proceeded to Rome with them and with letters of mandate from King Otto, he was confirmed by Pope Innocent III with the pallium, as is customary.

In the same year King Philip held a solemn assembly of princes in Bamberg, where many bishops, abbots, dukes, counts, and nobles gathered. There the bones of the holy emperors Henry and Cunegund were raised up, and they shone forth with many miracles. There was a very great gathering of peoples there, from both distant and neighboring regions. At about these times the order of the Friars Preachers began, through the institution of St. Dominic, a man of outstanding holiness, who was originally from Spain. Pope Innocent confirmed his institution.

*1202.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Marquard, namely in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1202, King Otto and Arnold, archbishop of Cologne, with other princes, held a conference at Maastricht by the Meuse concerning the commonwealth and various affairs of the kingdom; and among other matters they restored peace between the duke of Brabant and the count of Guelders, who with great opposing armed forces had often already prepared themselves for war, not without very grave injury to the poor.

In the same year Conrad, bishop of Strasbourg, died. Henry of Veringen succeeded him, a good, religious, and devout man, a lover of peace and a most upright defender of the clergy; in the fourth year after his election he was scarcely able to obtain consent from Pope Innocent to receive the office of consecration from the archbishop of Sens, because he was compelled to side with King Philip, which displeased the pope, who always favored Otto for the reasons alleged above.

In the same year also, on the feast of St. Nicholas, bishop of Myra, Conrad, bishop of Würzburg, formerly chancellor of the imperial court, was cruelly killed in the public street of the city of Würzburg by Bodo and Henry, knights of Ravensburg, and by their servants Herald and Conrad. Henry, called Cascus because he took delight in cheese, succeeded him and presided for four years, seven months, and [unclear] days; he was a learned man and very much instructed in all Scripture, who governed his church quite well. Those parricides, however, led by penitence and seeking Rome, after two years received from the supreme pontiff such a penance as he gave in his letters patent, sounding as follows: Innocent, bishop, servant of the servants of God, to all the faithful of Christ who shall inspect these letters, greeting and apostolic blessing. What an execrable crime and singular outrage they committed who, with sacrilegious daring, wickedly laid impious hands upon the Christ of the Lord, cruelly killing Conrad of Würzburg, bishop of good memory and chancellor of the imperial court, it is more fitting to weep over than to relate.
