He himself, moreover, is the first among all the emperors to have been deposed by a pope. The scholastics dispute, and the case is still undecided, whether a pope can depose an emperor; since that question does not pertain to us, let us leave it undiscussed. In this same year also Magnus, duke of Saxony, died, and the duchy, together with the march, was entrusted to Count Lothar of Supplinburg, who afterward became emperor.

In the year of the Lord 1107, which was still the first year of Abbot Bruno, the venerable and holy woman Paulina died, on the day before the Ides of March. For many years she had led a life almost eremitical, enclosed for the love of Christ near the borders of the Thuringians, in the diocese of Mainz, where both in life and after death she shone with countless miracles. Present with this matron in her illness were Werner, her son, a monk of this monastery; Gerung, first abbot of the cell of St. Paulina, once also a monk of this monastery; and many other brothers. After celebrating the solemnities of masses in the place where she was ill, they commended her passing to the Lord. When at last she had happily died, they carried back her holy body, enclosed in a little vessel, to the cell previously founded by her; and, after celebrating her obsequies with due honor, they reverently buried her before the altar of the Holy Cross. And because mention has occurred of Abbot Gerung, who was a monk of our monastery of Hirsau, it seems fitting that we should recount his merits somewhat more fully.

Gerung was by nation a Swabian, born from the family of the Buchaugienses, who, after he had for some time learned manners and letters in the city of Augsburg, proved to be a young man of good disposition and became a monk under Abbot William in the monastery of Hirsau. He was so zealous in learning and keeping monastic discipline that [unclear]. Thereafter he fulfilled the office of cantor for some time with sufficient distinction; in this office both his art commended his voice, and his voice his art, with singular harmony. He was also a most tenacious emulator of regular discipline, and for that reason was twice sent by the holy father William to Cluny to learn the order of monastic life. For he was of mature gravity, and in the censorship of monastic rigor very severe, especially toward those who, having often been proclaimed for some transgression, had neglected to amend. Yet he did not lack the virtue of discretion, since, in caring for the diseases of souls, he mingled the austerity of strictness with a certain gentleness. Weighing each matter prudently, he was by no means lax toward offenders. In bodily stature he was handsome, well proportioned, so that he was neither excessively short nor monstrously tall. In conduct he knew how to present himself as an example to all, so that he knew how to adapt himself without fault to individual assemblies, both secular and religious. Because [unclear]. He copied out [unclear] with his own hand. For he was an excellent scribe. When the new monastery had been completed by St. William, and after his death the congregation had passed from St. Aurelius to that larger monastery under Abbot Gebhard, Gerung was appointed by him prior of the brothers who had remained at St. Aurelius. At last, by the election and counsel of his brothers, he was ordained first abbot of the aforesaid monastery, which is called the Cell of Paulina down to the present day, and he was sent with ten other monks of this monastery of ours for the establishment of the order according to the desire of St. Paulina. For St. Paulina herself, in that place of her paternal inheritance where she took up the service of Christ and continued for many years in most holy life until death, founded at her own expense a monastery of our order, in the times of Emperor Henry IV, which she handed over to the monks of Hirsau, and [unclear]. Whoever desires to know the merits of this man more fully should read the Life and Deeds of the often-mentioned St. Paulina. Gerung was, as we have said, a man of holy life, and since he was well disposed toward the Roman Church and Christ’s vicar, he suffered many persecutions and wrongs from the servants and friends of Henry [unclear].

In the aforesaid year a great controversy arose between King Henry and the apostolic lord Paschal concerning the investiture of prelates, which was the seedbed of many evils in the Church. Hence the pope in the council of Troyes confirmed by his ban that no one should receive investiture or ecclesiastical dignity from a lay hand, until this question between him and the king should have been settled by synodal discussion.
