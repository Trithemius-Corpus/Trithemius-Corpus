Thus far I have served ungrateful monks more than enough. But this monastery, over which I now preside unworthily, although it is burdened by poverty, has neither such great anxieties nor so many ruinous, contentious occupations with peasants. By God’s grace I live content with my lot; and the poorer the place is in transitory goods, the richer I reckon myself in abundance of peace and tranquility. Indeed, according to the counsel of the holy Christ-loving Paul, having the necessary food by which the little mortal body may for a time be sustained in life, and clothing to keep off Boreas, I seek nothing more. The globe of the earth, sea, and islands, which you write is for sale at Worms, beautifully drawn, I would indeed wish to be able to obtain; but to spend forty florins on it, no one would easily [unclear] me. A few days ago [unclear] for a modest sum a beautiful sphere of the world in small size, recently printed at Strasbourg, and likewise, in a large arrangement, the globe of the earth spread out on a plane, with the islands and regions newly discovered by Amerigo Vespucci the Spaniard in the western sea and toward the south almost to the tenth parallel, together with certain other things pertaining to that inquiry. As for your advising me to put the cause of this matter before my most reverend lord the bishop, you are certainly doing your duty; but you seem to me to weigh too little the occupations of princes, and their perpetual and very great affairs, since you think the studies of men at leisure should be recommended to minds most occupied. Do not doubt that I am most tenacious of our old friendship; and no distances of lands will ever weaken the affection which I once poured out toward you. Therefore, what you bid me do, I exhort and ask that you too not be reluctant to do. I pray that we may live as friends in such a way that the memory of each in the other may avail before God. For this is the true cause of friendship: that we seek nothing other than equality of hearts in the manifestation of God, and the advancement of souls from mutual exhortation. But if one of the friends should fall into some calamity of the raging world, he must be consoled by the other with all his strength. If he has erred, he must at once be recalled by his friend to the way of truth. If he has grown lukewarm in saving study, he must be stirred up; for friendship will be true and rightly commendable if it accomplishes that for which it was instituted. And therefore the holy fathers judged the law of friendship to be most just, by which it is commanded that each man love his friend no less than himself. I heard that Heinrich von Buna died long ago; but I have not heard that his books and the globe of cosmography which he once bought from your workshop remained with the princes of Saxony, as you suppose. For he has a surviving brother, to whom, when dying, he consigned everything. I congratulate you warmly on your prosperity, and rejoice with you as a true friend, yet wishing that you remember the little verses of Hildebert, archbishop of Tours, who says: All mortal things hang on uncertain chance, and by their own changeableness promise flight. Whatever you have today will perhaps leave you tomorrow; or even now, while you speak, it ceases to be yours. You indicate that you have composed marvelous little works by lamplight since the time when I was with you in Dirmstein, which you promise to show me when I come to you. But since there is no reason for me to come to you, indeed not even the will to interrupt my quiet, you will do well, and do what most befits a friend, if you send me as soon as possible the titles of all your works, or certainly, what I would more desire, the little works themselves; once read by me, or even copied, I promise they shall be faithfully returned to you as quickly as possible. Perhaps you wonder that I said I had no reason to come to you; but hear the reason. The monastery over which I preside, though unworthy, is not yet known to be subject to the annual chapter of the Bursfeld fathers, since it has been newly reformed; for this reason they cannot at all burden me with the office of visitation, as they did before for twenty continuous years, not without great expense to me. Hence, by Jesus’ providence, the opportunity of resting is granted me, and no occasion for wandering, as before, is offered. And that is why I said I had no reason to come to you. Yet I would very much wish that you were with me even for one day; I too would show you little works at which you would marvel. But we shall see one another sometime, if the Lord Jesus, who can do all things, wills it. Meanwhile, whenever opportunity offers itself to us, let us mutually exhort one another by letters in his love. Pray for me, brother, to God, that he may keep me in his way and mercifully forgive me all my transgressions, of which there is no number, on account of the grief of his most bitter passion. Farewell. From Würzburg, on the 12th day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1507.
