Lest we should seem ungrateful for so great a grace bestowed upon us through your kindness, [preserving?] the everlasting memory of that immense gift, we freely and steadfastly promise your whole body, and we wish ourselves and our successors to be bound, that for the use of two vicarages newly established in your church we shall give you from the revenues of our monastery, every year in perpetuity, fifty malters each of rye, of the Mainz measure, to be delivered at Mainz in every eventuality at our expense and risk; namely, that for one vicarage in the present month we shall give fifty malters of rye, and thereafter in the next following month, and thenceforth, for each vicarage’s lifetime, every year in perpetuity, we shall give one hundred malters of dry rye without chaff, of the aforesaid measure, to be delivered at Mainz, as has been said, at our risk and expense, between the two feasts, namely the Assumption of Mary and her Nativity. Adding that, as soon as we shall have any plough-teams furnished with beasts of burden, without any deceit or fraud we wish to be bound in perpetuity to bring your wine, which shall have grown for you in Monzingen each year at vintage time, whenever we are requested, to your cellar at Mainz in our wagons and at our expense; provided, however, that if the occurrence of some misfortune should befall, we shall be wholly excused. And for the due firmness of these things we grant the present writing, strengthened by the protection of the seals of the lords abbots in Eberbach and Otterberg, with the consent and knowledge of their convents and of our monastery. Done at Mainz in the year of the Lord 1259, on the day before the Nones of March.

You see how the canons of the greater church of Mainz, in order to make their prebends fatter, sold into annual servitude to the Cistercians, for one hundred malters of rye, a monastery that previously had been free and of our order, to no small loss, injury, and disgrace of our order, unmindful of the benefice received four years earlier from Otto, abbot of the same monastery, the last of our order, who had handed over to them the parish church in Odernheim with notable liberty, to be possessed forever with all its revenues.

In the same year Albertus Magnus, provincial of the Order of Preachers, by the provision of Pope Alexander IV, was made bishop of Regensburg in Bavaria. 1260.

Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, died. Werner of Falkenstein, provost of the greater church, succeeding him, presided for twenty-three years and six months. He governed his church excellently and wonderfully enlarged it in revenues and ornaments.

In the same year Louis, count palatine of the Rhine, of whom we spoke above, who had killed Maria, daughter of the duke of Bavaria, his wife, took another wife, Anna by name, daughter of Conrad, duke of Poland. By her he begot a daughter named Agnes, and a son Louis, an excellent man, concerning whom there will be occasion to speak later in his proper place.

In the same year also Jacob of Lapide and Simon of Guntheim, knights, waging serious hostilities against the Worms people, [took] many of the citizens captive; but afterward peace was made between them, and the captives were released upon payment of money. 1261.

In this year, which was the ninth of John our abbot, there was a very great flood of waters in these regions. The Rhine rose to such a degree that it overturned many houses and buildings; even people living around the Rhine, struck with excessive terror, abandoned their homes, and many fled to the protection of the mountains. In these times there was very great dissension between Engelbert the archbishop and the citizens of Cologne, which endured for many years, not without damage to both parties. 1262.

In the tenth year of John, abbot of this monastery, a certain man came from the lower parts to these lands. He was called Master Theodo, a magician and wondrous conjurer, who performed many incredible marvels. For in the town of Kreuznach, publicly in the marketplace before all the people, he cut off the head of his servant, and after the space of half an hour restored it to the body lying there. He seemed to walk with dogs in the air, as if going hunting, ascending and descending, and shouting in the highest places as if from afar. They thought he had devoured an armed man. He also devoured wagons loaded with wood or hay together with the horses, as those who did not know the method thought. He did other astonishing things too, which I have considered superfluous to write in this place.
