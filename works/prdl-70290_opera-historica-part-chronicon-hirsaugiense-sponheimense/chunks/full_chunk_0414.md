*MCCCXXXIII.*

In the twenty-fourth year of Willicho, abbot of this monastery, Wolfram, bishop of Würzburg, died on the day before the Kalends of July. Otto Wolfskeel succeeded him and presided for fifteen years, having been promoted and ordained at Liège on the twenty-first day of July by Pope John XXII, against Hermann of Leuchtenberg, chancellor of Emperor Louis, out of hatred for that emperor. For, as we said above, after the death of Emperor Henry VII, Louis, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, brother of Count Palatine Rudolf, by the election of the princes, after Frederick duke of Austria had been defeated, was raised to be king in the year of the Lord [unclear], in the twelfth indiction. He soon came into great discord with Pope John [XXII?] on the following occasion. The king’s chancellor, inserting falsehoods as truths into the letters which the emperor had ordered to be written to the pope, called the pope “the beast rising from the sea,” and put in many similar things. By these the pope was provoked to great indignation, and he excommunicated the emperor with all those adhering to him. This same chancellor at last confessed this malice of his at the moment of his death. But afterward, in the year of the Lord MCCCXXVII, Louis set out for Rome, was reconciled to the pope, and, having received the papal blessing and absolution, returned home. To this excommunicated Emperor Louis came William of Ockham, an Englishman by nation, of the order of Friars Minor, a man most learned in philosophy and sacred letters, fleeing after being [unclear] by Pope John [XXII?], I do not know for what reason, and he implored his protection, saying: “Most just emperor, defend me with arms, and I can and will defend you with writings against the pope’s injuries.” And he remained with him until the day of his death, and wrote many dreadful things against the same pope.

In this same year there grew a great abundance of wine, so that at vintage-time, because of the excessive plenty, many lacked vessels, and one vessel after another was filled with the best wine; and two measures of good wine were sold for one Heller penny.

*MCCCXXXIV.*

In the twenty-fifth year of Willicho, abbot of this monastery, Henry, archbishop of Cologne, died. Walram, son of Gerard count of Jülich, provost of Liège and treasurer of Cologne, a man most learned in both laws, succeeded and presided for sixteen years, six months, and nineteen days. He was a peaceful man, who governed the church entrusted to him in good peace, quite vigorously and usefully. This archbishop Walram, among the many buildings which he constructed in various places during the time of his pontificate, also founded the Carthusian monastery in Cologne, which he honorably endowed for the support of the brothers; although in the course of time it was wonderfully enlarged, both in revenues and in buildings, by the offerings of the faithful.

In these times also Adolf, count palatine of the Rhine, John and Simon, counts of Sponheim, also the count of Nassau and Count Sylvester of Dhaun, were mutually allied with one another against Baldwin, bishop of Trier. By setting fire to the town of the same count and by plundering, he reached as far as the gates and caused them much damage in grain and wine. After this, leaving the town of Kreuznach because he could not take it by storm, he came with his army to Sponheim and cruelly laid waste everything he found outside the castle, burning and devastating everything. He burned down with fire the borough that had been before the castle, took the inhabitants, and carried off the spoils and plunder of everything he had found. He consumed by fire all the villages around Sponheim, Beckenau, Beckelnheim, Rudelfheim, Weinesheim, with the others adjoining.
