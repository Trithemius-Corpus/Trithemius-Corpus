From there, carried by ship, he reached the town of Bingen, where in the monastery of St. Rupert, duke, on the other side of the River Nahe, the virgin beloved of God, St. Hildegard, as mistress, presided over devout virgins serving the almighty Lord under the rule of our holy father Benedict. To her he had previously sent by letters words of holy admonition, together with certain small gifts, which are preserved in that same place as relics.

When Bernard, the man of God, came to the holy bride of Christ, after a mutual greeting in Christ he ordered the books which that virgin had written by divine inspiration to be brought to him. After he had examined them carefully and admired them beyond measure, he is said to have told those standing around: “These writings are not human inventions, but divinely inspired through the Spirit; nor can any mortal man understand them unless he has first been truly reformed inwardly in his soul according to the likeness of God.” To this the chaplain of the holy virgin, a monk of most devout conduct, replied: “Reverend father, what you have said is indeed true. But many people, not only unlettered laymen but also religious and learned men, torment the spirit of Christ’s handmaid with daily slanders, when they assert that these things are not divinely inspired, but are either women’s dreams and ravings of a ruined brain, or deceitfully sent in by demons.”

To him the man of God said: “We do not wonder, dearest brother, if those sleeping in sins regard divine revelations as dreams, knowing as true what the holy Apostle said: ‘The animal man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God; for it is foolishness to him, and he cannot understand, because it is examined spiritually.’ For to all those who, as though sleeping, [lie?] in the sins of lust, greed, and pride, divine admonitions seem to be dreams deceitfully sent in. But those who say that these things are sent in by demons have not known the hidden and mystical signs of divine inspirations; and they should be judged like those who said that our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ cast out demons by the power of Beelzebub.”

He also said to holy Hildegard: “And you, dearest daughter, do not fear the reproaches of men, since you have God as your defender; for they will perish like straw, but the truth of the Lord remains forever. We too, for love of you, will take pains that these writings of yours, once read and confirmed by the lord Apostolic, may safely be brought into the light.”

The holy virgin Hildegard offered to the man of God, at his request, a particle of the relics of St. Rupert, duke and confessor. In return for these he too afterward [unclear] certain things [unclear] of the count of Sponheim, under whose temporal authority the aforesaid monastery was built, and in which he also had a daughter, a nun named Hiltrud, a virgin of most holy conduct, who with blessed Hildegard had been [instructed?] in the love of God under the teaching of blessed [Jutta?]. Present there with that count were Cuno, abbot of St. Disibod, and Bernhelm, first abbot in Sponheim, with many other clerics, monks, and laymen. There at that time Bernhelm, abbot of Sponheim, obtained the body of St. Rupert.

Meginhard, duke and count in Sponheim, under whose temporal authority the aforesaid monastery had been built, obtained the body of that duke, whole with skin and flesh, from blessed Hildegard and her whole congregation of holy virgins; and with great reverence and honor, as he continued the descent of the Rhine to his monastery, he cured at Boppard, in the midst of the people, a paralytic, [one with a disabled hand?], and a deaf woman in the same city, with all the people looking on. Finally, after staying for three days in the city of Cologne, he cured twelve lame persons, two maimed persons, five blind persons, and ten deaf persons, restored speech to three mute persons, and performed countless other signs. At Aachen, when he was celebrating Mass, he restored walking to a lame man and sight to four blind persons before a multitude of peoples. In the cities also of Liège and of the Nervii, which is now called Cambrai, he restored speech to many, hearing to the deaf, sight to the blind, walking to the lame, and health to those sick with any disease.

*1149.* In the aforesaid year of Abbot Volmar, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1149, in the twelfth indiction, Crafto, count of Sponheim, son of Meginhard the elder and brother of the younger, betrothed as his wife Clementia, daughter of Adolf, count of Homberg, a beautiful and modest virgin most devoted to Christ. Since she had resolved to devote the flower of her virginity to Christ, she sought and obtained the consent of her mortal bridegroom. Therefore that venerable virgin Clementia became a nun of our order in the monastery of St. Irmina in Oeren at Trier, and served almighty God in great holiness for many years.
