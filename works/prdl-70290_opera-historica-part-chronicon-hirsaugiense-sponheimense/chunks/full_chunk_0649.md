*Johannes Trithemius.*

The very long delay of your return to us has affected me with sorrow, since on account of your absence we are in great tribulation, and are harassed, mocked, and reviled by everyone, as if we were the cause of your most just indignation; and a great outcry of all has arisen against us, saying that we are worthy of burning or of perpetual exile, because we drove away our abbot, a good and holy man, wise and most learned, without cause. So great are the reproaches of men against us that we do not know what we must do, and we greatly fear that we shall soon be expelled from the monastery. But your paternity knows that, apart from the son of perdition, none of us was at fault; rather, we all grieve deeply, and the rash deed of the rivals never pleased us. Now all who were present say that they did not act with the intention of doing injury to your paternity, but because of threats made by the abbots of Mount St. James, by Magnus and Frederick, our familiars. And in truth I believe that it is so, because there was no other cause for their being seized than the threats. The duke’s official, who sent the satellites of his wicked soldiery against our household, has now been removed from his office, and in his place Meginhard of Coppenstein, your sworn vassal, has been substituted. In the name of the prince palatine, however, the prefecture is held by Francis of Sickingen, most devoted to you, who both, together with the neighbors and all the friends, [unclear] especially your arrival. The abbot also of Mount St. James grieves over your absence more than all. Therefore we ask most humbly, most reverend and most beloved father, and by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ we entreat and beseech you, that you would deign to return to us as soon as you can, and not desert the whole flock of your sheep because of the crime of a few. Why do you remain outside so long, sweetest father, when there is no one who either can or wishes to harm you? For even that Duke John loves you greatly and desires that you return, saying that he never thought any evil against your person. All our brothers also, the dearest sons of your paternity, most ardently desire your return to us, except Judas, the most impious traitor. Yet even he pretends that he greatly desires your coming, but wickedness lies to itself; the outcome of the matter will prove this. For I know that he will flee from the monastery as soon as he hears that your paternity is approaching us. Brother Johann Nutius, our senior, and all the brothers desire your well-being and humbly commend themselves. Farewell from Sponheim, on the twenty-second day of the month of November, in the year of the Lord 1505.

*LIIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to his beloved son in Christ, Nicolaus Remittus, his prior, greeting.*

Your letters, dearest son, would have moved our mind from its purpose, so that in the middle of winter we would have hastened our return to our homeland, had not the condition of our promise bound us to the prince to remain longer. For the promise commands us to remain until Easter, lest we offend so great a prince, who is very favorably disposed to our affairs, contrary to his wish. You write that our return is desired by many; we believe it, and in due time, as we promised, with the Lord granting it, [unclear] about to receive the reverence of the promises. But we greatly wonder that the abbot of Mainz grieves so much over our absence, since he himself is the chief author of all the evils against us; at his clandestine and false instigation our familiars [unclear] into scandal for us, and the tongue of the unblemished old man did not spare our most serene prince and legate, the archbishop of Cologne, the count palatine, and the margraves; if we had shown it to even one of them to be read, he would long since have paid the penalty for his rashness. That good abbot acted most unjustly against us, and, out of hatred of others, persecuted us unjustly, whether knowingly or unknowingly. May God forgive him and not impute to him the sin that he committed against us. A strange reflection for an old man: he grieves over our absence, he who first gave the counsel that we should withdraw for a time, avoiding envy. But the fact that an outcry of men has arisen against you does not relieve us at all, nor does it delight us in any way, but rather saddens us, especially for those among you who are innocent of the evils against us. For we know that you did not all conspire against us, nor for that reason do we believe you all innocent. In all these things nothing saddens us so much as that the innocent are reviled together with the guilty. Our innocence consoles us, to which even you, our enemies, will bear witness: how justly among you and without offense we lived, always putting your advantage before our own convenience.
