But Saint Jutta, daughter of Count Stephen, was mistress of the virgins of Christ on the aforesaid Mount St. Disibod; under her their number was greatly increased, and many nobles round about offered their daughters, under her governance, to serve our Lord Jesus Christ. All these served under the Rule of our holy father Benedict and under obedience to the abbot, shut away separately in a narrow and well-walled enclosure; no man except the abbot had access to them.

For this custom was then very celebrated in almost all our monasteries and those of regulars: that the nearer the nuns were to men, the more easily, by their vigilance, they might be protected from the snares of the ancient enemy. So it was in the aforesaid monastery of St. Disibod; so in the monastery of St. John in the Rheingau; so in Schönau; so at St. Alban’s; so at St. James’s; so in Hirsau under Abbot St. William; likewise in Frankenthal. Were there not around the monastery of Limburg three convents of our order established for nuns, namely Seebach, Hausen, and Schönfeld, of which the first still flourishes, suitable for holy virgins, while the other two are desolate?

And at the monastery of St. Martin of Trier, beside the walls, above the bank of the Moselle, toward the north, there was the convent of St. Symphorian, in which many most holy nuns of our order were renowned; today no trace of it appears except a single column alone, by which the church was once supported. And Bernhelm, the first abbot of this place mentioned above, built a small convent at the foot of the sloping mountain on the western side of this monastery, as we have also said before; in it he enclosed his sister Mechthild, who had first been enclosed at St. Alban’s for love of the heavenly life, together with another woman named Sophia. And in the same reclusory there were, for many years, several holy and most devout virgins succeeding one another, whose names we do not doubt are written in heaven.

But afterward, when nuns, because of the closeness of the places, began to be a snare and a scandal to men, and when on both sides they walked not according to the spirit, as before, but according to the flesh, we see that almost all the convents, when the nuns failed or were even expelled, were totally destroyed through neglect or some other occasion. For today among us, apart from the name of the place, which is called Clausa from the enclosure of the virgins who once dwelt there, no trace of the convent or structure is seen. But let us return to the history.

Therefore the holy virgin of Christ of whom we have spoken, Jutta, was renowned in this life for many miracles: among other things she changed water into wine, and several times crossed the flowing waves of the river Glan with dry foot. Hence, not without a great reputation for sanctity, she passed from this world to heaven; her life and most holy conduct have been written down with sufficient diligence. After her death the holy virgin Hildegard was appointed mistress of the sisters, under Cuno, abbot of the aforesaid convent. After a few years had passed, with the whole congregation of sisters, by permission of the abbot and forewarned by divine revelation, she passed over to Bingen, and there she remains today; on the mountain of St. Rupert the Confessor she built for herself and her sisters from the alms of the faithful.

*1137.*

In the year 1137, in the thirteenth year of Abbot Bernhelm, Berthold, prior of this monastery, died on the twelfth day before the Kalends of July, a man in every respect most religious, and a distinguished cultivator and lover of the divine Scriptures, to whom nothing was ever sweeter than either to write books or to read them. He was so diligent in divine worship that he was always the first in choir and the last to go out after all the others. No one ever saw him idle, no one sad or lax; for in due time he did some good thing: he either read, or wrote, or certainly devoted himself to prayer. He was so frequent in prayer that you would have thought he could think of nothing other than prayer; and again so fervent in the reading of the Scriptures and in manual work that he seemed to love nothing less than prayer.

Anshelm succeeded him in the priorship, formerly a monk of St. James of Mainz, who had been sent here among the first twelve: a good, gentle, sweet, and mild man, born of a military family in Bickelnheim. He presided in the office of prior for forty-three years under three abbots, Bernhelm, Crafto, and Adelger. Under his direction regular discipline advanced greatly, both by the merit of virtues and by the number of persons. Faithfully following also in the footsteps of his predecessor, he enlarged the library of this convent not a little during the time of his office. For around these times the monks of this convent and of St. Disibod had a great zeal for the Scriptures, and with all eagerness wrote books of sacred reading.

In this year Adelbert the elder, archbishop of Mainz, died on the ninth day before the Kalends of July and was buried in the monastery of Eberbach, which he himself had founded.
