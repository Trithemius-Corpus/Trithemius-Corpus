When he had seen this, that impious man hastily went out of the church and, running to the river Nahe as if driven mad, threw himself in and drowned. But his companion immediately fled; later, however, he returned and related the course of the matter to the mistress and the nuns of the said monastery. Then a certain priest arrived, who had celebrated there that same day; seeing a miracle of such novelty, he spread out the corporal before the image and collected the milk flowing with blood. This blood with milk, preserved in a glass vessel, we have seen in the aforesaid monastery down to the present day; at the petition of the abbess we even transferred it with our own hands from an old broken vessel into a new glass. When the miracle was at once made public, King Albert, who was still in the town of Bingen, came with the other princes to the monastery of St Rupert; seeing so dreadful a deed, he was astonished, and for the restoration of the monastery he bestowed many gifts in honor of the Blessed Virgin. In memory of this miracle a chapel was built there in honor of the Mother of God, in which the image, often renewed in the meantime, is seen above the altar.

When matters had been settled with the archbishop of Mainz, the king, advancing further against the archbishop of Trier and the archbishop of Cologne, subjected both to himself and compelled them to seek favor, not without their very serious losses.

*1303.*

In the fifth year of Dietlib, our abbot, Albert, the seventeenth prior of this monastery, died on the seventh Ides of May, a man of many virtues, who showed the greatest diligence for the restoration of regular observance and restored no small part. After him Abbot Dietlib chose as the eighteenth prior of this monastery Henry, a monk, the son of the sister of the aforesaid Albert, young indeed in years, but in character and wisdom an old man. He presided in the priorship of this monastery for twenty-nine years; of him we shall speak later in his place after Wilhelm. During the whole time in which he was prior there was a sufficiently good reform, although for many years it did not attain the first institution which had existed at the time of the foundation.

*1305.*

The bishop of Mainz, whom Peter, born of Treviri, succeeded, presided for sixteen years, a shrewd and industrious man, who issued many constitutions and ruled the church of Mainz quite magnificently.

In the same year, on the fourth Kalends of May, Eberwin, bishop of Worms, died. Emerich of Cronenberg succeeded him and presided for ten years, a peaceful and devout man, whom both people and clergy venerated with wonderful affection; when he was sent by the apostolic pope as bishop, they willingly received him.

*1405.*

In the seventh year of Dietlib, our abbot, on the feast of the commemoration of St Paul the Apostle, King Albert, being at Speyer, celebrated the obsequies of the king of Bohemia with great pomp in the greater church. While the bells were being rung, the great bell fell; and, breaking two vaults, it fell to the ground and was divided into three parts.

At these times the citizens of Koblenz contumaciously rose up against Diether, archbishop of Trier, their lord, forgetful of the fidelity of their oaths. Therefore the archbishop, moved to anger, summoned to his aid many princes and nobles, whom he hired for large sums of money, and encircled Koblenz with a siege. When it was at last taken at the dedication, he constrained the citizens to obedience. For this victory he spent incredible sums of money; hence he pledged almost all the revenues and proceeds of the church, and stirred up hatred against himself among both clergy and laity, especially the nobles of the province of Trier.

*1306.*

In the ninth year of Dietlib, our abbot, on the day of the Blessed Virgin Mary, otherwise the Purification, when the ice broke up, very great losses were inflicted on the cities and villages around the Rhine and the Main. The bridge at Frankfurt was broken, and many nearby houses were submerged in various places. At Cologne also two towers near the Rhine, shaken by that tempest, fell; many persons of both sexes and of every age were drowned in the Rhine in various places.

At the same time all the Jews were expelled from the kingdom of the Franks, their goods having been confiscated.
