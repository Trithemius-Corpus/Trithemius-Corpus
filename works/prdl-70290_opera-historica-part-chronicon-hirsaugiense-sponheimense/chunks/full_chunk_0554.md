The anniversary day of the dedication of the same altar was set for the twenty-ninth day of the month of July, that is, the fourth day before the Kalends of August, on which day Trithemius himself was elected abbot of this monastery, as was said at the beginning of his appointment. At the consecration of this altar there was present Jacob Wimpheling of Schlettstadt, theologian, poet, and most eloquent orator, and he composed this tetrastich ex tempore:

While Trithemius, John, was head of the Sponheim house,
a father with cheerful dignity,
to you, divine Mary, his mother, and to the venerable
Andrew, this present altar was dedicated.

For the most blessed Mary, Mother of God, is the chief patroness of this citadel; after her, her holy mother Anne; and third, the apostle Andrew. Moreover, Mary Magdalene, most fervent lover of Jesus Christ, was two years later assumed as co-patroness, because of her relics, which were brought from Gaul by Trithemius and deposited there. Finally, for the adornment of the same altar, John the abbot in the course of time obtained from various princes, bishops, abbots, and other prelates of churches and monasteries many most precious relics of saints; bringing them with joy into this our monastery, he had them enclosed, as best he could, in panels and beautifully adorned in shrines, and laid up with reverence beside the aforesaid altar. For there are many particles, notable in quantity and in the merit of the saints, to be preserved forever with the greatest honor.

In the same year the aforesaid abbot John described some miracles of the most holy mother Anne, and the Catalogue of German Writers, which he had begun almost four years before, at the urging of Master Jacob Wimpheling of Schlettstadt, named above; correcting it this year from the rough drafts, he completed it as best he could.

In the same year also, on the fourth day before the Kalends of April, Rudolf, bishop of Würzburg, died, a man burdened with old age and with the disease of the stone, and was buried in his greater church. After his death he was cut open, as is customary, by his order, and a stone of incredible size was found, which weighed thirty-three lots; and as a sign of wonder it is preserved and shown in the greater church. He died at the age of one hundred, leaving much money and wealth.

In the year noted above, on the third day before the Nones of January, John of Colnhausen died, once abbot of this monastery and afterward transferred to Seligenstadt, as was said above.

In the same year also, on the second day before the Ides of September, in a certain village not far from Worms, a woman gave birth to a monstrous and unusual offspring, having two bodies, distinct from the head downward, whose heads above were joined into one at the forehead, nor could they by any means be separated from one another. Even today two twin girls are living with heads joined and bodies divided, each carrying out her own function either by sense or by intellect; while one sleeps, the other watches, and while the one weeps, the other can laugh or even do something else. This monstrosity is said to have happened by accident. When the pregnant mother was speaking somewhat merrily with another woman in the cemetery, standing with her face turned close to the other, a certain man came up, for the sake of joking, and modestly knocked the heads of both together at the forehead; terrified by this unexpected event, the woman gave birth to offspring in the likeness of the accident.

In the year likewise noted above, at the summons of King Maximilian, a very great assembly of princes from the whole Roman Empire was held for several months in the city of Worms, amid a great concourse of peoples who could not be counted. There, while the king sat in majesty with the prince-electors, as is the custom, and the other princes, bishops, and abbots stood in their order, on the fourteenth day of the month of July the aforesaid prince-electors, and afterward the rest, having sworn their oaths, received their feudal rights from the king himself according to custom. On the following day Maximilian, king of the Romans, with the consent of the princes, elevated Eberhard, count of Württemberg, into a duke: a vigorous, pious, magnanimous man, and worthy of the honor of the duchy, since he was a singular lover of religious and clergy, a most steadfast defender, and a reformer of many monasteries.

In the same year the monastery in Odenheim, in the diocese of Speyer, was transferred from our order to seculars, with the consent of the supreme pontiff at the urging of the apostate monks, who, having no legitimate cause for their transfer, suggested falsehoods to the pontiff as truths. Concerning the insolent abbot of those apostates, who was the occasion of all the evil, someone composed this tetrastich:
