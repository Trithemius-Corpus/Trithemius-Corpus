*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius*

... celebrated at [Nuremberg?], at which Peter of Santa Pudenziana, sent by the pope, was present. There [unclear] Engelbert, elect of Cologne, was confirmed as archbishop by the same cardinal. Likewise in the same year Otto, formerly emperor, abandoned by all, made ill by sorrow of mind, died after many distresses.

In the same year also Abbot Lutfrid, seized by illness and old age, after many labors by which he had governed this monastery quite usefully for 11 years and 5 days, rested in the Lord in April: a man gentle in disposition and of singular meekness, who lived as both monk and abbot in all tranquility and peace among the brothers; he made various buildings, and did not diminish the revenues.

*Concerning Everhard, twenty-third abbot of this monastery, who governed for 15 years; concerning the various events of that time in Germany.*

When the venerable Abbot Lutfrid had entered the way of all flesh and had been given ecclesiastical burial according to custom, the brothers, deprived of their shepherd, assembled together and began to deliberate on the election of a new abbot. At length, after mature deliberation among themselves had first been held, and after the grace of the Holy Spirit had first been invoked, by unanimous consent they elected as twenty-third abbot of this monastery lord Everhard, a professed monk of this community, a man of complete integrity, distinguished in learning and morals. Canonically elected, confirmed by Conrad, bishop of Speyer, and solemnly ordained according to custom, he governed this monastery vigorously for 13 years and some days.

He was born in Swabia, begotten of free and noble parents; in his boyhood he was set by them to love letters, and, advancing with age in all studies of the liberal arts, he became learned. Then, coming to the religious life, he was made a monk and was ordained among the brothers. Ordained, moreover, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s nativity 1216, in the fourth indiction, in the month of April, as we have said, he took every care to show himself to all, in morals and life, a useful shepherd, instructing those subject to him in regular observance no less by example than by word, which he both greatly increased and maintained.

For he was so circumspect in ordering external affairs for the benefit of the monastery and the brothers that one would openly attest that he could in no way be wholly free for the spirit of inward life. For he was wholly intent upon the observance of monastic discipline, and so solicitous and circumspect concerning the conduct of the brothers that he was both called and truly was the shepherd of all. He was also diligent and provident in the care of temporal affairs.

*Against the provost of Roth.*

The abbot, against the rebellious [unclear], wishing to avoid obedience, appealed to the bishop of the aforesaid church of Augsburg, thinking, in the manner of the other abbots of the said church, that he was immediately subject not to the abbot but to the bishop. Abbot Everhard, taking this provost’s rashness ill, opposed him steadfastly, and before the aforesaid bishop and the whole chapter of the church of Augsburg he triumphed gloriously by judgment. For when the rights and privileges of the monastery of Hirsau had been examined, it was there declared by all that the provost in Roth was subject not to the bishop, but without any intermediary to the abbot of this monastery for the time being: namely, in such a way that his institution and deposition, according to the rule of our most holy father Benedict, belonged to the abbot alone, and that no one else whatsoever should in any way interfere in his ordination.
