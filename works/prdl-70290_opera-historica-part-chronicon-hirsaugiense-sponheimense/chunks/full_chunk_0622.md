*Of Johannes Trithemius*

I agreed to go into the March, especially because my aforesaid most serene prince, the Palatine, not only gave me his consent for that very thing, but also advised it. Meanwhile perhaps my brothers will experience what benefit our absence brings them; their charity toward their father has grown too cold, since, when it could have resisted the rashness of rivals in our absence, it disdained to do so.

On the fourth day of August we left Cologne; on the vigil of Saint Lawrence the martyr, after vespers, we entered Neometum. The prince Margrave, however, after assigning us expenses for the journey with three servants, left Cologne with his men on the penultimate day of July itself, making his way through Westphalia and Saxony to his own region. We left the Abbot of Limburg in Cologne, and with him our brother Master Jacob, whom we would rather have taken with us into the March, had the abbot's requests not stood in the way. In his cases against his adversary the Count of Leiningen, whose officials burned the monastery of Limburg [unclear], he obtained from the royal majesty as commissioner Jacob, Archbishop of Mainz, who, after hearing the parties' allegations and examining witnesses if any are produced, will try to bring about harmony between them not by law but amicably. And if the matter can be reconciled, it has gone well; but if it cannot, which I rather believe, the whole business, with the acts, will be referred back to the king.

That impious tyrant, hateful to God and men, a count whose name is unworthy of memory, denies the most audacious [unclear] knowingly before the majesty, but he plainly lies, since he is convicted by open testimonies. The man full of the devil seeks evasions; he seems to sigh for the Greek Kalends. O evil head among the sons of the devil, more like your father, [unclear], do you think that you can escape the vengeance of God? By your own authority you caused the monastery, with its distinguished church, to be burned, out of hatred and vengeance; therefore you have incurred the penalty of the canon which is set forth in 23, question 8, chapter *Pessimam*. You are a member of your father the devil, rightly to be punished, as in 41, question 2, *Cum devotissimam*, and in the following chapter. Almighty God is not ignorant of your horrible crime because he dissembles for a short time; nor will you therefore escape the wrath of the just judge because you have not at once paid the penalties you have deserved. Divine power indeed takes vengeance slowly, but it will compensate the delay by the severity of the punishment. But let the man go to ruin, unworthy of any man's memory.

But you, Roger, we ask that you come to us at Neometum as soon as possible; we have many necessary matters to discuss with you before we proceed into the March. We shall remain here some days for certain affairs of ours; when these are settled, we shall soon be on our way. We have written to your prior, asking that he permit you to come to us, which, as we trust, he will not refuse. Farewell, from Neometum, on the twelfth day of August, in the year 1505.

*XXXII. Johannes Trithemius to his most beloved brother Johannes Nuntius, greeting.*

We grieve for your illnesses, most loving brother, from our whole heart, and we suffer with you more than we can either say or commit to letters, humbly beseeching the mercy of the Almighty that he may deign mercifully to pour out over you the bowels of his compassion, and may grant you in all your adversities, for love of him, patience and the everlasting consolation of his inward sweetness. You exhort us, and with suppliant devotion ask that we return to our monastery, not doubting that very shortly all our adversaries will return into favor with us, since they are not a little sorry that they began this game of rashness, and all desire our return with great longing.

The same things that you write we have heard from others, and we do not doubt that they are true; but at this time we cannot return to the monastery. We are going into the March of Brandenburg, and will remain there for some time; for this journey the most serene prince, the Count Palatine, at the request of Margrave Joachim, not only gave us his consent but also advised that we go. Meanwhile it will become possible to see what [unclear] are plotting against us, and how much they favor holy religion, who, armed with envy and malice, have persecuted me without cause because of hatred for the Prince Palatine. My prior was aware of every conspiracy of the rivals against me; we are not ignorant of what moved him. For he believes that, if we, provoked by insult, should withdraw from the monastery, he would succeed us in the abbacy. And in truth both things can happen: that we may withdraw shortly, and that he may succeed us. For we were long ago forewarned by a most certain revelation that we shall not remain in the abbacy of Sponheim until death, although we are still ignorant of the time of withdrawal. We do not yet have a successor designated for us, although we can plausibly suspect that the prior will thrust himself in.
