He was a sufficiently industrious man, who governed the monastery well in temporal matters. But he utterly detested reform, and lived according to the flesh, leaving sons [and] daughters, not without loss to the monastery and grave danger to his own soul.

In the same year also, 1461, after the eleventh hour, at the twenty-eighth minute, in the night after midday, on the Kalends of February, that is, in the very night preceding the feast of the Purification of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin, Johannes Trithemius was born, afterwards the twenty-fifth abbot of this monastery, in the village of Trittenheim, four miles from Trier in the descent of the Moselle, as will be said below.

*Concerning Ulrich of Zeiskheim, the twenty-second abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for five years and twenty-three days; concerning various deeds of that time.*

After Conrad, abbot of this monastery, had died and been buried, the brothers who were then conventuals assembled together on the following day, that is, on the sixth day before the Ides of March, in the morning at the ninth hour or thereabout, at the chapter-house, to elect a new shepherd. After discussions had been held among them, at length they unanimously elected Ulrich of Zeiskheim, born not far from Germersheim. Confirmed by Adolph, archbishop of Mainz, through his vicar in pontificals, he governed this monastery, once ordained, for five years and twenty-eight days.

In his youth he had been handed over by his parents to the monastery in Fels, in the diocese of Strasbourg, which was afterwards transferred from the order; he gave all his time to ease, games, and feasting, and neither drank in knowledge of letters nor loved the observance of the order. But after many years, when he had lived too extravagantly and prodigally among brothers who were all proprietors and excessively dissolute, weighed down by the burden of his debts, he could no longer remain there, being troubled by the excessive pressure of his creditors. Therefore, with the permission of his abbot intervening, he secretly departed from there, and, coming here, was received as a conventual of this monastery.

But when he was appointed abbot he did not change his former ways. Giving himself to feasting and to wandering and useless excursions, he brought this monastery to extreme poverty and want. For in five years he contracted so many debts, and secretly sold and alienated so many goods, meadows, fields, rents, and revenues, that, all computed together, in the time of his rule he harmed and damaged this monastery by more than three thousand florins. In buying and selling he spent much improvidently and uselessly, since he often sold for six florins a horse which he himself had bought within a few days for twenty gold pieces. In his accounts it is noted that he bought twenty-four horses for his own person in five years, which, as soon as he had bought them, deceived by the words of the sellers, he would habitually sell again at a much lower price.

He sold annual rents and returns without the knowledge or will of his convent in many places, namely: in Odernheim fields for 131 florins; in Meisenheim; in Upper Heimbach he sold three marks of perpetual rent; in Budesheim, near Bingen; in Lorchersheim; in Geisenheim; in Winkel; in Kreuznach; in Heimersheim he sold many precious fields for a great sum of money; in Sobernheim. And in various other places he secretly sold many things, which remain alienated from the monastery down to the present day. In his time he constructed nothing inside or outside the monastery, nor did he restore ruined buildings in any respect. He also paid off, from the substance of this monastery, debts, formerly not small, which he had contracted in Fels. In Mainz also he contracted some debts before he came to this place, which nevertheless were afterwards secretly paid by him from the substance of this monastery.

He also spent much shamefully, after the example of his predecessor; for each publicly kept a concubine, sometimes in the monastery, sometimes in the surrounding villages, on whom they spent much, to the loss of the monastery and the ruin of their own souls. But enough has been said about these things so far; let us pursue the rest of Ulrich’s deeds in order.
