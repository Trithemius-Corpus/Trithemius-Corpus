The village of Armsheim, together with its new and very beautiful church, was burned to the ground by his command and miserably destroyed; the village of Bafenheim, together with the church and our court lying next to it, was reduced to ashes by his order; likewise the village of Lansheim with its church and bells utterly perished by fire; likewise Windesheim, Heidesheim, Budeseheim, Laubersheim, and very many other villages, the names of all of which it would be too tedious to recount, were burned in like madness and wholly perished.

There was a village near the river Nahe, close to the town of Bingen, which by its old name they called Monsterium, having a very beautiful church. A few years earlier the Count Palatine had bought it from the hands of a certain Count of the Rhine, against the protest of Berthold, archbishop of Mainz, placing in it a garrison against the attacks of the men of the Rhine. Hence very grave dissensions had arisen between the church of Mainz and the Palatines; and on their account the men of Mainz, having at this time gained the opportunity of evil wars, supplied the cause, not to say the pleas, for its burning. For for a long time, in the name of the Palatine, a certain Albert Goler of Ravensburg, prefect of the Stauronenses, had harassed them and had made them subject to various inconveniences. Therefore, when the occasion they had long desired had now been given, the men of Bingen obtained from the Count of Hesse, by their entreaties, the burning of the aforesaid village.

But when the village was already burning, Master Henry, pastor of the church of Bingen, was summoned to rescue the most holy body of Christ from the fire; for when he came to the church, already burning in its upper part, he drew the sacrament from the hagiotheca and, suffused with tears, said before everyone: “O most wicked of men, who do not even spare the Lord God your creator, but strive to drive him who contains all things from his dwelling: believe, dukes, believe, prince, that Almighty God will not long let so rash an injury go unavenged.” How truly he spoke, the vengeance that followed proved by divine judgment.

Moreover Wilhelm, Count of Hesse, after spending [unclear] days in the aforesaid place near Stauronesum, and after all things round about, far and wide, had been consumed by dreadful devastation, led his army through the town of Bingen. On the following day, advancing against Ingelheim, he set his battle line in order and thought to attack the castle, which they call the palace of Charlemagne; but his attempts were checked in the following manner by the diligence of the Palatines. As the Hessians approached the castle, when they saw the gates open, they thought the garrisons had turned to flight. And so, bursting forward in a mass to the gate, each, gaping for plunder, hurried to get ahead of the other. Behold, suddenly the Palatines who had lain hidden inside, setting fire to engines prepared beforehand in a concealed arrangement, which from their sound we call bombards, directed them against the enemy, killing many by the blows of the instruments and turning the rest to flight. And soon, when the castle was shut, they rushed to the walls and towers and by the violence of the engines stoutly kept all away from access to the castle.

Meanwhile, while these things were being done around the castle, the adjoining village, having been set on fire, was burning, while all the villagers within that castle protected themselves by the defense of the walls. Indeed many of the Hessians, entering the houses of the burning village for the sake of plunder, were led astray by their own negligence and perished in the fire. The Hessians, thrown into confusion and having suffered a great slaughter of their men, raised the siege and crossed over to Mainz; having crossed the river, they took by surrender a certain castle of the Count in Hanau, which is called Homberg or Alteburg.

While these things were being done by the Hessians, Alexander, Duke of Zweibruecken, assisted by the forces of his allies, surrounded Pilickheim with a siege. *Pilickheim is besieged.* Since he could not overcome the strength of his adversaries, he withdrew ingloriously from the siege of the town. Therefore, when the army had been removed from the siege, it attacked and plundered the villages and monasteries round about, situated under the lordship of the Count Palatine, and whatever was not redeemed with money it laid waste by fire and burning. The Alexandrians attacked and plundered the church at Clingenmonster, which a few years earlier was remembered as belonging to our Order, and also the monasteries of the Cistercian order, Vtersdal in Otterburg, together with Hartvigshausen of the Holy Spirit, and also that of the nuns of St. Lambert; they extorted money for remitting the burning. Our courts [unclear]. And evils were multiplied in all the land of the Palatine.

But while the enemies were raging everywhere around us against the Palatine, almost all the courts of our monastery of Sponheim were either destroyed by fire by the Hessians or utterly desolated by the incursion of plunderers. For with the fruits of the earth consumed by the army of the enemies, we suffered an intolerable loss of temporal goods, scarcely recoverable in thirty years. Since Trithemius our abbot remained in Stauronesum for several months, with the enemies around the monastery day and night, we fear that our losses cannot be recovered in our age.
