*1335.*

In the fifteenth year of Abbot Sigismund, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1335, on the day of the apostles Simon and Jude, a very great and exceedingly tempestuous wind blew in the regions of the Rhine and Swabia; it overturned trees and many buildings in various places.

In the same year Otto, duke of Bavaria, of whom we made mention above, son of Duke Stephen and brother of Henry the Elder, died without children; his duchy devolved by right to his brother.

*1336.*

In the following year, which was the nineteenth of Abbot Sigismund, Willichin, or Wilhelm, the eleventh abbot of my monastery of Sponheim, died in the twenty-eighth year of his rule, on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of April.

In the same year, on the day of St. Augustine the bishop, Walram, bishop of Speyer, died and was buried there in the church of the Preachers before the high altar. Gerard of Ehrenberg succeeded him, elected on the day of St. Catherine, and presided for twenty-seven years, one month, and six days. But after Willichin, Henry, born at Kreuznach, was appointed twelfth abbot of my monastery of Sponheim; he presided for two years, one month, and six days, not with sufficient usefulness, as the time required.

*1337.*

In the twentieth year of Abbot Sigismund, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1337, there was everywhere a great mortality of men and livestock, and grain was sold at a high price, so that the poor were oppressed by great want, and many perished from hunger and lack of food.

*1338.*

In the following year, which was the twenty-first of Abbot Sigismund, Edward the Second [III?], king of England, came to Emperor Louis at Koblenz, seeking aid from him against Philip, king of France. Louis, after receiving money, promised him much support, but could not keep his promises, because he had princes unwilling to obey; and the king of England, frustrated in his hope, lost the money spent without profit.

In the same year Reinald, count of Guelders, who was afterward raised to duke, led out a great army and declared war against the people of Liege and the Brabanters, because in the previous year, while he was with the emperor, they had miserably laid waste his land by fire and plunder. He had with him Adolph, bishop of Liege, whom the citizens had formerly shamefully expelled. In this war more than nine thousand men fell on both sides. The victory went to Count Reinald; from it he became not only rich, but famous in every land and acceptable to all the princes.

*1339.*

In the twenty-second year of Abbot Sigismund, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1339, on the day of St. Kilian the martyr, around midday, there was a great eclipse of the sun for almost two hours.

In the same year, around autumn, the Suitenses [men of Schwyz?], after a dissension had arisen in the city of Berne, cruelly killed several of the citizens.

Also in the same year Emperor Louis held a solemn assembly of the princes at Frankfurt, where, among other things, he raised Reinald, count of Guelders, to duke, adding to his duchy the county of Zutphen, because of his singular prudence and the magnificent deeds done by him. For he had been the eleventh from the beginning in the line of the counts of Guelders, a prudent, bold, warlike, fortunate man, and in every respect magnificent, who vigorously accomplished many things in peace and in war.

At the same time also Wilhelm, count of Julich, was created margrave by Emperor Louis with the consent of the princes: a man worthy of such honor, since he [unclear]. In this year there was a fierce war between Philip, king of France, and Edward, king of England, over the county of Normandy, which each contended belonged to him by right. For the king of England, having hired troops at great pay, entered France with a strong force, laying waste to everything by plunder and fire, although it profited him little, because he did not obtain Normandy.
