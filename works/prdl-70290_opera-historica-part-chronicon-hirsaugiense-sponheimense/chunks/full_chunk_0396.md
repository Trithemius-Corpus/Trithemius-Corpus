*Chronicle of Trithemius. 1400.*

*Our Clausa is made a vineyard.*

Dietlib, abbot, in the third year of his rule, utterly desolated and destroyed the enclosure of the sisters beside the monastery on the western side, built from the beginning of the foundation of this place by the first abbot and at this time collapsed through excessive age; and he had the stones carried away and planted a vineyard in the same place. And thus thereafter nothing remained down to the present day except the name of the place, the Inclusory. For it is still called Clausa from the enclosure of the sisters who once dwelt there, and, apart from the name, it shows no trace of its former condition, as is seen.

*1401.*

In the fourth year of Dietlib our abbot, on the fifth day before the Kalends [unclear], Frederick, bishop of Speyer, died. Sibodo was bishop of Speyer. Heinrich of Wernberg was archbishop of Cologne. After the death of Wichbold, archbishop of Cologne, Heinrich of Virneburg, the second of this name, succeeded and presided for twenty-six years, a man more inclined to war than to peace, since during all the days of his pontificate he had disputes with the city of Cologne, counts, princes, nobles, and neighbors, and taxed his clergy and religious with very heavy exactions beyond what was customary and due.

In the same year also Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, Diether, archbishop of Trier, Heinrich, archbishop of Cologne, and Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, conspiring together, made peace and a perpetual treaty among themselves, which greatly displeased Albert, king of the Romans. Therefore, coming unexpectedly with a great army, he first invaded the land of the aforesaid count palatine; after it had been miserably devastated, he compelled him to surrender. Then, moving his army against the archbishop of Mainz, among other damages and fires inflicted on the region, he besieged the town of Bingen, which belonged to the jurisdiction of the church of Mainz. The nuns of the monastery of St. Rupert, very near the aforesaid town on the other side of the river Nahe, having heard of the king’s arrival, all fled and withdrew to [lbingen], taking with them privileges, relics, and whatever they could carry off in the shortness of the time. Therefore, when King Albert came to the town of Bingen, he stationed war engines and part of the army in the aforesaid monastery of St. Rupert, and made a stable for horses out of the house of those serving God, and began to attack the town vigorously, which also, after a few days had passed, he received into his power. But when the aforesaid archbishop saw that he could in no way resist the king’s power, he sought pardon from him and obtained favor, after resigning into the hand and power of the king himself the towns of Lahnstein and Bingen, with the castles Klopp, Ehrenfels, and Scharfenstein, together with the other conditions.

Meanwhile, while the archbishop, being with the king in that same town of Bingen, was negotiating the conditions of peace, and all who had had residence in the monastery of St. Rupert beyond the Nahe, now that the town had been captured, had betaken themselves into it, leaving the monastery abandoned, empty, and stripped of all things, two wicked men, who out of want were following the king’s army, entered the aforesaid monastery of St. Rupert with the mind and intention of carrying away anything they might find. But after they had examined all the places and corners of the monastery most diligently, they found nothing; for everything had previously been carried off by those who had occupied the place during the days of the siege. Therefore, going out from the cloister, they searched the church, to see if by chance they might find something left in it. Now at that time in that church, before the entrance of the choir, which was in the lower parts above the ground, not raised up as it is now, toward the south, there was in the cloister an altar consecrated in honor of St. Catherine, virgin and martyr; above it, in an arched recess in the wall, an image of the most blessed mother of God and virgin Mary was painted quite fittingly, having a crown on her head, in which there were four crystal stones, artfully fixed into the wall and the crown, and a fifth on the breast of the image itself. When, therefore, those thieves found nothing in the church, one of them, so that he should not go away altogether empty, climbed onto the aforesaid altar and began to dig those crystals out of the Virgin’s crown. The other, rebuking him, said: “What are you doing, wretched man? Stop despoiling the mother of mercy; through her, if salvation ought to come to us, and not through another grace, we shall be able to find it.”

But that sacrilegious and impious man, scorning his rebuke, after he had dug the four crystals out of the crown, also began to dig the fifth out of the breast of the holy Virgin. A wondrous thing happened: as soon as that impious man presumed to violate the sacred breast, the image began to sweat all over. Seeing this, the sacrilegious man shuddered somewhat, but, hardened in wickedness, did not cease from the crime he had begun. Therefore he tore the stone out of the sacred breast of the image; when it had been torn away, immediately blood mixed with milk burst forth from the very wound in great quantity.
