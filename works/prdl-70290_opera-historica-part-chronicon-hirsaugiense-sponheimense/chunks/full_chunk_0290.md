*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

*The emperor’s crime*

In the same year, Emperor Louis expelled John, son of the king of Bohemia, from the duchy of Carinthia, which he had previously given him after receiving money; and he joined his wife, seized without a judgment of the Church, to his own son, to the scandal of many. Hence for this as well as for many other crimes he was again excommunicated by Pope Clement VI, and at last, as we shall say, deposed from the empire.

*William, count of Holland, is killed in war.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Wighard, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 1345, William, count of Hainaut, Holland, and Zeeland, and lord of Frisia, around the feast of the Nativity of Blessed Mary ever Virgin, led a great army against the Frisians, having with him the count of Horn and many other nobles and very brave men. But the Frisians, surrounded by ditches full of water, came out against them in great fury, and, using their customary skill, when they had them enclosed among the marshes, killed some with the sword, while others they miserably drowned by suddenly releasing the waters. Among these Count William too was suffocated and died without children. After his death, his sister Margaret, wife of Emperor Louis IV, as the nearer heir, by decree of the emperor and princes, came down into Holland with her son William in great state; she at once subjected all her brother’s land to her own authority; and with the consent of the inhabitants she handed it over to her son William to govern. When matters had been settled in peace, she returned with her people to Bavaria. Therefore William, having obtained his uncle’s dominion, was accustomed to prefix such a title to his letters: William, by the grace of God count palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria, count of Hainaut, Holland, and Zeeland, and lord of Frisia, etc. He was, however, a proud, restless, and warlike man, who did not know how to defer to his own mother. From this time the aforesaid counties began to belong to the dukes of Bavaria; by them they were governed in quiet possession for almost eighty years, until the year of the Lord 1425; then they passed to the dukes of Burgundy.

In the aforesaid year Henry, archbishop of Mainz, having been summoned by Pope Clement VI and not appearing at the curia, was excommunicated.

*1346.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Wighard, but the year of the Lord 1346, Pope Clement VI, in a public consistory and with the consent of the cardinals, by sentence deposed in his absence Henry of Virneburg, archbishop of Mainz, contumacious and rebellious against the apostolic commands, previously, as we have said, summoned and excommunicated; he stripped him of every pontifical honor, and substituted Gerlach of Nassau in his place. Yet Gerlach did not obtain possession of the archbishopric [unclear], dealing with Gerlach not without great harm to the church of Mainz and the subject people.

In the same year a very grave dispute arose between the brothers Edward and Reinard over the duchy of Guelders; it lasted for sixteen years, to their great loss and the devastation of the whole land. For while quarrels and wars were being waged between them, many thousands of men were killed, villages were deserted without inhabitants, castles too were destroyed, and the land of the whole duchy remained untilled, the peasants having either been killed or put to flight. But finally Edward, after Reinard had been thrust into custody, alone obtained the duchy, as we shall say in its place.

In the same year also Pope Clement VI, who said that he had deposed Louis from the empire by sentence, commanded the princes that within a fixed term which he had set for them, unless they wished to lose the right of election, they should elect another emperor in Louis’s place, one who would obey the commands of the Church as a son should; otherwise he would necessarily have care of the empire as supreme head. Accordingly, assembling at Rhens below Boppard near the Rhine on the second day of the month of July, Baldwin archbishop of Trier, Walram archbishop of Cologne, Gerlach, to whom the pope, as we have said, had provided the church of Mainz, John king of Bohemia, and the duke of Saxony, unanimously elected, in his presence, Charles, firstborn son of the aforesaid John, king of Bohemia, as king of the Romans. The archbishop of Cologne anointed him in the town of Bonn with great pomp, as is the custom. For they could not safely go down to Aachen, because the people of Aachen zealously followed Louis’s party. But Adolf, count palatine of the Rhine, Henry archbishop of Mainz, although deposed by the pope, the margrave of Brandenburg, the dukes of Bavaria, and almost all the cities of the empire followed and defended Louis’s party with all their strength until his death.
