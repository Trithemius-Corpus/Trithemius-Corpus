*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

...the vigor of morals, the purity of affections, the subtlety of understandings, the holiness of desires, the brightness of works, the eloquence of virtues, the dignity of merits, and the sublimity of rewards. Divine love is an unfailing treasure: whoever has it is rich; whoever does not have it, even if he possessed the riches of Croesus, is the poorest of all. God first loved us, not with a common love, but sweetly, wisely, and strongly.

Sweetly indeed, because he assumed human flesh for us; wisely, because he committed no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth; strongly, because for us he became obedient to the Father unto death, even death on a cross. Behold, virgin, Christ in his very self showed you beforehand the form of divine love which you must follow, if you wish to enter his bridal chamber. Love Christ Jesus himself sweetly, so that you desire nothing besides him; prudently, so that you are not deceived by the vanity of the flesh; love strongly, so that in the adversities of this world you are never cast down from your holy purpose. For there is need of these three columns of divine love, since whoever loves Christ sweetly loves him alone and desires nothing contrary to him, as Augustine says: “He loves you less, Lord, who loves anything together with you which he does not love for your sake.” Therefore you love sweetly if you love Jesus for no other thing than for himself, and indeed for him alone. For you know what he himself says in sacred Scripture: “I am the Lord your God, strong and jealous.” He alone wishes to be loved by you; he admits no partner, nor can love be sweet if it is shared among a diversity of objects. You should also love the Lord Jesus wisely, since he is the wisdom of God the Father, who wishes to be loved not only sweetly but also wisely; otherwise the spirit of error will very easily mock your zeal, when you have neglected the knowledge of a wise lover. For the cunning enemy has no more effective medicine for removing the love of Christ from a person’s heart than if he can bring it about that one walks in it without reason and incautiously. Therefore Scripture commands salt to be offered in every sacrifice, just as God made and ordered all things wisely. The manner of loving God is that you love him as much as you can, since the more you have loved him, the better it will be for the beloved.

Love your Lord Jesus strongly too, O virgin, that is, perseveringly, so that neither prosperity nor adversity may soften or turn your mind away from love of him. Jesus must be loved with the greatest fervor unto death, no less in adversity than in favorable circumstances, and not on account of any accessory thing, but for himself alone, because Jesus is the most worthy cause of love for him. Let the Lord Jesus be your portion, O cloistered virgin: nothing is to be cared for by you, nothing thought of, nothing loved except Jesus himself, to whom you have devoted yourself in mind and body, whose love is sweeter than all honey; whoever truly loves him desires nothing in this world except him. The love of Jesus is sufficient in all things for the one who loves; it suffers no want, neither yields to adversity nor is softened by prosperity; it is constant in itself, abundantly sufficient for itself, since inwardly in the Lord Jesus it possesses all things, requiring none of those things that seem to be outside. For whoever wishes to have Jesus as a friend necessarily despises all things. Therefore, as the cloistered virgin approaches the love of Christ, let her stand in fear and prepare her soul for temptation, since whoever seeks the friendship of Jesus should know that he must endure the enmities of many; and for this reason let him prepare himself for humble patience, lest he lose holy friendship before he perfectly attains it.

Indeed, when the soul has joined itself to the Word of God, let it know without doubt that it will at once have enemies, and that those whom it formerly had as friends will very often be turned into adversaries. Therefore one must persevere strongly in the love of God, and the spirit must be strengthened against whatever adversities occur, lest, placed in the middle of the road, it fail on the journey or, terrified by the magnitude of its labors, turn back. For if Christ Jesus, who had no sin, had to suffer and so enter into his glory, how much more must we wretched human beings, conceived, born, and brought up in sins, who by sinning daily make ourselves worse, necessarily endure many adversities; we who suffer by our own demerit whatever evil befalls us. If Scripture does not hesitate to recall the many tribulations of the just, let it seem wonderful to no one if we sinners, stripped of works of righteousness, though converted and penitent, endure many adversities.

Hence [unclear] says: “For patience is necessary for you, so that, doing the will of God, you may receive the promise.” It belongs to Christians, and especially to the cloistered, to overcome injuries by keeping silent and fleeing, rather than by answering or resisting; and the more the profession of monastic life binds us to perfection, so much less is it permitted for us to repel force by force, but rather we must endure whatever adversities with greater patience. We are Christians and cloistered persons, very greatly bound to humility; for this reason all grievous and bitter things must be borne patiently by us for the love of Christ. In order that I might prove myself a servant of Christ, though a useless one, behold how many things I have endured without deserving them, which I could have turned back upon my rivals,

*Hebrews 10.*
