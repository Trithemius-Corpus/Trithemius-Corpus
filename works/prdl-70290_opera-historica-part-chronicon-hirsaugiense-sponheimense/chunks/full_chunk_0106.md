This synod was presided over by Wezilo, archbishop of Mainz, successor of Bishop Siegfried, about whom we remember having already said more above; he pronounced Pope Gregory deposed. This Wezilo was a learned man, both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, sweet in speech and most eloquent, and in his time he was held to be so great a preacher of the word of God that he had no equal. William, the most holy abbot, summoned to this synod by royal letters, scorned to come, [unclear] by a subtle contrivance, preferring boldly to subject himself to dangers to his property and body in defense of the vicar of Christ rather than to be stained by agreement with the wicked.

In the same year Pope Gregory VII died at Salerno. He was first succeeded by canonical election by Desiderius, abbot of the monastery of Monte Cassino, of our order, who was called Victor III.

*1086.*

In the seventeenth year of Abbot William, which was the year 1086 from the Lord’s Nativity, King Henry, angry at Saxony and the country on every side, laid it waste with a great army by plunder and fire. Hermann, however, whom the Saxons, by order of Pope Gregory, had set up as king against Henry, gathered as large an army as possible and met him near Würzburg in the place called Bleichfeld; there, when battle was joined, an innumerable multitude on both sides [unclear]. Hermann obtained the victory and took the city of Würzburg; yet King Henry soon recovered it by the strong hand of his men.

In this same year the Carthusian order began to be founded in the diocese of Grenoble, which happened as follows. Saint Hugh, then bishop of Grenoble, saw in a dream that in the solitude of Chartreuse a dwelling was to be built for God by his favor, and also seven stars providing him guidance for the journey. And behold, drawn by the odor of his holiness, seven men came to him; all of them, kindled by one desire, were seeking a place suited to the eremitic life, but had not yet found it. The first of these was Bruno, born at Cologne, master in theology at Paris, a man most famous for religion and learning; the other four were men of letters and two were laymen. Saint Hugh received them graciously and made them attain their wish. For by his counsel, aid, and companionship, they entered the solitude of Chartreuse and built the dwellings necessary for their purpose. Around the same time flourished Stephen the Levite, a man most renowned for miracles, abstinence, and holiness; born in Auvergne, he migrated to the territory of Limoges and practiced the eremitic life for almost fifty years with the few who had gathered to him. From him the order of Grandmont began.

In this same year Marianus Scotus, monk of Fulda, a distinguished chronographer, about whom we made mention far above, died at Mainz, enclosed for the love of Christ, and was buried with due honor in the greater church of Saint Martin.

*1087.*

In the eighteenth year of Abbot William, which was the one thousand eighty-seventh year of the Lord, Pope Victor died after drinking poison in the sacred chalice; many report that this was done by the plots of King Henry and Wibert the antipope.

After him Otto, bishop of Ostia, was elected as true vicar of Christ, and was called Urban II; he sat twelve years and four months. He was first a cleric of the church of Reims, afterward became a monk of Cluny of our order, then prior of the same monastery, from prior bishop of Ostia, and finally from bishop was made pope.

Hermann, having laid aside the false name of king, returned to his own possessions by permission of the emperor; after a few days he was killed and died in the siege of a certain town.

The empress Bertha died and was buried at Mainz, but afterward she was transferred by the emperor to Speyer.

*1088.*

In the following year, which was the nineteenth of Abbot William and the year of the Lord 1088, Wezilo, archbishop of Mainz, died; Ruthard succeeded him. In this same year the city of Augsburg was taken by the treachery of the Swabians on the Lord’s Supper day, and on the second weekday after the feast of Easter it was destroyed by the same men.

At these times Adelbert the monk was illustrious in this monastery of Hirsau; under the father Saint William he carried out the office of prior energetically for not a few years. For he was a man adorned with every beauty of virtues, in holiness of conduct by no means degenerate from a good master [unclear].
