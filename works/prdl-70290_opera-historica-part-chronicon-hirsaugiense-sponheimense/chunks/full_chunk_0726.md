*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

... of the Wise Man, who says: “For a little with justice is better than many revenues with iniquity.” For wealth, just as I did not possess it, so I never loved it. I have been content with moderation; every extreme is excessive and full of hardship. But enough about these things. I am most eager to know how you are, what your talented sons are doing, whom I heard delivering speeches many years ago when they were still quite young, how far they have advanced in learning, and what they profess in the Athenaeum. For I think that with age they have become most learned, since already in childhood they flourished with such wonderful talent. The education of your sons undoubtedly redounds to your praise, since you trained them in ancestral virtues, performing the duty of the best of fathers in morals and of a most learned teacher in philosophical studies. May almighty God, through his most merciful kindness, deign to make you joyful and happy in the wisdom, prosperity, and safety of your sons, and to preserve you forever, mindful of us. From Würzburg, 20 July, in the year of Christ 1507.

*XXXVII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James the Greater the Apostle in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, to Johannes Gotfridi of Mandel, most vigilant pastor of the church: greetings and love.*

Whenever an opportunity is given me, I neither ought nor can restrain myself from writing to you, a friend truly most dear to me, to inform you of my circumstances. It is plainly most sweet to me to see letters from you often, and more frequently to send mine to you. My brother, Master Jacobus Trithemius, recently delivered to me your letter, when he was directing his journey from Trier through Sponheim to us. In it you lament that I am absent far away, and you declare that you are vehemently afflicted in your mind because I have utterly resigned the abbey of Sponheim. You assert that not only are you troubled and cast down in mind because of my resignation, but also that all the priests round about are lamentably saddened because I have deserted my place and departed into foreign lands. This was done after mature consultation with friends; nor does it repent me, nor, if it did repent me, could it be recalled. I thank you, and all my friends too, who grieve over the injuries done me. Repentance will not move me. Nor will it repent me to have resigned the monastery of Sponheim, if perhaps he should wish that he had not inflicted insult. For it is better for me to pass my life in peace of mind than, under a captive prince, to be driven by continual waves of disturbances. Nor do I greatly weigh the poverty of this monastery which I have taken up, since I know that at Sponheim I always lived in the utmost poverty. Nevertheless, as far as moderation requires, I have been sufficiently provided with all things necessary for the body, both for myself and for those who dwell with me; I seek no more. For no one can despise riches so easily as one who has always lived in moderation. For me, as a Christian philosopher and at the same time a monk, the richest thing is the mind, since I have always been content with my lot, and never either loved riches that I did not possess or desired to possess them.

That my rivals think that after my departure from Sponheim I am unknown to bishops and princes, I count as nothing; nor is there any need for them to know our fortunes, if any have been favorable, lest they waste away from greater envy on that account. But you, who are accustomed to rejoice in the peace and tranquillity of a friend, will understand most fully from the account of this brother, who has delivered these little letters of mine to you, how differently our affairs stand from the judgment of our rivals. For rivals are not to be cared for, nor will their envy disturb the uprightness of my mind. As for the fact that the officials of the neighboring princes round about detest the monks of Sponheim on my account, just as it does not benefit me, so it does not please me. For I understand well enough that this aversion of men will harm in no small degree that poor monastery, whose poverty I especially pity. I continually pray God that he may deign mercifully to provide for it in its necessities. You also, since you are neighbor to the place, I admonish, ask, and exhort with what love I can, that you not set great store by the injuries of the sons, which they inflicted on a father who did not deserve them, but rather attend to the place dedicated to God and to Saint Martin; whenever you see them oppressed by want, do not neglect, as far as you can, to bring help for the love of God. For they have been afflicted enough; they have paid heavy penalties for their rashness. The abbot who desired the abbey is infirm; he was not the last cause of my resignation, and I have heard that his appointment has not pleased everyone. I fear very much, though may the Almighty grant that it be in vain, that just as he secretly persecuted me, his pastor and abbot, so, God permitting, who is just, he himself will one day suffer open persecution from his own subjects.
