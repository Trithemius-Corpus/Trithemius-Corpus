This Adelger was born of his father Theodo and his mother Amalberga, citizens of Mainz, honorable and devoted to Christ. In his boyhood he was entrusted by those parents to letters in the city of Mainz. Through the diligence of his teachers, as he grew older, he made no small progress in writings, especially secular ones, and surpassed many of his contemporaries in conduct and knowledge. Then, when he was twenty years old, out of love of wisdom he betook himself to the University of Paris, then the most famous in the whole world, and, remaining there for seven years, made magnificent progress in every branch of knowledge, both divine and human. When the seven years had passed he returned to Mainz; because of the reputation of his learning he was appointed scholasticus there, where he received many talented young men as pupils. At length, however, after his parents had already died, seeing that many were going to perdition by the broken paths of vices, he abandoned the world with its vanities for the love of God, and coming here under Abbot Craffto he was made a monk. His way of life among the brothers was beyond reproach, humble and quiet, burdensome to no one, never troublesome to anyone. Finally, when he was appointed abbot, he took care with every effort and solicitude to show himself a useful shepherd to the brothers, and to instruct them in living well no less by example than by speech. For under his rule monastic discipline flourished here in praiseworthy fashion.

*MCLXXVI.*

In the year 1176, Abbot Adelger built the sacristy beside the high altar on the left, for greater convenience. It had previously been beside the northern apse, where the chapter room now is, placed between the refectory and the church.

In this same year died Clementia, a virgin nun in [Horreo Treverensi?], on 21 March, formerly the betrothed of Lord Craffto, count and second abbot of this monastery, daughter of the count of Hochenberg. This most devout virgin was a most fervent lover of Christ; intent day and night on prayer and contemplation, she desired nothing more than to please God in all things. Finally she departed, not without a great reputation for sanctity, in the forty-eighth year of her age, the twenty-seventh of her conversion to our order, in the ninth indiction. Buried in the church of St. Irmina.

*MCLXXVII.*

In the year 1177, Adelbero, the first cellarer of this community, formerly a monk of St. Alban’s at Mainz, who had been sent here with the first monks, died in this same year on 11 November, in the eighty-sixth year of his age. He held the office of cellarer vigorously for almost fifty-three years, and with great diligence and charity always applied the necessary care to domestic affairs.

Saint Hiltrudis also, a virgin and nun at Bingen, daughter of Count Megenhard of Sponheim, sister of our above-mentioned second abbot Craffto, in this same year on 17 November rested in the Lord; blessed Hildegard, her teacher, bore witness to her sanctity.

*MCLXXVIII.*

In the third year of his rule, Abbot Adelger sent Erenfrid, his monk, to Rome for the dispatching of certain business of this monastery. Returning from there, among other relics he brought back a notable particle of St. John the Baptist, which was received with great reverence and honor. This same Abbot Adelger, for the adornment of the church and reverence for the relics, had small reliquaries made: some of white ivory, others of wood overlaid with gilded bronze plates, which he filled and adorned with diverse relics of saints. Among the many volumes of sacred reading that he acquired for the use and benefit of the brothers, he also had the New and Old Testament copied in a beautiful script, in three large volumes, by the hand of Godefrid, a monk of this community, who was an excellent scribe and a most vigilant restorer of books of sacred scripture. In this same year the monastery of St. James on the fair mountain near Mainz, which some years before had been destroyed on account of the killing of Archbishop Arnold, began to be rebuilt, with the permission of Emperor Frederick I and the assistance of Archbishop Christian. Archbishop Liutpold of Mainz founded this community in the year of the Lord 1053.

*MCLXXIX.*

Anselm of Bickelnheim, kinsman of St. Hildegard, the second prior of this monastery, died on 21 December, in the forty-third year of his priorate. He was among the first twelve sent here from the monastery of St. James, as was said above. For he was a good man and a notable lover of regular discipline. At his death he left after him in this community twenty monks well instructed. He increased the library to no small degree and trained very many excellent scribes.
