*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

... he knew [them] to be incorrigible, he resigned the abbacy after he had governed it for some years, preferring to remain in humility with his Hirsau brothers as one cast down than to preside fruitlessly in an abbacy over reprobate monks. Having therefore left the abbacy of the aforesaid Gotzaugian monastery, he returned to Hirsau and, received by the brothers with great charity, obtained the place of prior and continued among them a tranquil life, as he had been accustomed before, without reproach.

Afterwards, however, at the monastery of Schonrem, by the counsel and assent of the brothers of this community, he was appointed supreme prior and rector by Abbot Volmar; there he presided for almost ten years with sufficient usefulness, and enlarged that priory no less in spiritual than in temporal matters.

At length, after the death of Abbot Manegold, since no one more useful than he was found among the brothers at Hirsau, by common counsel and the unanimous consent of all he was elected abbot of this monastery in the aforesaid year of Christ’s nativity 1165, in the fourth indiction, on the Nones of August, and he presided, as we have said, for eleven years. In the observance of regular discipline he was quite zealous and fervent; but because the days were evil, and it was necessary to redeem the time, he governed the brothers not as he wished, but as he could. For already then they had begun to grow lukewarm from the fervor of their first institution, nor was it easy to call back all at once those who were slipping away too much. For this reason he received many injuries from weak subjects. Therefore, compelled by necessity, lest from bad they become worse, he kept silent and, with prudent moderation, as far as he was permitted, passed things over for the better course. *[The monastic profession?] had grown lukewarm at this time.*

As for the state of temporal affairs, he did not diminish the substance of the monastery, but enlarged it; and besides the many donations made by the faithful in his time, which are contained in the Book of Donations, he himself also acquired not a few estates and meadows for the use of the brothers. This Abbot Rupert also built, in the greater monastery of Hirsau, on the northern side, a chapel with beautiful woodwork in honor of St. Nicholas, the great archbishop of Myra, in which he was accustomed rather often to celebrate the solemnities of Mass. *The chapel of St. Nicholas is founded.*

This chapel, after 278 years, Abbot Wolffram, since it had almost collapsed from excessive age, destroyed with the consent and permission of Reinhard, bishop of Speyer; and transferring that altar itself into the church of the monastery, to the left side of the altar of the Holy Cross, he consecrated it in honor of Saints Peter the Apostle, Nicholas, Ulrich, and Aurelius, bishops, and Gall, abbot, through the ministry of Peter, bishop of Myra, vicar-general in pontificals of the aforesaid prelate Reinhard, as we shall say in its place.

In the course of the first year of Abbot Rupert, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 1162, *St. Elisabeth, nun*, the holy and God-beloved virgin Elisabeth, a nun of our order, mistress of the virgins of Christ at Schönau in the diocese of Trier, sister of Eckbert, abbot there in the monastery of monks, of whom we made mention above, in the thirty-sixth year of her age, the twenty-fourth of her conversion to the order, and the thirteenth of the visitation of heavenly grace, with the Lord calling, [unclear: of Saints Peter the Apostle, Nicholas, Ulrich, and Aurelius, bishops, and Gall, abbot, through the ministry of Peter, bishop of Myra, the aforesaid vicar-general in pontificals of Bishop Reinhard, consecrated it], as we say in its place.

For in the eleventh year of her religious life, which was the twenty-third of her age, [unclear], she was buried in the monastery of men, there in the principal church toward the north [unclear]. Her life, manner of life, miracles, and revelations her brother, the aforesaid Abbot Eckbert, described for the edification of many. This virgin, when she was twelve years old, was given to the monastery of the poor virgins of Christ at Schönau, to the Lord God under the rule of our holy father Benedict and under the direction of Hildelin, first abbot and founder of both [monasteries?]; [unclear] angelic [visitations?], and, divinely illumined, to reveal the secrets of minds. And for as long as she lived thereafter, she was refreshed by divine revelations, and this especially on Sundays and on the feasts of saints; she had been given there, where she lived so holy, so pure and religious a life that she deserved more often to be divinely consoled in this mortality by many wondrous revelations.

For the [manner?] of the visions, or revelations, was this. A certain suffering of the breast came upon her, so that, having suddenly fallen in the church, she would remain as if lifeless, lying quietly or sitting motionless, and no breath could be perceived in her. While this rapture lasted, her spirit was carried away to various places, and in the meantime she was instructed by angelic speech. At length, after a long rapture, having recovered her spirit, she would utter most holy words, sometimes in the Teutonic tongue, which was her native language, and sometimes in Latin, although she had almost no [knowledge] of the Latin language
