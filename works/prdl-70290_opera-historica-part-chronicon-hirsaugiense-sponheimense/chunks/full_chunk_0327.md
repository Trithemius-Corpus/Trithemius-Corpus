*Chronicle of Trithemius. 1156.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Craffto, on the twelfth day before the Kalends of November, the hospital of the poor, with its attached chapel, into which the right of the parish church had long before been transferred from Dalen, was consumed by fire and wholly perished. Therefore the parish church was again transferred to that place adjoining the monastery, where even today it is seen, separated from all human dwellings, so that it cannot easily perish by fire. For the first parish church was in Dalen from the time of St. Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, for almost 278 years. And then, under the elder Archbishop Adelbert, at the instance of Count Megenhard, the right of the parishioners was transferred into the chapel of the hospital, which had been situated toward the south near the monastery. Finally, when in the aforesaid year of Craffto that church with the hospital had been burned, a new church began to be built on the slope of the mountain beside the monastery, into which the entire right of the mother church was transferred. But the hospital was not rebuilt, both because of the monastery and because Count Gottfried seemed at that time to care little about the support of the poor. The graves also of the dead, with their bones for the greater part, were first transferred from Dalen to the hospital, and then to the new cemetery; although in both places many remained which at that time were not found. For often meanwhile, both in Dalen and in the place of the hospital, bones of the dead are found in digging and ploughing.

In this year Hermann, count palatine of the Rhine, etc., died.

*1160.*

In this year, which was the ninth of Abbot Craffto, on the third day before the Nones of November, in the [unclear] indiction, the parish church at the foot of the mountain around the monastery, newly built, of which we have already spoken, was consecrated by Bishop Henry, vicar of Arnold, archbishop of Mainz, in honor of the holy and undivided Trinity, the Holy Cross, and St. Giles, abbot and confessor. He enclosed in the altar relics of St. Vitus, of St. Maurice, of the holy Moors, of St. Alexandra the martyr, of St. Egricius, archbishop of Trier, of St. Boniface the bishop, of St. Emerentiana the virgin, of St. Irmina the virgin, and of the eleven thousand virgins. But the baptistery had from the beginning been placed in the church of the monastery, and for that reason another was not built in the parish church, because the one that existed before could suffice for so large a parish family.

*1163.*

At this time, for nine continuous years, the bodies of the holy eleven thousand virgins and of the other saints who suffered with them were found in the suburb of the city of Cologne and raised from the earth; some of these were found even without names. From them St. Elisabeth, a nun and mistress in Schönau, learned, by angelic revelation, the names, merits, and ranks of many, and wrote to Gerlacus, abbot of the monastery of Deutz, of our order.

*1168.*

In the thirteenth year of Craffto, abbot of this monastery, St. Elisabeth, a virgin nun of our order and mistress in Schönau, passed from this world to the Lord Jesus, whom she had always loved with a pure heart, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of July, in the thirty-sixth year of her age, and in the [unclear] of the visitation of the heavenly light, in holy revelations, on Friday at the ninth hour in the morning. This holy virgin, in the twenty-third year of her age, began to be consoled by angelic visitation and address, and thereafter had many revelations from God, which she arranged in part in the German tongue. But her brother, a monk in Schönau and afterward abbot, reduced all the writings and revelations of his holy sister into the more ornate style and into the form in which they are now read. These two precious lights of the world were at one and the same time: Hildegard at Bingen, on the mount of St. Rupert, and Elisabeth in Schönau; both, often visited by divine revelations, brought many secrets, according to the will of God, to the knowledge of men.

*1167.*

Craffto the abbot, in his sixteenth year, obtained from Pope Alexander III, through the mediation of a certain Cardinal Benedict, a large particle of the wood of the Lord’s Cross, which he adorned for this monastery with gold and gems. Finally, he adorned our church quite well with jewels, crosses, relics, and ornaments, all of which, however, failed with time, except for a precious mitre, which, although under many abbots it was often pawned, nevertheless in time, God willing, was brought back to the monastery. This mitre Craffto himself, in the first year of his promotion, caused to be made of pure silver, gilded on top, throughout with pearls and precious stones, many of which have been removed, with the help and, for a great part, at the expense of his mother.
