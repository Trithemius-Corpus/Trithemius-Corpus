For in his time many people, for the love of God, handed over many goods to this monastery, and he himself also bought several things for a price. Among others, moreover, there was at that time a certain canon named Ruthardus, who had a brother in Hirsau, a monk, [and was] resident in the church of Mainz; for the remedy of his soul he handed over to the monastery of Hirsau an estate at Bingen, which his brother the monk, by order of the abbot, sold for [unclear] marks of silver, and spent the price for the use of the brothers.

After some time, however, Hedericus, nephew through his brother of the aforesaid Ruthardus, himself also a canon of Mainz, conferred twelve talents on this monastery; for these, Hugo his paternal uncle, at the request of Abbot Manegold, bought three yokes of vineyards in Gruningen for the monastery.

Rupertus also of Tubingen [gave] many goods to the monastery of Hirsau in the time of the abbess [unclear], and also to co-purchasers and others of the convent, of accounts and of others for the common matter. But that benefits of this sort are not as if by the authority of the kingdom or empire, but by the authority of the monastery itself; nevertheless they are held in this way, as if they had been by the authority of the Kingdom or Empire, and ought to pertain to it. And therefore it is said that this is the donation of the monastery of Saint George in Tubingen, which Johannes, bishop in Tubingen, had made. And for that reason it is said that this monastery is now in the possession and hand of the church of Saint George in Tubingen, which Johannes, bishop in Tubingen, had held, who founded this monastery. And all men who have been benefited from this donation shall have this monastery, just as also all men who have been benefited from the donation of Johannes, bishop in Tubingen.

In the first year of Abbot Manegold, that is, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1157, there was a great earthquake in Asia in various places, which utterly destroyed Tripoli, Damascus, and a certain other city. Moreover the city of the Catanensians was completely overturned; in it almost up to twenty thousand of a very great multitude of men, women, young people, and children perished, suffocated in a miserable disaster. In these days Sophia of Holland, wife of Theodoric, count of Holland, flourished: a holy woman and most devoted in the love of Christ, continually intent on prayers, fasts, vigils, and almsgiving; she is said to have been illustrious for miracles both in life and after death.

When this holy woman, about to visit the threshold of the holy apostle James in Galicia, had fallen among cruel robbers, who wished not only to despoil her of her jewels but even to deprive her of life, she so constrained and bound them by the power of her prayers that they could in no way move from the place or lay hands upon her. Therefore, astonished, they sought pardon from her; having received it, they suddenly fled away. At last, however, after many works of piety, when she had already set out in terror to visit the holy sepulcher of Christ at Jerusalem, and had most devoutly visited the sacred places, on 26 September she happily rested in the Lord; and she was buried in the hospital of the Teutons in Jerusalem.

In the second year of Abbot Manegold, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1158, Emperor Frederick, with the princes of the German kingdom, around the feast of Pentecost entered Lombardy with a strong hand, and took the city of Brescia with many others. Moving his army from there, with fifty thousand fighting men he besieged Milan, and assaulted it fiercely for many days. And when the Milanese saw themselves surrounded by a tight siege, and could expect no help from anyone, they handed the city over to the emperor. When this had been done, the emperor sent his German soldiers back to their homeland for the most part, and imposed on the defeated Milanese an annual payment of tribute.

In the same year Godfridus, abbot of Saint James of Mainz, and Burchardus, provost of Saint Peter’s, together with almost the whole clergy and the citizens of Mainz, began to hold their archbishop in great hatred, and petitioned the emperor against him. Only the Creopoli, whom they call butchers, defended the archbishop’s side with all their strength; whence both by Emperor Frederick and by the archbishop of Mainz, who succeeded Arnold, they were granted a certain singular liberty and privilege, down to the present day, for such great fidelity.
