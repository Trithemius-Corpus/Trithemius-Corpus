Taking boldness, therefore, from the true miracles of Christ, they devised similar things about their own teacher, promising themselves no less belief among the people than they saw believers had given to the holy preachers, whom they despised as simple and unlearned. Hearing that Christ, led from the midst of the Jews to the precipice, passed through untouched, they fable that their Apollonius vanished suddenly from Caesar’s sight and was made far outside the city. Likewise, the Lord Jesus recalled three dead persons to life in the mystery of baptism; they lie that Apollonius raised a dead girl at Rome. Over the baptized Lord the Father’s voice was heard; Apollonius is said to have been addressed by Hercules and the other gods of the Gentiles. If this is true, they were necessarily phantoms of demons; if it is not true, Damis, certainly, or Apollonius himself, invented this as he did many other things. Likewise, after his resurrection the Lord Jesus entered to his disciples though the doors were closed; Damis lies that Apollonius, bound in fetters at Rome, made himself free as often as he wished.

It would be far too long and tedious to hear if I wished to bring back to the pen all the miracles which Philostratus narrates concerning Apollonius; no reasoning supports how he could have performed them, because it is manifest that he was a mortal man and a worshipper of idols, and that he often held the pernicious error of many things concerning the transmigration of souls into bodies. He introduces a noble young man among Hiarchas and the wise men of India, slandering letters and all philosophy out of hatred of Homer, because, although he had once been a high-souled commander in the Trojan war, through Homer’s envy he had received no remembrance among the illustrious. Then, therefore, the man, clothed again [in a body?] and mindful of the former injury, assails all writers with one obstinate judgment. What is more insane than this folly: that a man should remember what he had been twelve hundred years before, for they report that so much time elapsed between the destruction of Troy and the age of this Apollonius of Tyana, when he cannot know where, or what, or how he was before he came forth from his mother’s womb? Or how will this fable be able to have even according to the Pythagoreans and Platonists any likeness of truth, who place very many years for transmigration’s return to the first point? But, since shame forbids me to speak longer about these most vain ravings, which are vainer than dreams, I ought not do so; rather, the remaining matters must be pursued in brief narration.

Such is that manifest fiction of Damis, contrary to every reason of nature, in which, in the meeting of Apollonius with the Indian philosophers, with Hiarchas presiding, he introduces miracles unheard of and nowhere seen: that the seats of the philosophers burst forth from the earth; that the sun supplied perpetual fire for the sacrifices; that a bowl, often drained by the philosophers with a little cup, again suddenly sweated forth abundant full liquid, so that as often as it had been drunk up, so often it would again overflow full. I pass over the other things devised with similar presumption: singers raised on high, demons expelled from men by letter, predictions of the future, declarations of hidden names and things, the interpreted twittering of swallows and birds, the exposed crimes of disguised specters. Whatever miraculous thing his followers wrote concerning Apollonius, in a few words I shall disclose to you, most reverend father, as you command, my opinion concerning the miracles of the Tyanean.

The miracles, signs, and prodigies which in the eight volumes of Philostratus are read as ascribed to Apollonius, in my opinion, firmly and without doubt to be held, are either fabricated and false, or, if they did occur, were perpetrated without any hesitation by the cooperation of demons. That they are fabricated, lying, and false is strongly persuasive to me from Damis himself, the disciple of Apollonius, who, as often as he writes something great, difficult, and miraculous about him, always introduces his fellow disciples as absent, and himself either alone or present with very few; and more often he records that no one was with Apollonius when he did great things or spoke with specters. But if the persuasion mentioned should not sufficiently win trust for my words, and someone should wish to defend the miracles of the Tyanean, whoever he may be, let him tell me first: if Apollonius performed the miracles ascribed to him, by whose power could he have done them? By his own, namely, or another’s? Certainly not by his own; nor had he obtained any natural science for performing them, since the power of natural magic by no means extends itself to most such things as are narrated about him. For Apollonius was a corruptible and mortal man, who must be believed to have been able to do nothing by his own power beyond the common order of nature that deserves the name of miracle. It follows, therefore, that if he did anything of this kind, it was performed not by his own power, but by another’s. But true miracles are performed by divine power alone, and the Tyanean could in no way have done these, since, being a worshipper of idols and full of much superstition, he was far removed from knowledge of the true God.
