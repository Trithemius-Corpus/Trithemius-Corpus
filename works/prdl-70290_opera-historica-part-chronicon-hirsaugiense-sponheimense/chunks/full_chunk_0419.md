*Sponheim.*

They received the king of the Romans with reverence. When Charles learned this, he gathered a great army and advanced against Gunther; but, seeing him stronger than himself, through the efforts of Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, he drew the duke of Bavaria over to his side. Although they were joined by blood, nevertheless he married his daughter. Then, after three months, King Gunther returned to Frankfurt and began to fall ill; seeing that he had been deceived, he began to negotiate an agreement with Charles, who was then present, through the mediation of the margrave of Brandenburg. Having received two cities in Thuringia and two thousand two hundred marks of silver, he resigned the royal seal, the royal insignia, and the kingdom into the hands of the aforesaid Charles before the princes.

There was a report at that time that Gunther had been poisoned; for, having received a cup from the physician, he is said to have said to that physician: “Master, if it is good afterward, you drink first, and I shall drink afterward.” When the physician, compelled by necessity, had drunk first, the king drank afterward. On the third day the physician died; the king, made more ill by swelling of the body, followed him after the space of a month, while still alive [unclear].

Therefore Charles IV, elected king of the Romans in the year of the Lord 1346, as we have said, obtained the empire peacefully after Gunther’s death. He ruled for thirty-one years, a good, wise, learned, and magnificent man, perfectly endowed with knowledge and skill in five languages. He had four wives. The first was [the daughter?] of Rudolf, count palatine, brother of Adolf; she died without children. The second was from France; when she also had died without children, he took a third, the daughter of the prince of Sweden, by whom he begot a daughter, whom he gave to the margrave [unclear], afterward king of the Romans. The fourth wife was Elizabeth, daughter of the duke of Stettin in Pomerania, by whom he begot Sigismund, king of Hungary, and John the duke.

In the same year, on the vigil of the Assumption of blessed Mary ever virgin, Walram, archbishop of Cologne, died. William, a canon there, succeeding him by apostolic provision, presided for twelve years and nine months. He ruled well, freed the church from many debts, and redeemed everything that had been pledged.

In the aforesaid year 1349, William, abbot of this monastery, because of the devotion which he had toward the most blessed Mary, mother of God, erected in her honor a new chapel from the foundations in the circuit of the cloister. He completed it in that same year and had it consecrated in the following year, as we shall say there.

In that year also there arose in Hungary a certain new sect of Flagellants, which very quickly stained all Germany with its error. For men went about naked, males and females together, with head covered by hood or cap, and their private parts covered with a linen veil, carrying crosses in their hands and scourges with knots at the tips, to which spikes were fastened. With these, in church and outside it, under the appearance of penance, which they imposed on themselves without the judgment of priests, they scourged themselves, singing certain songs mostly contrary to the Christian faith, very often prostrating themselves on the ground in the form of a cross, praying, crying out, and striking themselves to the shedding of blood. When the ignorant populace began to admire and praise this pernicious novelty as holy and pleasing to God, and many began to imitate it, the bishops of the churches, fearing that the error would grow immeasurably if they dissembled, brought the matter to the examination of the apostolic see. By command of Pope Clement VI, all were either recalled to public penance or handed over to secular judgment and perished by fire. Thus that heresy, refuted in a short time, ceased.

In the same year also the human race was scourged by a threefold plague. For first there was a mournful and pitiable [plague], almost throughout the whole world, beginning from India and everywhere creeping through all the provinces, over the space of three years as far as England; everywhere on earth [unclear] there was no place in all Europe where that contagious calamity did not rage. The second plague was hemorrhoid, that is, a flow of blood, in which also many thousands of people, especially in Germany, perished. The third was that which was called by the name of sacred fire, which consumed the bodies of people, both dead and living, down to the bone. So savage was that calamity that it even consumed the bodies of the buried dead more swiftly in themselves. These times were full of calamity and misery and exposed to such great dangers that from the origin of the world, apart from the universal flood, we do not read [unclear].
