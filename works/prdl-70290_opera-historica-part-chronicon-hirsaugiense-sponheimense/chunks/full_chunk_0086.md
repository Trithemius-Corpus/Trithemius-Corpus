Pope Gregory VII delivered this privilege of the liberty that had been instituted to Abbot Wilhelm; a copy of it, written out again by his own hand, he deposited in the chest of St. Peter, as testimony to all the aforesaid things and as a perpetual memorial of the liberty.

Finally, out of the exceeding affection of charity with which the apostolic man embraced the abbot, and as a sign of love, he bestowed upon him some relics of saints, which he carried with reverence to the monastery. While the blessed father was staying for some time in the same city of Rome, he fell into a very grave illness from swelling of the belly, which grew so severe that even the most experienced physicians despaired of him.

At length he ordered himself to be carried to the church of Blessed Mary ever Virgin; and, placing his wounded belly at the altar, he implored the aid of that same advocate of the poor with most earnest supplication. And suddenly, amid the words of prayer, he began to recover, and, restored in a short time to his former health, and the business for which he had come to the curia having been completed, he happily returned to Hirsau, with the Lord accompanying him. His brothers received him with great joy and gave thanks to almighty God through him, both for the safety of their father and for the privileges obtained from the Apostolic See.

When these things had thus been accomplished, the most reverend Abbot Wilhelm, since he believed that adequate provision had been made for that monastery, its brothers, and the possessions acquired and legitimately to be acquired in future, by a sufficient privilege of liberty and immunity, from then on devoted all his zeal to winning the souls of many for Christ. And indeed in a short time the possessions of the monastery, by God’s bounty, were notably enlarged under him through the offerings of the faithful, so that from these he could maintain a plentiful number of monks without much burden.

He therefore gathered together all who wished to serve God in truth, noble and low-born, rich and poor, learned and unlearned, without regard to persons; and although the monastery had been endowed by Count Adelbert at the beginning for fifteen monks, he himself [unclear] one hundred fifty [unclear] under Wilhelm. *Number of monks under him was 124.*

Hence, since the site of the monastery of St. Aurelius seemed too narrow for receiving so great a multitude, he built a new monastery across the bridge with stone flooring, of which we shall speak later. But now, after we have recorded the consecration, endowment, liberty, and the other privileges of the monastery obtained through Abbot Wilhelm, together with the foundation by Count Adelbert, let us resume the order of the history, long interrupted; and, beginning from the first year of Wilhelm, let us touch more briefly upon the sequence of times and the more noteworthy things done in Germany, especially concerning the order, in such a way, namely, that while attending to matters outside, we nevertheless do not neglect the abbot’s deeds and the growth of the monastery under him.

Therefore, in the first year of Wilhelm, Peter Damian, cardinal bishop of the holy Roman church from our order, of whom we made mention much earlier above, was sent by Pope Alexander II as legate to King Henry in Germany, and at [Francen-] [unclear] expounded with great [unclear] and wisdom.

In the same year Megimward, abbot of Hildesheim, took up the abbey of Augia, an entrance to it having been opened by a great largesse of money. For in these times in which Henry IV was reigning, bishoprics, abbeys, provostships, and the other ecclesiastical dignities were not conferred upon the worthy by election; and the simoniacal heresy stained almost the whole Teutonic kingdom with its depravity. From this many evils arose in Germany, because the king, on account of this and many other matters, was excommunicated by Christ’s vicar, was rebellious and contumacious toward the supreme pontiff, and persecuted all who obeyed him even unto death.

In the following year, which was the year 1070 of the Lord’s Nativity, Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, Hildolf, archbishop of Cologne, and Hermann, bishop of Bamberg, summoned by Pope Alexander, came to Rome. There the bishop of Bamberg was accused of having invaded the episcopate through the simoniacal heresy, with money having been given; yet he pleased the Roman pontiff so much with gifts that he returned from him to his homeland not only unpunished, but even rewarded with the pallium, as though an archbishop. Adelbero also, bishop of Worms, made from a monk of St. Gall, as we said above, died in this same year, suffocated, as they report, by his own fatness; Adelbert succeeded him.
