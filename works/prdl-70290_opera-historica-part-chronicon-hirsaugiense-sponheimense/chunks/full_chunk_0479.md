First, that from then on he should not continue his residence outside the monastery, but rather should remain with his brothers in the monastery, intent on his own salvation and theirs, especially now that the count was dead, by whose service he was no longer being pressed. The first of these two things he did; the other was not within his power to do, although he often attempted it. For, on account of the privileges long shut up in the vault of the castle of Trarbach, to which he himself could not have access, because of the impeded execution of the dead count's testament and his own captivity and detention in Winterburg, he conceived a hostile mind toward Duke Stephen; and, on the advice of legal experts, attacking him judicially, first through the commissioner of the Council of Basel and finally through the most clement prince Louis, Count Palatine, he began to compel him to restitution and satisfaction. But meanwhile, being carried off by death, he left the undertaking begun but unfinished, and nothing further was done.

*1439.*

In the seventh year of our abbot Gobelin, Pope Eugene, in order to disperse the Council of Basel, stirred Louis, Dauphin of Vienne, the first-born of the king of France, to arms, by entreaties and by money, as was reported, against the Roman Empire and the German nation. Having gathered a great army of twenty-five thousand armed men, he came into Alsace. Around Basel, Strasbourg, and the surrounding cities they committed many burnings, plunderings, and damages in villages and fields. They had a very fierce war with the Swiss not far from Basel, in which they lost no fewer of their own than they had killed of the others. These things were done in the month of March. At length Louis, Count Palatine, with the aid of the neighboring princes, of the cities of the empire, especially the people of Strasbourg, Basel, and Schlettstadt, powerfully drove out of Alsace that troop, which was called the Armagnacs, together with their prince the Dauphin, many of them having been killed, so that out of that whole multitude of twenty-five thousand very few escaped. After this great slaughter of the Armagnacs in Alsace, plague, famine, and dearth followed in all the regions on this side of the Rhine; many thousands died.

In the same year the Council of Basel issued one bull for the reform of our order in Germany, which begins thus: “Among the manifold cares by which, amid the varieties of matters and business that arise, we are distracted for the universal Church,” etc. Given at Basel, on the tenth day before the Kalends of March, in the year from the Nativity of the Lord 1439. In this act of the council, twelve abbots of our order were appointed visitors and reformers of all the monasteries of our order throughout Germany, especially throughout the province of Mainz. Among them was one John, abbot of St. Matthias near Trier, who in this year visited here for the third time, supported by the authority of the aforesaid council. Yet in the end he made little progress, because the monks, accustomed to depraved morals, cared little for the words of the one admonishing them, since, born and brought up in deformity, so to speak, they did not wish to live otherwise than they had learned. For excessive poverty and lack of temporal goods had led them to despair of spiritual life, whereby they believed themselves unable to do what they easily could have done.

In the same year, on the twenty-sixth day of the month of April, which was Jubilate Sunday, the tenth provincial chapter of our order was held at Nuremberg, in the monastery of St. Giles, over which Hartung of St. Peter’s in Erfurt, Nicholas of St. Basil’s in the Black Forest, and Henry Amerbach, abbots of monasteries of our order, presided. In this provincial chapter the bull of the Council of Basel written above, issued for the reform of our order throughout Germany with much maturity, as we have said, was presented to the fathers. Receiving it with reverence, the fathers commanded that it be observed by all.

In the year also noted above, Raban of Helmstatt, archbishop of Trier, considering that his decrepit age, exhausted in strength, was not equal to the burdens of so great a pontificate, after taking counsel with his own men, especially with his coadjutor John, bishop of Liège, resigned that archbishopric of Trier, with the consent of the whole chapter, to Jacob of Sierck, who before him had been elected in the dispute with Ulrich of Manderscheid, as we said in its place. To obtain quiet he returned to his church of Speyer, which in the meantime he had held in commendam. But soon he also resigned the bishopric of Speyer in favor of Reinhard of Helmstatt, provost of the greater church, to whom the pope had made provision for that same bishopric, with the consent of the chapter of Speyer. And thus Reinhard, confirmed as bishop of the church of Speyer, presided for nearly seventeen years in tranquillity.
