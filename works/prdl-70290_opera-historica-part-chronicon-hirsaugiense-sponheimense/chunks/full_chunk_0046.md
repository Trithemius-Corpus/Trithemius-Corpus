*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

... a little book *On the Calamity and Desolation of the Fatherland*, another little book, and certain other things, as Meginfrid reports.

In this year Theodoric, archbishop of Trier, died. Under him the canons of the major church there, having cast aside the regular life which up to this time their predecessors had maintained in the same church, ceased to be regulars and became secular in name and in manner of life. By their evil example the canons also of St. Paulinus at Trier, of St. Castor in Koblenz, those of Mainz and Speyer, and those of very many other churches, at different times indeed but with one spirit of impiety, cast aside the community of the regular life.

In this same year Rupert, archbishop of Mainz, successor of Hatto II, also died. Willigis, a good man and an outstanding worshipper of God, succeeded him. He built the churches of St. Martin the bishop, which is called the major church, and of St. Stephen the protomartyr, in which he was also buried. He also enlarged the monastery of St. Victor outside Mainz, and did many good things for many churches and monasteries.

In these times Robert, king of France, flourished, a just, learned, pious, and religious man, a lover and defender of monks, clerics, and all learned men. Among other little works of his talent he composed that sequence on the Holy Spirit which we sing in our order at Mass on the second and third day of Pentecost, which begins: “Come, Holy Spirit, and send from heaven the ray of your light.” He also published the responsory “Judea and Jerusalem,” and that one concerning St. Peter the apostle, “Cornelius the centurion,” along with many others. He was of such great religion that, enrolled in the catalogue of the saints, he shone with many miracles.

At the same time Æthelwold, a monk of Glastonbury, disciple of St. Dunstan, first abbot of Abingdon and then bishop of Winchester, flourished in England, a learned man and distinguished both in life and in learning. The teacher of this Æthelwold was, as we have said, St. Dunstan, a monk of our order, then abbot of Glastonbury, afterwards bishop of Winchester before him, and finally archbishop of Canterbury, a man of most holy life and distinguished by the radiance of miracles. His feast day is observed on the fourteenth day before the Kalends [of June?], on which he also died, in the thirty-third year of his pontificate, the seventieth of his age, and the year 988 of the Lord’s nativity.

*980.*

In the twenty-eighth year of Abbot Sigerus, which was the year 980 of the Lord’s nativity, Adalbert died, formerly a monk of the monastery of St. Maximin at Trier of our order, the first archbishop of Magdeburg in Saxony, from the pain of the stone, in the thirteenth year and eighth month of his episcopate, and in the [unclear] indiction, a devout and very learned man.

After his death, Otheric, a monk of Corvey, was elected, a holy man and most learned both in the divine scriptures and in secular letters. For some time after Windichinus he had charge of the monastic school in the aforesaid monastery. He consented unwillingly to the election made of him, and prayed the Lord not to permit him to become bishop; and this he obtained. For on the journey to the Roman curia for confirmation, he died, not without a reputation for sanctity.

In the thirtieth year of Abbot Sigerus, Emperor Otto II waged a dangerous war in Calabria. In it Henry, bishop of Augsburg, was killed with many other bishops, Duke Otto with many nobles, and Werner, abbot of Fulda, with many others; and the emperor barely escaped by swimming.

In these times Adalbert, formerly a monk of the monastery of Fulda and bishop of Prague in Bohemia, flourished, a learned and holy man and an outstanding propagator of the Catholic faith. After he had undergone many labors for Christ, he was crowned with martyrdom by the Prussians for the Christian faith and enrolled in the catalogue of the saints. His feast day is observed on the ninth day before the Kalends of May. Oswald also, formerly a monk of Fleury, a monastery of our order, archbishop of York, a [learned?] man [unclear].

*982.*

In the year of the Lord’s nativity 982, on the Kalends of September, Sigerus, abbot of this monastery, in the thirtieth year of the rule he had undertaken, [died]. He was buried before the altar of the holy father Aurelius, a devout and religious man; down to St. William no one like him succeeded in the abbacy of Hirsau. Lupoldus is recorded [unclear].
