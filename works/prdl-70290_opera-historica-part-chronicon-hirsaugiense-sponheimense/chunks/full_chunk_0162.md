*126 Monastery of Hirsau*

In these times also the convent of nuns of our order at Lobenfeld, in the same diocese of Worms, in the district called Kreichau, about two miles from Heidelberg, was founded through the work and at the expense of the aforesaid bishop Buggo, and was sufficiently endowed for the support of the virgins of Christ. Around these times too the count of Leiningen, named [Emicho?], founded a monastery of brethren, canons under the rule of Saint Augustine, in the thickets, commonly called Heyna or Hegina, in honor of Saint Peter, prince of the apostles, and enriched it with many goods and revenues. Its church the often-mentioned bishop Buggo, in whose diocese the monastery was built, consecrated in the year of the Lord 1122, and appointed Hartung as first provost; he first instituted there the life of regular canons.

In the fifteenth year of his rule, Abbot Volmar, provoked by the insolence of certain secular ministeriales of his, went to the emperor Lothar while he was celebrating the Nativity of the Lord at Speyer, and called upon him as helper and defender of his monastery of Hirsau and of the brethren and the dependents of the same. He at once ordered the disturbers of holy religion to be brought before him; such great fear seized them that, while Volmar lived, no one presumed to trouble the men of Hirsau.

*1136.*

In this same year, which was the 1136th from the Lord’s Nativity, on the second day before the Kalends of January, there died, of holy memory, Jutta, a most devout virgin of Christ, sister of Count Meginhard of Spanheim, founder of St. Disibod, who, enclosed with certain other noble holy virgins, led an eremitic life. She was enclosed in the year of the Lord 1112, in the fifth indiction; three others were with her, namely the holy virgin Hildegard, who later, as we shall say, founded a monastery of nuns on Mount Rupert, in that place where the Nahe flows into the Rhine very near the city of Bingen; Jutta, the daughter of her brother; and another virgin of the same name. The number of virgins under her instruction increased. As long as she lived, St. Hildegard did not withdraw from her side. This holy woman, among other miracles of her holiness, changed water into wine and crossed the waters of the river Glan with dry feet.

She was buried in the aforesaid monastery of St. Disibod, which at that time was of our order, in which she herself, holy woman, had taken the habit of conversion. Her life, conduct, deeds, and miracles are found written down.

In the same year, on the fifth day before the Kalends of November, there was a very great wind, which overturned many towers and buildings. The sea also, having gone beyond its bounds, suddenly and unexpectedly submerged a great part of Flanders together with its inhabitants.

In the same year, around the feast of the Assumption of Blessed Mary ever Virgin, the emperor Lothar launched an expedition into Italy against Roger, prince of Apulia, at the urging of Pope Innocent II.

*1137.*

In the sixteenth year of Abbot Volmar, which was the 1137th from the Lord’s Nativity, the emperor celebrated the Nativity of the Lord and Easter in Italy, but Pentecost at Bari with a general assembly of the Italian princes. There Bruno II, archbishop of Cologne, died and was honorably buried in the church of Saint Nicholas. Hugo, dean of St. Peter’s at Cologne, succeeded him by [unclear] command of the emperor, and Pope Innocent ordained him there; yet in the same year he died in exile in Italy, before the city had learned of his ordination. Roger, prince of Apulia, on account of whose insolence the emperor, invited by the pope, had entered Italy, had placed [unclear], who by land and sea were oppressing the region with unheard-of calamities. In similar fashion he had acted through almost all Italy. After celebrating Pentecost, the emperor surrounded the castle of Bari with his army; taking it at last with great labor, he burned it and leveled it to the ground. The robbers seized around the burned tower were all hanged, more than five hundred in number.

*Adalbert died.*

*Speyer is burned.*

At that time Mainz was burned, together with the principal church.

In the same year Adalbert the elder, archbishop of Mainz, died on the ninth day before the Kalends of June. In the following year Adalbert the younger succeeded him, as we shall say. The greater church of Speyer also, with no small part of the city, and the town of Goslar, were consumed by fire on the same day on which Mainz was burned.
