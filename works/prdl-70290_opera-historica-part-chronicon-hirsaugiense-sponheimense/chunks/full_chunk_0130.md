*Monastery of Hirsau*

At length, when the synod had been completed, Henry V, intending to celebrate Pentecost, went to Merseburg, and there had Henry, long ago designated archbishop of Magdeburg but impiously rejected by the emperor’s adherents, consecrated. Then he shut Mainz up under siege, while his father was inside; and afterwards, coming to Wuerzburg, he expelled a certain Erlung, whom the emperor had designated after the death of Bishop Emehard, and through Ruthard, archbishop of Mainz, enthroned Rupert, provost of the same church, who had been elected both earlier and then by the clergy and people.

After this, entering Bavaria, he besieged the castle of Nuremberg, held by those favoring the emperor’s party against the Church, and after almost three months successfully took it. Departing from there, he disbanded the army and betook himself to Regensburg, where he held an assembly of the princes.

Summoned to this royal conference, Gebhard, abbot of Hirsau, attended; by his wisdom and eloquence he drew all alike into admiration and love of him. It happened, however, that John, bishop of Speyer, died in those same days. When Henry the younger king learned this, by the counsel of Ruthard archbishop of Mainz, Henry of Magdeburg, Rupert of Wuerzburg, Udo of Hildesheim, Frederick of Halberstadt, Gebhard of Constance, Eppo of Worms, and other bishops and princes then present, he designated the venerable Gebhard, abbot of this monastery, who was present, as bishop for the widowed church of Speyer, also conferring on him the abbey of [Lorsch?], of our order, situated in the diocese of Worms.

Then, driven away from his father through the treachery of the people of Regensburg, the king came to Speyer. Having obtained it together with his father’s treasures deposited there, he had this Gebhard consecrated bishop with great solemnity by the often-mentioned archbishop of Mainz, Ruthard, and enthroned him on All Saints’ Day, that is, on the first day of November, in the year of the Lord 1105, indiction 13, while Paschal II was ruling the Roman Church, in the sixth year of his pontificate, in the forty-eighth year of Emperor Henry IV, in the fourteenth year, four months, and one week of this Gebhard’s abbacy at Hirsau; after the election made at Regensburg concerning his person, [unclear].

Before his episcopate, however, as long as he was abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, he was always of great name and most celebrated fame among all Roman pontiffs, cardinals, bishops, abbots, kings, dukes, counts, princes, nobles, and peasants; and among all men the abbot of Hirsau was held, proclaimed, and praised as a good, religious, learned, wise, and most eloquent man, with great glory of honor. For wherever his name was brought into conversation, admiration of his virtues, wisdom, prudence, and integrity of life always stirred the hearers to love and praise of so great a man.

But after he left the monastery of Hirsau, which at that time seemed not much inferior to the church of Speyer in riches, revenues, fame, and honor, and assumed the episcopate, he immediately lost the renown of his name and the excellence of his reputation together with the abbacy. All men, noble and low-born, friends and enemies, household members and strangers, began to contemn, despise, and mock him as proud, vain-glorious, greedy, and foolish, since he had left so distinguished a monastery and a place glorious with most celebrated fame, obedient sons devoted in all things, and holy men, for the sake of an episcopate. For he was held in greater reverence and veneration as abbot than as bishop. For although as abbot he was noble by birth, composed in morals, circumspect in speech, gentle, wise, and eloquent, nevertheless, as soon as he assumed the episcopate, by some manner of divine permission, I know not how, he so completely lost all these things together that in morals he was rustic, in speech unskilled, and in conduct began to be burdensome to everyone.

It is to be feared, however, that all these things, together with not a few other adversities, befell him by divine ordinance for this reason: that he neglected to amend what had been commanded to him by the holy father William through the vision of the elder, of which we have spoken, just as he had threatened. Those who revered the abbot mocked the bishop. Moreover, on a certain day, while he was present, seeing and hearing through the windows, citizens with women and boys, in a certain place where he was staying, led dances and sang songs mockingly composed about him, to his insult. But this did not turn out well for them. For knights who were his friends, when they had heard these insults against their bishop, came running and suddenly scattered those [beaten?] people.
