And first, indeed, at Milan, he was crowned according to custom with the iron crown; and then at Rome, at St. John Lateran, he was exalted with the golden crown and the imperial blessing by three cardinals sent for this purpose by the pope from Gaul, with great pomp and solemnity, on the day of holy Pentecost. After he had been crowned, he made Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine, and many others knights in the same church. Meanwhile, while the emperor was staying in Italy for almost two years, the third army, which he had left in Germany, advanced bravely in fighting against the counts of Württemberg, rebels against the empire for many years, and obtained seventy-two of their eighty fortifications and castles, and killed many of them in war. But the emperor, waging many wars most bravely in Italy, defeated and overcame with a strong hand the Lombards, the Pavians, the Turinese, the men of Asti, the Veronese, the Piacentines, the Genoese, the Pisans, the Sienese, and also the Romans, and all the rest who resisted him, and powerfully compelled them to come into submission to him. This Emperor Henry was a wise, just, and gracious man, vigorous in arms, a most upright worshipper and defender of the Christian faith, ready in counsel and courage, a lover of the clergy and defender of the poor.

*1311.*

In the second year of Abbot Henry, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1311, Pope Clement V proclaimed that the Council of Vienne was to be held; it lasted two years. In it the book of the Clementines was published, and many things were decreed for the benefit of the Church. There the order of the Templars, which had stood for almost 184 years, was condemned by Pope Clement himself and destroyed throughout the whole world in a single day, at the urging of Philip, king of the Franks, through whose promotion that pope had been elected. The Templars were very wealthy; in order to acquire their possessions for himself, as many maintained, the king charged them with the false crime of heresy and prepared to extinguish them entirely.

In the same year Guido, bishop of Utrecht, coming to the Council of Vienne, was made a cardinal by Pope Clement V through the mediation of the king of the Franks and certain cardinals who spoke on his behalf; nevertheless, being content with his church of Utrecht, he did not conduct himself as a cardinal.

*1312.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Henry, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1312, Otto, king of Hungary and duke of Bavaria, died, leaving one son, fourteen days old, whom, together with the two sons of his brother, Henry, aged six, [unclear] he entrusted to citizens in Landshut and in Straubing to be brought up under guardianship. But after his death, when Louis, duke of Upper Bavaria, together with the nobles of the land, opposed this commission, he carried off the boys.

In the same year Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, received from Henry the emperor himself, his brother, Boppard and Wesel, towns on this side of the Rhine, with certain others, as a pledge for the expenses which he had incurred in Italy and elsewhere on account of the empire, as is clear from letters given in the camp before Florence, in the aforesaid year of the Lord 1312, in the tenth indiction, on the sixth day before the Ides of December. After receiving these, the archbishop, leaving the emperor in Italy, returned to Trier.

In the same year also died John, duke of Brabant. John the third of the same name succeeded him; he had as wife Margaret, daughter of Louis, king of the Franks, by whom he begot three sons, all of whom died before him without children, namely John, Godfrey, and Henry. He also had three daughters, of whom the first, Joanna, first married William, count of Holland. After he was killed by the Frisians, she was betrothed to Wenceslaus, count of Luxembourg, brother of John, king of Bohemia, and of Charles, the fourth emperor. The second was Margaret, who married Louis, count of Flanders. The third was Mary, who married Reynald, duke of Guelders.

*1313.*

In the thirteenth year of Abbot Henry, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity [1313], Henry, emperor of the Romans, the seventh, in Italy, having raised new armies and being about to advance against Robert, petty king of Apulia, was overcome on the journey at Buonconvento by the disease of dysentery, and died on the day of Saint Bartholomew, not without great loss to the whole Roman Empire; and the empire was vacant for one year. There are those who write that he perished by poison, through the administration of Brother Bernard of the Order of Preachers, his confessor and chaplain, by whom they say he was poisoned in the consecrated host when receiving communion on the day of the Assumption of Blessed Mary ever virgin; and for that reason all the Preachers were marked by the pope with this punishment, that up to this day and thereafter, when communicating persons at the altar, their priests give them communion with the left hand. But since we think this wholly fabricated, we therefore with just reason refuse to give credence to those who say such things.
