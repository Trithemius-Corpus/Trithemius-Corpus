*Of Johannes Trithemius. XXXV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, sends greetings to Willibald Pirckheimer, senator and patrician of Nuremberg.*

My magnificent Willibald, it has been most sweet to me, not without great pleasure of mind, to reread often your letter, full of consolation and learning; in it I clearly understood both your [unclear] and the common and sincere compassion of my friends toward me.

To you, therefore, most distinguished man, and no less to all my friends, I have and give undying thanks. If any opportunity is given me to repay it, I shall see to it that I show myself willing and most ready. I embrace your admonition, by which you finely and most modestly exhort me to endurance. Everything done against me without my deserving it I have already before now committed to God, and I have resolved not to seek vengeance, knowing [unclear]. [unclear]. How I served ungrateful monks, now [cast out?], I seem already to have learned sufficiently. [unclear]. For although I knew very well the saying of the wise, that [unclear], I always hoped for better things, until suddenly, with experience as my teacher, I was compelled to know that [unclear]. Hence Hildebert, archbishop of the Gauls, spoke finely:

All mortal things hang beneath uncertain chance,
and in their own mobility answer to flight.
Whatever you have today will perhaps leave you tomorrow,
or even while you are speaking, it ceases to be yours.

But there is an eternal providence in heaven, to which all lower things are subject, which governs all things with just and right judgment. We, created by it, saved by it, and to be made blessed by it, ought always and everywhere to praise, bless, and glorify it, giving thanks from the purest minds no less in adverse circumstances than in prosperous and favorable ones. For it is to me a sign of salvation, and a hope by no means small of the compassion of God that will come upon me, that the persecution of the wicked has gone before, as the holy [unclear] says: “For all who wish to live piously in Christ will suffer persecution.” 1 Tim. 3.

I have withdrawn from the territories of evil men, lest an occasion for taking vengeance be left to me, and lest it fall to me any longer to hear the snarling of creatures of darkness, to whom nothing has usually been more burdensome than learning, nothing sweeter than idleness, nothing more familiar than envy. May they prosper, to be changed for the better when he wills it who is judge of all, in whose decision I have placed the insults inflicted upon me.

The position proposed to me through Celtis and Peutinger, as your letter indicated, is not fitting for me to accept, since I am a monk and a professor of regular discipline, and especially for this reason: lest I seem to have disappointed my most reverend lord, my bishop of Würzburg, and his expectation, trust, and confidence concerning me. For he has set me over this poor monastery, holding a singular hope about me; he pursues me graciously and supports me most mercifully. I must not be ungrateful for the benefits of so great a prince; nor is it fitting for my religion to have a dwelling outside the monastery in cities. For if I had wished to live on the stipends of princes, it certainly would not have been necessary to dwell here in poverty. For there are many who would receive me with a large provision and maintain me with honor.

But I could never be induced to allow myself to be joined to courtly flatterers, since philosophy forbids it no less than religion; and I do not know how religion can be preserved unharmed in the tumult of a court, if anyone has ever been able to do so. I was born for letters, whose constant study dreads the tumults of courts, loves solitude, and detests a public gathering in the city. Poor in possessions, I live here in poverty, I who have never loved riches, knowing that Seneca prudently warned his disciple that one cannot have leisure for studies and for acquiring riches. Content, therefore, with my condition, whatever it is, I shall not seek riches. To serve princes, just as it is contrary to my purpose, has likewise never pleased my will. According to the saying of the holy Christopher Paul, “having food and clothing,” I wish to be content with these. Yet I give those men, my friends Celtis and Peutinger, the greatest thanks I can, who, having concern for me, do not disdain to take thought for my poverty and desolation.
