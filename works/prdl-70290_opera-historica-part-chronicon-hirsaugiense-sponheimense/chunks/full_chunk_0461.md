In the same year Sigismund, Emperor of the Romans, and Cardinal Branda, legate of the Apostolic See, meeting in an assembly with certain other princes, settled for a fixed number of years that long contest and the dissensions among the dukes of Bavaria, which had lasted for a year and six months with great damage and devastation to Bavaria, restoring peace and concord among the dukes.

In the same year also John, Duke of Brabant, founded the University of Louvain, which, at his request, Pope Martin V confirmed, permitting lectures to be instituted in every faculty except theology. But Pope Eugenius, Martin’s successor, afterward granted lectures in theology, as in the other faculties.

In these times also a great expedition of princes, bishops, nobles, and commoners was made against the Bohemians, who had separated themselves from the unity of the Catholic Church by many errors and heresies, Pope Martin V granting plenary indulgences, from penalty and from guilt, to all who should set out to war against them. Therefore, when a very great multitude of Catholics advanced against them, at first our men emerged victorious, many towns, castles, and villages of the heretics being overthrown, and many being killed. But afterward, when our princes began to thirst more for plunder than for the defense of the Catholic faith, they perceived the emperor’s mind stirred against them, whose kingdom they preferred to despoil and divide among themselves rather than to let stand intact. From this point fortune changed, and the Bohemians began to rush more strongly upon our men and to inflict greater losses than they had received from them. O ever-blind ambition of human greed: while it does not know moderation in possessing, it loses even what it has. The matter of the Bohemian heretics would then have been finished, if our princes had not put avarice and ambitious desire before love and defense of the Catholic faith. Thus our men, slain, despoiled, and confounded by the Bohemians, returned in disgrace to their own lands. In this expedition of the faithful John, the last Count of Sponheim, was present in person with his retainers; he fought most bravely against the schismatics, beyond the rest. When one morning at sunrise he had resolved, suddenly and unexpectedly, to attack a certain heretical town with some other Catholics, the gate being open for the sending of livestock into the fields, a little sheep, by chance rolling itself under the feet of the horse on which he was seated, threw him headlong a little before the gate; and before he could mount his warhorse again, the townsmen shut the gates of the town and killed all of our men who had entered at a headlong run. But the count, shut out against his will by the danger, or shall I say the benefit, of the sheep, was saved.

In the thirty-second year of our abbot Bernard, on the vigil of John, Reynald, Duke of Jülich and Guelders, died without children. After his death the people of Guelders accepted as duke Arnold, Count of Egmond, who, after receiving the rule of the duchy of Guelders, [unclear] for his posterity. Further, Adolf, Duke of Berg, obtained the duchy of Jülich for his son Rupert, through the aid of Theoderic, Archbishop of Cologne, down to the present day.

In this same year Abbot Bernard deposed the secular administrator and appointed two monks, Frederick and Wilhelm, who were to collect all the revenues and proceeds of this monastery, expend them for necessary uses, and each year make an accounting for each item.

The condition of the monastery at that time was, however, quite poor, dissolved no less in temporal than in spiritual matters. The monks with the abbot were often under excommunication, sometimes by the order because of disobedience and rebellion, sometimes by a judge because of debts not paid to creditors on time. The divine offices were frequently interrupted; unless a temporary relaxation had been obtained, it was not permitted to celebrate them. The abbot lived in Trarbach and was seldom in the monastery; each man, free from fear, did what seemed right to him. The monks lived without a rule, miserably fallen into every wantonness and pleasure.

In the aforesaid year, after the death of Rudolf and Albert, dukes of Saxony, Sigismund, Emperor of the Romans, conferred the duchy of Saxony, together with the principate of the election, upon Frederick, Margrave of Meissen and Landgrave of Thuringia, and upon his heirs forever, excluding Duke Eric, brother of the aforesaid Rudolf and Albert, who was still living; when the title of Duke of Saxony had been taken away, he wished him to be called Duke of Lüneburg thereafter.
