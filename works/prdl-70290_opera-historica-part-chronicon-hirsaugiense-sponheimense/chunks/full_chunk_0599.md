*Familiar Letters IX*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to his beloved brother Nicholas, his claustral prior: greetings and sincere charity in the Lord.*

Our affairs having been disturbed by the malice of wicked men, the mind is drawn away to other matters, and rightly considers where at last it may find rest. The authors of rashness do not spare innocence, and those who cannot harm us extend, as we have heard, a lying and slanderous tongue against our people. But we, setting little store by their curses and praising God’s mercy, bless him; of his protection at the fitting time we are certain. For why should we care for the follies of men raging in continual fury like the sea, men in whom everything is settled by foolishness, and nothing by wisdom? For we know that through many tribulations the kingdom of heaven lies open; nor do we refuse to suffer what chance offers, keeping Christ’s passion continually before our eyes. Sorrows have never been absent from us from the day on which we were elected to the abbacy; but we have placed our mind, furnished with the supports of the divine scriptures, in that place from which, by God’s mercy preserving it, it will be able to be overturned from the state of its tranquility neither by prosperity nor by adversity. For we are not ignorant how destructive a thing unrestrained sadness is for men, if once it has clung to their minds, although it is not easy to pass life without sadness. Innocence alone consoles me as I suffer injuries, and the Cynonoti will see what sort of Christian philosopher I am. Nor does their quibbling satisfy me, by which they say that what they rashly presumed was not done out of hatred of me, since it concerns the account of our name in no small degree.

But these times demand something else. At the beginning of the month of July there will be an assembly of princes in Cologne, to which we also have been summoned by the most serene prince elector Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg. Therefore, lest because of our long absence the established order of the monastery’s affairs should collapse, we entrust our office meanwhile to your charity, yet without prejudice to our dignity, until either we shall decide to return to the monastery, or [unclear] this same most serene prince elector [unclear].

My cellarer and universal procurator for [unclear] years, a man indeed shrewd and cunning, but stirred up too much by hatred against us, together with a few from the number of the donated brothers, favoring the Transylvanian parties, is laboring in unjust machinations against us and our prince. We do not need another’s testimony, since we intend to keep the prior’s words in lively memory. When he was lately here with us, among other things he confessed that the duke’s officials had said such words about us: “If the abbot had adhered to your parties, just as he is commonly said to have favored the Palatine ones, he would not have fallen into all these evils.” I know that their testimony in this matter is true. But who stirred up the duke’s mind against us, unless, as the prior himself reported, the aforesaid brother Heilmann and that donated brother Claudius, who, always slandering the Palatine parties, made the Transylvanians hostile to us without cause? Almighty God, who knows all things before they happen, knows that I am not lying. Whenever any one of the ducal officials entered the monastery, known to the aforesaid informers, mention of us was brought into the midst, as though we favored the Palatine affairs too much. This was the one and first cause of the Cynonoti’s rivalry against us; although the charity of the brothers made it known to us almost ten years ago, nevertheless we were unable to remove it safely enough. Let us add also another, concerning the fief of the castle of Sponheim, which you too are not unaware of. The third cause of the man’s hatred against us is the unwearied continuation of our studies in good letters; to him, to speak in his own words, the sight of books is the greatest horror.
