*Sponheim.*

The hardships of poverty. All the buildings of the monastery were ruinous, and the individual things that could be seen were covered with extreme [unclear]. The utensils of the house either did not exist, or had previously been removed through fear of the reform, so that those arriving first found neither table-linens, nor dishes, nor almost anything else necessary for the management of the house. Moreover, as soon as the brothers of the reform began to inhabit this place, they were necessarily compelled not only to take money on loan, but also to ask grain and wine on loan from others for the sustenance of life. The first brothers who came for the reform of this monastery were from the monastery of St. James near Mainz, four in number, namely Johannes of Kolenhausen, who had been cellarer there; he was ordained abbot. Heinrich of Geilenhausen, who remained here until death for eighteen years. Bruno, who did not remain long. Johannes Hocheim, who was appointed cellarer, but did not remain. For indeed, because of the excessive poverty and want of this monastery, having been brought up comfortably at St. James, they despised remaining here. When they returned to St. James, others sent back in their place remained for a time; and when these also departed, still others were sent to this place. Thus for many years none of the monks coming from St. James, except the aforesaid Heinrich, had stable permanence in this place, until at last young men were gathered, coming from the schools, instructed and professed according to the custom of the order, and finally put a necessary end to these excursions. Great at that time was the poverty of this monastery. Fish were very rare in the brothers’ food; but often, instead of fish, pears, cooked by a certain drying in an oven, afterwards softened again in a cauldron and covered with broth, were prepared for the brothers’ meal. In want they had voluntary patience for the love of God, laboring under great extremity both in food and in clothing, desiring nothing more than to relieve as quickly as possible, by their moderate frugality, the monastery from the burdens of its existing debts. The ornaments for the beauty of the Lord were few and exceedingly meager; apart from the pontifical mitre there were no jewels in the church. There was no library, because the unreformed monks pursued not books, but games and pleasures. For only eight volumes, apart from the Bible divided into two parts, of small price or value, were found in this monastery at the time of the reform. For, as we said far above, after regular discipline began gradually to decline in this place, and the substance of temporal goods together with the integrity of monastic life to fail, the monks, who were then all proprietors, dispersed a library once sufficiently precious, secretly selling the books for a low price, so that nothing remained of so many volumes that was of any value. But now, pursuing the institution of the new abbot, let us complete the order of the history; for, just as we cannot explain all the misery of this monastery, so we judge it superfluous to dwell longer on the blameworthy life of the unreformed monks.

*Concerning Johannes Kolenhausen, twenty-sixth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for fourteen years, eleven months, and three days; concerning various deeds both of his and of that time.*

Now in the year written above, 1459, in the second indiction, on the third weekday after the feast of St. Bernard, that is, on the twenty-second day of the month of August, about the eighth hour, the aforesaid abbots, commissioners of lord Adolf, archbishop of Mainz, entering the chapter-place of this monastery with the notary and witnesses, caused all the monks found here to be called to them anew. When they came and were sitting in the aforesaid place, in the presence of Johannes of Lansheim, chancellor, and of others who had been sent by the prince, the often-named commissioners and visitors attempted by every means to counsel and persuade them piously and paternally, that, casting aside deformity, they should submit their necks to the reform of the former holy manner of life, and conform themselves, for the salvation of their souls, to the practices of the brothers whom they had brought to reform this place. But since they in no way wished to acquiesce in the instructions and pious admonitions of the fathers, the aforesaid visitors and commissioners, by authority granted them by the ordinary, removed them all from the monastery, depriving them by sentence, for the present, of every right in the election or otherwise concerning the monastery.

Then, directing their intention to the institution of a new abbot, which had devolved upon the archbishop since there were no persons incorporated in the monastery, and using the authority committed to them, with the counsel and consent of the brothers present for introducing the reform, they nominated, deputed, and instituted Johannes Kolenhausen, otherwise of Butzbach, monk and cellarer of St. James near Mainz, then present, in the name and stead of lord Adolf, archbishop of Mainz, as abbot of this monastery.
