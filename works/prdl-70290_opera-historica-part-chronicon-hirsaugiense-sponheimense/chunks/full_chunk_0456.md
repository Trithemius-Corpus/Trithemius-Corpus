*1405.*

In the fourteenth year of our abbot Bernhard, his sister Anna, wife of Wilhelm, duke of Mons, came to King Rupert while he was staying at Heidelberg, accusing her son Adolf, who had seized his father and now had expelled both parents from the duchy of Mons, and asking counsel and help against her insolent son. The king, her brother, pitying her calamity, marked the son by an imperial sentence and soon compelled him to reverence and obedience toward his parents.

In the same year, around the Nativity of the Lord, Duke Albert of Bavaria died, son of Emperor Ludwig, Count Palatine of the Rhine, count of Hainaut, Holland, and Zeeland, and lord of Frisia, a pious, good man and a most humane prince; he was buried in Holland. By his wife Margaretha, daughter of the duke of Poland, he left three sons: Albert, who had Straubing with the duchy of Lower Bavaria, who was the first among the brothers to die, without children, and who had no wife; Johann, who was elected bishop of Liege and afterward ruled Holland for several years after the death of the following brother; and Wilhelm, who, after his mother’s death, ruled Holland, Hainaut, Zeeland, and Frisia for a few years, and died in the year of the Lord 1410, leaving one only daughter, named Jacoba, of whom we shall speak in her place.

In the same year, on the day of Saint Scholastica the Virgin, the clergy of Worms left the city for the preservation of their liberties. For one year and eleven months, others say for two years, they remained outside the city, each college in the place anciently reckoned to it. At length an agreement was made, as we shall say below in order in its place, through King Rupert and his son Ludwig.

Also in the same year, on the fourteenth day of the month of May, Eckard, bishop of Worms, was buried in the middle of the choir of the greater church. He was succeeded by Matthaeus of Cracow, who presided for six years, a man learned in sacred letters, a Doctor, who wrote many little works pertaining to the instruction of the faithful and the formation of morals.

In the year noted above, Bernhard, abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, purchased one manor in Winzenheim, with houses, fields, vineyards, meadows, gardens, and all its appurtenances, from Peter, son of Conrad the tailor; and before the Schultheiss and the Schöffen there, as is the custom, he received it as the perpetual property of the monastery. But after some years, as we shall say below, falling into displeasure against his convent, out of anger he alienated both this manor and another without the consent and will of the convent, to no small peril of his soul.

*1406.*

In the fifteenth year of our abbot Bernhard, on the day of the martyrs Vitus and Modestus, in the morning, about the seventh hour, there was a very great eclipse of the sun. Many wars followed in various lands and provinces of the Roman Empire.

In the same year, after the feast of Easter, certain princes, counts, and other nobles of various rank of this province came together as one and took counsel against the citizens of Mainz. They inflicted great losses on them for the insults and damages which they said they had previously suffered from the people of Mainz. Also, on a certain night, they came in force to the gate of the city, pretending to be associates of that same city, and were let in. Since they were strong and many in number, they beheaded certain of the more powerful citizens, plundered whatever citizens they could, inflicted irreparable losses on them, and withdrew. In this same year, at last, the citizens of Aachen swore allegiance to King Rupert after many losses.

*1407.*

In the sixteenth year of our abbot Bernhard, around the feast of Saint James the Apostle, an agreement was made between the clergy and the citizens of Worms through Rupert, king of the Romans, and his son Ludwig, count palatine of the Rhine, after many expenses had been incurred by both parties in the Roman curia and elsewhere. And thus the clergy, after they had been in exile for two years and five months, returned to the city.

In the same year the new praetorium in Cologne, which they call the citizens’ house, was begun; it was completed in seven years, and its expenses, as they say, exceed fifty thousand gold florins when each item is counted.
