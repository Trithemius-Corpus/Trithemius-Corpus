*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

*Concerning Lupold, the eighth abbot of this monastery, who presided for only 4 years; concerning his character and the deeds of his time.*

After the most reverend Abbot Siger had been buried, as we have set forth above, the brethren came together as one, to provide themselves with a shepherd according to the rule of our most holy father Benedict. After the casting of votes, as is customary, they unanimously elected as abbot one Lupold, a monk from among themselves, on the sixth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord 982, in the tenth indiction, in the eighth year of Emperor Otto II: a very old and venerable man, who presided over this monastery for only 4 years and 3 months. This Lupold was Bavarian by race, Regensburg by nation, born of parents of middling rank but nevertheless honorable. He entered the monastic order under Diethmat, abbot of this monastery, while still young, in which he lived more than 40 years most religiously and without complaint, walking before his own face in peace and tranquility of spirit, troublesome to no one, burdensome to no one. For he was a man of very great simplicity, and perhaps quieter than the morals of his subjects and these times required; he was an example of monastic life, not a preacher, since his own holy life commended him, and no impiety of others stirred him.

In these times, after Gero, archbishop of Cologne, Warinus was bishop of the church for 9 years. He renewed the monastery of St. Martin in Cologne, of our order, concerning which we spoke above, magnificently endowed it, and handed it over to the Scots to possess forever, under the rule of our most holy father Benedict.

Around these times Erhard, monk of Luneberg in Saxony, flourished, very devoted to the divine Scriptures and not ignorant of secular letters. He left many little works of his talent for posterity to read; for among them he wrote, from choice reading, commentaries on Genesis, on Exodus, on Leviticus, and on most other books of the Bible.

Alger also, a monk of Corbie in Saxony, and for many years a scholastic, before his conversion of seculars and after his conversion of monks, a man most learned both in divine Scriptures and in secular letters, flourished in the same times. Among the other monuments of his talent he wrote on the Sacrament of the altar and on free will, little works not to be despised. Arnulph also, or in our German manner Arnold, a monk of this [unclear] although the remaining little works of his talent are [unclear], nevertheless we do not doubt that he wrote more and other things. For the talent of so great a man could not have been summoned to writing by the publication of one little work only, or have ceased.

In these days there also flourished holy monks of our order, namely Benedict, Nathanael, John, Isaac, and Christian, disciples of blessed Adelbert, bishop and martyr of Prague, concerning whom it was said above. After they had, with the Lord’s help, accomplished by their preaching the conversion of many people in Hungary, Prussia, and Poland, at last in Poland they were crowned with martyrdom for the name of Christ, on the ninth day before the Kalends of May.

At about these times there also flourished Libatius, monk of the monastery of St. Alban the martyr near Mainz, a man most holy in character and way of life. Pope Benedict VII ordained him and sent him into Russia to preach the Gospel to the nations, where by word and example he acquired for Christ an innumerable people.

Orthwin also, the tenth bishop of Hildesheim, from a monk of the monastery of Magdeburg of our order in Saxony, learned and fervent with zeal for religion, is held to have been distinguished in these times; and he presided over the church of Hildesheim for almost 29 years.

Odo also, abbot of Corbie, made bishop of Beauvais in France, was distinguished around these times. Both for his learning in the Scriptures and for the integrity of his way of life, he was held in great reverence among all, and more than once fulfilled apostolic and royal embassies in his greatest affairs.

In these days there also flourished Bruno, from a monk of the monastery of Corbie in Saxony made bishop of Verden, brother of Duke Hermann, a man worthy of a prelate, since he is said to have been nobly instructed both in monastic institutes and in ecclesiastical disciplines.

Gebhard also, bishop of Augsburg, formerly abbot of the monastery of our order called Ellwangen, situated in the same diocese, flourished in these times; by his character and learning he adorned both horns of the altar.
