This duke of Bavaria and Saxony begot, by the aforesaid wife, the daughter of Emperor Lothar II, Henry surnamed the Lion, duke of Brunswick and Saxony, a warlike and remarkable man, about whom the ancients sang marvelous things in German songs, and whom princes have been accustomed to depict in their halls down to the present day.

The story of his pilgrimage tells how, namely, sewn into a horse-hide and carried away from a ship by a griffin, he escaped the peril of the sea; how he fought with griffins, killed a dragon, crossed the sea with a lion, and at last, when his wife Matilda, daughter of the king of England, was preparing to take another husband, he was suddenly brought back unharmed by a demon, together with the lion, the companion of his journey and [unclear] danger, from very remote places to his castle. There are many similar things, which one should believe were written more from the invention of poets, under a certain mystical meaning, than that they actually happened. For the ancient Germans, after the custom of the Greeks, with whom it is known they had much intercourse, were accustomed to cast the brave deeds of heroes into songs fit for teaching, and, by concealing the deeds of mortals under the symbols of figures and riddles, to compare them with the immortals.

*1134.*

In the same year also, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1134, Saint Norbert, archbishop of Magdeburg, first founder of the Premonstratensian order, an outstanding propagator of holy religion, died on the eighth day before the Ides, and passed to the Lord in the eighth year of his episcopate. Conrad, son of a certain count, succeeded him.

In the same year Emperor Lothar besieged Speyer and, when it had at last been worn down by long famine, took it; and he received Conrad, duke of Swabia, rival of the kingdom and empire, into surrender and favor.

*1135.*

In the fourteenth year of Abbot Volmar, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1135, the emperor celebrated the Nativity of the Lord at Aachen. In this same year the body of Saint Matthias the Apostle was found at Trier, in the monastery of Saint Eucharius of our order; from him the name of the monastery was thereafter changed, and it is called Saint Matthias down to the present day. Bruno, archbishop of Cologne, and Frederick, duke of Alsace, who were not well disposed toward the emperor, were judged worthy to be reconciled through the intervention of the princes.

At the feast of Pentecost, by order of the emperor, a great assembly of princes was held in the city of Magdeburg, at which the king of Denmark and many other princes were personally present; all of them swore a very firm peace for ten years, and the rest of the multitude was compelled to do the same.

In the same year Emperor Lothar expelled the nuns from the monastery of Lutera in the diocese of Halberstadt, and, after restoring the place for the better, introduced monks of our order from the monastery of Bergen near Magdeburg, appointing as their first abbot Everhard, a venerable monk of the aforesaid monastery. Moreover, the church itself [unclear] in honor of the holy apostles [unclear] was called by the common people. But after it had been changed and newly restored by King Lothar, it was called royal Lutera, that is, Königslutter, down to the present day.

In these times also, namely in the year of the Lord 1135, Buggo of the church of Worms established the monastery of Frankenthal. In it he placed a multitude of virgins serving Christ in chastity, after providing the necessary things.
