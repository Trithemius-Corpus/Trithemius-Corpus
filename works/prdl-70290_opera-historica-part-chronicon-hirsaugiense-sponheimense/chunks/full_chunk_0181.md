*The Monastery of Hirsau.*

*Manegold is elected abbot.*

... some impediment might intervene through the contrivance of the wicked because of a long vacancy, and might somehow have been able to weaken that most free election of a new pastor. After much deliberation beforehand, the brothers who had votes in the election immediately came together on the same day as one, with the mind and intention of setting a new pastor over themselves. Therefore, after the grace of the Holy Spirit had been invoked, in the year of the Lord 1157, in the fifth indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of April, at about the third hour of the day, while Frederick I was reigning as emperor, in the sixth year of his reign, the conventual monks of this monastery unanimously elected as abbot Manegold, a venerable monk, a Frank by birth, sprung from the kin of Hartwig his predecessor, being almost sixty years of age. After a long and humble refusal, yielding to the prayers of the brothers, he took up the burden of pastoral care, but deferred the office of blessing until the Saturday of the Ember fast after the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, because he resolved to offer himself to be well tested by the brothers.

*He is consecrated at Speyer by Bishop Gunther, and presided for eight years.*

At length, scarcely induced by the urgent prayers of the brothers, he set out for Speyer to be blessed, with a large and distinguished company; and on the aforesaid Saturday of the autumnal fast, after twenty-five of his monks had been promoted to holy orders by Gunther, bishop of Speyer, he himself on the following Sunday solemnly received from the same pontiff the gift of blessing, six abbots standing by, vested in sacred vestments.

*Life of Abbot Manegold.*

He presided in the abbacy for eight years, four months, and six days, a man of good and praiseworthy conduct, and he did many good things for this monastery. He was a Frank by birth, as we have said, born of honorable parents; when he was set to learning letters in his youth, he turned out sufficiently learned and educated. He had, so to speak, drunk in the fear of the Lord from infancy; hence he was accustomed to take delight not in games, not in running about, not in the frivolities of boys, but rather to devote himself, so far as his age allowed, to the study of letters and to prayers.

*Manegold becomes a monk.*

For, having by nature obtained a good intelligence, and having drunk in the taste of eternal wisdom, he learned to love nothing more in this life than the knowledge of the Scriptures. At length, by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, he was made a monk under Abbot Volmar, and lived among the brothers so piously, so religiously, and so humbly that he scarcely had an equal in the whole congregation. Walking before God and men in all tranquillity of mind and action, he was burdensome to no one, troublesome to no one, but in all things showed himself a worthy monk according to the Rule of our holy father Benedict.

*He becomes succentor.*

He was indeed short in bodily stature, but great and distinguished in the virtues of the soul. Finally, after the years of his initial monastic training had been completed, he was appointed succentor by Abbot Volmar; and while placed in that office he caused more than sixty volumes to be copied for the adornment of the library and the use of the brothers, his friends and secular kinsmen providing the expenses for this. After this, when in all things he had shown himself lowly and irreproachable, upon the death of Ditho the prior he was appointed prior in his place, and by word and example he was a rule of holy conduct for the brothers. In admonishing he was gentle, pious, and meek; in correcting, serious, since he knew very well that the governance of souls is the most difficult of all arts, and therefore must be exercised with greater zeal. To the [unclear] and [disobedient?] he showed himself stern and fearsome. Hence it came about that the good and humble embraced him with the greatest love, while the slothful and those who had no care for their own souls feared him as a lord and a singular avenger of their iniquity.

*He becomes cellarer.*

Then, so that he might be tested in all things, he was appointed chief cellarer by Abbot Volmar, and Adelhelm was substituted in his place; not long afterward Adelhelm was appointed at Mediolacum in the diocese of Trier. When he had been made cellarer, he applied great care and solicitude in administering his office, and yet did not neglect the inward conduct of the spirit. Again, after some years, when Gerlach the prior had died, he became prior a second time, and in his usual manner vigorously carried out zeal for regular discipline. Always first and last in choir, he promoted the worship of the divine service with the greatest diligence. He loved the divine Scriptures wonderfully, and as long as he lived he devoted all his zeal to the copying of authentic books of the saints.

*He is elected abbot.*

Finally, after the passing of Abbot Hartwig had been happily completed, he was elected the seventeenth abbot of this monastery, as we have said, in the year of the Lord 1157, in the fifth indiction, on the eighth day before the Kalends of April; and on the tenth day before the Kalends of October, that is, on the Sunday of the autumnal Ember fast after the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, which was the day of Saint Maurice the martyr, receiving the office of blessing from Gunther, bishop of Speyer, he presided for eight years, four months, and six days. *He is consecrated at Speyer.* He was a man adorned with holy morals: gentle, calm, humble, peaceable, mild, and affable, and chaste in mind and body
