But if we believe Cicero, not only is Fortune herself blind, but she also commonly makes blind those whom she has embraced. Therefore, when Chilo the philosopher was asked what fortune was, he answered: Fortune is an ignorant physician, for she blinds many. But hear with what weapons blind fortune, stirred up by the envy and ill-will of our rivals, is trying to rage against me, so that you may understand that you are not the only one among mortals for whom his own household has raised up grievous wrongs. For Truth, which knows all things, cannot lie, when in the holy Gospel it foretells that its faithful would suffer tribulations and enmities for justice and most sacred religion, not only from outsiders, but also from their own household and intimates. By what means these things have happened to us, both from our own people and from strangers, we shall inform you in a brief account.

Since in the year just past, as can hardly be unknown to you, a conspiracy of many princes was waging a most savage war, utterly contrary to the customs of the Christian religion, against the most clement prince Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, under whose protection my monastery is known to stand secured by right of the county of Sponheim, I was compelled by fear of the enemy to withdraw to Stauropolis, the town nearest the monastery. *Kreuznach.*

Meanwhile Pyrophorus, that count of the Hessians, carrying out plundering, burnings, and devastations of sacred buildings, villages, and the most beautiful villas around Stauropolis without mercy, among other things devastated the better courts of my monastery and burned them with fire. But we remained with our notable library, which you once examined among us, in the aforesaid town for five months, until at last it was permitted to see an end of the evils. When peace had at length been restored to the province and we returned to the monastery, we gradually learned of various schemes against us by certain rivals, with some of our own people consenting, which had arisen meanwhile. Since, trusting in the innocence and uprightness of our mind, we had disregarded them, not considering or weighing the hatred of the Cynonoti that lay hidden against us, the fury of the malignant grew with time.

It happened, however, that on the first day of the month of April of the present year, having been summoned to Budoris, a town of our most serene prince Philip, Count Palatine, in the matter of the burned monastery of Limburg, I went out from my monastery, fearing nothing less than the evil that afterward happened in my absence. For on the fifth day thereafter, while I was at Budoris on the common business of my order, certain attendants of the Cynosnoti, sent to my monastery, broke the locks, took away clothing and such goods as they found, seized certain members of the household, and, laying upon them I know not what false crimes, delivered them over for not a few days to the public custody of the aforesaid town, to our manifest insult and reproach. When these things were reported to us at Budoris, they moved our mind not moderately, and not undeservedly did it distress us that we had received so signal an insult from those whom we had never offended even by a word.

Yet, deliberating with friends as to what would be useful for us to do, we concluded at last that we should not return to Sponheim before we knew that so great a wrong had been corrected by lawful satisfaction. And so we remained among the Budorites and Neometes for almost three months, not doubting that our people grieved more over the prolongation of our absence than we ourselves were saddened. The Cynonoti have some causes of hatred against us, and invent others. The love and loyalty with which we have always favored the affairs of the most clement prince Philip was not the least cause of the persecutions against us. I shall therefore give rashness room for a time; I shall go with the most serene prince into the March of Brandenburg, something which, though asked of me for many years, I had never before been able to accomplish. Nor shall I return soon, unless that commotion against us grows quiet.

For whatever fortune brings upon men who are truly wise will turn out for good, and often it happens that from adverse events a better fortune follows. Therefore in every event we need courage and temperance, so that we neither at any time cast down our mind in a time of things opposing us, nor in prosperity are vainly lifted up beyond what is fitting. Though fortune take away everything she has given, diminish riches and honors, annihilate dignities, if the mind, sustaining the divine ray, perseveres on its summit, she has harmed nothing. The mastery of affairs, as Valerius says, is accustomed to collapse; a good mind is preferred to riches, for the possession of a good mind undergoes no assault of a harsher fortune.
