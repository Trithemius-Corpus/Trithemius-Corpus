*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

I first replied to these letters in the Easter season, since they were presented to me not by a regular messenger, but by a chance carrier, who brought us fish given by the prince himself on the sixth day of the month of March, which was the Saturday after Reminiscere in Lent.

*XV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, to the excellent brother Nicolaus Basellius, monk of Hirsau, once his hearer and disciple: greetings.*

I feel compassion for your sickness, and I pray the mercy of almighty God that he restore you as quickly as possible to your former health. And indeed, as you write, devastation and solitude befell me, according to the prophecy of Isaiah, not without great contrition, since, while the war of many men was raging against my most serene prince, the Palatine, almost all the estates of my then monastery of Sponheim perished either by fire or by hostile devastation. When at last the war had been quieted by the authority of the King of the Romans, certain men from [Cynonotis?], not very favorably disposed toward the Palatine prince, rose up against me in hatred of him and, secretly raising up rivals against me, made Duke Johann hostile. What evils afterward followed upon me, when you were with me at the end of the month of June at Neumagen in the year one thousand five hundred and five, you heard more fully. But afterward, in time, I learned what I had not known before: that all the things which my rivals attempted against me they undertook at the urging of certain of my brothers, together with the abbot of Mainz, an enemy of all studious men. For indeed, unless he had supported the wicked men while I was absent and suspecting nothing of the sort, they would never have committed [unclear].

Do not wonder, Basellius, nor suppose that it was rashly done, that, provoked by the injury of my rivals, I resigned the abbey of Sponheim. In it, through twenty-three years, how many useless occupations opposed to my purpose I endured, neither can I say nor would you easily believe. Yet you are not wholly uninformed, since you lived with me for a whole year, carefully weighed the character and pursuits of each man, and found among us many haters of good letters. What were we to do any longer with ungrateful monks, whom we could never persuade to correct their manners according to the purity of the Rule and to devote themselves to the sacred study of the Scriptures, which would especially become monks? We dismissed them when the opportunity was received; nor does it repent us, having obtained a place which, though poor, is nevertheless quiet and by no means unsuitable for our studies, in which we shall be able usefully to devote ourselves both to ourselves and to the reading of the Scriptures. Since you wish to know who are the heads of the faction against us, recall to mind what you once heard at Sponheim, and you will easily separate the good from association with the bad.

Blessed be the Lord God almighty, who delivered me from the midst of Babylon and rescued my soul from the depth of useless occupations, so that henceforth I may walk before him in peace and tranquillity of mind, which indeed I had always desired with my whole heart. Concerning the chronicle of your monastery of Hirsau, I want you to know, dearest [Irbusius?], that I left it unfinished, together with the books and all the collected materials, in the monastery of Sponheim. There seek whatever is of your right, and think about its completion as you will; for the former agreement no longer pertains to me, because I have renounced the abbey together with all things belonging to it. I shall nevertheless be ready to do what I can for the honor of your monastery, if your abbot wishes. Farewell, mindful of us in your prayers to God. From Würzburg, on the fourteenth day of the month of March, in the year of the Christians 1507.
