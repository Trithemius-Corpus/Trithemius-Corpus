We promise ourselves empty hopes, full of deception, when we think that we shall live for very many years, though we must nevertheless necessarily die at some time. For what if we have lived for a long time? Shall we therefore not die? Between him who has lived thirty years and him who has lived a hundred, after the same unavoidable end of living, death, has arrived, tell me, O Philobius, whose condition do you think is the better? Both were born, both lived, though not for equal years, both died. Have you not read the senarius of Menander, who says:
[unclear]
For he whom God loves dies young.

“Happy is the death of men,” says Boethius, “which neither inserts itself in sweet years, and, often called, comes in sorrowful ones.”

There are two things that frequently make death in old men unhappier than the passing of the young. One is that the dying old man sets out with a heavier burden of sins; the young man, however, could sin so much less, in proportion as he lived for a shorter time. For, as Seneca says, we make life restless for ourselves through fear of death; so great is human madness that by a certain fear of death men are driven to death. Therefore it makes no difference at what time we die, but we must take the greatest care that it befall us to die well. And we shall die well if we have lived well, walking not according to the flesh, but according to the spirit. For the holy Apostle says:
[unclear]
Let us walk in the spirit; let us not fulfill the desires of the flesh.

Wherefore let us abandon all things that are in the world, seeking nothing more than what is necessary for the sustaining of the body, and even that itself scarcely, as the last thing, so that, free and unencumbered, we may be able to await our end, which threatens daily. For we grow old in silent years; even as we grow, life little by little decreases. We have lost infancy, then [unclear], then adolescence also passed away. Youth too has swiftly gone by; whatever time of our life passes has wholly perished and cannot be recalled. Of the time that remains there is no certainty, nor any moment of standing still; indeed, we divide with death this very day which we are spending. Why, then, do men, since they do not fail to know that they are mortal, devote themselves so greatly to gathering perishable riches, which they know they cannot carry away with them when they die? Nature can be content with few things, because, loving the middle, it shudders at extremes, as Palladas said very beautifully and prudently in a Greek poem:
[unclear]
For moderation is the best thing, since heights are apt to bring on dangers, while extremes contain insult.

Let us therefore tear our minds away from all desires of the world; let us abandon the way of perdition and fraud, so that, the more we now see, as the years incline toward old age, that day drawing near on which one must necessarily depart from this life, the more we may consider how pure we may depart, how innocent we may come to judgment. Nor should we imitate those who, accustomed to crimes, when the powers of the body are now failing and they ought to have remembered that they are about to pass from here, plunge themselves into heavier sins, so that those who in youth had been wanton may now in old age not undeservedly be called mad. Let each person shake off from himself every bond of the world, the flesh, and the devil, while it is permitted him, while there is opportunity, and turn himself to God with his whole mind, so that he may be able to await securely that day on which he will put off the present life and assume another. Before old age let him take care to live well; in old age, to die well. Farewell, always anxious about the end. From Cologne, on the thirty-first day of the month of July, in the year 1505.

*XXXI.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Roger Sycambrius: greetings.*

I wish you to know, dearest Roger, that the most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, has obtained by his prayers from our most illustrious prince Philip, Count Palatine, with my consent, that I should follow him into the March and remain there for some time. For during four years, having a desire for us, he has very frequently solicited me by letters and messengers, wishing to have me with him in his realm for a while. For the prince is a young man, in the twenty-second year of his age, competently instructed in letters, very strong in intellect, and has the greatest affection for the study of letters. Until now, however, it could not conveniently happen that I should absent myself for so long a time from my monastery; but now the envy of rivals has given a cause by which I can absent myself no less cheerfully than willingly for any length of time...
