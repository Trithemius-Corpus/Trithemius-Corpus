*Of Sponheim.*

We, brother Albert, by the grace of God bishop of the church of [unclear], acting in pontifical matters in the place of the venerable father in Christ and our lord Henry, Archbishop of Mainz; *copy of the indulgences, [unclear] of the bishop*; to all the faithful in Christ to whom the present letters shall come, greeting and sincere charity in the Lord. Desiring to invite all Christ’s faithful to works of piety by suitable means pleasing to God, to all truly penitent and confessed persons who shall stretch out helping hands to the monastery of St. Martin in Sponheim, or shall come to it for the sake of devotion and prayer on the individual feasts of the glorious ever-virgin Mary, of all the apostles, of the patrons, and of the dedication, or who follow the body of Christ when it has been carried to the sick person or persons, or who shall go around the cemetery or cloister, praying for all the faithful departed there and everywhere, by the mercy of almighty God and the grace of the blessed apostles Peter and Paul, relying on the authority which we exercise, we mercifully relax in the Lord forty days of indulgences, together with one quarantine, from the penances enjoined upon them. Moreover, the dedication of the aforesaid monastery, celebrated hitherto on the Sunday next before the day of the Nativity of the blessed Virgin Mary, we have transferred to the Sunday next after the day of St. Martin, to be celebrated perpetually in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. Given and enacted under our seal, in testimony of each of these things, in the year of the Lord 1338, on the vigil of St. Bartholomew the Apostle.

In the same year also Edward, king of England, came to the Emperor Louis at Koblenz, asking aid from him against the king of France. Louis, after receiving a great sum of money, promised him support, but did not keep his promises, and the king of England did not get back the money he had given.

In the same year also Reynald, Count of Guelders, who was afterwards first created duke, led a great army against the Liégeois, who in the preceding year, when he was absent and with the Emperor, had miserably devastated his land with fire and plunder; he had with him Adolph, bishop of Liège, whom the citizens had formerly expelled in disgrace.

On the other side the Liégeois led out a much larger army, and they met not far from Upper Maastricht; and when the aforesaid battle had been joined, by a miracle they were defeated by the aforesaid count with fewer men, many of them being captured, from whose spoil he became rich and renowned. In this battle more than nine thousand fighting men from both sides were said to have fallen. Reynald the count, having obtained so notable a victory, became not only rich from the plunder and the ransom of captives, but also named and famous in every land, and pleasing to all the princes; and he built many fortifications and castles, and enlarged his county.

In the second year of Henry, abbot of this monastery, on the day of St. Kilian the martyr, around noon, there was a great eclipse of the sun, which lasted more than two hours; and later in the same year, in autumn, the [unclear] in Verona killed many persons.

In this same year also Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, lent a very great sum of money to Edward, king of England, receiving from him as pledge the royal crown, a jewel of inestimable value. For in the preceding year the same king had declared war on Philip, king of France, on account of the county of Normandy, which he asserted belonged to him by hereditary right; and, having hired many princes and nobles from Germany for a great stipend, he entered France with a strong hand, and, though it profited him little, he raged with fire and plunder against [unclear].

In the same year the Emperor Louis, having summoned all the princes of the realm, held a court at Frankfurt in mid-Lent. There, among other things, sitting in his imperial majesty, he took Reynald, Count of Guelders, from the number of counts and placed him in the company of dukes, on account of his singular prudence and the magnificent deeds he had performed, creating him duke of Guelders, with the title of the county of Zutphen added. He was the eleventh count in the order of succession who ruled Guelders, and from a count he became the first duke, of whom we spoke above thirteen years earlier. At the same time also William, Count of Jülich, was raised to margrave by the Emperor Louis, with the consent of all the princes, on account of his many virtues and magnificent deeds, by which he seemed worthy.

*1340.*

In this year there was a very great pestilence throughout all Germany, and it laid low many thousands of people; a great famine also followed, and it was misery to live and danger to die.
