*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

Virnenburg. The fifth was Duke Albert, who founded the monastery in Gimeneo; through him the line of dukes is continued. The last of the sons was Duke Otto, who had as wife Elisabeth, daughter of the duke of Bavaria. Of the daughters, the first, Agnes, married Andrew, king of Hungary, and after him the duke of Vratislavia. The second, Elisabeth, married Frederick, duke of Lorraine, to whom she bore Rudolf, who succeeded his father in the duchy. The third, Anna, married Hermann, margrave of Brandenburg. The fourth was Catharina, who was betrothed to Henry of Luxembourg, king of the Romans; when he died before the wedding, she married Rupert, king of Apulia. Ulrich, count of Oetingen, a vigorous and warlike man, took as wife the fifth and last of his daughters, named Gutta.

In this same year in which Albert was elected king of the Romans, the city of Cologne, which for seven years, the same number of months, and nine days had lain under excommunication and ecclesiastical interdict because of disputes and dissensions held with Archbishop Sifrid, with the consent of Wichbold, his successor, was absolved by Pope Boniface VIII, and divine offices were again permitted to be celebrated in it. In the same year also, on the fifth day before the Kalends of April, John II died, the ninth abbot of my monastery of Spanheim; Dietlib, formerly chancellor of John, count of Spanheim, succeeded him and presided for six years and six months. He renewed again the observance of regular discipline, which had utterly collapsed.

In the same year, on the fifth day before the Ides of November, Bohemund, archbishop of Trier, died. He had built anew from the foundations the castle of Neumagen, near Trittenheim, the village of my birth, on the Moselle. He was buried at Hemelrad, a monastery of the Cistercian order.

After him Diether of Nassau, brother of the former king Adolf, not elected nor postulated, but out of hatred for King Albert ordained archbishop by Pope Boniface VIII, was sent to Trier. He presided for almost eight years. He was of the Order of Preachers, and although he was always opposed to Albert, he was nevertheless able to do nothing.

In the same year also, on the vigil of Saint James the Apostle, lord Emicho, bishop of Worms, died, buried in the major church under the monastic habit, as they say. Eberwin of Kronenberg succeeded him and presided for three years and [unclear] months. At the insistence of his citizens he consented that from the community sixteen men should be admitted to the council of the senate, which had not been done before.

In his time the convent of nuns of the Order of Preachers, which is called Liebenau, outside the walls of the city of Worms near Neuhausen, was founded by a certain citizen named Jacob Engelmar, abundantly provided with the necessary goods for the support of the handmaids of Christ, and that foundation itself was confirmed by him.

*1299.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Gotfrid, which was the one thousand two hundred ninety-ninth year of the Lord’s nativity, Pope Boniface VIII condemned the heresy of those who called themselves Fraticelli, and utterly destroyed it everywhere.

In the same year, on the fourth day before the Kalends of November, John, count of Holland, died at Haarlem, without children. After his death John, count of Hainaut, son of Adelaide, sister of the former William, king of the Romans, assumed the duchy by right of kinship and joined all Holland to his dominion. He had taken as wife Philippa, daughter of the count of Luxembourg, by whom he begot John, the first-born; William, successor as count of Holland; John, lord of Beaumont; Henry, canon of the church of Cambrai; Margaret, countess of Artois; Adelaide, countess of Clermont; Mary, countess of Bourbon; and Mechtild, countess of Nyede. Against this John, King Albert held an assembly of princes, asserting that the county of Holland had fallen vacant to the Roman Empire; yet by the counsel of the princes he granted it to him in fief.

*1300.*

In the seventh year of Abbot Gotfrid, and in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1300, Pope Boniface VIII first instituted at Rome the jubilee of plenary remission and indulgence of all sins from punishment and guilt, and commanded that it be celebrated again after a hundred years. This was a new thing, previously unheard of: because such indulgences had been announced throughout the whole world in the preceding year, so great a multitude of people set out from all Germany for Rome that it seemed utterly infinite and innumerable. And from that time the use of the jubilee began in the Roman Church; there had previously been no account of it. Among the many princes, bishops, abbots, and other prelates who then sought Rome, Gotfrid too, the twenty-ninth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, went there with certain brothers for the grace of the jubilee, in hope of eternal reward. So great a multitude of people came to Rome from all sides in that year that it was scarcely possible to walk through the city, broad and vast though it is.
