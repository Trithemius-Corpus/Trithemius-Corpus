Hence, when by now through habit he had become familiar and was feared by no one, a certain little boy serving in the kitchen began to despise him, mock him, and assail him with insults, and as often as he could he poured the filth of the kitchen upon him. When he had often asked the master of the kitchen to restrain the boy from his insults, and had at last threatened that he would [avenge?] the insults, he was laughed at by him, who said: “You are a spirit, and do you fear a boy?” To him the demon replied: “Because you scorn to correct your boy at my request, in a few days I shall show you how much I fear him.”

Having said this, the spirit departed in anger. Not long afterward, when one day after vespers the boy, alone in the kitchen, was sleeping from weariness, the spirit came, suffocated him, cut him into pieces, put him in a pot, and began to cook him at the fire. When the master of the kitchen perceived this, he began to curse the spirit. The latter, provoked, on the next day, when a roast had been set on spits at the fire for the bishop and the courtiers, pressed [unclear] and sprinkled it over with horrible toads, with the blood of the same and venom. And again, having been assailed with insults, he hurled him from on high by an illusory bridge into a pit; and, while keeping watch most diligently above the walls of the city’s castle, he compelled all the guards to keep watch.

A certain man, about to set out on a long journey, since he had an unchaste wife, said to the spirit Hutgin, as if in jest: “Good companion, I entrust my wife to you until I return; see that you guard her.” And when, in the man’s absence, the woman would set out her portion with adulterers and would try many lovers in succession, this spirit always placed himself invisibly in the midst, and, throwing the men from the bed to the ground, permitted none to come even to touching the woman herself, to say nothing of unlawful intercourse. Thus the woman, almost every single night and almost at every hour through the whole time, brought in ever new lovers; yet as soon as each tried to touch her, the spirit threw him far off onto the ground. At last, when the husband was returning and was still far from home, the spirit entrusted with the charge met him gladly, saying: “Your arrival is very welcome to me, for by it I am freed from the very restless labor which you imposed on me.” To whom the husband said: “Who, then, are you?” “I am,” he said, “Hudgin, to whom, when you were lately going away, you entrusted her to be protected; behold, I have guarded her for you, though with the greatest and continual labor, safe from adultery. But I beg you not to wish henceforth to hand her over to me to guard. For I would rather guard the pigs of all Saxony than your one wife herself; by so many tricks she tried to get around me, by so many ways and so many methods to misuse her own body.”

This spirit also performed innumerable other miracles, both serious and ridiculous, all of which cannot easily be written down; nor, if they were written, [unclear]. They say that by means of a ring made from laurel leaves, with certain other things added, he made [unclear] very learned for some time. At last, eliminated by the aforesaid bishop Bernard through ecclesiastical censures, he was compelled to withdraw from the province.

*1133.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Volmar, which was the year 1133 of the Lord’s Nativity, King Lothar set out for Rome with a very great army of princes, bishops, abbots, nobles, and knights, to restore Innocent to the apostolic see. Passing therefore through Italy, he took very many fortifications that resisted him; and at last, on the second day before the Kalends of May, he entered Rome with the highest favor. Since Anacletus, who was Peter Leonis, was now dead, he restored Innocent to the apostolic see. By him Lothar himself was consecrated emperor on the third day before the Nones of June, at the Lateran in the church of Saint John; after some wars and victories he returned to Germany, bringing with him as captive a certain prince of Italy.

In the same year, on the fourth day before the Nones of August, on the twenty-seventh day of the moon, at the sixth hour of the day, an eclipse of the sun occurred, to such an extent that stars appeared in the sky. Great irregularity of the air and an inundation of rains followed through the whole harvest-time. The cousin also of Emperor Lothar, having crossed [the river?] with Goitido his brother, Hermann of Cuch was killed.

*1133.*

In the thirteenth year of Abbot Volmar, Emperor Lothar celebrated the Nativity of the Lord at Cologne. There a rash tumult of the townsmen arose before him, and, before it had yet been quieted, he departed thence. But he celebrated Easter at Halberstadt with a great attendance of princes. There Magnus, king of the Danes, placed himself in the power of the emperor, gave hostages, and swore an oath that he and his successors would not obtain the kingdom except by permission of the emperor and of his successors. After this, allowed to return in peace to Dacia, on the day of holy Pentecost he was killed by his rivals, together with five bishops, sixty priests, and his father Nicholas. Meanwhile Emperor Lothar, celebrating the feast of Pentecost at Merseburg, performed with great solemnity and pomp the wedding of his daughter Gertrude, giving her as wife to Henry, duke of Bavaria, surnamed the Proud, a very strong and daring man.
