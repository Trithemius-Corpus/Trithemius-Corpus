*1430.*

In this year, which was the thirty-ninth of our abbot Bernard, Otto de Zegenhan, archbishop of Trier, died at Koblenz from an old disease of the stone; and, carried back to Trier, he was buried before the altar of the Blessed Virgin in the cathedral church: a truly magnificent man, endowed with singular devotion toward God, who governed his church excellently and did many good things. The head of St. Matthias the Apostle, which for many years had been kept in the castle of Ehrenbreitstein near Koblenz among the treasures of the pontiffs, he transferred with great devotion to the cathedral church of Trier, where it is kept in great veneration and honor in that same church down to the present day. In the same church of Trier he instituted the Mass of the blessed Mother of God, ever-virgin Mary, so that it should be solemnly sung there every day in perpetuity after matins by the choristers. He was a lover and benefactor of the poor, and a most powerful subduer of the proud. He was the first to reform the monasteries of our order around Trier, with much labor and expense; for this holy work he appointed Johannes Rode, taken out of the Carthusian house, as abbot of St. Matthias, as we said above. But after the death of the most reverend archbishop Otto, many evils followed in the church of God at Trier. For the canons, disagreeing in the election, the greater and sounder part elected Jacobus de Sirck, scholasticus of the cathedral church and provost of Würzburg, a good man and worthy of every honor. The other, weaker part, namely Friedrich de Cracovia, provost of Trier, and another canon called de Pittingen, elected Ulrich von Manderscheid. From this division the church of Trier suffered very great disadvantages. For Ulrich, although weaker in right, was nevertheless more powerful in arms, and with the aid of his people, and of the counts of Virneburg and other nobles, he held the castles and fortifications of the archbishopric against Jacobus, who was unequal to him in strength, and received the customary homages from the subject men of the church. Hence Jacobus, compelled by necessity, invoked the aid of the supreme pontiff. But the pope, after the parties had been summoned and the cases heard, rejected the election that had been made, and, setting both aside, assigned the archbishopric of Trier to Rabanus de Helmstat, bishop of Speyer, through the promotion of Ludwig, count palatine of the Rhine. From this arose infinite evils, and the church of Trier suffered damage amounting to more than 300,000 florins. For the counts, barons, and soldiers of the whole country, adhering to Ulrich, scorned the apostolic mandates; and, raging against Rabanus, they besieged for almost two years the city of Trier, which favored him, hurling stones and balls at it in vain from the neighboring mountains. But at last, with the matter unaccomplished, they withdrew in confusion. Finally the pope, understanding that his commands were being despised, placed the territory of the diocese of Trier under ecclesiastical interdict for almost four years. At length Conrad, archbishop of Mainz, Dietrich, archbishop of Cologne, and Friedrich, bishop of Worms, intervening in their stead, put an end to the disputes by friendly negotiations; and thus lord Rabanus peacefully held the archbishopric for the greater part for almost five years, after, that is, the four years during which those disputes had lasted. *The count of Virneburg attacks Rabanus.*

In this year, which was the fortieth of our abbot Bernard, Gottfried, prior of this monastery, died, the twenty-sixth in the order of priors, in the ninth year of his priorate. In his time there was either no observance of regular discipline in this monastery, or only a slight one, although abbot Bernard labored not a little for reform. After Gottfried, Friedrich was made prior of this place, the twenty-seventh since its foundation, a man of that time and a zealous defender of the customs which he found; and he presided for seven years.
