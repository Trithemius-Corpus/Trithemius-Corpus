*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

In the same year also Theoderic, count of Holland, died, and the province, together with the daughter of the deceased, was handed over to the count of Lo. William, Theoderic’s brother, taking this very ill, gathered soldiers, expelled that same count of Lo from Holland, and took his brother’s daughter.

In these times also Pope Innocent III composed five books of Decretals, which he ordered to be read in the schools and observed everywhere in legal proceedings; this was done, and is done down to the present day. Lupold, bishop of Worms, who had sought the archbishopric of Mainz, as we said above, adhered to King Philip against King Otto and Pope Innocent, and committed many evils throughout the whole province. In all the wars he was personally present with Philip his king against Otto; he fought as a knight, plundered the poor, and spared neither monasteries nor churches. And when the soldiers said to him, “Lord, it is not lawful for us to plunder churches and monasteries,” he would answer, “When you take away the bones of the dead, then you are plundering churches and cemeteries.” At last he was excommunicated by Pope Innocent and deposed from his pontificate because of the countless crimes he had committed. Finally, out of hatred for King Otto, he was restored to his office and benefice.

*1204.*

In the eighth year of Abbot Marquard, which was the year 1204 of the Lord’s Nativity, around the month of [unclear], King Philip, having assembled a very great army, entered Thuringia and burned and devastated a certain city of the landgrave, named Wizen[see?]; also [unclear] the king of Bohemia with a very great [unclear]. The landgrave, seeing the ruin of his land and that he could not resist the king’s anger, came to him as a suppliant through the intervention of certain princes, seeking his favor, and obtained it. After this, through the mediation of the archbishop of Trier and the bishops of Speyer and Constance, he gained over to himself Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, at a council at Andernach: in order to make the duke of Brabant and the remaining nobles of Lotharingia faithful to himself, he gave him nine thousand marks, restored Saalfeld, long before taken away from the church of Cologne, and promised that he would give very many other things. Enticed by these promises, Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, making little of the oath he had formerly sworn to Otto, and not fearing perjury or the pope’s excommunication, came after the feast of St. Martin to that same Philip with the duke of Brabant at Koblenz. Both offered him the oath of fidelity, which afterward did not turn out to his prosperity.

*The princes go over to Philip. Philip again gains Thuringia for himself.*

*1205.*

In the ninth year of Abbot Marquard, on the feast of the Epiphany in the year 1205 of the Lord’s Nativity, in the eighth indiction, King Philip held a great assembly of the princes adhering to him at Aachen, where the archbishop of Cologne came to meet him with very great display. There the king, after taking counsel with his men, so that the princes might not lose their free election according to the ordinance of antiquity, laid down the royal name and crown, and asked and obtained that he be re-elected by all in concord. Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, with great solemnity, together with Maria his wife, daughter of the emperor of Constantinople, again anointed and crowned him, while Otto was held by a very grave illness and deprived of the help of almost everyone. In this year the summer was dry, the winter harsh.

In the same year the most reverend Marquard, abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, after he had energetically and usefully governed this cenobium for eight years, six months, and twenty-one days, at last weakened by a subsequent dysentery, rested in the Lord on the thirteenth day before the Kalends of February, in the year of the Lord 1205, in the eighth indiction, and was buried, not without the tears of the brothers, before the altar of St. Nicholas. [Unclear], he triumphed over all. In many things he increased the revenues of the monastery; he restored the supply of resources [unclear] with many other structures; he began, in excellent stonework, the wall before the doors of the monastery, which, forestalled by death, he did not complete. For he was a man noble in blood and character, distinguished in intellect and wisdom, known to kings and princes, who in the time of the schism of the empire between Philip and Otto conducted himself so prudently toward all that he won merit before God and praise from men.
