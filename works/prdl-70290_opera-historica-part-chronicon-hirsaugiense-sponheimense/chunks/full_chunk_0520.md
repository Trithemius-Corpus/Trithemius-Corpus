After this the Palatine moved his army to the greater and the lesser village of Bockenheim, both well fortified with ditches and defenses, which belonged to the counts of Leiningen; he surrounded them both with a siege and took them in eight days, the nobles and knights with certain others having slipped away in flight, except for four men-at-arms, fifteen horsemen, and twenty foot-soldiers, who were found and captured, while some of the foot-soldiers were drowned. All the walls, fences, towers, and every fortification were destroyed, the ditches leveled, the houses burned, and both villages utterly destroyed and laid waste, on the fourteenth day of the month of June.

When these things had been done, the high-spirited Prince Palatine moved his army against Duke Ludwig and besieged the castle and town of Vimen, which that same Duke Ludwig held in pledge from the church of Mainz, and from which he had inflicted many losses on the Palatine. When the canons of Mainz heard this, fearing that if the Palatine took the castle it would be alienated forever from the church of Mainz, they intervened by way of agreement: namely, that the prince should allow the horsemen and foot-soldiers in the castle to depart with their goods; that he should also restore the castle and town to the church of Mainz, no longer to be granted into the hands of Duke Ludwig; and that he himself should receive from the church of Mainz four thousand florins for his expenses.

These things were done and agreed on the day of St. James the Apostle, in the year written above. From there the Palatine, with the very powerful army of his men, besieged Lambsheim, a town very strongly fortified by its site, waters, ditches, walls, and defenses. In it were thirty-four nobles and horsemen, and more than three hundred foot-soldiers hired for wages from the Swiss. When, after two days of siege, they had seen the assaults and strength of the Palatine forces, they obtained security to depart, with an oath that during this dissension they would do nothing against the Palatine, and, leaving the town and the horsemen behind, they departed. Then the prince took the town with a strong hand, captured Philip Luster, the prefect, with fifteen nobles, thirty-one horsemen, and twenty foot-soldiers, but had those foot-soldiers drowned. The citizens were compelled to swear to the arms of the prince on the Sunday after the feast of St. Peter in Chains.

After this the same high-spirited prince besieged Dürkheim, the greatest and most excellent among all the towns of the counts of Leiningen; for its defense and strength those counts themselves, for twenty continuous years, had spared neither labors nor expenses. The prince therefore placed a garrison in the monastery of nuns at Seebach, of our order, and with all effort, by engines, bombards, ditches, and various methods of war, he began to storm the town; and after many labors and dangers he received it in surrender on the Sunday after the feast of the Assumption of blessed Mary ever Virgin, in the year noted above. Its walls, towers, and all its fortifications, as is seen today, he leveled to the ground and utterly overturned. All whom he found there belonging to Duke Ludwig were led away as captives. The townspeople, however, rendered homage and an oath of fidelity and subjection to the same Prince Palatine. Among others three counts were captured: Philip and Diether, counts of Leiningen, and Peter, lord and a certain French count; also twenty-four men-at-arms were captured, one hundred and six horsemen, the best who were in the whole circuit, forty hired foot-soldiers, fifty-one citizens, and seventy men from the county of Leiningen stationed there for defense, who together with the townspeople were assessed by him for money. In this storming of Dürkheim nearly two hundred men, noble and non-noble, fell on the side of the Prince Palatine; on the side of the counts in the town, however, eighty-nine.

After these things the high-spirited prince moved his whole army against Duke Ludwig, and first besieged Sobernheim, a town of the church of Mainz pledged to Ludwig himself with others, as we said above. When the citizens heard this, they at once came into his surrender, knowing that they could neither resist so great an army nor have any help from their duke. Montzingen also, Merxheim, and Nosbaum in like manner, in the same year and on the same day, namely on the vigil of Bartholomew the Apostle, [were taken?] at the siege of the little castle of Landsberg and of the castle and village of Moscheln; and then for the first time Duke Ludwig sought pardon, since by now he had lost many castles and towns and was bereft of all auxiliaries. Agreement was therefore made between him and the Palatine, and on the Monday after the day of St. Aegidius the Confessor it was concluded at Heidelberg with the necessary letters and documents: namely, that all castles and other things which the Palatine had taken should remain in his power. This agreement did not please Emperor Frederick, but he could not break it, because Duke Ludwig thereafter would rather have had the emperor as an enemy than, at his command, offend the Palatine again. The other princes around him also, all fearing the Palatine’s strength, did not presume to mutter against him.
