For St. Ambrose says: In a virgin, let modesty everywhere be the companion of each virtue; it ought to be inseparable from virginity, so that [unclear] may be kept away and virginal honor preserved. Let a virgin's speech be prudent, modest, rare, not so much prized for eloquence as for shamefastness. Let all admire, when the virgin is silent, her bashfulness; when she speaks, her prudence; and in her conduct let them proclaim the discipline of a most religious life. Let your heart, O bride of Christ, monastic virgin, be pure from every stain of worldly desire; let it be clean from every spot of disordered affection, so that it think of nothing in this life except Jesus and love nothing except his sweetness. Let the sweet name of Jesus resound in your mouth whenever it must be spoken; let your whole mind sigh continually in his love; let no consolation be sought in this world except Jesus; let constant remembrance of Jesus be kept in the virgin's heart. For this above all befits Christ's bride: in love of him to keep her heart constantly occupied nowhere else, so that, truly burning in remembrance of him, she may be able to say: I languish with love, not worldly but heavenly, not carnal but spiritual, not for any mortal man but for Jesus Christ, the eternal King, who is judge of all the living and the dead, of whom the holy martyr Ignatius said in a certain letter: My love has been crucified. And let the monastic virgin, knowing that her love is the Lord Jesus Christ, and that he was piteously crucified for all of us, be always engaged in the exercise of his most bitter passion by reading, meditating, and suffering with him; and let her pass no day on which she does not at least once recall the labors endured for her by so great a loving bridegroom. For she is not a true lover of Christ who does not occupy her mind, even to tears, in meditating on his most bitter passion, but rather, given over to the laughter and frivolities of the world, forgets her most sacred lord and bridegroom. How, indeed, you ought to exercise yourselves usefully in remembrance of the Lord's passion, you have long since heard from us in many sermons. For the continual remembrance of Christ's benefits toward us is the greatest exercise of devotion and the most fruitful exercise of spiritual progress. Therefore, in order that you may be able to please him alone to whom you have devoted yourselves, all things that are transitory must be despised by you for love of him, because you cannot serve God and attend to the vanities of the world. Pray for me, sweetest daughters in Christ, that the Almighty may bring me back unharmed and keep me everywhere in his most holy will. About to give place to those who wrong me, I go into a far country, uncertain of the day of my return. Farewell in Christ.

From Speyer, on the 26th day of the month of August, in the Christian year 1505.

*XLI.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. Martin in Spanheim, to the prior of the community and to his sons in Christ, his most beloved brothers, with most loving [charity?].*

When on the twenty-seventh day of the month of August we were leaving [Neometum?], about to enter this March of Brandenburg, it had been our intention to hasten our return before the feast of St. Martin, and to betake ourselves to [unclear], to the most serene prince, until the rashness of our rivals against us should be brought to its proper end. But when we came to Berlin, the town of the most illustrious margrave's continual residence, on the eleventh day of the month of September, we were received kindly by him and are also being treated generously. Since we ought not resist his will, we have agreed to remain with him until the feast of Easter, and indeed not without advantage to ourselves, as we trust.

But lest the necessary change of plan be hidden from you, or some unfavorable suspicion about us arise among you, we have decided to send to you Brother Theodoric, our chaplain, this bearer of the letters, so that, when he reports our situation, you may know everything. Concerning our return to my monastery of Spanheim, brothers, this [unclear]: I could ever, if I wished, pursue the advantages of my own interest. There are not a few kings and princes known to me who are sympathetic; by their favor I could, if I wished, not only avenge the wrongs done to me, but also find sufficient provision for my state. But for love of him who endured death on the cross for us, I wish neither to avenge the insults done to me nor to abandon you, unless perhaps [I am] first abandoned by you. But if this happens, you will find that I have wandered so long through uncertainties not for my own sake, but rather for yours.
