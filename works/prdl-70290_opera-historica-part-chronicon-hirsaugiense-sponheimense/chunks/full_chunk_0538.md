... more zealously to acquire, Greek and Latin, so many and so great, that today in all Germany no library is said to be like ours; in it are held so many rare, so many secret, so many varied volumes, known to few. For today in this monastery the number of books is so great that one place does not suffice for storing them; for they are more numerous than can be estimated. Of their number we shall speak in the nineteenth year of John Trithemius, our abbot, because then a registration of the volumes was made by him. For the library of this monastery is today proclaimed in every land to such an extent that many princes, bishops, doctors, masters, nobles, and very distinguished men, not only from nearby but also from distant regions, stirred by the name and fame of John Trithemius the abbot and of his volumes, wished to visit this place and did visit it, men to whom previously not even the name of the monastery was known. Among these it will not be useless to mention even a few, briefly, who, more eminent than the rest, deigned to visit the present monastery because of Trithemius’s fame. John Camerarius Dalberg, bishop of Worms; John Capnion of Pforzheim; Conrad Celtis Protucius, poet laureate; Franciscus Bonomus of Trieste, secretary of the queen of the Romans, a notable orator and poet: these four men are most learned in every science, most erudite orators and poets, truly profound contemplators of theology, most skilled in the Latin, Greek, and Hebrew languages, distinguished no less for piety than for prudence. Some of these, not content to have seen the place and the man once, returned a second and a third time; the longer they remained here, the more they were held by desire for the volumes, the place, and the man.

But that most learned man, Jodocus Beysclidus, a patrician of the city of Aachen; also Henry Bunaugius, knight, well instructed in all scripture, secretary of the princes of Saxony; John Cuspinianus, a Frank, poet laureate, physician of the renowned city of Vienna in Austria; Andreas Stiborius, a Bavarian, theologian and most profound mathematician; Jacob Wimpfeling of Schlettstadt, theologian, notable orator and poet; Rutgerus Sieamber, canon of Deventer, a man of much reading; Aquinus, a Swede by nation, the subtlest of all mathematicians; Wilhelm Monapus, arithmetician, mathematician, and singular theologian; John Palyonidorus, a man of great study; Martheus Herbenus, inhabitant of Maastricht, eloquent orator and most excellent musician; Jacob, prior of the Wilhelmites at Bruges, historiographer; Aegidius Delphius, Parisian theologian and poet, most outstanding of all; John of Westphalia, theologian and most diligent historiographer; John Tholassus, a Bavarian, canon of Regensburg, jurist and truly admirable astronomer; Adam Werner of Themar, jurist and praised poet; Jason the Italian, poet; Jacob Draconcius, celebrated orator and poet; Jodocus Gallus of Rouffach, theologian, preacher of the church of Speyer, most pleasing orator and poet; Franciscus Wiler, poet and theologian; Gabriel Biel, most famous theologian; John Spegeler of the Order of Minors, theologian, most vigorous defender of Mary’s conception against the Maculists; Theoderic Gresmund the Younger, excellent poet and jurist; Mutianus Rufus, rhetorician, historiographer, and most learned poet; John Vigilius of Sondheim, jurist, patron of philosophers; John Lanshemius, provost of Deventer, a studious man composing many things.

All those written above, stirred by Trithemius’s fame, some from nearby places, some from remote ones, but all coming here from their own places, some once, some twice, and several as many as three times and more, came to see Trithemius and the library of this monastery. Among them were many others whose names do not come to memory, stirred by the same spirit; some of them, for a month, others for two or three, and several for the space of one year, with their expenses paid, remained in this monastery out of love for letters, both Latin and Greek, in which Abbot Trithemius was held to be abundantly furnished. But since he was a gentle man and a most upright lover of peace, and had heard that some of the brothers were murmuring because he tolerated either monks or even foreign secular men in the monastery for the sake of learning, although without detriment to the monastery, he dismissed them all. Afterward he could with difficulty be persuaded, although many offered themselves, doctors and other wealthy men, to take anyone on for instruction. For he had simple and untrained monks, among whom, apart from two or three, scarcely anyone who loved letters was to be found; for this reason he preferred to labor in writing with outsiders rather than with his own. But we, since we know that his life has been described more fully by a certain disciple and friend of this place, though an outsider, restrain our pen, especially since he is still among men, knowing that he, placed as it were on a summit, has not lacked the embitterment of those below, which harmed us and the monastery, not him.

*1484.*
