*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius, 43*

The bones of Charles the Great, once emperor, until then unknown to many, were sought and found at Aachen by Otto III, emperor.

In the same year Evergerus, archbishop of Cologne, died; Saint Heribert happily succeeded him in the archbishopric. He built, opposite the city of Cologne beside the river Rhine, where a castle had been destroyed, a monastery of our order in honor of Blessed Mary ever Virgin, which we call Deutz; in it he established an abbot and monks according to the rule of Saint Benedict, and enriched it with many estates and ornaments.

In the same year the emperor, with many cardinals, bishops, abbots, and princes, [unclear] was about to visit Poland with a strong army, among other affairs of the kingdom, the tomb of Saint Adalbert, bishop of Prague, lately martyred for the faith of Christ, as we said above. At that time it shone forth in Poland with innumerable and astounding miracles. He obtained one arm of the same martyr from Boleslaus, duke of Poland, and, receiving it with great devotion, placed it at Rome in the church of Saint Bartholomew. The same emperor also, in honor of the most holy martyr Adalbert, with the consent of the supreme pontiff Sylvester II, appointed his brother Gaudentius, a monk of our order, as first archbishop of the church of Prague, where his brother had been bishop. Since he was a learned and most holy man, by his preaching he converted many peoples round about to the faith of Christ.

In these times Ansfridus flourished, formerly a count, then a monk of our order, and from monk the eighteenth bishop of Utrecht. Others say that from bishop he was made a monk in the monastery of Saint Mary of our order, which he himself built. And this agrees more with the truth and with the chronicle of the people of Utrecht, which says that in the year of the Lord 1005, as a son chastened by God, he lost the light of his eyes and received the habit of sacred religion in the aforesaid monastery, now called Saint Paul’s, of which he was the founder, and persevered in holy purpose with much devotion and sanctity. He presided over the church of Utrecht for [unclear] years, and in his time did many good things for monasteries and churches.

After him Adelboldus, a monk, as some report, of Bobbio, of our order, ruled the church for eighteen years, a most learned and prudent man, who for some time was chancellor and counsellor of Emperor Henry II, of whom we shall speak in his own time. Exercising the dexterity of his talent, he wrote many distinguished little works. For he arranged the deeds of his lord Emperor Henry II in one volume; he was the first and only one after Charles the Great among the emperors to deserve to be enrolled in the catalogue of the saints. He also wrote various little works, both in verse and in prose, on the praises of the Holy Cross, of Blessed Mary ever Virgin, and of various other saints. He also waged many wars for ecclesiastical liberty against Theodoric, count of Holland.

Around these times a certain monk, returning from Jerusalem, was lodged for some time with a certain hermit in Sicily near Mount Etna, which was vomiting flames. When he had heard from him that in that same mountain, Vulcan, the souls of the dead were being tormented by flames, and that many of them were freed every day through the prayers of the faithful, and especially of the Cluniacs, he made it known to the abbot; by whose ordinance the commemoration of faithful souls was established in the aforesaid monastery on the day after the feast of All Saints, and [unclear], about which we have made mention elsewhere.

Remigius, abbot of Mettlach of our order in the diocese of Trier, also flourished in these same times, a man most zealous in the divine scriptures and eminently learned in secular letters. He maintained a public school of monks in his monastery, in which he instructed not only his own subjects but also monks sent to him from various other monasteries.

Theodorus, monk of Saint Matthias the Apostle, also flourished in these times: Theodorus, a monk of Saint Matthias of Trier, a contemporary and fellow-pupil of the above-mentioned monk Theodoric, a man distinguished in morals and learning. He presided gloriously for many years over the public school in the same monastery, and by writing various works took care to make his talent known to posterity. Of these there survives a collection of readings on the Apocalypse of John, not to be rejected. He composed various works on music and astronomy.
