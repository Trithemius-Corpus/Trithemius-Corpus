*Chronicle of Trithemius*

*A dead man came back to life and preached many things.*

[unclear], commonly called Mannendal, situated not far from here, is so named. In it there was a certain good and respectable man, named Adelbert, who had a wife and children, whom he supported by the labor of his hands. When he was sixty-two years old, he fell into very acute fevers; as they gradually increased slightly, he seemed to be near death. Therefore, after confession and fortified with the ecclesiastical sacraments, he came to his end, and on the aforesaid day, when the sun was already setting, he departed from human affairs. Thus dead, he lay silent the whole night, while his friends around the lifeless body performed the funeral rites with tears, according to custom. But when morning had come, and the sun was now rising, when everything that seemed necessary for burial had been prepared and he was just about to be buried, suddenly, in the presence of all who were there, he sat up alive; and when they all fled from him, he himself also rose from the place where he had been lying and, going at a rapid run to the church, remained fixed in prayer until the ninth hour of the day, nor would he give any answer to those who questioned him.

But when at last he had raised himself from prayer, looking around he saw the crowd standing by, wondering and asking what was happening concerning him. “God has done this well. O folly of men! O impiety! How hidden are the things that await them after this life!” Meanwhile the parish priest of that church, named Udo, who was archpriest, a learned and religious man and one who feared God, came up and said: “What is it, Adelbert? Were you dead at the end?” “I was dead,” he said; “by the mercy of God I am alive, as I was before; but hereafter I must live otherwise than I did before.” The priest said to him: “Did you see anything of the future life?” “I saw,” he said, “many dreadful things.” And when he was asked to tell what he had seen, he answered: “A law has been imposed on me, without which I can neither say nor do anything.”

But when he had been brought back into the priest’s house, he said to him: “Lord pastor, I have been ordered to disclose to you and tell you everything that I saw and heard. I cannot tell all the people, lest the secret mighty works of God come into derision among those who think according to the flesh.” Therefore the pastor sent a messenger here to the monastery and made everything known to Rupert, our abbot, asking him to deign to come without delay to Mandal and hear the mighty works of God. Taking with him John the prior, he hastened there; and a few of the villagers having been summoned, as many as the pastor there judged suitable, they sat in the presence of the man who had been dead, to hear what he would say.

Opening his mouth, he said: “When I was being pressed toward death by a very grave illness, and was lying thus, with all my senses darkened, it seemed to me as if there occurred a very great earthquake, in which heaven and earth clashed violently together. Suddenly my soul, made outside the body as if from darkness into light, in a way I do not know, could see the whole world all together at one time with the greatest clarity. O good God, how far removed is the opinion of mortals from the truth of that life which follows after the present one. My soul, then placed outside the body, was so bright that it beheld so clearly all things that are in the world: all provinces, cities, castles, villages, peoples, men, kingdoms, lands, mountains, hills, waters without matter, indeed the whole sum of the entire universe; and it understood all things properly and distinctly, all together at one time, so that I seemed never to have understood anything in this world so well.

“And what I am about to say is marvelous above all things. For although I knew that I had neither body nor eyes, nevertheless I saw everything that is in the whole world. I cannot now sufficiently wonder at my own ignorance, by which I no longer understand what I then understood. For the soul is a spirit; all things were done with me in the spirit. Now I cannot understand any of those things otherwise than if they had been shown to me through the senses.”

At this point Abbot Rupert, interrupting his words, said: “We understand that what he says is true, and there is no need to linger here longer, since it is established by reason and faith that none of the things which separated substances either [unclear] or suffer can come to our understanding without the service or comparison of the senses. But go on to say what you began to say.”

He therefore continued, saying: “When I stood thus within myself in the greatest wonder, I suddenly saw, as if from the right and the left, innumerable spirits coming toward me with force. Those who came from the right were good spirits, but those from the left were evil and perverse. The good angels, taking me up, suddenly lifted me upward into the air, as if in a moment, in the blink of an eye. It seemed to me that the whole air was filled with demons, who, like sparks flying thickly in the manner of snow, filled everything. Around me, wherever I looked, there was a dreadful fire and flame, which seemed to torture me so much that no suffering of this world ever seemed more grievous to me. Moreover, those malignant spirits, striving to snatch me away while the angels defended me, set before me the very grievous [unclear] of my sins.

“Therefore I was led to the judgment of almighty God, which I cannot remember without horror. What I saw and heard I cannot tell, nor can any mortal who has not seen those things believe it. There all my sins were bare, manifest, and open to all spirits, both evil and good, whatever I had committed in all my life, even in the smallest thought or deed; and for these they accused me most severely. But the very sins that I committed suddenly came back to my memory; I do not know how. Indeed, they themselves accused me so gravely that the accusation of the demons seemed lighter. It is not possible for me to say how heavily there are weighed and punished the things which I did not think were sins. I will say one thing about myself, by which the rest may be estimated. It happened twenty years ago, as I was going to Kreuznach, that I met a poor man asking, a half-beggar, who, when he had asked me the way, I do not know to what place, I said: ‘This is the direct road to the place—’
