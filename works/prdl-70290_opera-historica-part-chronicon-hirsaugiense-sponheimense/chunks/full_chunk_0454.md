*1398.* In the seventh year of our abbot Bernard, on the day before the Kalends of September, Henry Viner of Sponheim died, brother of Henry, monk and cellarer of this monastery, a singular supporter and benefactor of the present place; he himself also assisted Abbot Bernard in all the things that he undertook for the honor of the order.

In the same year Wenceslaus, king of the Romans, without the knowledge and will of the princes and electors, created Galeazzo Visconti of Milan as duke; and this was one of the articles on account of which he was afterward deposed from the empire.

*1399.* In the eighth year of Abbot Bernard, a great fire broke out in the city of Kreuznach, in the new town, and the ravening flame consumed more than half of it. And in the same summer the village of Sponheim was accidentally burned by fire; a large part of it perished in the conflagration, and it caused much damage.

In this year also great wars were waged in the land of Berg, and much damage was inflicted on the poor by burning and plundering; the village of Molenheim too was captured.

*1400.* In the ninth year of our abbot Bernard, on the twentieth day of the month of September, about the tenth hour or thereabouts, there assembled at Upper Lahnstein Frederick, archbishop of Cologne, Werner, archbishop [unclear], duke of Bavaria, with many other princes, and they deposed Wenceslaus, king of the Romans, from the kingship because of the following articles which they set against him. Item, that he alienated the city of Genoa from the Empire after receiving money, handing it over to the king of the French without the consent of the prince-electors and of the Empire. Item, that without the consent of the princes he created the Visconti of Milan as dukes, as we said above, assigning to them all Lombardy as a title. Item, that territories, dominions, and castles which had devolved to him by imperial right he alienated from the Empire, granting them to others as perpetual property. Item, that he martyred and killed honorable persons, both secular and spiritual, and especially a certain master in theology, and exercised many other cruelties, against learned men and clerics as well as against nobles and the poor. Item, that he fostered robbers, who despoiled merchants and all travelers, and it was safe for no one to go anywhere under his rule, not even by water. Item, that after receiving money he gave parchments under the seal of the royal majesty. Item, that he united and allied himself with the king of Poland, who took his stand with the pagans against the Teutonic lords in Prussia rather than with Christians. Item, that he summoned many men to his court without reasonable cause, solely in order to extort money from them unjustly and without any occasion of justice. Item, that he brought that praiseworthy University of Prague, which his father had established, almost entirely to nothing, expelling the doctors and holding them in hatred; because, always intent on feasting, drunkenness, and luxury, he did not care for the affairs of the kingdom, but, acting negligently in all things, fostered wars and seditions. Item, that, concerning the foregoing and many other crimes and negligences, although often admonished by the princes openly and secretly, he never took care to amend himself even in one matter. These things were done at Upper Lahnstein, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s incarnation 1400, on the twentieth day of the month of September, about the tenth hour or thereabouts.

Moreover, the duke of Saxony and the margrave of Brandenburg, though summoned for the appointed date, did not come; notwithstanding their absence, the aforesaid princes nevertheless deposed Wenceslaus by themselves as a man wholly useless for the kingship, since he had spent all his time in Bohemia in idleness and lust. After his deposition, the princes who had assembled unanimously elected Rupert, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, as king of the Romans. Frederick, archbishop of Cologne, anointed and crowned him around the feast of the Epiphany of the Lord in the greater church of Cologne. He presided over the Roman Empire for ten years: a man sufficiently Christian, a cultivator of justice, vigorous in military affairs, a lover of the clergy, and a most upright defender of the poor. Pope Boniface IX confirmed his election, holding valid the deposition of the slothful king Wenceslaus.
