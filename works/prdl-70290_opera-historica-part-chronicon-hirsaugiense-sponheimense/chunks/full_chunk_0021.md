*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

[unclear], a pupil of Rabanus, [wrote?] four books of the Gospels in verse in our Teutonic language, formed according to certain rules, so that very few are found today who understand his writings. He also wrote many other things, of which we made mention in the smaller catalogue of the Germans.

Werenbertus also, a monk of St. Gall, a man most learned in every kind of knowledge, both secular and divine, is held to have been distinguished among writers in these times; he composed many monuments of his talent for posterity to read.

Ruthardus too, a monk, one of the first of this monastery of Hirsau sent from Fulda, formerly a hearer and disciple of the monks Rabanus and Strabus, a man most learned in every respect, excelling in meter and prose, was among the writers of this age of a most celebrated name. As Meginfridus, monk of Fulda, writes in the Chronicle, he was a man conspicuous in every kind of knowledge and holiness, and of such learning and uprightness that, when Emperor Louis, after the death of Bishop Haymo, wished to ordain him bishop in his place for the church of Halberstadt, he refused to consent, uttering a memorable saying: “Give the bishopric to another who is fit for it; I have held the quiet of the regular life and the study of the Scriptures incomparably preferable to all the riches and honors of the world.” Among the other little works of his talent he wrote the passion of St. Boniface the archbishop, most beautifully in heroic verse, in two books. He was the first, among all whom I have read, who ventured to gloss with a commentary the Rule of our most holy father Benedict. The prologue of that exposition begins thus, after the opening of the Rule itself: “the third, logic, that is, moral; for physics, [unclear], that is, natural; the second, ethics, that is, rational; for physics is derived from what is physis, nature,” etc. He wrote, in addition, several little works on music, geometry, arithmetic, and the other arts of humanity. For he himself was the first, in the monastery of St. Aurelius, as Meginfridus is witness, to begin to have the monks read the divine Scriptures and secular letters, according to the custom of Fulda. He was indeed so learned in every kind of knowledge that not only monks from other monasteries, but also secular persons, flocked eagerly to hear him.

*865.*

He died full of days and holiness in the twelfth year of Abbot Gerung, which was the year of the Lord 865, in the fourteenth indiction, and was buried in the church of St. Aurelius on the eighth day before the Kalends of November.
