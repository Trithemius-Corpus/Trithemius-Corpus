*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

...who through uncleanness make themselves excluded from service at the altar. Concerning those fallen persons who have sacred orders, however, it must not by any means be asserted that, as they ought to be considered with voluntary humility, they can no longer in any way make use of those same orders, if, appeasing God by the sacrifice of a troubled spirit and of a contrite and humbled heart, they have earnestly striven to advance in virtue. For neither before God is anyone's office rejected because of a fault which he himself forgives, nor among men is the dignity of sacred order discolored because of a person whose sin they themselves do not know; for by these two causes a fall of the flesh bars access or return to sacred orders.

But since it is almost always hidden whose iniquities have been remitted, whose sins covered, and whose by no means, it is always safer for one conscious to himself of a crime, and [unclear], to abstain through praiseworthy humility than to presume through culpable security. For the former can always be done without God's disapproval; the latter ought not be done unless God approves it. But anyone who grants assent to this opinion, by which after a fall return to the office of sacred order is allowed, unless he is supported by some reason or by the authority of the Scriptures, wishes to attribute agreement to the legal epistle of blessed Pope Callistus directed to all the bishops established throughout Gaul, and to blessed Gregory to Secundinus [unclear]; for there they establish this opinion with such firm and almost identical reasons and authorities that it in no way needs the proof of others. But since the same blessed Gregory forbids this very thing in certain other epistles of his, lest he be contrary to himself, he is understood to have forbidden it in open cases, but to have allowed it in hidden cases after fitting penance. For a crime which is neither imputed by God nor thought to exist by men does not reject nor disfigure, as we have already said; nevertheless, in such a matter it is safer for anyone before God to fear more than to [unclear].

That he must necessarily be prohibited who of his own accord reveals a hidden crime by humble confession I by no means think, lest the saving power of confession be obstructed for many; for who would be able to choose it at all, if by this occasion he should be led from a hidden [unclear] into the certainty or suspicion of some crime? For it is established that they should confess their sin, but no one is compelled by custom, without his own consent, to cease from the office of his order. Rather, he seems guilty of a great crime who [unclear], because by thus compelling [unclear] he drives and compels many, still not confessed, to fear and flee confession.

For it is plain enough how much less evil it is for any sinner, after an offering [unclear] and with hope [unclear], to approach the altar with a humble mind, than with hatred of confession, despair of pardon, a hardened heart, desire, or even as if [unclear], to thrust himself in. Therefore he to whom permission is granted is by no means [unclear]; he commands a lesser fault, in which he has involved another not by consenting but by prudently guarding against a greater one. But he would seem to be a participant in, and author of, the greater crime, if by helping he drives many, even if unwillingly, yet inordinately by fearing more what is lesser than what is greatest. But it is manifest that those who, by humble contrition, trample upon proud shame, denouncing themselves and consenting of their own accord to the judgment of cessation, minister much more safely when permitted, or even ordered, than those who, with human shame, refuse justly to judge themselves between God and themselves. Although this is thus allowed, it is very dangerous to forbid the unwilling, as has been said above, rather than the willing. And since it is necessary for both, either to abstain for a time [unclear], because those who do not gladly cease from that to which they have not well advanced are then to be relaxed; but those who abstain of their own accord are even to be recalled to that which they do not presume. For in the former, if they are recalled, presumption is cloaked under the covering of obedience; but in the latter humility is aided by the strength of obedience. This renewal is not to be undertaken indifferently, but is to be carried out with great confession of the prelate receiving it and with discretion.

For it often happens that someone does not shrink from the office assigned to him through horror at the crime committed, nor overcome human shame by a life of humility, but through the darkness of folly, the very hard obstinacy of shamelessness, [unclear], or through negligence does not desire his office. But a man of this sort is never either to be recalled to sacred office or [unclear], unless the prelate is moved not so much by [unclear] as by the humility of supplication, by the intercession of a contrite change of life; then indeed, if the prelate, comforting him not so much concerning any merits as concerning the great mercy and multitude of the compassions of God, sends him back to his office or invites him, he seems to have acted not so much rashly as prudently.

For in matters of this kind pusillanimity must often be anticipated, suspicion of contempt excluded, benevolence shown, compassion displayed. In this way those who are such persons, the more kindly they are advanced to perfection, the more effectively they are bound more strictly in a good purpose. I could have inserted more than has first been written here, but because the brevity proper to a letter, which protests that it has already extended itself too much, does not allow it, I have simply set forth, at your fatherhood's command, what I thought about the matters asked. May almighty God so direct us in these and in all things that must be done by us in the uprightness of truth that for them he may repay himself as the reward. If I were present before you and your holy congregation, of which, by the merits of many, I daily hear greater things in concord, from which much good is reported and eagerly sought, I would prostrate my whole body with a suppliant mind, so that you might grant me again and more fully in fraternal charity to be a perpetual participant in the good things which God works in your holy monastery. But since, being very far away, I cannot do this, I presume, from [unclear], to ask of your holiness that you ask this very thing for me, not with body humbled but with kindly [unclear]; not concerning merits, but to ask of your holiness that you ask this same thing for me and grant it to me with that [unclear], and that you ask it for me from the same your sons and grant it with that.
