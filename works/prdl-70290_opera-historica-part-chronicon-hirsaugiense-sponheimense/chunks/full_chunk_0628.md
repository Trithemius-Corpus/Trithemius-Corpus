Your fatherhood knows our poverty, and that the temporal substance by which we might for any longer nourish so great a multitude of persons serving God, burdened with services to others, is very slender, and that provision has been made for us in no way sufficiently. Therefore, bending our knees to the ground, we all ask that, if it seems advisable to your fatherhood, you speak some good word on our behalf to the Archbishop of Cologne, and one conducive to our advantage, so that he may intervene for us with the Count and, by his intercession, see to it that so heavy a yoke of Pharaoh be removed from our necks, or at least mitigated somewhat. I send with the bearer of these letters the procuratorial document in my case, together with the brother, just as you instructed me. I beg you to be willing to arrange the rest in such a way that I am not entirely deprived of my paternal inheritance. Farewell happily. Given from Seebach, in the year 1505, on the 24th day of the month of July.

*XXIX.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to Roger Sicamber, his sweetest brother.*

On the 18th day of this month of July, in the morning at about the tenth hour, our most serene monarch Maximilian, King of the Romans, left Cologne with many princes, about to undertake an expedition against your Sicambrians. They all went down armed by ship along the Rhine, directing their line first toward Arnhem, the town of your Guelders, which the royal army had already for some time surrounded with a hard siege. The princes who had gone down in the ships with the King, together with their retainers, were these: Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, my prince-elector; Frederick the Margrave, his paternal uncle, who resides at Ansbach; Louis, firstborn son of Philip, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, our prince-elector; Frederick, Duke of the Saxons, prince-elector; John his brother, Duke of Saxony; George, Duke of Saxony; likewise the Duke of Mecklenburg; the Duke of Brunswick; the Duke of Luneburg; the Duke of Julich; the Landgrave of Hesse; and many other princes, dukes, and counts, whose names I did not observe. When they came to Arnhem, the townspeople at once surrendered themselves to the King, and all the Sicambrians, struck by fear, swore submission to King Maximilian. But the Duke of Guelders himself also accepted the royal conditions, and peace was restored in the land to men after a long interruption. On the 28th day of July, however, the King, returning to Cologne together with the princes, entered in the same order and array in which he had gone out. On the following day all the bells throughout all Cologne were rung for many hours, for congratulation on peace and concord. These are the things that were done with your Sicambrians; I did not wish the sequence of them to be unknown to you. There are, however, very many who do not believe that the Sicambrians will keep their promises to the King, because the people readily despises oaths and is always too prompt for novelties. But between the Archbishop and the citizens of Cologne it is not yet certain that any agreement has been made, although it has been attempted several times. These are the things, Roger, which seemed to us to be written this time. We ourselves also shall very soon follow the letters themselves to Neometum. Farewell, and pray for us. From Cologne, on the 29th day of the month of July, in the year of Christians 1505.

*XXX.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to Johannes Capellarius, mathematician, greetings.*

My departures from Cologne are now already near at hand; therefore, if it is irksome to come to us, farewell. This morning my most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, left Cologne, to return to his own territories through Westphalia and Saxony. I shall follow him after three days, when first certain letters and affairs concerning my person have been dispatched in the royal chancery. We ask, therefore, if it can be done, that before we leave here you visit us once more, so that we may bid one another farewell in brotherly charity, especially since we know not at all whether henceforth we shall ever see one another mutually in bodily sight. For I have been sworn into the March of Brandenburg, wholly uncertain about my return, chiefly for this reason: that, having been too gravely offended by the insults of serious rivals, I know that I could not easily be persuaded to return to Sponheim, although, if I wished, there would be very many at hand who would avenge these insults with signal vengeance. But I long ago persuaded myself that the Lord’s precepts must be obeyed and that injuries are not to be avenged; rather I shall imitate that saying spoken by the Lord Jesus to the apostles: “If they have persecuted you in one city, flee into another.” For we must have greater care for the soul than for the body, according to which we were made in the image of God.
