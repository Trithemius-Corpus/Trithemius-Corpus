*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

...with the permission and blessing of their venerable pastor, withdrawing to the hermitage of a neighboring solitude, and imitating the purpose of the ancient anchorites, they despised all the comforts of this world for love of the heavenly kingdom. This institution of arduous life was not permitted to be undertaken indiscriminately by just anyone, but only by those whom the holiness of their past life, under the testimony of all the brethren, together with more advanced age and experience in fighting against the devil, had commended. We read that some of these men, even after death, were not without miracles, to which even an angelic vision gave authority. Indeed, so holy in these times was the life of the monks of Hirsau that they admitted none into their fellowship except holy men; or certainly, having received them, they tested them more harshly and sanctified them according to the Rule. No one among them, unless he was good and sought God in truth, could remain, since he necessarily would have had to endure as many very severe censors of his wickedness as he found monks in the monastery. For this was the greatest desire of them all: to seek God in truth, and, despising transitory things, to love him above all things. Therefore, since all were of one and the same purpose, they could by no means endure among themselves a man of another will. But now at last let us fittingly call back the pen to the history that has been interrupted for a little while.

Therefore, when Pope Urban had died, as we said before, in the eighth year of Gebhard, and in the one thousand ninety-ninth year of the Lord, Reiner, an abbot of our order, first succeeded in the pontificate by election. He was called Paschal II, an Italian by birth, a devout and steadfast man, who by wisdom and diligence steadfastly freed the Roman Church from the tyranny of the wicked. He sat eighteen years, five months, seven days. He was descended from the Roman nobility, of holy conduct and good testimony; from being abbot and cardinal of Saint Clement, both by the designation of Pope Urban when already at the point of death, and by revelations of the saints, he was elected by the unanimous consent of all.

*1101.*

In the ninth year of Abbot Gebhard, which was the one thousand one hundredth year of the Lord's Nativity, Wibert of Ravenna, invader of the Apostolic See, died: a man indeed distinguished in talent, eloquence, nobility, and dignity of person, but then of no good use either to Rome or Ravenna. He, who mounted above one man still living, although he was compelled, as they say, himself outlived three men succeeding one another in turn, being an exile from both sees, namely the Roman and that of Ravenna.

Some of his supporters testified among the people that divine lights had shone at his tomb. Therefore the supreme pontiff Paschal, already named, inflamed with zeal for God, ordered that he be dug up and cast into the Tiber; and this was done.

In the same year Godfrey, king of Jerusalem, died; his brother Baldwin was compelled by the election of the people to succeed him in the kingdom.

Otto, bishop of Strasbourg, also died.

*1102.*

In the following year Hartwig, archbishop of Magdeburg, who by another name was called Udo, died by sudden death. Concerning him that terrible sentence of the strict judge Jesus Christ is circulated, who in the aforesaid church of Magdeburg, called in Greek Parthenopolis, that is, [unclear].

[unclear] but in the order of bishops of the same diocese he was the eighth; he governed with energy, by word and example, the church committed to him by God. His most holy life, written in a clear discourse, is extant; his feast is observed on the day before the Kalends of October. He was buried before the high altar of the aforesaid monastery of Saint Michael, in which he had been a monk.

In the same year Conrad the king, son of Emperor Henry, of whom we made mention above, died in Italy and was buried at Florence.

Rupert, bishop of Bamberg, also died. In his place was appointed Otto, from the monastery of the community of Monk's Mount outside the walls of the same city, a man of our order and of most holy conduct, who was famous for many signs and miracles in life and after death. Because he was the son of a most powerful count, he founded a certain monastery of our order in the diocese of Würzburg, which is called [Vraugia?], in honor of Saint Lawrence the martyr, and magnificently endowed it with estates and possessions for the support of the monks. The first abbot of this monastery of [Vraugia?], appointed by Saint Otto, was Eckhard, a venerable monk.
