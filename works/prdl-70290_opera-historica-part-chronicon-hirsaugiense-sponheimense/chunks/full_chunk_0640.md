*Familiar Letters*

...we have often desired, that it might be permitted us to imitate true and holy wise men, who, through abstraction, truly united [unclear] to themselves, with the most ardent desire of love, held the path of salvation toward God. What have we to do with the world, since we are soon to depart from here? Behold what reward the lovers of the world have given me, because, as a prelate of monks, I could not serve God without the world. Never, as you know, have I sought anything worldly; I have loved nothing earthly; I have fled and despised all the pleasures of the world; I have suffered persecution from men undeservedly, without demerit, without cause, without fault. But almighty God, whom alone I desire to please, has permitted this tribulation upon me neither unjustly nor without cause; and I give thanks to his goodness, because in truth I have recognized that this injury of my rivals against me is of no little profit to me. I accept what is mine with patience, insofar as the Lord Jesus grants it. Those who inflicted insult before men upon one who did not deserve it may see with what zeal they were led to inflict it. I am not moved by the injury done to me, while I seem [unclear], but with tranquillity of mind I can say: A mind conscious of right despises the falsehoods of fame, knowing as true what they, credulous, have disturbed into blame. Have no doubt about me, most loving teacher: I shall do, according to my strength, what befits a lover and disciple of Christ, and with a tranquil mind I shall bear the things he has set before us to be borne. Why should I not bear patiently, who am a Christian monk, not ignorant of the great reward? Pray for me, and farewell. I wrote this from the city of Neumagen on the twentieth day of the month of August; I arranged for it to be presented to you, by Narcissus the physician, at Paris in the house of Carolus Bovillus, theologian, born at Saint-Quentin, whom you know, our friend; nor do I doubt that it will be faithfully delivered to you. Farewell again. In the year of Christ 1505.

*XXXIX.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Carolus Bovillus of Saint-Quentin, theologian at Paris, greetings.*

I was surprised, Carolus Bovillus, at Narcissus, the bearer of this letter, because he departed from us for Paris without your knowledge, since I wished to have letters from you. Yet he reported that you are well; from him you will hear the state of our affairs, and what we have in mind concerning these things. We remind you of your promise made last year, when you were with us in our monastery of Sponheim for fourteen days: that you should complete as soon as possible those very profound questions of sacred Scripture, the headings of which we long ago set down at Sponheim, and transmit them to us, as you promised. For they will be for us an everlasting memorial of your name, a mutual testimony of affection. Everything you set forth in letters delights us greatly, because, after the manner of the ancient doctors, you are a solid, lucid expounder of truth, neither excessive through multiplication of words nor cut short by deficiency in matters that are necessary. The things you wrote about the intellect greatly pleased me. For they contain the true theology of Christians, pure and complete, which confers knowledge on the mind and desire for the highest good on the affection, existing in itself pure, whole, bright, without the scars of external traditions. Never has the curiosity of certain men pleased me, who mingle human things with divine, and defile sacred Scripture, which is pure and sufficient for us in itself, with the loquacity of the Gentiles. Against these blessed Jerome speaks in a letter to Pope Damasus: One disputing from the Scriptures ought not to seek out the arguments of Aristotle; nor should a little stream be drawn from the river of Tullian eloquence; nor should ears be soothed by the little flowers of Quintilian in a scholastic declamation. Rather, a discourse of plain, everyday likeness is necessary, redolent of no lucubration, which explains the matter, sets forth the sense, and makes obscure things manifest. For the Scripture of God is manifest; the testimony of God is lucid, granting wisdom to little ones. The divine letters do not need folds, nor enfoldings, nor refoldings, because these do not grant wisdom to little ones, but leave their minds in confusion and compel them, striving through their whole life, to wander from men. Not those who fold, not those who refold, but those who explain and elucidate me, says sacred Scripture, will have eternal life. Farewell. From Neumagen, on the twenty-second day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1505.
