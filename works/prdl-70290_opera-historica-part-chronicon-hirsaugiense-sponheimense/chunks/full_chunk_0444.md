1370.

In the twentieth year of Philip our abbot, Gerlach of Nassau, archbishop of Mainz, died, and in the election of a successor there was very great difficulty. For first Cuno, archbishop of Trier, was postulated for the church of Mainz; under Henry of Virneburg, Gerlach’s predecessor, he had for some time been its administrator. Content with his own church, he refused the dignity offered to him. Afterwards Adolf of Nassau was elected by the chapter; against him the emperor sought to have his nephew John, son of Wenceslaus duke of Luxembourg, whom the common people called the Gallic, promoted as archbishop of the church of Mainz, a good and vigorous man, who died poisoned by venom.

In the same year lord Cuno, archbishop of Trier and administrator of the church of Cologne, obtained from the aforesaid chapter of Cologne that they unanimously elected Frederick of Saarwerden, his nephew and their canon, as archbishop of the church of Cologne, in a castle not far from Koblenz, called the Chapel: a young man indeed in years, but of good promise and singular prudence. Ordained by Pope Urban V at Rome on the fourteenth day of the month of November, in the presence of the cardinals, he presided for forty-four years, quite usefully and vigorously, and did many good things for his church.

In the same year Emperor Charles IV, now heavy with age and consulting his own repose, obtained from the princes that his son Wenceslaus, king of Bohemia, was made king of the Romans; with him he governed the Roman Empire for eight years.

1371.

In the twenty-first year of Philip our abbot, on the vigil of St Bartholomew the Apostle, a great battle took place between Wenceslaus, duke of Brabant and Luxembourg, brother of Emperor Charles IV, and William, duke of Jülich, near Baesweiler. In it the duke of Jülich emerged victor, and led away Wenceslaus, duke of Brabant, captive with many other nobles and counts, from whom he extorted an incredible sum of money. But after he had held the duke of Luxembourg captive for eleven months, through fear of his brother the emperor he released him free without exaction of money. In this battle more than eight thousand fell on both sides, among whom there were more than forty in number of the knightly class. In the same war Edward, duke of Guelders, who had betrothed the daughter of Albert, count of Holland and duke of Bavaria, was struck in the forehead by an arrow by his own servant called Hermann Leers, whose wife he was abusing, and died. When he was dead, his brother Reynald, released from ten years’ custody, was again accepted by all the people; since he was very fat, surviving scarcely three months, he died, and the stock of the dukes of Guelders failed in him, and William duke of Jülich obtained the duchy.

1372.

In the twenty-second year of Philip our abbot, Peter of Schönberg died in the fourteenth year of his priorate. In his time, and thereafter for many years, there was almost no discipline of regular observance in the present monastery. For they were all proprietors, and cared little for the integrity of monastic conduct. After Peter, Abbot Philip appointed as twenty-third prior of this monastery Frederick, a professed monk, who presided in the priorate for eighteen years, a worldly and secular man, who cared either nothing at all or [unclear] for religion.

In the same year John, archbishop of Mainz, son of Wenceslaus duke of Brabant and Luxembourg, nephew of Emperor Charles IV, died in the castle of Eltville, poisoned by venom, as it seemed. When he was dead the chapter elected as archbishop of Mainz Adolf of Nassau, son of the sister of the late archbishop Gerlach, who before John, as we have said, had been elected. But Pope Gregory XI, wishing to elevate Louis, son of the margrave of Meissen, bishop of Bamberg, as archbishop of Mainz, as he had promised him, denied confirmation and the pallium to justice, as was reported. Adolf, aided by his friends, though not confirmed, obtained the places and fortifications of the archbishopric, and for almost seven years litigated at Rome with Louis. At length Louis himself perished, overtaken by fire, and Adolf, confirmed as archbishop, possessed the church of Mainz in peace. He presided, from the time of his election until his death, for almost sixteen years.

Also in the same year Emperor Charles, with the empress, bishops, ten dukes, nine counts, ten knights, eighty-two nobles, one thousand one hundred horsemen, and another innumerable multitude, came to Aachen; and, after an imperial court had been held there, he freed his brother William, duke of Brabant and Luxembourg, from captivity under the duke of Jülich by threats, not by money, whom he had earlier refused to release for a great sum.
