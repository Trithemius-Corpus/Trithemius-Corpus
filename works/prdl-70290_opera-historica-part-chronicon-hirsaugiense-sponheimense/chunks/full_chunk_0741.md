*Familiar Letters, XLII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St James in the suburb of the city of Wurzburg, sends abundant greetings to Master Matthaeus Herbenus, most upright of friends.*

[unclear]

The excessive distance of places causes me to be able to send letters to you rather rarely; yet I am not therefore separated from you in spirit, although in body I am far absent from you, whom I have always loved in the true charity of Christ and set among my foremost friends. Two years have passed since I sent my last letter to you from Cologne Agrippina; with it I had also sent a copy of my letter to Briselius, our common friend, so that you might be made more certain about the things that were then being done with me. Meanwhile, for lack of messengers, I have written nothing to you, nor have I seen any letters of yours, which I think has happened for the same reason. When the assembly of princes at Cologne had been dissolved, I entered the March of Brandenburg with the most serene prince Elector Joachim, and I stayed with him for nine months.

But afterwards, having returned to Budoris with the intention in mind of returning to Spanheim, when I had plainly learned that my brothers, Melantius the prior, whom you know especially well, together with my Cynonotian rivals, had conspired against me [unclear], I changed my plan; and, having accepted the abbacy offered to me, in which I now live, that of St James near Wurzburg, I resigned Spanheim with its wicked monks. For what was I to do in the midst of rivals, who were always carrying on either hidden or open enmities against me, so that there could never be any security for me, but continual suspicion of plots would immediately arise on both sides, according to the saying of Achilles to Hector in Iliad X, speaking thus:

[unclear]

[Greek unclear]

*Paraphrase.*

There is no firm concord between lions and men,
Nor do wolves and sheep have one mind,
But they devise evils against one another on both sides;
So it will not be that I and you love each other mutually.

The fathers of our order interposed their efforts and tried to call us back to mutual concord, and with all their strength attempted to persuade me to return to the monastery; but it did not seem safe to me to trust enemies reconciled by their necessity, not by their will, remembering the wisdom of Pittacus, who finely warns: *[Greek unclear].* That is:

[unclear]

Nor should you regard an enemy as your friend.

And Menander:

[unclear]

[Greek unclear]

that is:

You will think speech from an enemy friendly.

I therefore yielded to envy and rivalry quite seasonably, and I do not regret it, having by God's favor obtained good quiet and peace, where henceforth it may be freer for me to be attentive to myself. For I have always hated the tumultuous occupations of the world, and have loved rather solitude of place and tranquillity of mind, even to the envy of certain men, as you yourself are not unaware, who for not a few days once stayed with me in the monastery of Spanheim for love of Greek letters, and saw quite thoroughly both my own habits and those of my brothers. But I shall dismiss these matters. Rather I shall set forth for you the circumstances of our friends, since I do not doubt that you very gladly wish to know them.
