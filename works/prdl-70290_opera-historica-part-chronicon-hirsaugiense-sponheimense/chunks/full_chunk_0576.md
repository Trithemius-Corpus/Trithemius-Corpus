*Sponheim.*

The Archsteward and most worthy prince-elector summoned to Heidelberg all the prelates, bishops, abbots, counts, nobles, and communities of the cities and towns of his principality, and with them held counsel and negotiation concerning certain conditions pertaining to the reform of peace between himself and the princes of the empire.

In the same year noted above died Berthold, of the counts of Henneberg, a prince worthy of eternal memory and the most prudent of all men in his time, of great wisdom, and also of much experience in conducting the affairs of the Church and the empire. He presided well over his church, recovered many things that had been alienated, paid its debts, and loved peace.

He was succeeded in the pontificate by the dean of the greater church, Jacob of Liebenstein, a Swabian by birth, a good and very modest man, who did not complete a fourth year in the archbishopric, being afflicted with stones throughout the whole period of his rule and held back by various bodily illnesses.

In the year noted above, on the first day of the month of April, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of this monastery, setting out from the monastery in the morning at about the seventh hour with the messenger of the prince palatine and one servant and [unclear], took the road toward Heidelberg, having been summoned by the command of that prince and likewise by letters, in the matter of the burned monastery of Limburg, to give counsel that it be built at Wachenheim. At that time, however, he was rather weak in body, suffering from tertian fevers. When he had come to Heidelberg, disordered by the intemperate air, his illness was increased, to such an extent that he could not return as quickly as he had arranged. While he was thus ill at Heidelberg, on the fifth day after his departure from here, at the instigation of the Devil, there occurred here a grave disturbance, which, judging it very injurious to himself, he bore heavily. May almighty and merciful God pardon all who either gave counsel for this evil or aid. This deed, undertaken very rashly and without counsel, provided the abbot with cause for many tribulations and persecutions, and stirred up more rivals against him.

But he, since he was cautious through prudence and endowed with singular patience, committed the whole matter to be settled by divine mercy. Thereafter he has not returned here to the monastery up to the present day, not because he was prohibited by anyone, but because he feared that afterward he could never have peace with his rivals. Therefore, giving place to wrath, he left the place out of love of quiet, as we shall say later.

Also in the year noted above a great assembly of princes was held at Cologne at the beginning of the month of July, summoned by royal mandate, as we said above, for the confirmation of peace and concord, and it lasted until the end of the same month. In this assembly the following princes appeared: Maximilian, king of the Romans; Hermann, archbishop of Cologne; Jacob, archbishop of Trier; Lorenz, bishop of Würzburg; likewise the bishop of Bamberg, the bishop of Utrecht, the bishop of Liège, the bishop of Gurk, Petrus Bonomus, bishop of Trieste, and certain others whose names do not occur to us at this time.

Furthermore, the names of the secular princes then present are these: Philipp, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, elector of the empire, and his two sons, Ludwig and Friedrich; Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg, elector of the empire; Friedrich, duke of Saxony, elector of the empire, and his brother Johann; Friedrich, margrave of Brandenburg, who lived in Ansbach, paternal uncle of the aforesaid elector Joachim; Heinrich, duke of Brunswick; Erich, duke of Lüneburg; Wilhelm, duke of Jülich; Heinrich, duke of Mecklenburg; and certain others with many counts and nobles.

When he was about to go from the March of Brandenburg to the aforesaid assembly, Joachim, the prince-elector named above, sent ahead a mounted messenger with letters to our abbot Johannes Trithemius, who was then staying ill at Speyer in the house of the abbot of Limburg. By these letters he asked that he come down to him at Cologne, sending him travel money. Trithemius gladly agreed to the request of so great a prince, in whose eyes he had already before found great favor. Therefore, although he was still weak in part, and on account of a fall with his horse was suffering from a flux in his left leg, and for that reason limped a little, nevertheless, to please the prince, he hired a carriage and together with the messenger went down to Mainz, and from there by boat as far as Cologne, where he remained for a whole month with that margrave.

But in truth, since he could not yet fully know the authors of the injuries done to him, he resolved to delay his return to the monastery for some time, until he should see what the intention of his rivals was aiming at. When this became known to the often-mentioned prince, he urged him more insistently to go with him to the lands of the Margraviate of Brandenburg, to stay with him for some months. Trithemius did not wish to do this without the counsel of the illustrious prince Philipp, count palatine, whose great kindness he had experienced both then and before.
