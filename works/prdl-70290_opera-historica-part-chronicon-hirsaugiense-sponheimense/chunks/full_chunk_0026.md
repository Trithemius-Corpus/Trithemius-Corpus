*How great was the ancients' diligence in letters.*

Whenever an abbot did not have in his own monastery a monk suited to undertake this office, it was no cause for shame to seek a suitable one from some other monastery. For they considered it the greatest disgrace to neglect the teachable minds of monks; and, so that they might instruct them well in morals and letters, they spared neither labor nor expense. Thus that most learned monk Sigebert of Gembloux was for a long time instructor of the monks in the monastery of St. Vincent at Metz, although he belonged to another monastery. So too Strabo, a monk of Fulda, in Hersfeld; Notger of St. Gall in Stavelot; Albinus, an English monk, in Fulda; Milo of Saint-Amand in Corbie; and many others in other monasteries held the office of teaching for a time where they had not begun their monastic conversion.

This most holy custom first began in England, where that venerable monk and priest Bede, made instructor of monks, left after him many very learned disciples.

*How useful the study of letters is for monks.*

How greatly this instruction of monks benefited our order no one is ignorant who has studied the histories of the ancients with some care. For many monks, because of their incomparable knowledge of letters, were raised to the honor of the episcopate, and they wonderfully adorned our order by life and learning. For even if the study of letters conferred nothing on the order except a useful occupation for monks, it would nevertheless deserve to be promoted by us with the greatest respect; for unless the mind of a cloistered person is occupied by some agreeable exercise, it slips into many and varied miseries. Understanding this well, the holy fathers wished the reading of the Scriptures to be altogether familiar to monks.

*The body of St. Aurelius is hidden beneath the earth.*

In the church of St. Aurelius he made a subterranean [chamber], in which he hid the body of that saint, together with a tablet containing an inscription of his name and dignity, so carefully that it could not have been found by any human being. It remained there, altogether unknown, down to the time of Count Adelbert.

For not long afterwards, when the Northmen had been driven from the empire, the Hungarians began to devastate Bavaria, Swabia, and Saxony; and since battle after battle against them followed throughout every province of Germany, the body of the most holy prelate necessarily remained concealed in safety.

*Hatto, abbot of Augia, becomes archbishop of Mainz.*

The same man was obscure in lineage, distinguished by no nobility, but keen in intellect; regarding him it is difficult to judge whether he was better in counsel or worse. He presided over the church of Mainz for twenty-one years.

*892.*

In the third year of Abbot Haderad, which was the year of the Lord 892, the Hungarians invaded Carinthia, and, when battle was joined on Holy Saturday, they were defeated and slain.

*The Northmen invade Prüm again.*

In the same year the Northmen again invaded the monastery of Prüm. The abbot, slipping away in flight with the brothers, barely escaped. After entering the monastery they killed some of the monks, and, slaughtering a great many of the household, led the rest away captive.
