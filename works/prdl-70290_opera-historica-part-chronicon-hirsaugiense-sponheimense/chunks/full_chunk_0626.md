For he thinks, and certainly is not mistaken, that it is far more excellent to investigate and know the reason of divine and human things than to cling to the piling up of perishable wealth or the accumulation of empty honors. Since these things are fragile and earthly, and pertain only to the cultivation of the body, no one can be made better by them. For wisdom is nothing other than truth, in which the highest, immutable good is held and discerned; it is in no place and is lacking to no one, because it is wholly everywhere. This alone is our reason for philosophizing: the desire for blessedness. But the true blessedness of our mind arises from knowledge of the Highest and love of truth.

Now therefore, O Kymolanus, let us seek true wisdom, which consists in the sole faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, once formed; without knowledge and love of him no one has ever become blessed, as the holy bishop Augustine says in a certain place. For whoever wishes to have salvation without the Savior, and thinks that he can become prudent without the true wisdom, which is Christ, will labor not as sound but as sick, not as wise but as foolish, in continual sickness; as a fool he will remain in the darkness of ignorance. For true wisdom Christ is the way, faith the guide of the journey, unstained humility the guardian, contempt for all earthly things the adornment and garment. Exhorting us to this, holy Christophilus says: “Let no one deceive himself. If anyone among you seems to be wise in this age, let him become foolish, that he may be wise. For the wisdom of this world is folly before God.” The wisdom of God is despised by those who glory in the wisdom of vanity.

A good Christian, a lover of God, a despiser of the world, an enemy of vices and cultivator of virtues, is truly wise, even if he knows none of the wisdom of the world; nothing is alien to him except what is inconsistent with virtue. Wherever he goes, he carries all his own things with him; the whole world is his possession, because he uses all of it as his own. He is not broken by fear, nor changed by power; he is not lifted up by prosperous things, nor cast down by sad ones. For where true wisdom is, there is strength of soul, there is constancy, there is fortitude. The wise man, founded in the love of Christ, is always the same: he is not diminished, as Ambrose says, nor increased by changes of circumstances; nor does he waver like a child, so as to be carried about by every wind of doctrine, but remains perfect in Christ, founded in charity, rooted in faith. Therefore the wise man knows no want of things, knows no changing passions of the soul, but shines like the sun of justice, without stain, before God and men.

But that we may become such men, O Kymolanus, we must labor with the greatest care, since there is no greater or more miserable care than to apply oneself to vain, earthly, and perishable things, which not only confer no good at all upon the soul, but rather afflict it with the greatest evil. Let us learn useful and necessary things, the knowledge of which promises blessedness; and let us not imitate those who do not know necessary things because they have learned superfluous ones. Farewell. From Cologne, on the 22nd day of July, in the year 1505.

*XXVII. Johannes Trithemius to Johannes Cantherius Frisius, doctor of medicine, greeting.*

Because all things are succeeding prosperously for you, as we truly rejoice with a friend, we admonish you above all in this: pass through temporal goods in such a way that you do not lose eternal ones. You have taken a wife; you have not sinned, but nevertheless you have sold your freedom. For married life and the freedom to philosophize cannot coexist in one man. For you write: “I have married a young wife, beautiful, noble, and of the very best conduct.” A rare bird is a woman beautiful, noble, and well conducted. And according to Euripides in the Medea: [unclear]. “Safety,” he says, “has been achieved through very great deeds, when a woman does not disagree with her husband.”
