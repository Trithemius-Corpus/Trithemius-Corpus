*XXIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius to Johannes Steinemoel, priest of Mechelen.*

An opportunity of writing to you has been offered me through this man, both known to you and, as he asserts, joined to you by the singularity of a certain close relationship, who was going to pass from there to Mechelen. We write, therefore, so that you may know what seems to us should be arranged concerning your return to us, lest you run in vain. First, then, we make known to you that, by reason of the war raging around us in the previous year, our affairs have collapsed, and, fortune being blind, a grave envy of rivals has arisen against us. Reason has persuaded us to yield place to it for a time, so that either the enemies themselves may deliberate better, or we, by the counsel of friends, may provide for our affairs elsewhere. We are therefore going to the March of Brandenburg with its most serene prince, now present here in the royal convocation, who long ago desired this of us many times by letters and messengers, but now in person by words. But we shall go wholly uncertain about our return, because, after the grave insult received from our own people, we think of nothing less than returning to Spanheim, unless the divine will shall have ordained otherwise; to it our weakness must always be subject and consenting. You must therefore defer your return to Spanheim until you hear that we have at last returned there, unless perhaps you wish to make a journey of sixty miles in vain. We have been unable to attend to the affairs of your Vincentius because of the unrest of the times; nor indeed can they any longer be of concern to us, since you, vainly and all too boastfully, carry about for sale, for shameful profit, those secrets of the more hidden philosophy which you received from us, as we have been made certain, thinking yourself to be someone great because you can boast that you were a disciple of Trithemius. This is not the path of true wisdom, my Johannes, nor does it befit your age to place hope in vanity and to cast pearls before swine for the price of a cheap reputation. You proclaim certain great things about us, so that you yourself may be regarded as great in the estimation of the people; and while you overstep the rule of true praise, you do us a grave injury, forgetful of the very ancient proverb: praise sparingly, blame more sparingly. Your praises displease us most vehemently, because they are rather reproaches of us than commendations. For it does not belong to all men to understand the secrets of nature and to discover, in known things, something hidden beyond common use. But when you promise certain things about us which others do not understand, what else do you believe remains, old man, except that they will say that we are either given to superstitions or sunk in vanities? We know that you were not introduced by us on that condition; but because you have transgressed the law of fairness set between us, what you would do in greater matters had reasonably to be foreseen by us. Cease, therefore, from these vanities, we exhort you, and consider that you are a minister of Jesus Christ and mature in old age, whom it does not befit to hunt after mortal glory by vanities. Farewell, mindful of us before God. From Cologne, on the 20th day of the month of July, in the year of Christians 1505.

*XXV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, to Johannes Bracht, of the monastery of St. Matthias, greetings.*

I believe that you are by no means unaware, ornament of friends, of that tribulation which that malignant enemy has stirred up against me, who, armed with the dart of envy, drove the first men from the paradise of innocence. He has driven me too from my monastery, not indeed unwilling, but willing through the recollection of injuries, and he has brought upon my mind nausea and a certain horror of the former place, which I had loved most tenderly. I am now living here by a certain necessity as a courtier, soon to go with the most serene prince Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, Elector of the Roman Empire, to the borders of the Saxons, and perhaps to return late; meanwhile, may the persecution stirred up against me either cease, or, if it has gained strength from my absence, may mature providence furnish us a remedy. For the earth is the Lord’s and the fullness thereof; his power is immeasurable, and the compassion of his utterly inexhaustible piety is such that we trust help from him will be present for us everywhere at the opportune time, and we in no way doubt his protection.
