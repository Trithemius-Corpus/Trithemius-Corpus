*Of the Hirsau Monastery*

Around the feast of the Nativity of Saint Mary, very early in the morning, while he was sleeping in the palace at Goslar, suddenly a thunderclap came and shook the wall that was at the head of his bed; then, by the force of the lightning, it knocked several nails from the king’s shield, and scorched at the point the sword that the king had placed at his side. Terrified beyond measure by this, the king leapt outside.

*1108.*

In the second year of Abbot Bruno, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1108, a monastery of our order was newly founded in the diocese of Augsburg, called Lorch; and for the support of the monks it was magnificently endowed by Frederick, duke of the Swabians, father of Frederick the first emperor.

In the same second year of Abbot Bruno, and the year of the Lord 1108, when Gebhard, bishop of Speyer, formerly abbot of this monastery, was afflicted by continual pains of illnesses, so that he seemed wholly useless for managing the affairs of his church, having taken counsel with familiar friends, he resolved to abandon the bishopric entirely, together with its cares, and to return to the monastery of Hirsau, from which he had been absent, so that, since he had not wished to remain alive with those whose abbot he had been, at least when dead he might be able to remain with them forever. And when, after everything necessary for the journey had already been arranged, he began to make his way, seated in a vehicle, from the mountain of St. Michael called Ebernberg, where he was staying, a rumor spread among the people of Speyer that their bishop had hastened to go into flight and desert them. Therefore, gathering in a great crowd, before the bishop had completed half the journey, they seized him, begging that he not remove himself entirely from them; he began to resist vigorously, and said that he did not wish to burden them with his long illness. And when, after many words exchanged between them, they would in no way permit him to depart, one man rose from the midst before them all and said: “Men of Speyer, it seems to me neither just nor honorable to send away from us our bishop, whom we have lawfully received, because of illness, and to burden the monks of the monastery of Hirsau with his sickness, contrary to the order of brotherly charity. Let us rather, from a few suitable men, provide him the necessities of his life as long as he lives.” This man’s counsel pleased them all; and after assurance had been given to the bishop by oath that, when it should please God, they would bring him dead to Hirsau, since they had prevented him while alive, he too gave his consent to their will. Therefore a dwelling suited to his illness and sufficiently pleasant was chosen among the marshes of the Rhine, which to the present day is called Bruchsal. There, after suitable and faithful attendants had been assigned, they caused the bishop to remain, and through the same attendants they provided him everything that was necessary. Thereafter, remaining in that same place for only three months, as his illness became much more severe, he died on the Kalends of March, in the year of the Lord 1108, in the first indiction. His body was brought back by the people of Speyer to this monastery of Hirsau, in accordance with the promise they had made to him while he lived, and was buried with great honor in the greater monastery before the principal altar of Saints Peter and Paul. And this was the end of Bishop Gebhard, who after Saint William the abbot had presided over this monastery for fourteen years and three months, and then, having been ordained prelate of the church of Speyer, presided for only one year and four months. Bruno, also called Einhard, succeeded him.

In this same year Ruthard, archbishop of Mainz, grieving deeply over the desolation of the monastery of Saint Disibod the bishop, in the county of Spanheim, which before the present time had been miserably despoiled and destroyed by its [unclear] for many years, expelled the clerics, unjust possessors of the holy [unclear], and, transferring the monastery, which had collapsed from excessive age, onto the mountain, founded it anew. At that time Burchard, abbot of Saint [unclear], was held in esteem among the people of Mainz. To him the aforesaid Archbishop Ruthard committed the monastery of Saint Disibod with all its appurtenances, and appointed him its first abbot as well as abbot of Saint James. This Burchard, by order and at the expense of the aforesaid archbishop, on the day before the Kalends of July in this year, placed the first foundation stone on the aforesaid mountain for the new monastery; and, having received monks from the mountain of Saint James, he established a community according to the rule of our most holy father Benedict. This same Archbishop Ruthard also built one provostry of our order in the Rheingau, not far from Vinicella, on a mountain that is commonly called Bischoffsbergk, and willed it to be subject to the greater monastery of Mainz. To this provostry Rudolph, count of Rheingau, and Dancmodis his wife, with the consent of their son Louis, handed over estates and not inconsiderable revenues for the remedy of their souls.
