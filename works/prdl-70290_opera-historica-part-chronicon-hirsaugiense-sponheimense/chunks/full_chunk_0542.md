*Chronicle of Trithemius, 400*

For he first laid the foundations of our life, which flourishes illustrious under the name of Bursfeld. Sober and chaste, pious, upright, and quiet, whose food and manner of life were frugal and austere. He also had been seized with great love for the mother of Christ, and he wrote her praises. He himself, pious, was loved with great brotherly affection, and died on the day on which the holy Virgin gives birth. Therefore we all pray to God with just reason that he may grant him to dwell in the starry heaven. Trithemius the abbot set this up for you according to your merit, whom your vows at Sponheim brought as father.

“He laid the foundations of our life”: because he himself, by mandate of the fathers of the Chapter, first arranged the ordinal of the divine offices and the statutes, which we call ceremonies, into the order and form in which they are now held. He was present at the election of our abbot Trithemius; therefore it is said in the epitaph:

*Gerard becomes abbot of St. John’s.*

“Whom your vows at Sponheim brought as father.”

Gerard of Montabaur, cellarer of the monastery of St. James, succeeded him, and presided for eight years and nine months: a good man and a lover of regular observance, who governed the monastery well in both states.

*1488.*

In the year of our abbot John Trithemius, on the fifth day before the Kalends of April, Henry of Gelnhausen died, a monk of this community, sent here from the monastery of St. James at Mainz at the beginning of the reform: a man of competent learning, trained in verse and prose, leaving behind certain little works of his talent, not to be despised for reading, namely: *On Spiritual Instruction*, addressed to certain novices, an exhortation and a long letter; also the *Life of Saint Benedict*, described in elegiac verse; sermons against plurality of benefices; and various letters.

In the same year Maximilian, king of the Romans, was shut up and captured by the Flemings in the town of Bruges, and was detained for a long time in custody in a certain respectable dwelling, all thirty of his attendants being wholly excluded except for a single cook; others of the nobles were captured, none killed, while the rest took thought for themselves by flight. For the Flemings desired to exclude the king, whom they hated, from the rule of their lands, and to have his son Philip, still a youth and a boy, as lord, whom they themselves would govern according to their own will rather than be governed by him. But the great-souled king refused to give consent to their wicked desire; for that reason he was captured. Meanwhile Frederick, emperor of the Romans, having learned of his son’s captivity, assembled a very great army from the princes and cities of the Empire, all detesting the disgrace done to the king; and with three thousand four hundred twenty-two horsemen, and a company of five thousand nine hundred and [unclear] foot-soldiers, and with two hundred twenty-two wagons, he entered Flanders, devastating the whole land outside the cities with fire and plunder; he besieged Ghent for six weeks and freed his son.

In this year John Trithemius, our abbot, who otherwise was always intent, as far as he was allowed, upon the study of letters, began to commit his thoughts and discoveries to writing, lest his mind should seem to be shut up with harmful thoughts. Secondly, even if the things he wrote should profit no one, nevertheless in the course of time they would perfect his own understanding and, by practice, train his talent to a certain facility. Nor did his opinion deceive him: by the daily and continuous practice of writing he became so ready in dictating whatever came to hand that even the most learned all wondered at the facility of his discourse; and those who saw him writing extempore, on any subjects whatever, without premeditation, affirm that his discourse flowed rather than was written.

Therefore at the beginning he wrote a certain lengthy composition in four parts, *On the Origin, Acquisition, Beauty, and Usefulness of the Virtues, in General and in Particular*; because he could not polish it, on account of its excessive length and his various occupations, as he would have wished, he gave it to be burned in the fire. He also began a great work *On the Praise of Virginity*, which he has not yet completed. In the third year of his rule, moreover, he wrote certain treatises, some of which he corrected in this year; and afterward, at the request of friends, he permitted them to go forth in public and to be printed.
