He also drove his successor, Ruthard, a venerable man, from the see of Mainz, and compelled him to live in exile for nine years in the greatest misery in Thuringia. The abbot [Giselbert?], a son of this community, together with sixty monks, he expelled from the monastery of Hasungen because of the obedience which they were showing to the supreme pontiff, and he miserably dispersed the possessions of that monastery.

He also furiously drove many pontiffs from their churches, expelled abbots and their monks from monasteries, and utterly destroyed many monasteries together with churches. By many visions of saints his attempts were always hindered, as a certain holy abbess in the regions of Bavaria, instructed by a vision, used to relate. Moreover, as long as Emperor Henry IV lived and ruled, the brothers were distressed among themselves by frequent threats and terrors concerning the destruction of this monastery, threatened by him. Yet, trusting in the help of the Most High, for whose love they had the earthly king as an enemy, they strove without ceasing to defend their cause not with arms but with prayers.

*M X C I I.*

In the second year of Abbot Gebhard, Conrad, son of Emperor Henry, born of his first wife Bertha, began to rebel against his father and, persuaded by letters of the Roman pontiff, to draw the empire to himself. But it will be worthwhile to have set out from the beginning the cause of this rebellion. King Henry, the father of Conrad, had taken Praxedis as his wife at Cologne seven years earlier; he began to hate her with excessive hatred, so that the hatred was greater than the love with which he had previously loved her. Therefore, inflamed with excessive hatred against her, he placed her in custody; he allowed many of his agents to do violence to her, while he himself looked on. Indeed he came to such madness that he even invited his own son Conrad to have intercourse with her. When Conrad refused to defile his father’s bed, the king, exhorting him to the illicit embraces of his stepmother, said among other things:

“Why do you timidly delay to lie with the woman assigned to you, who is not your stepmother? For you are not my son, as you suppose, but the son of a certain foreign prince of Swabia, whose likeness you plainly bear imprinted.” Beyond measure indignant at these words, the son nevertheless prudently concealed his anger. After a few days, with certain men who agreed with him, he went to the supreme pontiff Urban II; by him he was honorably received, and he was solemnly [unclear] as king; in Italy and Lombardy he reigned against his father.

He was, moreover, an excellent man, conspicuous for every goodness and uprightness, humble, modest, full of charity, and of the highest reverence and piety toward the clergy and monks. But, surviving only a few years after his coronation, he was carried off by an untimely death. The queen, however, after many injuries and unheard-of evils innocently inflicted upon her, by God’s mercy escaped from custody and, fleeing, came to Matilda, the most powerful queen of the Lombards.

Matilda received her with honor and brought her to Urban, the supreme pontiff. Therefore the empress, prostrate at the feet of the bishop, miserable, with abundant tears and deep [unclear], made known the whole misery of the calamity she had endured. The pope, having compassion on the queen, after words of necessary consolation, gathered a synod in the city of Rome. In it, publicly declaring King Henry a heretic, a simoniac, the chief of the Nicolaitan heresy, and disobedient and rebellious toward holy mother Church, he excommunicated him again for the unheard-of crimes committed against his own wife, and [declared] him a schismatic, enemy of God [unclear]. A dragon was seen in the sky; a great pestilence and mortality of men and beasts followed.

*M X C V I.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Gebhard, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1096, the first expedition to Jerusalem was undertaken under Peter the monk and Godfrey of Bouillon, duke of Lorraine, to recover the holy sepulchre of the Lord, with an infinite multitude from all the nations of Europe. The deeds of this pilgrimage have been very fully described by many; to insert it into this chronicle would be too lengthy and tedious.
