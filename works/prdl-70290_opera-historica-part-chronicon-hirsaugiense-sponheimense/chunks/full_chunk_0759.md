You propose soon to enter the cell of the master, whom, after imitating him in holy conduct for a short time on earth, you will never be ashamed of forever in heaven. But meanwhile, teacher, to whom do you entrust the pupil to be instructed, if for the love of Christ you inhabit the wilderness, and he inhabits the world? O Libanus, half of my soul, if it is possible, hear me as I entreat you, so that before you carry out your too harsh purpose in this matter, you may approach Melanius, faithful to you, indeed most faithful, with whom the most wise arch-count Theophilus is constantly present; perhaps by his prudence he will raise up for you a new Pelagius from the disciple. Will you thus depart to Majorca, Libanus, exposing Triandricus to confusion, and Megalopius also to grief, of whom the one promised, the other has long, breathless, been awaiting your arrival? You understand what I mean; see to it that even now we experience your very strong love toward us as still stronger. The books of the divine Pelagius on heavenly doctrine, which we requested from you through Melanius as intermediary, we now acknowledge that we have obtained; for them we both thank you and, according to agreement, will faithfully send them back. Libanus, eager for my affairs, I live here so much the freer for myself, the poorer I am in the things of the world, reducing the ternary according to my powers to unity, and restoring the mind to itself, so that a purer understanding may escape to the goals of the principle rightly set before it. I have had a contention for a long time with the Melantians, who are trying to overthrow part of the binary, and for some time they have possessed, not by right but by a certain violence, the seventh portion of the little field under the equinoctial. That dealer in pigments of ours revolves in his usual fashion in a circle, little fixed in place; as often as he returns to us, he always thrusts new winds upon our ears. Know that the sea, by the bounty of God most good and greatest, is calm, so long as it shall please him who has given everything whereby we may sail happily, we who have this sad leaf: that, when the storm has passed, it makes us ashamed to remember inconveniences. If at any time you learn that the arch-count, the most vigilant teacher of Melanius, is nearer to you, I would wish you not to pass him by, inasmuch as he is most loving toward you, and is in Christ the common friend of many, and has often done good to us. I have given this letter of mine to a certain man unknown to me to be carried to you; he testified that he was going to use [his journey] into Gaul, with instructions that he should deliver it at Paris to Carolus Bovillus, prince of theologians among the philosophers, through whom it may come safely to your hands. When you decide to write back to me, arrange for your letter to be carried to me through the same Bovillus, if you cannot otherwise, a man both known to me and a friend. Farewell, mindful of us before God. From Würzburg, 6 October, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*LX.*

*To the most serene and unconquered prince and lord, Lord Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, Duke of Stettin and Pomerania, Archchamberlain of the Holy Empire, prince-elector, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, sends greetings.*

Most serene prince, first of all I make known to your Excellency that I, the most humble petitioner of your Serenity, by the mercy of God most good and greatest, am sound and unharmed, well in my usual manner, and desiring nothing more than that all things concerning the state of Your Serenity, and also of your wife, son, and brothers, may be carried on with prosperous success. The book for which your Serenity kindly wrote to me in the month of May I gave, according to your wish, to my scribe to be copied; when it was finished in the month of September, I had it bound in covers with as much grace and beauty as was possible for me, and, according to your custom, gilded throughout on the outside surface. I wished, moreover, to send that book to your Majesty as soon as possible, and I would not delay even a few days, except that quartan fevers are keeping back the messenger whom I was going to send; as soon as he has recovered, he will carry the book to your Highness. The other things, however, which your Serenity entrusted to me to work out by night, I have already partly published and partly they remain in my hands to be completed up to this very day. For I shall take care, to the best of my powers, that I be found mindful and grateful for your benefits toward me; although I cannot repay thanks for them, nevertheless I shall by no means cease to have and to give thanks.

As for the fact that I have not sent the truly desired volume to your Serenity with the bearer of the present letter, you must judge that it was done with reason. For he is a young and poor scholar, who for some time was my servant; by my encouragement he is about to return to the study which he had interrupted, and will go to that Frankfurt gymnasium of your Serenity. To him I indeed entrusted this letter, but I did not dare entrust the book because of fear of dangers. Yet I hope that, when my messenger has shortly recovered, he will go more joyfully to your Grace. The silver cup, gilded inside and out, which your Clemency sent me by the same messenger, I confess that I received with the greatest gratitude of mind. For it I give as many thanks as I can, and hold them immortal. For it will be for me a great memorial of your Serenity and a sign of your benevolence
