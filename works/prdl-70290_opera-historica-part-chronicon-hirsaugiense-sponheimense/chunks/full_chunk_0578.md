*Chronicle of Trithemius*

...that of the princes having at last been dissolved, Joachim the margrave, travelling through Saxony, returned to his own lands, after first assigning to the abbot abundant provisions for the journey and a quite honorable retinue. And so, in the year noted above, on the sixth day of the month of August, Johannes Trithemius the abbot left Cologne with his companions and went by boat up to Mainz. For he did not wish to enter the March unless he had first made to the prior the necessary report concerning the monastery. From Mainz, therefore, he again went up to Speyer and, summoning to himself Nicolaus de Reinig, his prior, entrusted to him all care and administration of this monastery; and, having arranged matters as he could, he entered the March through Thuringia. Coming to the town of Berlin, where the margrave has his residence, he was received by him with great joy and honor, and was abundantly provided for in all things according to princely munificence. He remained with that prince in the March for nine months, composing various little works for his praiseworthy instruction. For that same prince was most learned in every variety of writings, then in his twenty-fourth year, but in prudence and wisdom famous far beyond his age and very distinguished; burning exceedingly with love of saving knowledge, he had for many years desired to have Trithemius as teacher.

In the year noted above, while the assembly of princes was continuing in Cologne, Macharius, abbot of the burned monastery of Limburg, was present, wishing the cause of his men against Emicho, count of Leiningen, to be examined and judged by justice before the royal majesty. Therefore, at the king’s command, he offered his petition in writing, in which he most plainly accused the aforesaid count concerning the burning of the monastery, because it had been done by his command. The count, on the other hand, denied that he had ordered the burning, and said that he did not know who had set it on fire, because at that time he had been lying sick with fever in Grevenstein. But the abbot was able to prove by the testimony of many that the plundering incendiaries of the aforesaid monastery had gone out from Durenburg, commonly Hartenburg, the count’s nearby castle, and had returned there with the monastery’s spoils; and, what was of greater proof, he said that they were still living in that same castle. But Maximilian, greatly occupied by the war against the Venetians and by other affairs of the realm, entrusted the case to the archbishop of Mainz, who was then absent, to be amicably settled. In his hearing many matters were conducted over two years, but no agreement between the parties followed. Meanwhile the count nonetheless occupied Mount Limburg, where the monastery had stood, and the fields, meadows, woods, pastures, and fisheries around the abbey, and retained their revenues, with their useful profits, for almost six years, until the death of that abbot. When he finally died, his successor entered into an agreement with the count concerning the plunder; under its conditions no mention was made of the burning, but it was left to the abbot’s discretion whether he wished to proceed against him at law.

In the same year the aforesaid Abbot Macharius, wishing to find a secure place in which he might serve the Lord God with his monks in peace, received the parish church in the town of Wachenheim, after a lawful exchange had first been arranged, from the hand of the provost and convent of the Premonstratensians in Lautern, with the bishop of Speyer and also the prince palatine, whose town it is, consenting. The exchange having been made, and the necessary written instruments having been given and received on both sides, the aforesaid abbot established the place of his residence there and decided to make the aforesaid parish church into a monastery, in which he intended to remain with his monks. For it is agreed that he was moved by many reasons and considerations not to restore the monastery burned on the mountain; of these we know two to have been principal. One was that the place, lying open, was not fortified, and he feared that in the future the same thing might happen which he now grieved had been attempted. The other was that on the mountain he suffered great scarcity of water. Therefore he chose a place in the aforesaid town for building a monastery, where there would be both abundance of water and walls would easily prevent the incursion of impious men. And so he built certain necessary dwellings in the parish courtyard, in which he used to remain with a few monks; the rest, because of great poverty, he sent away to various monasteries of our order. But on all the days afterward during which that same Abbot Macharius lived, there were disputes and lawsuits between him and the aforesaid count because of the causes written above.

Likewise, in the aforesaid assembly of princes, in the year written above of Trithemius our abbot, King Maximilian, on the thirtieth day of the month of July, restored peace and concord among the mistrustful princes, and commanded it to be observed by all under imperial penalties. In the same assembly of princes, King Maximilian, on the twenty-ninth day of the month of July, summoned to himself Johannes Trithemius the abbot, who was with the lord margrave, as we have said.
