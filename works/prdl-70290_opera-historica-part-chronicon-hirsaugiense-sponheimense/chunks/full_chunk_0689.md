It will not go on to sadden me a second time, since, taught now by experience, he has learned not to trust vain things, and that the false hope of men always fails in tribulation. Do you not see, man, that a small viper kills a broad bull with its bite, and that monks hidden in secret undermine the rule of a shepherd? A drop hollows stone not by force but by falling often; and often a humble crowd has cast down a proud ruler. For many years now, through the envy of certain brothers, whose infamy for public crime had long held them, a seedbed of wickedness began to sprout. So dangerous is it for a ruler to punish contumacious sources, who ascribe deserved penalties to injury, always covering with silence the time in which they may destroy the censor who corrects them. But the Lord Jesus, who will not forget his poor unto the end, will be my consolation; in him I have placed all my hope. He will give peace to my heart in a fitting time, and, when he judges the hidden things of mortals, will bring them into the light. I beg you again and again to pray to his mercy for me. Farewell. From my aforesaid monastery at Wurzburg, on the last day of the month of October, in the year of Christians 1506.

*IV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James at Wurzburg, to Johann Gotfrid, pastor of the church of the Valley of Men [Manthal], greetings.*

That you attend upon me with brotherly love I have plainly enough; and therefore I could not conceal, nor perhaps ought I to have concealed, from you the whole sequence of my affairs. For true friendship, confirmed by the love of Christ, is not accustomed to hide from a friend anything that befalls by fortune, so that if favorable things come to pass he may rejoice with him, but if adverse things, he may suffer with his suffering friend. I do not write to you the causes of my long wandering from the monastery of Sponheim, since they are known to you, but rather that, moved by the injury of rivals, I have at last made provision for my affairs. Why should I not do so, after receiving so notable an insult beyond my deserts? It was necessary for me not to spurn the peace offered, and to embrace among strangers what was denied by my own. Indeed, what the face of regular observance among the monks is today at Sponheim, and what it was once when I was present, you, as a neighbor, cannot be ignorant; and how much I long ago [unclear] for ungrateful men, who care for nothing less than spiritual things and pursue nothing more zealously than carnal things. With the greatest cares and labors for their advantage I afflicted myself for twenty-three continuous years; you see what reward I bring back. Those among them who had more influence, siding with our rivals against us, stirred up against us the most pious duke, using lie, fraud, and deceit, until, persuading him of false things as true, they brought about what they had long been planning. We resolved to yield the monastery for a time, so that the opportunity might not be lacking for sounder counsel, by which envy might pay its deserved penalties. After dwelling nine months in the March of Brandenburg, we thought it certain that rashness would change its counsel, and that the brothers, at least the innocent ones among the wicked, would approach the mind of the prince. Meanwhile they often asked us by letters to return to the monastery; but the duke, whom, without our being consulted, they had rendered hostile to us by false teaching, they disdained, moved by shame and fear, to persuade to the contrary opinion. Returning at last to Budoras, we called the prior to us; from his accustomed report we learned that all things were at peace and that absolutely nothing was to be feared, and that not only all the neighbors and brothers, but also the duke with his men, were asking [unclear] that we should return to the monastery as quickly as possible. At last he persuaded our mind to return, once the annual chapter had soon been celebrated, which was due to be observed at Mainz at the beginning of September. On hearing this he pretended to be glad and departed, though in truth he was sad at heart, because we are certain that he had always dreaded rather than desired our return to Sponheim. For unless our opinion deceives us, the picture 26 painted in the refectory gave him hope of the abbacy. But when the prior had gone away, soon through Johann Damius it became known to us what eulogies, written at Mainz on the mountain of St. James by the hand of a certain monk of the monastery of St. Johann at [Rinckaug?], Peter Slarpion, he had caused to be written in the name of the whole convent of Sponheim; yet these, intercepted by the caution of the very man who reported it, did not come into our hands. When this treachery of our men became known, we stood firm in our decision that henceforth we would by no means return to Sponheim, nor yield in this matter to any persuasions of any men whatsoever. Truly that saying of Christ in the Gospel has been fulfilled in me: that a man’s enemies will be those of his own household. O brief memory of benefits among cloistered men, since there is little faith in monks who seek not God but themselves.
