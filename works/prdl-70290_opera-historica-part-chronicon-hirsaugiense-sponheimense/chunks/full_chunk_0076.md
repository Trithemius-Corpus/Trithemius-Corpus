*On William, the twelfth abbot of this monastery, who presided for twenty-two years; on the deeds done by him, and on his most holy way of life.*

After the deposition of the most devout man, Abbot Frederick, no small dispute arose among the brothers as to who should suitably be put in his place: one who by word and example would show himself a useful shepherd to the sheep, and who would not neglect the provision of temporal things, without whose support religion cannot subsist. For they feared the violence of the count; since with his help Frederick had been deposed from his dignity, they not unwisely feared his anger if they presumed the same against a successor.

At that time there was in the monastery of Saint Emmeram at Regensburg a certain monk named William, a very learned and religious man. Some of the brothers had known him before, and the rest had often heard his reputation for good conduct widely celebrated.

Therefore, after long deliberations held among themselves, at last, inspired by a wise counsel from God, with concordant unanimity they resolved that they would elect no one other than the aforesaid monk William, whom they knew, as one very well imbued with the Scriptures and adorned with holy morals, would be a pastor not without usefulness. So they sent two from among themselves, with an embassy from Count Adalbert, to the monastery of Saint Emmeram at Regensburg, to ask in the name of all that William be given to them as abbot. The messengers came to Regensburg and set forth their embassy to the abbot and community, as they had been ordered. They sought William as the shepherd of their souls and, after showing their necessities, obtained him. This William, when [unclear] was received with the joy of the brothers, soon [unclear] into abbot, by the consent of all who [unclear], did not raise himself up in pride, but by fasts and prayers entrusted the matter wholly to the Lord. Compelled by the command of his superior, however, after a few days he set out on the journey with the brothers who had been sent, and, with the Lord accompanying him, came to the monastery of Hirsau.

He was received with great joy by the brothers; soon, by the consent of all who [unclear], [unclear] began [unclear] the count, so that he might restore an innocent man to his former dignity. When he saw him obstinate in his rashness, he considered returning to his own monastery whence he had come. When this became known to the brothers, they threw themselves at his feet and very humbly begged him not to abandon them, so desolate; [unclear] they wrap him about, and at last with difficulty obtain that he remain. He refused either to be ordained by the bishop, or to stand or sit in the place of the abbot, as long as he knew that Frederick, his predecessor, was still surviving in this life. When he, not many months later, departed to the Lord, William at last consented to his own ordination. But he would not consent before the monastery was handed over in every way to freedom. For the advocate-counts had entirely reduced the monastery into their own jurisdiction, and did not wish to permit any freedom to the place offered, in accordance with the terms of the first foundation, in the fullest manner.

Count Adalbert, therefore, seeing the constancy of Abbot William, namely that he would by no means involve himself in the governance of the monastery unless the count himself withdrew his hands from it and granted the place perpetual freedom, pretended that he would obey in all things, whatever he foresaw to be necessary for the business of this matter. And so, at his insistence, Count Adalbert, with secular cunning, composed false [charters?], which he obtained to be secured with the royal seal. But after five years, understanding the deceit, William overcame it through the mercy of God, as we shall say in order in its proper place. Therefore, since William was holy and innocent, truthful and faithful, measuring all by his own virtues, and believing that the monastery had now been freed from servitude, he consented to be ordained as abbot.
