*Sponheim.*

*1510.*

In the fourth year of Nicholas our abbot, a great assembly of princes was held in the city of Augsburg, and lasted almost three months. At it Caesar Maximilian appeared in his own person, and discussed with those same princes many matters concerning renewing and continuing the war against the Venetians. At the same assembly of princes Ludovicus Helianus of Vercelli, ambassador and envoy of the king of the French, appeared; on the tenth day of the month of April he delivered an elegant oration to the senate of the princes and cities of the Empire, in which he enumerated the perfidy, frauds, deceits, and many poisonings impiously and cruelly perpetrated by the Venetians, and declared in lofty style how great disasters they had publicly brought upon the Roman Church, and likewise upon the Empire, and no less upon the whole commonwealth of Christians, by their treacherous plotting. In the name of his king he urged the Emperor and the princes not to put faith in their deceitful promises, nor grant peace to them when they were falsely humbled, but by war, as they had begun, to humble their insolent pride utterly and wholly extirpate their unjust dominion; for the completion of this affair he promised every effort of his king. From Augsburg the same ambassador went down into Hungary, bearing letters and petitions in the name of his king of the French, by which he strove to forestall the Venetians, so that they might not be able to hope for any assistance of support from the king of Hungary.

In the same year a great dissension arose between the citizens and consuls of the town of Erfurt among the Thuringians. Growing within a short time to immeasurable proportions, it was a seedbed of many evils; wherefore many of the citizens of the council either fled from the town of their own accord, fearing the fury of the lower citizens, or, captured by the inhabitants, were consigned to very harsh prisons. Of these, a leading and more powerful citizen, named Hans Keller, was hanged on the gallows; the others laid this crime against him, that since he was the highest of the senators and the most powerful chief guardian of the treasuries, he had secretly stolen many things and spent them for his own uses.

Finally, from the aforesaid sedition a serious discord arose between Uriel, archbishop of Mainz, and Frederick, duke of Saxony; each of them contended that he was lord of Erfurt. The dissension gradually grew so great that, after troops had been gathered on both sides, they mutually prepared themselves for war. When this became known to Caesar Maximilian, he ordered them by imperial authority, through envoys and letters, not to proceed to war.

He entrusted the matter between them, to be attempted amicably, to the most wise prince and reverend father, lord Lorenz, bishop of Wurzburg, duke of Eastern Franconia, so that, taking to himself advisers of the archbishops of Trier and Cologne, and also of Margrave Joachim of Brandenburg, and of the towns of Frankfurt and Nuremberg, he might restore peace between them as soon as possible. If he could bring them back to peace, the matter would have been well handled; but if he could not, he was to refer the business back to Caesar.

Therefore about the middle of the month of August the aforesaid prelate, by imperial authority, summoned the dissenting parties to him at Wurzburg, that is, Uriel archbishop of Mainz, Frederick duke of Saxony, and the consulship of Erfurt. They sent their spokesmen and advisers, while the princes themselves, by way of caution, were not far absent. There were also present advisers sent by the archbishop of Trier, and also by Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg, and the consuls of Frankfurt and Nuremberg. But before the case had fully begun to be heard, Caesar’s letters arrived, by which it was ordered that that assembly ought to be transferred and held in the town of Schmalkalden, nearer to the Erfurters, for the proving of the witnesses in the matters that were being discussed. The appointed day was observed; the parties were heard; but no agreement could intervene, and therefore the matter remained suspended.

Likewise in the year noted above, throughout all Lent, crusade indulgences for the Master and the order of the Teutonic Knights in Prussia, against the Tartars and Ruthenians, adversaries of the Christian faith, were preached throughout the diocese and duchy of Wurzburg, and they lasted from the first Sunday of Lent until the feast of the Holy Trinity, to the salvation of many.

Also in the year noted above, Wilhelm of Bavaria, the elder son of Duke Albert, with the counsel and assent of his men, received into his hands the rule of his father’s principality, and rules until [unclear] Christian, who was burned with them, they had bought for nine groschen, and had cruelly martyred, with punctures dripping blood; yet he allowed the rest, innocent of such a crime, to depart.
