*Sponheim.*

*Another man admirable for his knowledge.*

But as we write these things, Ferdinand of Córdoba comes back to mind. In 1445, a young man of twenty years, a knight of the golden spur, Doctor of Arts, Medicine, and sacred Theology, he came from Spain into France with [unclear] horses, and by his marvelous knowledge turned the whole school of Paris to astonishment. For he was most learned in every branch of letters, and most upright in life and conduct; not arrogant and proud like that man of whom we have already spoken, but humble and full of great reverence.

*A memorable power of memory.*

He held the whole Bible in memory; likewise Nicholas of Lyra, the writings of Saint Thomas Aquinas, Alexander of Hales, John Scotus, Bonaventure, and many others in theology; also the Decretum and all the books of both laws; and in medicine Avicenna, Galen, Hippocrates, Aristotle, and Albert; and all the books and commentaries of philosophy and metaphysics he preserved in memory perfectly, as they say.

*A very acute disputation [unclear].*

In citing authorities he was extremely ready; in disputation acute, never surpassed by anyone. Finally, he read, wrote, and understood Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Arabic, and Chaldaean perfectly. Sent to Rome as ambassador by the king of Castile, he disputed publicly in all the schools of Italy and France and refuted everyone, while he himself was refuted by no one, even in the least point. Various opinions about him were current among the doctors of Paris, some carping that he was a magician and full of a demon, others holding the contrary. There were some who thought him Antichrist, because of the incredible knowledge of the Scriptures in which he seemed to surpass all mortals. He published certain commentaries on Ptolemy's Almagest and illuminated the Apocalypse of Saint John with a very beautiful exposition. He wrote certain other little works of his own talent, full of learning, whose titles we have not been able to recall to memory on this occasion. This was that Ferdinand who, by an astronomical prediction, long beforehand foretold [his death?] to Charles, Duke of the Burgundians.

*The cross-bearers [unclear].*

Also in the aforesaid year, the eighteenth of Abbot Trithemius, there came from Italy men clad in gray tunics, with heads uncovered, walking barefoot. They carried in their hands a small cross of wood; they had neither sack nor pouch, nor did they receive money; they also used staffs in their hands. They did not drink wine or beer. Only once a day, except on Sunday, did they refresh themselves, taking vegetables and roots with salt and water, without any fat. In church, prostrate on the ground in the shape of a cross, they prayed, carrying nothing besides the cross and the tunic. They went around through cities and villages and did not remain in one place more than twenty-four hours unless they were ill. Many among them were of the clerical order; they admitted into their company all who wished to do penance, except women. After five years had elapsed, their penance ceased.

*The Palatine comes to Sponheim.*

Also in the aforesaid year, on the nineteenth day of November, on the feast of Saint Elizabeth, Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, renowned Duke of Bavaria, came here to Sponheim with his sons Ludwig and Rupert, for the sake of speaking with Trithemius and at the same time seeing the monastery and the books. It was Friday, and he sent ahead cooks, bread, wine, fish, and everything necessary for food; for he did not wish to burden us in anything, and therefore provided everything most abundantly from his own resources.

*1502.*

*A great pestilence.*

In the nineteenth year of Trithemius our abbot, there was a great pestilence everywhere, in all the places round about, namely in Bingen, in Stauronesus, in Berckelnheim, and in the whole County of Sponheim. It consumed many thousands of people, raging over almost the whole world.

*A certain man is burned.*

In this same year, on the twelfth day of March, a certain poor man in Waldenhufen, named Leonard, was burned by fire because of coinage of pennies, false money which he had struck in a hidden house.

*Henry becomes abbot of Bursfelde.*

In the aforesaid year, on the eighth day of May, John, abbot of Bursfelde, the fourth in the order of the reform, died, a man most holy in character and life. Henry, a monk of the same monastery, succeeded him by election of the brothers.

*Likewise, a meeting of abbots at Nuremberg.*

The presiding officer at the convocation of princes in Nuremberg called to himself Johann Trithemius and certain other abbots. To them he opened his intention, indicating that he had thought of making one out of the three observant branches of our order, Bursfelde, Melk, and Kastl. But since the men of Melk and Kastl were unwilling to subject themselves to our constitutions, they returned to their own places with the business unfinished.

*Several desert the Palatine.*

Likewise in this year, several, withdrawing themselves from the service and fidelity of the Count Palatine, went over to his rivals, laying the foundations of a future war against him. Among them Emich, Count of Leiningen, was chief; departing from the Palatine, he subjected himself and his possessions to the Count of Hesse.
