The place is indeed small, poor, and modest, but very well suited to my purpose and studies, where I may always be intent, at my pleasure, both upon myself and upon letters. And so, for love of peace and quiet, I gave up the abbey of Sponheim, full of useless occupations, together with its most ungrateful monks, after I had presided over it into the twenty-fourth year.

This deed not only has not caused me regret, but delights me to such a degree that I would not wish to have remained there even with peace of every kind. I should bear the death of Libanius, my sweetest teacher, heavily, if I knew for certain that he had died. For I hope, through the mercy of God, that he is still living and, as he promised me in the previous year, will come to me shortly. In the month of August I sent him letters, both those of Your Serenity and my own, but I am still uncertain whether he has received them. I shall do what I can to know the truth; and if indeed he is dead, which may almighty God avert, [I shall take care that he not] seem to have been utterly taken away from us. For he has left very precious little works, composed by his brilliant talent; the more of these we shall be able to gather to ourselves, the less he will seem to have departed from us. But from the day on which I left your March and Your Highness down to the present I have been able to write nothing, because I could have neither a quiet place nor a suitable time. As for the praises of Saint Joseph, foster-father of the Savior, I shall begin the treatise as soon as possible, and the remaining items, marked in order on the schedule by Your Highness’s hand, I shall see to publishing as quickly as I can, according to the powers of my talent. Meanwhile I ask that Your Serenity patiently endure this delay, since, if the Lord Jesus has mercy and prolongs my life, in due time you will receive from me completed all the things you request.

But as to this, that Your Highness again asks for my return to you in the March at the feast of the Lord’s Resurrection or of Pentecost, I can write back nothing certain, because, having resigned the former abbey and undertaken this one, I have now subjected myself to the lordship of another prince. As far as lies in me, I could easily be persuaded, and I am both ready and willing to obey the just desires of Your Majesty in all things to the best of my ability; but on this occasion there are two things that seem to hinder the readiness of my mind. First, because in this new abbey I can in no way depart so soon and so far away, and remain abroad for a long time, without the consent of my most reverend prince; and the consent which the former Palatine gave is of no force today, since, the prince having changed, the privilege too must necessarily be changed. The other thing which seems to stand in the way, although it is not great, has nevertheless saddened me somewhat: that certain fathers of my order are scandalized at me because I frequent the courts of princes, chattering I know not what frivolous and fabricated things, as though I had previously gone to Your Highness to teach certain profane matters, and not for the honor of the Christian religion and to transmit understanding of the divine Scriptures. The credulous crowd rages against innocence in its customary way into vice; and since it does not possess the principles for knowledge of natural things, whatever it does not understand it ascribes to evil operations. No ignorant person grasps that what he marvels at can be possible, and each measures the force of nature according to the capacity of his own intellect, and is thereby deceived altogether like a blind man. But a mind conscious of right pays no attention to their cavils, rather laughing at the sloth of the unskilled, who despise the good which through sloth they have never learned. Yet let Your Serenity write to my most reverend lord bishop, and if he consents that I come, sufficient provision will have been made for each of us. Although, saving the will of Your Highness, it seems to me more advisable in many respects that my entry into the March be deferred until the year one thousand five hundred eight, lest, if I depart so soon, the tongue of rivals again spit out against me something of ill-speaking levity.

I have learned that the promise of the king of the Danes has been delayed, and I grieve. But I fear that he will have little success with the Swedes; and the more he tries to crush them, the more he will tear himself apart. For God Most High rules the kingdom of men, as Scripture says, and gives it to whomever he wishes. May almighty God deign to confirm your principate in every goodness and make it glorious, granting you every desire of your heart; this without doubt he will do, if with a perfect heart you walk innocently in his commandments with holy David, as you have begun. The reason why the messenger of Your Serenity returns later is that, deceived in his expectation, he sought me at Sponheim. May the Most High preserve Your Majesty unharmed; to whom I most humbly commend myself. From my aforesaid monastery at Würzburg, on the 25th day of the month of November. In the year of Christ 1506.
