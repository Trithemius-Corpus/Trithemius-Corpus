1139. In the eighteenth year of Abbot Volmar, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity [1139?], King Conrad celebrated the Lord’s Nativity at Goslar. Then, around the feast of James the Apostle, he moved an expedition into Saxony against Duke Henry. But when Henry met him manfully with a great army near the little town called Creutzberg, the king was compelled to return with the business unfinished. Those who took Duke Henry’s side were the counts palatine of the Rhine, the dukes of Bavaria, and Conrad, archbishop of Magdeburg. Days for bringing about concord between the king and Duke Henry were appointed by the princes at Quedlinburg; when Duke Henry had come there with his men, he ended his life by poison, as is reported: a powerful and warlike man, and the boldest of all in his time. He was buried in the monastery of our order at Konigslutter beside Lothar, his father-in-law, the emperor. His duchy Adelbert the margrave, mentioned above, obtained from King Conrad. Against him almost all the princes of Saxony conspired.

At about these times, when Embricho, bishop of the church of Wurzburg, was going to Mainz to carry out certain business, on the journey he had meeting him a certain monk, named Christian, a Scot by birth, who, bending his knees to the ground, begged that bishop that, for love of St. Kilian the martyr, who had been a Scot, he should grant to monks from Scotland, journeying abroad for Christ, a place or hospice in his city. Embricho provided letters strengthened with his seal, which we have seen and read whole and sound. For among the other miracles that he performed in this life, since he abstained entirely from wine for the love of God, and one day, sitting at table in the midst of magnates, could not have water, after making the sign of the holy cross over a vessel of wine set before everyone on the table, he changed the wine into water. All who were present marveled, since they had tasted wine that was excellent and suddenly saw it changed into water. Present at table in their own persons for this miracle were Piligrin, abbot of St. Burkhard, then of our order; Gebhard, provost of the New Monastery; Burekerus, Hentricus, Mukolinus, and Iringus of Zabelstein, with many others, both clerics and laymen. To transmit to posterity the perpetual memory of this most famous sign, the aforesaid Bishop Embricho, by the common counsel and assent of the whole clergy, gave and assigned forever, in the greater church of Wurzburg, a full and perpetual canonical prebend to the Scottish monks of the said monastery and their successors; thereafter they possessed and received it down to the present day.

In the nineteenth year of Abbot Volmar, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 1140, there died Diemo, monk of this monastery of Hirsau and formerly prior, a most devout man who was believed to be filled with the spirit of prophecy. For he is said to have foretold many things, the truth of which certain [unclear] proved. That holy Henry, abbot of Breitenau, formerly a monk of this monastery, of whom we spoke above, was present at his burial; he had come, not by chance, but rather, as we believe, called by divine ordinance, for the spiritual consolation of the brothers.
