*Chronicle of Trithemius* ... exactly compose and adorn a prelate, there is no need to write, since none of us who daily associate and live with him can fail to know it.

But when Abbot Johannes Trithemius, as we have said, had been transferred to Wuerzburg, Nicholas the prior succeeded in his place; he presides as long as God shall have wished. As far as bodily disposition is concerned, he is a man of middling stature, his body midway between fatness and leanness, dark of face, bald-headed, unsteady in his walk, leaping with a headlong gait.

He is pressed by daily poverty, because of the scantiness of the rents and revenues of the monastery; he can by no means attain the fortune of his predecessor, and therefore in many things he is compelled to have patience. For Johannes Trithemius, known and familiar to many princes, prelates, and doctors because of his learning of every kind, had from various persons many supports, all of which are now denied to his successor.

For, as we heard from Trithemius' own mouth and know by manifest proof to be true, in twelve years he acquired as a gift, from various princes, prelates, and other friends, more than a thousand florins for his labor, study, counsel, or editions of various writings; all of these he turned to the common use of this monastery and of the brethren. Nevertheless, as far as the increase of property is concerned, [unclear]. For [unclear] began to be present; he was not able to advance the governance to such a degree, because he obtained very few suitable and useful helpers. Hence, unless he had had frequent assistance from outsiders, he too would have borne the same poverty that we suffer today. Therefore it is no wonder if Sponheim is today pressed by great poverty, since the Trithemian subsidies have failed. I believe without doubt, indeed I dare to say that I truly know, that if Trithemius had remained with us in the abbacy down to the present day, we would now have been freed from all debts. For moments began to be present in which the familiarity of the greatest princes would by no means have abandoned him in his former poverty; how true this is we know from the report of those who associated with him at Wuerzburg after he had resigned our abbacy. But what? He understands too late who says after the deed, 'Alas, why did I do this?'

The chief reason why Trithemius left us is believed to have been this: that he was not unaware that our affections were less in agreement with him; then, that some seemed by clear signs to be panting after his prelacy; that there was no one among us who wished to come forward as a faithful, suitable, and zealous helper and co-worker for him, as much in spiritual matters as in temporal; and no less that those who presumed with the greatest certainty that they would be abbots in the future said nothing good of him anywhere, but rather whatever evil came into their mouths, both at Mainz, among the abbot and brethren of St. James, and in whatever other places, to whatever persons; through this much lay hidden from Trithemius, who clearly recognized the devices and hatreds of his rivals.

What was the good and learned man to do in the midst of rivals, when, because of the aversion and folly of certain men, he could neither profit himself usefully nor, with suitable co-workers lacking, provide useful things for his subjects? Therefore, since he was astute, he turned aside from evil and, by the Lord's mercy, found a place better than this one and suited to his holy studies. We, however, have fallen into the pit which some of us prepared for the undeserving Trithemius. Man sees in the face and is very often deceived in his judgment; but God, who searches hearts and reins, knows all things. 1507.

In the first year of Nicholas our abbot of Sponheim, Maximilian, the renowned king of the Romans, assembled a great and long-lasting gathering of princes at Constance, in which many things were seriously discussed concerning the continuation of peace throughout the kingdom, and concerning the undertaking of war against the proud, rebellious, disobedient Venetians, whose inborn rashness had inflicted many losses on the Church and had perpetrated very many deeds against the Roman Empire; it would be tedious to make mention of them here.

In the same year Joachim, margrave of Brandenburg, arch-chamberlain of the Roman Empire and prince-elector, sent letters with a messenger and gifts to Abbot Trithemius at Wuerzburg, asking him that he would deign once more to enter the March and to remain with him for the space of even two months, for fuller instruction both in the Greek and in the Latin language and for the completion of the other good arts. But Trithemius, a lover of monastic quiet, did not consent this time to go so far away, although the margrave promised him many things, and had earlier given him many.
