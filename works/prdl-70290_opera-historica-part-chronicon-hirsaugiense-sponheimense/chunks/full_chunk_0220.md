*MCCXV.*

In the tenth year of Abbot Lutfrid, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1215, Adolf, count of Berg, besieged and took the castle of Werden, and by mighty force freed Otto, bishop of Münster, who had there been put in chains by the emperor, as we have said, namely on the vigil of St. James the Apostle.

In the same year Pope Innocent held at Rome, in the Lateran church, a synod of 130 bishops for the recovery of Jerusalem; in it he condemned as erroneous certain little books of Joachim, abbot of the Florensian monastery in Calabria. Sending his letters throughout all the borders of Christendom, he ordered all suitable persons to preach the cross. Hence throughout various provinces an innumerable multitude were signed for love of Christ and the liberation of the Holy Land. Among them were Frederick, king of the Romans and of Sicily; Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz; also the bishops of Liège, Bamberg, Poitiers, and Strasbourg; Duke Henry of Brabant; the duke of Moravia; the duke of Limburg; the duke of [Ancei?]; the count palatine of Tübingen; the margrave of Baden; the count of Looz; the count of Jülich; the count of Sayn; the count of Berg; and many others, all of whom took up the cross at Aachen when John, scholasticus of Xanten, preached it.

In the same year Theodoric, archbishop of Trier, came to Cologne; through the agency of Duke Henry of Brabant he brought the citizens back to peace and to submission to King Frederick, according to the apostolic mandate, and absolved them from the excommunication and interdict under which they had lain for a year and five months. On the fourth day of the month of August he solemnly celebrated masses in the greater church of St. Peter before all the people.

On the same day Frederick entered the city and was received with great honor; after receiving the homage of fidelity, he remained for seven days.

In the same year also lord Lutfrid, venerable abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, obtained from Frederick II, illustrious king of the Romans and of Sicily, confirmation of all privileges granted to that monastery by kings and emperors, together with further concessions, as appears in the letters of that confirmation, which were issued at Haguenau in the year of the Lord’s Incarnation 1215, on the sixth day before the Ides of July, in the third indiction.

In the same year, when Theodoric, formerly archbishop of Cologne, had long labored at Rome before the pope for his own restoration and made no progress, he was compelled to return empty-handed; the pope wrote to the people of Cologne that they should elect another as archbishop. Assembling together, they elected as archbishop Engelbert, provost of the greater church and son of the count of Berg; he presided for almost ten years, a very good man, a lover of the clergy and defender of the poor. Frederick, count of Isenburg, his kinsman, afterward cruelly killed him.

In the same year Walter, bishop-elect of Basel, being accused of having entered office uncanonically, was summoned to Rome by Pope Innocent. Since he could not canonically clear himself of the charges brought against him, he was deposed by the same pope; Henry, succeeding by canonical election, was confirmed.

In these times there were many hidden heretics of both sexes, who sowed various errors in Germany, France, and Italy; many of them, when detected, were burned in the flames. In the city of Strasbourg, indeed, in this year more than 80 in number were seized together at one time, of whom few were found innocent. Brother Conrad of Marburg, the apostolic inquisitor, was accustomed, if they denied the heresy, to test them by the ordeal of glowing iron; those whom the iron burned he handed over as heretics to the secular court to be burned. Hence, with few exceptions, all who had once been accused and brought to his examination were condemned by him to the fire. There were those who believed that many were condemned innocently, because glowing iron found no one free from sins, even if he had never been infected by any stain of heresy.

*MCCXVI.*

In the eleventh year of Abbot Lutfrid, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1216, Pope Innocent died. Honorius III was appointed; he presided for ten years and two months. He confirmed the orders of Preachers and of the Friars Minor of Saint Francis.
