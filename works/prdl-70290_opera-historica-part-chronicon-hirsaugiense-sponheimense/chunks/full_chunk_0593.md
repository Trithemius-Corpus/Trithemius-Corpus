*Two Books of the Familiar Letters of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, to Various Princes, Bishops, and Men Eminent in Learning in Germany.*

*To the most distinguished man, Lord Matthias Held, imperial councillor and pro-chancellor of Germany, Jacob Spiegel of Sélestat sends greetings.*

Since, in view of your uncommon prudence, you are by no means unaware, most distinguished sir, that the writings of very learned men have always been dedicated to the highest men, you will judge it less intrusive, or even unfitting, for me to publish under the auspices of your name these books of the familiar letters of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim. For when I consider in my mind how splendidly you have deserved of good letters and of all students, to say nothing now of the benefits you have privately conferred on me, I thought I would not be acting quite candidly if I offered these to another rather than to you. And since you are engaged at the imperial Majesty’s court in this kind of sacred chancery business, so that in writing letters to the highest and most excellent princes and men you are always exceedingly occupied, you will, I think, be declared [unclear] also to read these at your leisure, which Trithemius wrote to the most eminent princes of Germany and also to all the most learned men of his age, and which they in turn wrote to him. Nor do I think it will be unpleasant to you if you grant a little time to reading them, so that you may form in your mind, as it were, a certain image of the former age, in which it first befell me to be received into court under the incomparable hero and most praised emperor Maximilian. I for my part confess that I am affected with no slight pleasure whenever in these letters I enjoy the recollection of excellent men, some of whom we know to have departed this life, while others are still surviving. And although the remoteness of time can do very much in human affairs, so that many things are obliterated by it, or even changed, yet by the benefit of this reading I no longer think of as dead those who among them have fallen asleep, nor any longer as absent those who remain alive. Nor can it be hidden from you in how great esteem Trithemius was everywhere held, on account of his incredible keenness of intellect and his most vigorous zeal in every kind of literature; and in prudence indeed he was so great that he was held in the highest honor by kings, princes, and by Maximilian himself, in every way the greatest emperor of his age, and was very often asked to enroll himself in the order of councillors. And it is fitting that the princes of our age imitate this singular humanity and benevolence of the most illustrious men of the former century toward men outstanding in learning, and foster with the greatest zeal those through whose service they attain so greatly to immortality.

But if Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, Frederick, Margrave of Brandenburg, and Hermann, Archbishop of Cologne, electoral princes, pursued the learned men of their age with such favor, it is right that in our own time, too, all the most learned should be liberally fostered. Moreover, in these letters one may see how religiously Trithemius thought about the Christian religion, with how great zeal he defended it against the preachers of that age, who more often cited Aristotle or Porphyry than Christ or the apostles from the pulpit, judging it most unworthy that the doctrines of the Christian faith should not suffice for the instruction of the Church of God, and indeed that the utterly empty inventions of the Aristotelians should be preferred to them. It is very pleasant, and full of consolation, that besides Christ, the apostles, and indeed all the saints, he so often by his own example also exhorts to patience, and says that the mind must be drawn back from the desire for vengeance, which is the greatest virtue of all, not so much in great and most powerful men as in the ministers of the Word of God, whom it is fitting to endure all things with a patient spirit for the glory of Christ to be made illustrious.
