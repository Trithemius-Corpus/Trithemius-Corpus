*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

...in Neuburg near Heidelberg, and, immersed in perishable affairs, he has almost put aside every study of letters. Our [Rogerius?] Sicamber, in his fashion, makes books and produces innumerable offspring, so that it would be tedious for me even to list merely the titles of each of the little works he has composed by lamplight. For he has completed, to use his own word, 136 little works of his lucubrations in four codices, and to this day he does not cease writing.

Jacob Wimpfeling is at Strasbourg, enduring a grave persecution from the hermit friars of Saint Augustine because, in a certain work of his entitled *On Integrity*, he wrote that Augustine himself was not a monk or hooded religious. *Wimpfeling’s persecution by the Augustinian hermits.* I feel compassion for the man, who bears trouble almost beyond his strength, whose noble talent, now in old age, is being compelled to the quarrels of lawyers. For they arranged for him to be summoned in person to the Apostolic See; I sent you, with my letters, his apology, composed in one hundred short verses [unclear], so that you may understand the man’s defense.

Sebastian Brant is still living at Strasbourg, employed on a public stipend of the senate. Johannes Nutius, that monk of Spanheim who never consented to the conspiracy of the wicked against me, is dead. I ask you again and again to intercede with God for his soul. In the abbey of Spanheim, Prior Melanius, whom you know, has succeeded me, because he did not prove sufficiently faithful to his pastor. May God not impute it to him, since perhaps he was deceived unknowingly. Farewell, sound and safe for Nestor’s years, remembering us before God. From Würzburg, on the sixteenth day of the month of August, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*43. Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James at Würzburg, sends greetings to Johannes Capellerius, mathematician at Paris.*

Most learned Johannes, I would send letters to you more often if I did not lack a supply of messengers. I believe it is not unknown to you that I left the abbey of Spanheim because of the envy of rivals, and therefore I did not judge it necessary to write to you about this change of mine. I made known to you two years ago the causes of everyone’s rivalries against me, when you were with me at Cologne in that great assembly of princes.

You ask what success I had in the March of Brandenburg, what I did there, with what gifts I was presented by the prince, and what I am now doing. This is the substance of your letter, and what your desire presses you further to know from me. By the mercy of God, who disposes and orders all things, I entered the March after a prosperous journey; and though I was lame from a flux in one leg, as you saw me at Cologne, I recovered completely in the March under the care of the prince’s surgeon.

I remained in Berlin with the margrave himself for nine months, performing the office of teacher. For I handed down precepts and rules to the prince, by which he became more learned in the Latin language without great labor. At his urging I also wrote certain *synthemata*, in the reading of which he will rejoice. For now he writes Latin elegantly enough, speaks fluently, and perfectly understands whatever he reads. At last, graciously rewarding me as I returned, he gave me three hundred ducats, and promised that he would give greater things in time. Later, when he heard that I had been established in this abbey, he also sent other splendid gifts. You have, briefly, the answer to what you asked.

And behold, here I now live for myself and for the study of writings so much more freely as I am less occupied with the cares of temporal affairs. The monastery over which I preside is rather poor and small, and for that reason involved in few affairs. *Trithemius’s writings.* If you ask for the list of my lucubrations produced meanwhile, I shall also set this out for you in a brief account. *Two books of familiar letters.* My familiar letters, which I sent to friends from the day on which I left Spanheim, namely the first day of April in the year of the Christians 1505, up to my entrance into this abbey, which was the fifteenth of October of the following year, I gathered into one book. To this I added a second also, which contains only the letters of the first year of my prelacy in this monastery; into it I inserted this one too.

Then I wrote an itinerary of my whole life from the time of my birth up to this year. After this I composed one book *On the Praises of Saint Joseph, Foster-father of the Savior*. *The work Polygraphia.* Likewise this summer I wrote a large work, which I entitled *Polygraphia*, in six books, for the aforesaid prince, Lord Joachim of Brandenburg. I also wrote certain other smaller works, the titles of which I think it superfluous to set down. Besides, when I have completed the things now in hand, perhaps you will learn of them.
