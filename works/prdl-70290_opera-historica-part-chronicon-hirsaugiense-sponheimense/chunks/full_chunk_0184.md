In these times Saint William flourished, formerly count of Poitou, a most renowned hermit, who wore a mail-shirt against his bare flesh and mortified his body with the greatest abstinence, glorious for many miracles.

When he was obstinately siding with the schismatics against Pope Innocent, Saint Bernard, by means of the most sacred body of the Lord, so firmly terrified him and brought him to such compunction that he not only laid aside all obstinacy, but, what is more, abandoned his county, despised all honors and riches, fled men, and entered the wilderness. In these times too the wondrous vision of the knight Tondalus is said to have taken place in Ireland; since it is everywhere very well known, there is no need for it to be inserted into this work.

In these times there flourished in the Church of God many very learned men, who published various little works for the instruction of the simple.

Gilbert, surnamed Porreta, a Frenchman by nation, formerly a scholastic of Paris and then bishop of Poitiers, a man most learned in every respect, wrote a notable exposition on the Psalms; likewise on the Gospel of John, and also on all the epistles of Paul; on Boethius, *On the Holy Trinity*; and in philosophy he published many subtle treatises.

Lucas also, abbot of Saint Cornelius, of our order, a very learned man, among other little works of his studies, published in these times a work on the Song of Songs of Solomon, not to be despised in reading. Richard of Saint-Victor, a Parisian canon, a man in these times, by everyone’s judgment, by far the most learned in the sacred scriptures, and distinguished no less by the holiness of his manner of life than by his literary learning, published many excellent volumes, the reading of which is set before both the learned and the [unclear]. He also wrote commentaries on the Psalms and on the Epistle, which are quite useful. We have already long since made mention of his little works in the book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*.

Around these times too Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris, gathered from various sayings of the holy fathers and transmitted to posterity the four books of the *Sentences*; today they are of such authority in the schools of theologians that they are preferred in daily reading to almost all others. He also wrote commentaries on the Psalms and on the Apostle, which are quite useful, and certain other works which have not come to our knowledge through reading.

In these same times another Peter also was famous, a priest of Troyes, whom people commonly call Comestor. He wrote an outstanding work to William, archbishop of Sens, and certain other little works not to be rejected in reading. Richard, a monk of Cluny, of our order, is held in esteem among the chronographers in these times; among the many things he wrote, he composed a history of the events of past time, quite pleasant and useful.

In these times also, under the first Emperor Frederick, Joachim, abbot of the monastery of Fiore in Calabria, of our order, flourished: a man indeed studious and learned, who, being regarded by many as a prophet, was also accustomed to foretell future things. He wrote on the prophet Jeremiah, on the prophet Daniel, on the Gospel of John, and on the Apocalypse of John; in all these he always attempts to foretell future things. He also wrote several works to Henry VI. Likewise on the Seven Seals in the Apocalypse; on the Psalter of ten strings; likewise the book *Concordia*. Likewise, concerning twenty-five future Roman pontiffs after his own time, he wrote one prophecy. He also composed many letters to various persons, and various works concerning future times. In this very year of the Lord’s nativity 1357, the sign of the cross appeared plainly in the moon.

In the same year the lord Hartwig, the aforesaid abbot of this monastery, on the eighth day before the Kalends of April, around the first watch of the night, released from the flesh, rested in the Lord, on the fifty-sixth [unclear].
