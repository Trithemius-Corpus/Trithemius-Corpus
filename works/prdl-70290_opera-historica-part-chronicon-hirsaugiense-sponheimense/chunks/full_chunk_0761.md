“This woman promises that with spells she can release what minds she wishes, but impose harsh cares on others; stop water in rivers and turn the stars backward; she calls up the spirits of the night; you will see the earth groan beneath your feet, and ash trees descend from the mountains.”

Then there are the things Lucan reports about that Thessalian witch, and Homer about Circe’s omnipotence. Of these, I admit, very many are of such false opinion, such superstitious diligence, and such harmful labor that, although they cannot fall under a non-wicked art, they nevertheless presume that they can be clothed with the venerable title of magic. Since these things, then, stood so, I greatly wondered, and was no less indignant, that no one had so far appeared who had vindicated so sublime and sacred a discipline from the charge of impiety, or had handed it down to us purely and sincerely.

For those among the more recent writers whom I have seen, Roger Bacon, Robert the Englishman, Peter of Abano, Albert the German, Arnold of Villanova, Anselm of Parma, Picatrix the Spaniard, Cecco d’Ascoli the Florentine, and very many others, but writers of obscure name, when they promise to hand down magic, have offered nothing except certain ravings supported by no reason, or superstitions unworthy of all honorable men. Hence my spirit was stirred within me, and, because of both wonder and indignation, I wished to philosophize, thinking that I, who from earliest age have always been a curious and fearless investigator of operations full of mysteries and marvelous effects, would be undertaking a work not unworthy of praise if I restored magic itself, the ancient discipline of all the wise, redeemed and purified from the errors of impiety and adorned with its own principles, and vindicated it from the injury of calumniators.

Although I had long deliberated on this with myself, nevertheless I had not until now dared to descend into this arena. But after the discussion held between us at Würzburg about these matters, your surpassing skill and learning, and your ardent exhortation, gave me boldness and courage. And so, having selected opinions from philosophers of proven trustworthiness, having purged away the introduction of the malicious, who, lying with feigned knowledge, taught that the traditions of the magi and marvelous operations were to be sought from the very condemned books of darkness, as though from the very [adepts?], and having dispelled the shadows, I have composed in these recent days three books on magic, embraced in a compendium of brevity, and have entitled them, under a less offensive title, *On Occult Philosophy*. These I now submit to your examination, since you possess the fullest expertise in these matters, to be corrected, assessed, and judged, so that if anything has been written by me in them that could tend either to contempt of nature, or offense against the heavenly beings, or injury to religion, you may condemn the error; but if the scandals of impiety have been dissolved and purged away, you may defend the handing down of the truth; and so that you may deal with these books of ours and with magic itself in such a way that nothing which could be useful is hidden, nor anything approved which can do nothing but harm, so that at last these books, approved by your examination, may be worthy sometime to go forth into public under favorable auspices, and may not fear to undergo the judgment of posterity. Farewell; spare these bold undertakings.

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Würzburg, formerly of Sponheim, sends greeting and affection to his Henry Cornelius Agrippa of Nettesheim.*

Your work, most distinguished Agrippa, marked with the title *On the More Occult Philosophy*, which you offered to us for examination through the bearer of the present letters: with how much pleasure we received it, no mortal tongue can ever express, nor can the pen of a writer relate. We are moved with the greatest admiration at your uncommon learning, you who, though young, penetrate such secrets, hidden from many even very learned men, and have been able to bring them to light not only beautifully and truly, but also properly and ornately. Therefore, first of all, we give thanks for your goodwill toward us; if ever we can, we shall undoubtedly repay thanks according to our powers.

We approve your work, which no one, even of the most learned men, will be able to praise sufficiently. Next, with whatever urgency we can, we admonish you, ask you, and beg you to proceed with zeal to the higher things with which you have begun, and not allow such excellent powers of your intellect to grow lukewarm in idleness, but to exercise yourself by your labor always toward better things, and to show, even to the ignorant, the light of true wisdom with which you are especially illumined by God.
