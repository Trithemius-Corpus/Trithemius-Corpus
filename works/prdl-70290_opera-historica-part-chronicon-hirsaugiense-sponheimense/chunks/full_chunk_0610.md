*XIII.*

*John Trithemius, Abbot of Sponheim, sends greetings to Matthew Herbenus.*

I congratulate you, my friend; I have rejoiced many times because at last, after the various wavering opinions of your mind, you have dedicated yourself at the sacred altars of the Lord, our Savior Jesus Christ, as a minister, priest, and sacrificer. This is the safer way to happiness, if in mind, works, and office you agree with what you have undertaken. For divine Scripture says in Leviticus: He who has a blemish shall not offer the loaves to his God, nor approach his ministry. If he is blind, if lame, if with foot or hand broken, he shall not approach to offer victims to the Lord, nor loaves to his God.

A priest has a blemish who, gaping after earthly affections, has stained the purity of his conscience with the foulness of vices. He is blind who, because of ignorance of the divine Scriptures, does not behold the light of heavenly doctrine with the understanding, and for that reason is rendered unworthy of the priestly office, because he is wholly useless for instructing the household of God in salutary doctrines. For since the office of a priest, according to the prophet's saying, is to answer when questioned concerning the law and to announce the will to the Lord's people, because he is called His angel, how will he teach the peoples entrusted to him, when he himself is wholly turned about in the darkness of ignorance?

But we readily believe that you can become learned in the law of the Lord, if you turn the zeal for secular letters, in which you have been fully instructed, with the desire of love to meditation on the holy Scriptures. For you flourish with a most noble talent; lifted up by its keenness, you may very easily grasp with love whatever you wish of divine instruction. And when you have escaped the vice of spiritual blindness, which indeed you easily can with the aid of the grace of the Holy Spirit, you must take the greatest care that you not be marked with the most shameful reproach of the limping man.

For a priest is lame, and broken in foot and hand, who in life and conduct does not walk toward the purity of evangelical truth, nor takes care to show himself, before the sheep entrusted to him, a follower of good works. Against him it is read in Exodus: Let the priests who approach the Lord be sanctified, lest perhaps I strike them. And again Scripture commands the priests, saying: Be cleansed, you who carry the vessels of the Lord.

For the priests of the Lord ought to be adorned with a twofold doctrine: namely, with uprightness of work and with the word of sacred preaching, so that they may not corrupt by wicked deeds the good which they preach to their subjects as something to be chosen. Certainly the doctrine of truth is always useful to the people of God, whether the one who teaches lives well or badly; nevertheless, in a priest it does not suffice without the example of good conduct, since by living badly he has made fruitless the good which by preaching he usefully set before the peoples entrusted to him.

Therefore no one, as Augustine holds, harms the Church of God more than he who, acting perversely, has obtained the name or order of priest. For none of his subjects presumes to rebuke this man when he sins; he is drawn more forcefully into an example of fault, when, vicious as he is, he is honored out of reverence for his order. Since, therefore, bad priests are the ruin of the people, we must provide with great caution that no one, scandalized by our example, should fall into the pit by reason of our occasion for sinning.

The multitude of those like you does not excuse you when you sin; rather flee the examples of the many who do not turn purely to priestly instruction. For it is a most foolish consolation to wish to be lost with many, rather than to be saved with few. For there are many priests, as Chrysostom says, and few priests: many indeed in name, very few in deed. See therefore how you sit upon the chair, because the chair does not make the priest, but the priest makes the chair. The place does not sanctify the man, but the man the place. He who sits well upon the chair receives honor from it; he who sits badly does injury to the chair.

You sit in judgment: if indeed you have lived well and taught well, you will be judge of all. But if you have taught well and lived badly, as judge of yourself alone you will condemn only yourself. For by living well and teaching well, you instruct the people how it ought to live rightly, and you show that you are a faithful minister of the Lord, to be rewarded by Him. But if you live well and do not teach well the people entrusted to you, you have sinned, because by not instructing the people you destroy them; and when they perish through your fault, how will you, a priest without a people, be saved? But when, living badly, you teach well, you prescribe to God the form in which He ought to condemn you.

For this reason I exhort you, Herbenus, dearest brother: take care as diligently as [unclear], so that you may be able to be adorned with both kinds of beauty [unclear].
