*Chronicle of Trithemius, 1325.*

In this year, which was the sixteenth of Abbot Willicho, an exchange of the following kind was made between Johann and Simon his brother, counts of Sponheim, on the one side, and Willicho, abbot of this monastery, with his convent, on the other side. The aforesaid counts gave to this monastery the estate which we still possess peacefully today in Basenheim, with all its appurtenances, free, released, and immune from every service, levy, and exaction whatsoever, to be possessed forever, in return for the castle of Coppenstein with its revenues, together with the little villages of Richweiler and Geilweiler, with tithes, rents, meadows, fields, woods, waters, fisheries, and all other appurtenances, as is clear in the letters drawn up concerning this matter and strengthened with the seals of the two aforesaid counts; their date is the year of the Lord 1325, on the day of St Lawrence, martyr.

This count Johann had as a concubine the daughter of one of his squires, a virgin of considerable beauty, whom he had violated; she at length bore him a son, whom the count his father had legitimized through the emperors Louis IV and likewise Charles IV. When he had been enrolled among the military class, he gave him the title of the aforesaid castle of Coppenstein, whose line of descent, multiplied, flourishes down to the present day. Then, at Count Johann’s request, Abbot Willicho assigned his aforesaid son as his advocate in the monastery’s village which is called Omme, [unclear] chickens to be given to him every year by the inhabitants of the same village as a fief: namely on this condition, that the eldest son of the lineage, by lawful successions, when the advocate has died, receive the fief from the abbot of this monastery, take an oath, and promise fidelity to the abbot in the manner of vassals. He shall have no other right over the men of the aforesaid village except what the abbot has established for him, nor shall he burden them in any respect, because every right in that village, in meadows, fields, pastures, gardens, vineyards, groves, hunts, waters and watercourses, roads and trackless places, cultivated and uncultivated, acquired and to be acquired, with lordly judgment and justice, the appointment and removal of the Schultheiss and the scabini, and universal temporal jurisdiction, belongs to the abbot, to whom alone, and not to the advocate, the aforesaid inhabitants of the aforesaid place owe homage. When it is necessary to make an inquiry in criminal cases for putting malefactors to death or mutilating them, this belongs to the office of the advocate, yet in such a way that from the revenues belonging to the treasury, and from the fine which they call Frevel, the abbot receives two parts and the advocate the third part. But if the abbot, moved by mercy, wishes to remit the fine for poor persons, the advocate shall not have the power to object, but shall also be bound to remit his own part. Finally, both the advocate himself and his heirs after him, after the succession in this year, which was the seventeenth of his rule, Abbot Willicho by writing; nevertheless Abbot Willicho could first come into possession in this year, which was the seventeenth of his rule.

Willicho, abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, in the seventeenth year of his rule, bought an estate in the village of Sponheim for seventy marks of Cologne denarii, three Heller being reckoned for one denarius, from Conrad, Rudolf, and Simon, brothers, and their sons, and with the seals of Johann count of Sponheim; their date is the year of the Lord 1413, on the Exaltation of the Holy Cross. And although the purchase was made in the year described in the letters, nevertheless into possession in this year, which was the sixteenth of his rule, [unclear]. The first duke of Guelders, named Reinald, who had held his father of the same name captive for eight years in the castle called Montfort, after he died in custody on the seventh ides of October, powerfully held the principality for almost twenty years; he had previously governed it for eight years, during which he held his father captive, under the title of administrator.

He was created duke by Emperor Louis IV, with the title of the duchy of Guelders retained together with the county of Zutphen annexed to it down to the present day.

In the eighteenth year of Abbot Willicho, King Louis, having often been admonished by John, the Roman pontiff, to come to Avignon, where the pontiff was then staying, to receive the crown of the empire, because he judged that neither sufficiently just nor safe, set out for Rome with many princes and a great army, writing to the pope that either he himself should come to Rome, or send some cardinals to mark him with the crown of the empire. Since the pope [unclear], he had himself crowned; this so displeased Pontiff John that he [unclear] him from the communion of the faithful.
