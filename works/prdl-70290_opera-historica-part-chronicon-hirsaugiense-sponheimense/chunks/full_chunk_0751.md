Familiar letters, without order, still lie scattered in loose papers; if I can, I shall take care at some time to gather them into one volume.

These are my writings, dearest Roger Sycamber, which I remember having written up to the present in the abbey of Sponheim, amid continual occupations with household affairs and very frequent visitations in the order, as you know, from the year of the Christians 1483, on the twenty-ninth day of the month of July, when I was elected abbot of Sponheim, down to the year 1505, namely the first day of the month of April, when I left Sponheim for the last time, never again to return. From the time of my departure from Sponheim to my entrance into this abbey of St. James, there passed eighteen months and fifteen days, during which I was able to write little, except certain familiar letters, which I afterwards here reduced into one book. But during the nine months in which meanwhile I had remained with the most serene prince Joachim, Elector in the March of Brandenburg, a fact not unknown to you, I was by no means idle, but at his command I composed certain little works, some of which I completed, while I left the rest unfinished. For at that prince’s urging I abridged the histories of several saints, with devotion to whom he burned most fervently, and I gathered many various supplicatory prayers concerning the same into one volume, which begins thus: Great and arduous work, for love of you, Prince, etc.

At the same time also, while I was staying in the March, at the command of the aforesaid prince I wrote, on various and different matters concerning antiquities arising from daily discussion, fourteen books in one volume, a work which I entitled Panalethia; its prologue begins thus: There are many things, great teacher, etc.

I also wrote another hieratic work for the same prince, necessary for driving away various diseases, divided into thirty-four sections; its first prologue begins thus: The things which I undertake to write for your majesty, etc.

The three volumes written above I completed to the end; but no one compels me here to state the titles of the remaining works which I have not yet finished.

These, dearest Roger, are the little works which I composed in twenty-three years as abbot of Sponheim; those which follow I wrote afterwards as abbot of the monastery of St. James the Apostle in the suburb of Würzburg, over which I now preside, unworthy as I am, being free for this application to letters so much the more freely as, because of the poverty of temporal affairs, I am less occupied in external matters. For knowing the place to be poor and quiet, I took it up the more willingly, because I hoped that there I could be more freely at leisure for the study of the Scriptures. For you know that several great, rich abbeys had been offered to me; trusting that this poor little one suited my purpose, I took it up, in which I have not yet completed a whole year, whence I have composed few works.

First I wrote one book in praise of the most holy Joseph, the foster-father of our Lord and Savior JESUS CHRIST, to which I added a rosary of fifty articles from the proclamations of his merits. Also an office with prayers, and a prose, which they call a sequence. This work begins thus: Often with great desire I resolved to satisfy my own devotion.

After this I wrote to my brother Jacob Trithemius two books of my familiar letters, the first of which contains certain letters, sixty-six in number, which I wrote to various persons throughout all that time from when I left the monastery of Sponheim until my entrance into this place. The second likewise contains sixty letters, which in this first year of this abbey I wrote to various persons. This work begins thus: You have reminded me quite opportunely, sweetest one, etc.

Besides this, in this very year I wrote a great and laborious work divided into six partial books, which I entitled, with a Greek word, Polygraphia, which in Latin means “much writing.” For it teaches, with wondrous natural subtlety, many, indeed infinite, modes of writing, of communicating most secretly and most safely and without any suspicion, universally in every language of the whole world, whatever shall have to be communicated or written. This work begins thus: We read that very many of the wise men of old, etc. But it should be noted, Roger, that it is fitting for no one to possess this work so much as great and powerful princes, by means of which they can communicate their most secret affairs to those absent without fear, so that the whole world could not discover their secret without knowledge of this book; and that knowledge is easily learned once the volume has been obtained and is retained in memory without labor. Therefore I resolved to offer this work to a certain prince, whose wisdom and virtue deserve every good thing.
