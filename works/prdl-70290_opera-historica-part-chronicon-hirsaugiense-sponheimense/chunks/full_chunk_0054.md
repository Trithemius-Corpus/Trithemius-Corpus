And indeed Everard received this end, worthy of his malice, he who was the author and occasion of the destruction of this monastery. When he was dead, Conrad the abbot and the monks who had remained were able to live in the monastery with greater peace and security, since he who had been the chief of all the evil had been removed from their midst. But of the things which had been carried off from his monastery, whether by the apostate or by the count and the other ministers of those men, absolutely nothing was restored; rather, as far as could be done, more things were continually alienated and came into the hands of laymen. In this misery and affliction Conrad the abbot, with his brothers, now reduced to a small number, grew old as long as he lived; after his death, twelve years later, as we shall say, the monks were expelled and laymen were introduced by the count.

In the same year in which Conrad was elected abbot, they say that grains of wheat and little fish rained from heaven in Gaul; yet these were of no use for human consumption, because within they were quite plainly too corrupt and foul. In that year also St. Dunstan, archbishop, whom we mentioned far above, migrated to the Lord on the 14th kalends of June.

In this same year, when Pope John XVI died, Bruno, a German by nation, a kinsman of the emperor Otto, became pope, and was called Gregory V. He crowned Otto to the Roman Empire. In the second year of Conrad the abbot, Theophanu, mother of the emperor, died, and was buried in the basilica of St. Pantaleon at Cologne, in the southern apse, which while she lived she honored with the highest reverence and munificently endowed with her own goods. She also, among other things, brought from Italy to the aforesaid monastery of St. Pantaleon the body of St. Alban the martyr, who had once suffered for Christ in Britain.

*DCCCCXCIIII.*

In the fourth year of Conrad the abbot, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 994, the monastery of St. Michael the Archangel at Hildesheim, of our order, began to be founded by St. Bernward, bishop of the same city, who was also buried in the same monastery and shone forth with countless miracles.

*DCCCCXCV.*

In the following year St. Adalbert, formerly a monk of our order and bishop of Prague, about whom we spoke above, who converted the Hungarians to the faith, completed his martyrdom among the pagans in Prussia, pierced through seven times and at last beheaded, on the 9th kalends of May. Pope Gregory V, having gathered a synod, decreed, with the Romans and all the clergy consenting, that the Roman Empire ought to remain among the Germans for all future times, so that the princes of Germany appointed by Otto III should have the right and power of electing the emperor. This constitution has now been observed down to our time, through five hundred and six years, with inviolable stability.

In these times flourished Gerbert, a monk of our order from the monastery of Fleury, a man in mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, arithmetic, and almost all the other [unclear]; Emperor Otto too is said to have been by no means among the last. Because of his incomparable knowledge, he was first appointed archbishop of Reims in Gaul by Hugh, king of France, and then driven out; by Emperor Otto he was appointed archbishop of Ravenna; and finally he became supreme pontiff in the city of Rome and was called Sylvester II. He wrote a fine work on the sphere. Also on the construction of the astrolabe and the quadrant. In arithmetic too he made fine compositions. Many greatly commend the dialogue which he had with Leo the abbot, the apostolic nuncio. We have seen several letters which he wrote to the more inquisitive of his disciples. This is Pope Sylvester, who is accused by very many of having entered into a pact and treaty with demons; on this matter we neither wish nor ought to give judgment. He sat in the supreme pontificate for four years.

In these times also, after the death of St. Maiolus, abbot of Cluny, of our order, St. Odilo, a man of outstanding holiness, was made abbot at Cluny. He presided over the same monastery with the greatest fervor of religion for nearly fifty-six years. Since he was a learned man, he is said to have written some little works, and especially sermons to the brothers, in which he showed the dexterity of his talent.
