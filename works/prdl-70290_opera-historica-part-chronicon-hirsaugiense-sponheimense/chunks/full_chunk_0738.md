What and how great lies they then devised against me, time has already partly brought to light, and has left no doubt that it will very soon bring the rest to light. But this one thing was the chief cause of all evils against me: that my rivals held in hidden hatred the most clement prince, whom, as I ought, I always loved. Nor only certain of the [Cynonoti?], but also almost all my brothers, together with the abbot and all the monks of the monastery of Belli Montis. But I, seeing that the wicked had prevailed against me, changed my place; and, having accepted the abbey in which I now dwell in peace, I resigned Spanheim together with its most ungrateful monks. And perhaps thus it pleased almighty God, by whose providence, as Saint Jerome says on Ezekiel, all things are governed: adversity dispensed to us by him, which is thought to be punishment, is medicine. To what great miseries the pastors of cloistered men are subjected, and by what great inconveniences they are battered, you yourself experience by daily [unclear]. Up to that point I judged that all things were to be endured patiently for the love of God. But when the betrayal of my own men had entered in against me, patience thereafter would have been wholly without fruit. Therefore, following Saint Benedict, leader of monks, I, a lover of peace, left the monks whom, hardened as they were, I could not advance to sounder things. Farewell, sound in mind and happy in body, praying for me. From Würzburg, on the twenty-eighth day of the month of July, in the year of Christians 1507.

*XL.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James in the Würzburg suburb, to Roger Venray the Sicambrian: sincere greeting and charity in the Lord.*

On the first day of the month of August I received, by an uncertain messenger, your letter sent to me, as appeared in the subscription, on the Kalends of May; in it [unclear] certain poems into my book [unclear] by no means reached my hands. After two days another man appeared, who presented me with your letter written on the seventeenth day of the month of July; he said that it had been entrusted to him by some other man, I know not who, together with two little works of yours, written in your hand. One of them contains certain familiar odes in various verse; the other, composed in prose, comprises a certain [unclear] disputation. The subject does not displease me, nor am I averse to the argument of your inventions, but I would wish you to devote your effort to more important studies, which your time of life, your age, seems to require of you. You have passed the fiftieth year of your life; what studies should henceforth become you is especially for you to consider. Youth advised us to one thing; old age bids us search out another. The sun of our vital strength is declining toward its setting; the evening hour gradually consumes the bodily powers that midday supplied. Henceforth care must now be taken of the soul, which remains immortal. We have yielded enough to the flesh. Let the little ass be nourished only with necessary fodder; all wantonness will fall far away. Let childish lucubrations give way; since in age you present a man, embrace compositions becoming to gray hairs, not fables. You have composed one hundred and thirty-six little works. It is a great thing, and in my judgment praiseworthy, that you have turned the leisure that regular discipline permitted to the study of letters, and have directed your mind rather to composing books than to the pleasures of the flesh. Yet little works are not to be weighed at all by number, but by usefulness: if they instruct the mind and understanding of the reader toward true wisdom; if, lifted from earthly things, they raise it to heavenly things; if they truly pierce it with compunction in divine love. The world now everywhere bubbles forth innumerable writers, as Flaccus says:

Learned and unlearned alike, we write poems everywhere;
everyone likes to rave as poets.

But the lesser part of those who write thinks of the edification of souls. From the abundance of the heart we all write, and in writing commit to letters those thoughts with which we are occupied. Hence we cannot write spiritual things that induce the fervor of divine love, because, shameful to say, we seethe, full of worldly and carnal thoughts. For no one without faith and the charity of Jesus Christ, who is the wisdom of God the Father, will be able to attain true and salutary wisdom, so that, namely, one who savors carnal and transitory things should write spiritual things.
