574 *Familiar Letters.*

The weary ox plants his foot firmly. Since, in the judgment of the wise, no one can be truly learned who has sworn allegiance only to the rudiments of a single faculty. But God has endowed you with a broad and lofty talent. Therefore do not imitate oxen, but birds; and do not think that you must linger over particular things, but confidently direct your mind to universals. For each person is considered the more learned, the fewer things he is ignorant of. Your talent, truly suited fully for all things, should reasonably be occupied not with few or lowly things, but with many and loftier ones. Yet we admonish you to keep this one precept: that you communicate common things to common people, but higher and hidden things only to higher and secret friends. Give hay to the ox, [sugar?] only to the parrot: understand the meaning, lest you be subjected to the hooves of oxen, as has happened to very many. Farewell, happy friend; and if there is anything in our possession that can be of use to you, command it, and you will understand that it has been done without delay; and, so that our friendship may daily gain strength, write to us rather often, and send something of your lucubrations, we urgently ask. Farewell again.

In our monastery at [Peapolitanus?], on the eighth day of April, in the year 1510.

*The End.*
