Not long afterward, under the same Louis, he carried it away into Germany, his homeland, and, as we have said before, placed it in the monastery of Hirsau, where to the present day almighty God works many virtues and great wonders through his merits.

*10. On Lintbert, the first abbot of this monastery, who presided for sixteen years, and on his manner of life and the deeds of that time.*

*838.*

In the year of the Lord 838, therefore, under the most pious Emperor Louis, Lintbert, a monk of Fulda, was appointed first abbot of this monastery by the election of the brethren who had come with him from the bosom of the monastery of Fulda. He was given the pastoral staff by Otgar, most reverend archbishop of the church of Mainz, and presided over the government of this monastery for sixteen years. He was born in Swabia of parents not of the lowest rank, according to worldly dignity, the brother of Buno, abbot of Hersfeld, who himself also had been for many years a monk of Saint Boniface under Abbot Baugolf. Moreover Lintbert, devoted to God from infancy, became a monk in the monastery of Fulda in the eighteenth year of his age, under the aforementioned Abbot Baugolf, who had succeeded Saint Sturm; and as his age increased, both under Baugolf himself and under his successors Eigil and Rabanus, he made great progress in holy conduct and in learned knowledge. Thus he put on the monk in the year of the Lord 796, in the fourth indiction. But he was sent to Hirsau and received the abbacy, as we have said before, in 838, in the [unclear] year of his age and the forty-second of his conversion, and ruled for sixteen years.

He was a very reverend man, known and familiar to kings and princes. From the time when he entered the monastery he not only lived irreproachably, but, what is judged to belong to greater virtue, shone with such holiness that in his way of life he furnished to all an example of true perfection. Some compositions of his teacher Rabanus on sacred letters are extant addressed to him, which seem to have been requested by the talent of a studious man. I have read under the name of Abbot Lintbert a most beautiful little work on the Song of Songs of Solomon, in which he described the desire and condition of the faithful soul thirsting for its God so beautifully, elegantly, and fittingly that, in my judgment, no one whom I have read has ever treated this subject more clearly. But that the author of this work is the same as our abbot Lintbert of Hirsau I do not hold as sufficiently proved. Yet one might persuade someone willing to believe it, because whoever he finally was, he wrote under Emperor Lothar, the son of Louis, as is clear from the preface of the work itself.

Since, however, this Lintbert our abbot was for a long time the hearer and fellow student of Rabanus of Fulda, a man most learned in all respects, it must be believed that he could only have turned out most learned. For Rabanus himself, while still a monk, had held the public monastic school in the monastery of Fulda, which he continued also as abbot; in it he instructed the monks in every way, not only in sacred Scripture but also in secular letters. Nor did he train only the monks of the monastery of Fulda, but also many pupils in every kind of learning who had been sent to him from various other places. Of these, very many afterward in the course of time were promoted to great dignities. He had had as teacher, from among the disciples of Bede the English monk, the most reverend man Albinus, monk and abbot of the monastery of Saint Martin of Tours, formerly teacher of the great Emperor Charles; from him he himself had drawn in Gaul what he later taught others in Germany.

But Lintbert, first the fellow student and afterward the hearer of Rabanus, did not neglect as abbot what he had learned as a monk from a good teacher. He was indeed a most zealous cultivator of regular discipline, who not only [unclear], but, going before them as a good pastor, was open to view by the example of good work. His meditation, day and night, was always continued in the law of the Lord; praying without ceasing to the Lord, he remained to the end a true disciple of Christ. As the fame of his holiness and learning spread through every region round about, many noble men entrusted their sons to be instructed by his teaching.
