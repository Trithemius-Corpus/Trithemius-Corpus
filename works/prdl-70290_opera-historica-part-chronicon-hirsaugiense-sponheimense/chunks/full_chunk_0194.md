In this same year certain heretics of the sect of those who are called Cathars, coming from the parts of Flanders to Cologne, began to live secretly in a certain barn near the city. But since they did not enter the church even on the Lord’s day, they were brought in by the people living round about. They were therefore brought to the examination of holy mother Church, and were found to be most obstinate heretics.

In these times flourished Eckbert, abbot of the monastery of Saint Florinus the Confessor in Schönau, of the diocese of Trier, of our order, brother of the blessed Elisabeth, a consecrated virgin, a man most learned in the divine Scriptures and of great name and reputation in his time among ecclesiastical teachers. Therefore the clergy and the leading men of the city of Cologne, knowing him to be a man no less venerable for the holiness of his conversion than distinguished for his learning in the Scriptures, summoned him to Cologne by letters and messengers to examine the aforesaid heretics. When Abbot Eckbert came to Cologne, in the aforesaid year 1163 of the Lord’s Nativity, in the eleventh indiction, on the second day of the month of August, he entered upon a public contest of disputation with three heresiarchs who seemed more acute than the rest, namely Arnold, Marsilius, and Theodoric. Although they were very sharp, nevertheless by disputing vigorously he convicted them, so that they were altogether unable to contradict him.

And since by no arguments, no authorities, and no admonitions could they be induced to renounce their error, but persisted most obstinately in their purpose, they were cast out completely by the Church and handed over into the hands of the laity. These led them outside the city and, on the Nones of August, handed them over to the flames: eight men and two women. Among them was one young woman; when, through the pity of the people, she was almost being saved, in case perhaps, terrified by the death of the others, she might acquiesce in sounder counsel, she suddenly slipped from the hands of those holding her, threw herself voluntarily into the flames, and perished.

These heretics were entangled in many errors, Cataphrygians, or Cathars, so called because they said that they themselves were pure and the only Christians, but all other people impure and heretics. Despising the rulers of churches, prelates, priests, and all the clergy, they called them deceivers of souls and snares of the devil. They mocked the sacrament of the Church and denied the most holy body and blood. Lust committed among themselves in whatever manner or with whomever they said was not a sin, alleging: “All things are pure to the pure.” They also professed many other abominable errors, about which we blush to write for the sake of decency.

Against these Cathars, Saint Hildegard, inspired by the Holy Spirit, wrote a letter to the canons of the greater church of Mainz, which begins thus: “In the month of July of the present year, which is the 1163rd of the Lord’s Incarnation,” etc. The origin [unclear] of this heresy had its source from the dregs of Tanchelm, about whom we spoke much earlier, in the fifth year of Abbot Volmar; in the lower regions, and especially in Antwerp, he disseminated most foul errors and revived the heresy of the Adamites.

And although through the brothers of Saint Norbert, the first founder of the Premonstratensian order, that same heresy was then for the greater part extinguished, especially in that region, nevertheless meanwhile it has so often sprouted again in various provinces of the world, and especially in the kingdom of Bohemia, in Alsace, and in Thuringia, that down to the present day it has infected many with the venom of its depravity; nor is it even today believed to be wholly extinct, although it is very hidden and known to very few. For, through fear of fire, the professors of this error have always been accustomed to love hidden lurking-places. Since mention has fallen upon Abbot Eckbert, we must say who and how great he was. He was [unclear] of the aforesaid most devout [unclear], abandoned the world and all things that are in the world for the love of Christ, and under Hildelin, first abbot of the monastery of Schönau, became a monk.

When he died, succeeding in his place, he was appointed abbot by the election of the brothers. He was a man, both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, in his time among [unclear], ready and eloquent before the people, and he left to posterity many little works of his talent, written in a polished style.
