*The Monastery of Hirsau*

... learned to love nothing except heavenly things. In the same place she served almighty God for many years in humble chastity, until at last, after the death of the venerable Jutta, when the number of virgins had increased, instructed by divine revelation, she moved to the mountain of Saint Rupert near the town of Bingen, where the Nahe flows into the Rhine, with eighteen nuns subject to her. There, with the help of Count Meginhard of Sponheim and other faithful of Christ, she built a monastery for sacred virgins, in the very place where Saint Rupert the duke, together with his mother, a holy woman named Bertha, had had a castle and dwelling in the times of Emperor Louis I.

Under her direction, among the other sacred women, was Hiltrud, a most devout virgin, the very beautiful daughter of the aforesaid Count Meginhard, concerning whom mention was made above. For love of the heavenly kingdom she rejected broad dominion on earth and received the habit of holy life under the discipline of Jutta, her father's sister, on the mountain of the holy bishop Disibod. Out of regard for this virgin her father, Count Meginhard, bestowed many goods on that monastery, and the right of the place, which belonged partly to the church of Mainz, partly to [unclear] of Disibod, [unclear]. Out of regard for this virgin her father, Count Meginhard, [unclear] many goods to that monastery, and handed it over to Saint Hildegard, giving and granting her every kind of freedom to choose whatever protector she wished; and he released that place forever from his own lordship, power, and advocacy.

Furthermore, Saint Hildegard, virgin of Christ, visited from childhood by divine revelations, although, apart from the Psalter and simple psalmody, she had learned from no human being either the art or the practice of the Latin language, nevertheless, wondrously illumined by the grace of the Holy Spirit, wrote many things and dictated many things, distinguished partly in Latin, partly in German speech, which she learned not by human perception or understanding, but rather by divine inspiration; and therefore her writings can be understood only by spiritual persons.

She wrote a large book of much subtlety, which she entitled *Scivias*, which begins: "And it came to pass in a new year." Likewise, three books *On Divine Works*. Three books *On the Merits of Life*. *On the Causes and Remedies of All Diseases of the Human Body*, an outstanding work, which she entitled *Medicine* when she had composed it, and it begins: "God, before the creation of the world, was and is without beginning." Likewise [unclear], which deservedly turns readers to astonishment. This little work begins thus: "Charity, which with abstinence from sins instructs faith." Instructed from heaven by vision, she also wrote the life of Saint Disibod the bishop, a brief indeed but useful little work, beginning thus: "And I, [a poor little one in female form?]."
