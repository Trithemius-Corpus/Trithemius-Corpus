He was indeed a learned man and very studious, and no less by sanctity and the merit of his life than by erudition in the Scriptures was of most celebrated fame and reputation throughout all Germany; in life and after death he shone with many signs and miracles.

His deeds are found described quite clearly. *Abbot Henry [unclear] shone with miracles. Rupert, abbot of Limburg.* To this Henry, as to a holy, learned, and proven man, Rupert, abbot of Limburg, of whom we have spoken above, was banished to Breitenau by order of the bishop of Speyer, on account of his abstinence of unwonted novelty, so that, brought back by him to the common mode of living, he might depart from his singularity. Concerning him the same Saint Henry the abbot makes mention more fully in a certain letter to the bishop of the church of Speyer. Finally, because of the fame of his most excellent sanctity, many men noble, powerful, and honored in the world, for the love of God, became monks in Breitenau under Saint Abbot Henry. Among these the foremost was Adelbert, count of Schoenberg, who, having despised the things and pomps of the world, although he was powerful and very rich, became a poor monk for Christ; and he lived with such purity that, not without miracles, he showed to all the monks in that place the traces of great sanctity. In this same year of Abbot Volmar II, a very violent force of winds overthrew countless buildings.

In the same year Saint Hugh, bishop of Grenoble, the first founder of the Carthusian order, a man singular among the bishops of his time in life and conduct, died. *Hugh, bishop of Grenoble, dies.* [unclear] the provost succeeded him, who raised from the earth with great honor the body of Saint Gotthard the bishop, which had lain buried for almost a hundred years. Berthold also, bishop of Hildesheim, died, and Bernard of the same church [unclear] of Cologne, of our order, of whom they spoke above, succeeded him. *The spirit Hudelin, appearing in Saxony, performed many miracles.*

In these times a certain malicious spirit, in the diocese of Hildesheim, appeared visibly for a long time to many people in rustic garb, his head covered with a cap; whence the country people commonly called him “the capped one,” that is, “ein Hedeckm” in the Saxon tongue. This spirit Hukin did many marvelous things and took pleasure in being with human beings, speaking, asking, and answering everyone familiarly, appearing sometimes visibly, sometimes invisibly. He harmed no one unless he had first been harmed, but mindful of injuries or mockeries, he hindered in turn those who inflicted insult upon him.

*The county is occupied by him.* When Burcard, count of Luca, had been killed by Count Hermann of Winsenburg, and the county of Winsenberg itself seemed exposed to plunder, the spirit [unclear] came to Bernard, bishop of Hildesheim, sleeping in his bed, and woke him, saying: “Arise, O bald man; summon the army, because the county of Winsburg, empty and desolate on account of the homicide, will easily submit to his command.” Rising, he summoned his soldiers, invaded the county, and obtained it; with the emperor’s consent he joined it forever to the church of Hildesheim. *The bishop [unclear] on the walls.*

The same spirit also, without being asked, often used to warn the same bishop of many dangers. Appearing rather frequently in the court of the same prelate, he was generally accustomed to serve the cooks quite diligently, and often to mingle conversations with them in the kitchen.
