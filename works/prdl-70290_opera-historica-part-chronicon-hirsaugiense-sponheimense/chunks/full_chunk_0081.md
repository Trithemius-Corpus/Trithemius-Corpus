*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius.*

...by word and example most strict in the observance of regular discipline, kindled by the ardor of the Holy Spirit, he recalled; with the greatest zeal he desired in a wondrous way to bring back to the monks of his own time that former fervor of spirit with which our holy fathers Benedict, Maurus, Placidus, Columbanus, Aegidius, Gregory, Boniface, Lullus, and almost innumerable others once burned toward God. Indeed, he erected seven monasteries of our order in different provinces, partly by himself in his own person, and partly through his monks and disciples, and built them from their foundations for the honor of God.

Of these the first was the cell of St. Gregory, which is called Richenbach, concerning whose foundation we shall speak more fully below. The second is the monastery of St. Gregory the martyr in the Black Forest, in the diocese of Constance, in which he placed monks from his own Hirsau community, men approved and most renowned in every religious observance. Over them he appointed as abbot Henry, his disciple, a monk of this monastery, a man of tested religious life, who by word and example encouraged his subjects to contempt of present things and love of things to come. The third, indeed, he founded in Bavaria in honor of St. Martin the bishop, where likewise from his own community he set monks and an abbot; and they, without doubt, having been trained in regular observances under a most holy father, could not easily have failed to be most religious, especially since the father, full of God, was accustomed with most prudent consideration to send to new plantations not any of the weaker monks, but the better ones whom he had.

The fourth he founded in Thuringia, in the territory of Erfurt, a monastery in honor of the holy apostles of Christ Paul [unclear], in which likewise he placed brothers from his own Hirsau community; and he entrusted that monastery and the brothers to Gilbert, formerly a monk of Hirsau and at that time abbot in Reinhardsbrunn, to be governed and managed until he had provided them with an abbot. But since the brothers who today inhabit that monastery of Erfurt say that Dagobert, king of the Franks, was their founder, perhaps someone will be able to accuse us of falsehood because we dare to call William, abbot of Hirsau, its founder. Therefore we bring forward as a witness of the truth the history of his life, in which the things we have said about this matter are plainly contained. Nor do we fear dispute, since if Dagobert was the first founder, William was not the first, but, after the ruin of the earlier monastery, the second founder.

The fifth monastery of our order he founded in [unclear] of the most glorious mother of God, the Virgin Mary, at the Double Water, building it anew; in it he placed monks and a rector from his own community, men venerable in their manner of life. The sixth he established as a monastery of the servants of God in a certain village called Wielheim; yet afterwards it was transferred to Mount St. Peter, to which likewise he gave monks and a shepherd. The seventh monastery of our order he founded in the province of the Charentini, which is called Lavense; to it he sent brothers from his own community, men of approved conduct, and he appointed over them as abbot Wetzilo, a venerable monk.

Moreover, many monasteries of our order in the provinces of Germany, almost collapsed through excessive age, he restored to their former state; some begun by others, but not completed, he carried on and brought to completion. Furthermore, after the holy father William had provided monks, abbots, and stipends, with the mercy of the Almighty cooperating with him through the offerings of the faithful, he wondrously increased the number of the brothers and provided all necessities. For he had one hundred and fifty monks under his discipline, praising the Lord daily, for whom he provided, frugally and according to the rule of our most holy father Benedict, what was necessary for body and spirit. In addition to this number of monks he had several other bearded brothers, whom in the customary term we call conversi; they, applying themselves to the labors of their hands, managed the care of temporal things according to the commands of the seniors and provided bodily necessities for the monks, who were always devoted to contemplation and prayer. They report, finally, that this most reverend father William first either instituted, or rather restored after it had been abolished, the use of conversi of this sort in the monasteries of Germany...
