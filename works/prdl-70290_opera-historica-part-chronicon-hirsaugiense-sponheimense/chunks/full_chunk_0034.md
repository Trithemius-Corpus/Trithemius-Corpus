*936.*

Thus in the second year of Abbot Diethmar, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 936, in the tenth indiction, Otto I was anointed king by the hands of Hildebert, archbishop of Mainz.

For when two bishops, namely those of Trier and Cologne, were contending with one another as to which of them ought by right to perform this office of consecration, by the judgment of the nobles it was assigned to Hildebert, bishop of Mainz.

He went up to the altar, took from it the imperial sword with its belt, turned to the king, who was bending his knees before him, and said: “Receive this sword, by which [unclear] all enemies of Christ, barbarians and bad Christians, handed over to you by divine authority, with all power of the whole empire of the Franks, for the most firm peace of all Christians. Amen.” Then, taking the bracelets and cloak, he clothed him, saying: “By these horns hanging down to the shoulders, be reminded with what zeal for the faith you ought to burn, and that you should persevere constant in defending peace to the end.” Next, taking the sceptre and staff, he said: “By these signs be warned that with fatherly correction you should rebuke those subject to you, and that first to God’s ministers, then also to widows, orphans, and wards, you should always show yourself protector and defender, and mercifully extend to them the hand of mercy.” After this, when the oil of consecration had been poured upon his head, he said: “The Lord has anointed you king over his people, so that the oil of mercy may never fail from your head, whereby here and in the future you may obtain mercy from him, your Creator. Amen.” Last, he placed the royal crown upon his head, saying: “He has crowned you with a crown of justice, by which you are to be reminded that you ought to administer justice to your subjects, so that, fortified by this royal ornament, you may appear revered by your own people and terrible to your enemies, and after the end of this life may be crowned with an everlasting reward with the angels. Amen.” When the bishop’s blessing had been completed, they placed the king on the royal throne with all joy.

*937.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Diethmar, the monasteries of St. Gall in Swabia, of Boniface at Fulda, and of the Scots were consumed by fire.

In the same year Duke Eberhard captured Henry, the emperor’s brother, whom his father, after expelling Arnolf, had appointed duke of Bavaria; but when the Bavarians were soon defeated by Otto, his brother was released from prison.

In these times, after St. Odo, Adamarus, abbot of Cluny, flourished, a man distinguished enough for holiness of life and learning in the Scriptures, who at last, deprived of the light of his eyes, [unclear] succeeded, who built the monastery of St. Peter the Apostle outside the walls of Mainz to the north.

*940.*

In the fifteenth year of Abbot Diethmar, [unclear], formerly abbot of our order, as we said above, archbishop of Mainz, died; Frederick succeeded him. The monastery of our order was founded in the city of Hildesheim, later transferred outside the city onto a mountain, which we call Berg. This foundation Otto I, emperor, began and completed; the first abbot of the monastery was Anno of St. Maximin of Trier, a man very learned in both divine and secular writings, and venerable no less for integrity of morals than for knowledge.

*943.*

In the eighteenth year of Abbot Diethmar, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 943, Diethard, monk and scholasticus of this place, died; he had presided over the public school of the monks for twenty-two years after Luthelm the monk. He was a man most learned in every knowledge of the Scriptures, and no less notable for [unclear] than for doctrine. He died on the twelfth day before the Kalends of October, in the first indiction. In the office of teaching there succeeded him Meginrad, a very venerable monk, who in his youth had been a hearer of Luithelm, who was abbot of the monastery of St. Gall, a man very learned in every knowledge; after his death, under the aforesaid monk Diethard, he made great progress in every knowledge, both divine and secular, and left behind him many very learned hearers.
