*Familiar Letters*

For if you have searched very much into them, you will often wonder. Necessary and useful things must be learned by us, the knowledge of which may illuminate the mind, separated from earthly things, into the knowledge of the highest truth, and may inflame it with love of everlasting goodness. It is foolish, while useful and necessary things have been omitted, to learn vain and useless things, and, with the Creator neglected, to occupy the mind with created things. Pernicious and plainly damnable is the curiosity of many men of this age, who, having despised the most salutary humility of Christ, in order that they may seem learned and great, most diligently investigate what Plato, what Aristotle, what Anaxagoras, what Democritus, what Pythagoras, and the rest of that kind thought, men who, it is agreed, were far removed from the knowledge and teaching of the highest truth, as though from among our own there were lacking philosophers and most learned men, by imitation of whom [unclear]. We have many better than the philosophy of the gentiles, whose teaching, as seems clear to us, we ought to imitate: namely the holy apostles of Christ, Peter, Paul, and John, and their followers, Origen, Athanasius, Hilary, Irenaeus, Chrysostom, Basil, Eusebius, Firmianus, Jerome, Augustine, Gregory, Bede, Rabanus, Bernard, and the remaining teachers of the catholic faith, whose certain wisdom concerning truth instructs Christian minds unto justice, truly illumines the understanding, forms charity, and gives life to souls. Imitate these wise men according to your powers, and by reading and learning their books frequently turn over useful things; do not allow the times of youth given to you to pass uselessly. Flee idleness as a pernicious plague of the soul, since it is especially contrary to industry and study, and consumes the mind as rust consumes iron. One must occupy the talents with good study, and the desire for true wisdom must be implanted more deeply, so that when old age arrives, the mind, formed by heavenly teachings, may stand firm before any faces of fortune whatever. And we pray that you not be ashamed to imitate us, your brother, who, as you know, after the years of life have passed, have spent all our time on the study of the Scriptures, even to the envy of frogs. For if we had not done this, where now, brother, do you think we would be, with fortune stinking against us, except lying everywhere? For although we have come out only a little learned, nevertheless with God's help we have learned to stand firm in ourselves, to despise the various deceitful faces of fortune, and to understand what the end of ignorance is. No one will take from us what is ours, since we have learned to carry our own things with us, making little of every loss of earthly things. Nor do we fear that the necessities of this life will be lacking to us, having the most certain promise of God in the holy Gospel, since he himself has care for us, and for those who seek first heavenly things neither want of food nor want of clothing will occur. Therefore, brother, we exhort you: imitate the studies of good men, not for vanity and profit, but for the cultivation of the mind, so that you may recognize the light of truth, love it when recognized, and possess it without end when loved. Again we warn you to flee idleness, since every idle man is in desires, and idleness, a spring of many vices, is hostile to the soul. Idleness softens the powers, destroys the light of minds, and makes a living man like a trunk and altogether useless. No one was ever made great by idleness, no one learned, no one excellent or of immortal memory. How does he live who lives for no one? But he lives for no one who is always idle. We were born for action, since nature, which has given us nothing more precious than time, abhors idleness and emptiness. After labor let there be rest for you, and not sloth, as much as is enough, so that you may rise stronger for action, not so that, with labor relaxed, you may grow completely numb in idleness. Love the study of the holy Scriptures, and you will never be idle. For what that carnal lover used to say about his own love, we have found true in the study of the Scriptures: let him who does not wish to become slothful love. True love is never idle. But farewell, and love good, honorable, and useful things. Again farewell. From Ursulus in the March of Brandenburg, on the twentieth day of the month of October, in the year of Christians 1503.

*XLIIII.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, sends greetings to Roger Sycambrius.*

Intending to send my chaplain to the regions of the Rhine, excellent Roger, I could not prevent myself from making you, by letter, more certain concerning the condition of our affairs. We live here by the mercy of God healthy in body, regarded as being in no low place with the prince, yet almost destitute of the consolation of learned men. The men are indeed good, but too barbarous and unlearned, more given to feasting and drinking than to the study of good letters. Rare here is the man devoted to the study of the Scriptures; but by a certain inborn [unclear] the customs of the inhabitants are pleasing, since they burn with great observance and devotion toward the religion of Christ. They visit the temples of God most diligently, the feasts of the saints [unclear].
