Our soul passed through a torrent of injuries and insults, unless the Lord Jesus had been present; perhaps our soul would have passed through the [water?] of intolerable despair, or at least would have avenged the injury in a manner not sufficiently religious. But blessed be the Lord Jesus, who did not give us as prey to the teeth of our rivals, so that we should be forced to remain longer under the rule of depraved men. Our soul, like an innocent sparrow, has been freed from the snare of the malignant hunters, whose envy knows how to spare no good man. The snare has been broken, and the vain confidence of the Cynonotes has been shattered, by which they tried in vain to recall us from holy studies into hatred of the just prince. But we, by the aid of our Lord Jesus Christ, have at last been freed from the many miseries that we endured in the governance of the monastery of Sponheim. The snare of worldly entanglement, wholly contrary to our studies, has by God’s providence been broken, and we have been freed from the servitude of most ungrateful men. The Lord Jesus has freed my soul from wicked lips and from the too-deceitful tongue of rivals.

You know, brothers, if only you were willing to bear witness to the truth, what unjust injury I received from the Cynonotes, who, having no case against me, moved by envy and spite, did not fear to violate ecclesiastical immunity. They now bring excuses for their rashness toward us, saying that they did what they did not from hatred of us but rather from love. But theirs was an evil love, and intolerable to us, by which our name, always innocent of every crime, is turned through the mouths of many into suspicion, as though on account of some wantonness of ours it had been necessary to rage so against the household.

Today the twenty-fourth year is passing since, canonically elected abbot of Sponheim, all the time that could fall to me for leisure, you yourselves being witnesses and judges, I always turned not to any pleasure of the flesh whatever, but entirely to the study of the Scriptures, intent day and night on reading, always writing or learning something. None of you ever found me idle; none saw or heard that I was given to the consolations of the flesh, or uselessly occupied in wandering excursions or walks inside or outside the monastery, nor devoted to feasting or drinking or conviviality beyond what was necessary, or sweating over any other frivolities or vanities. Nothing was ever sweeter to me than to search out the sacred Scriptures, and to be occupied in them at suitable times by day and by night, and always to read or write something good.

As testimony to our studies I summon and cite that distinguished library which I brought together by my labors, zeal, and expenses, not without vigilance and continual weariness, gathering no small multitude of volumes in every variety of studies, not only newly printed books but also books written by hand and pen, the total number of which exceeds two thousand. It would be superfluous to list our lucubrations, which we wrote at intervals in the midst of pastoral care and anxiety over temporal affairs; by them the name of the monastery of Sponheim, previously unknown and obscure, has been made quite famous. Nor, because we were intent on our studies, did we therefore neglect temporal property, as many monuments of our labors will testify; nor will you yourselves be able to deny it, seeing plainly what necessary and very beautiful buildings you have been raised in, and what ornaments we acquired in the church, and little bells, and various sacred vessels of the mystery, and also the most splendidly renewed registers of revenues. All these things, with how great labors and expenses we accomplished them, even if you do not understand, will nevertheless bring admiration to posterity.

Finally, for almost twelve years now, since we came into the acquaintance and friendship of certain princes and nobles, we brought you no small advantage from those things which had been bestowed on us by their munificence. Nothing was ever special to us, nothing our own, just as indeed it ought not to have been; but all things were always common to us with you, and there was never any singularity in us at all, neither in clothing nor in drink, and none of you is ignorant that this is true. We also always had the greatest care lest any of the things that seemed necessary to you should be lacking, and we saw to it that you suffered want in nothing, so far as was entirely possible for us. In spiritual matters, however, we never failed you, although few of you, after the departure of our brothers to whom holy studies were pleasing, loved spiritual exercises, but always occupied themselves with vain things. We wrote, we spoke, we continually preached the word of God to you, and we exhorted you, in season and out of season, publicly and privately, to the worship of true wisdom; but how much we accomplished, we blush to confess openly.

And because you made yourselves unworthy of our exhortation, perhaps by divine prudence we have been transplanted, lest the tree among thorns be pricked too long without fruit. The day has come which we desired for many years, on which the opportunity was given us to pour the blood of ungrateful sheep upon their own heads, and, shaking the dust of our feet against them, to pass by deaf [asses?], according to the Gospel precept. Hear, if you please, the chief points of what you committed against us, so that you may know that you have been abandoned by us for a most just reason. Our sacred studies always displeased you, and for all our writings you returned laughter instead of reverence, and our lucubrations never pleased anyone less.
