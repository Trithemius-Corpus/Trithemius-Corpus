Therefore, bringing a great armed army from Austria and Swabia, he first entered the lands of Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine, and, inflicting much damage on the poor, received him graciously when he came to surrender. Then, moving his army against the archbishop of Mainz, among the other losses that he brought upon him, he also surrounded Bingen, a town on the Rhine, with a siege. But the archbishop, seeing himself unequal in strength, sought and obtained the king’s grace; and coming to Bingen he lifted the siege by entreaties, and accepted the conditions of peace proposed by the king, resigning into the king’s hands the towns of Bingen and Lahnstein, with the castles Klopp, Ehrenfels, Scharfenstein, and certain others. When matters had been pacified with the archbishop of Mainz, King Albert led his army against the archbishops of Cologne and Trier, and subjected both to his rule; he forced them vigorously, not without losses on their part, to seek his grace.

In the same year, when a multitude had been stirred up in France against their rulers, craftsmen and men of the lowest condition revolted from the French; and, having become lords of the province, they overcame in war the great forces of the king of France and slew them most cruelly. But their savagery did not last long before they were all destroyed.

*A.D. 1303.*

In the third year of Abbot Henry, and of the Lord’s Nativity 1303, in the first indiction, Philip, king of the French, bearing with an unjust mind the arrogance of Pope Boniface VIII, caused him to be seized at Anagni, in his father’s house, while he was sleeping at night and fearing no such thing, and brought under guard to Rome. Worn out by grief of mind, he died thirty-five days later and was buried in the church of St Peter, after having first been taken from the tomb. Benedict XI, by nation a Trevisan, previously called Nicholas, cardinal of Ostia, succeeded him in the pontificate and presided only nine months.

*A.D. 1304.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1304, on the fifth day before the Kalends of March, Gerhard, archbishop of Mainz, born of Eppstein, died. After his death the see of Mainz was vacant for some time. Meanwhile, in the same year, at the beginning of the month of June, Pope Benedict XI died at Perugia, in the church of the Friars Preachers, of whose order he had previously been a professed member. He was buried with great pomp, not without a reputation for sanctity, and the Apostolic See was vacant for two months, until the following year, not without loss to the salvation of many souls.

*A.D. 1305.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1305, after a long vacancy of the Apostolic See, the cardinals at last assembled at Perugia and unanimously elected Bertrand, bishop of Bordeaux, as pope. He was called Clement V and presided eight years, ten months, and eighteen days. He transferred the curia from Rome into France, not without the greatest inconvenience to Italy, and especially to the city of Rome; and there it remained continuously for seventy-four years.

To this Pope Clement, while he was staying at Poitiers, Henry, count of Luxembourg, sent Master Peter of Aspelt, his most experienced physician, to obtain the archbishopric of Mainz for his brother Baldwin, for at that time, after the death of Archbishop Gerhard, it was vacant, as we have already said. When Peter came into France to the pontiff, he set forth the embassy of his lord, the aforesaid count, and poured out many prayers to obtain the archbishopric of Mainz for the aforesaid brother of his lord. But the pope, after very many reasons had been alleged, would not listen to the petitioner, and refused to give the church of Mainz to Count Baldwin.
