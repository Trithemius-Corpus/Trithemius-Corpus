1393.

In the second year of our abbot Bernhard, after a long dispute, concord was made between Friedrich, archbishop of Cologne, and the citizens, through the mediation of the count of Moers and other friends of both parties.

In the same year, in the archbishopric of Trier, in the village called Brechen, a child of horrible disposition was born. For the lower part of its body displayed the form and arrangement of a human being, but the upper part that of a most foul toad, so that horror even seized those who looked upon it. Many attributed this to the sins of its mother.

1394.

In the third year of our abbot Bernhard, Johannes, duke of Burgundy, who was the son of the sister of Duke Albert in Straubing, came into Bavaria with a great army and proceeded further against the Turks, having with him Rupert called Pipan, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, son of Rupert, later king of the Romans, and very many other counts and nobles with a great army. King Sigismund of Hungary, making known to all Christian princes the great insolence of the Turks, asked for aid. Therefore, when a very great multitude of warriors from the whole Christian world came to the said king’s aid, an expedition was organized against the Turks. And when they had come to a certain city of the Turks called Nicopolis, they surrounded it with a siege. But the king of the Turks came to the city’s aid with two hundred thousand men. King Sigismund of Hungary, going to meet him with his men at a distance of one mile, arranged the battle-line as best he could, appointing the duke of Wallachia captain of the armies. Johannes, the aforesaid duke of Burgundy, taking this badly, usurped the command for himself against the king’s will, and, taking up the army of his own men, who were almost six thousand in number, made the first assault upon the Turks with too little prudence. He was captured by them along with many others, and an innumerable multitude of Christians was slain by the Turks. But Sigismund, king of Hungary, fled and escaped by ship, while Rupert Pipan, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, returned to his homeland in great poverty.

1395.

In the fourth year of our abbot Bernhard, Conrad of Weinsberg, archbishop of Mainz, died in the month of October. After his death, Gottfried, son of the count of Leiningen, was unanimously elected by the chapter, a good and upright man, quite sufficiently learned in secular as well as divine writings. Against him Johannes of Nassau, canon of the church of Mainz, obtained the archbishopric from Pope Boniface IX for seventy thousand ducats, as was said; and, confirmed by the same pope and ordained, with Gottfried thrust aside against the will of the chapter, he presided over Mainz rather providently for nearly twenty-four years, a strict and crafty man, whose counsel was known to few.

In the same year, within the octave of Corpus Christi, there was a great earthquake in the night. Afterward a storm following with a mighty wind overthrew trees and houses.

Also in the same year, on the eighteenth day of the month of August, Albert, duke of Austria, died; he was the first to found the University of Vienna in Austria, as we said above.

1396.

In the fifth year of Abbot Bernhard, Nicolaus of Wiesbaden, bishop of Speyer, who had succeeded Adolf of Nassau, about whom it was said above, died. After him Rabanus of Helmstatt was unanimously elected by the chapter. He presided for thirty-five years; afterward he was raised to be archbishop of Trier, as we shall say in its proper place.

Around these times, Wernher, archbishop of Trier, transferred to Engers, for better profit, the toll which Cuno, first of all, had established from Koblenz to the chapel. He also captured the count of Solms, of the church of Trier, with many nobles in war and led him away captive; he ordered him to be kept in the castle of Montabaur for so long, until he obeyed the will of the one giving the order.

1397.

In his sixth year, Abbot Bernhard leased the monastery’s estate in Flonheim at farm; although he seems to have done this for the monastery’s advantage, nevertheless it is agreed that in the end it did not turn out for the monastery’s useful provision.
