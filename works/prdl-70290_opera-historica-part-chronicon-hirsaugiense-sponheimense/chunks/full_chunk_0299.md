In these times also the emperor Charles IV, since he was a learned man, inasmuch as he had devoted himself for a long time to letters in the school of Paris, and loved learned men above others, laudably established the university of Prague for the adornment of the kingdom of Bohemia. It was distinguished by him with many privileges and donations, and in a short time grew beyond all the universities of all Germany in glory and in the number of persons, until at length, after almost fifty years, stained by many heresies, which are known to all, it was reduced to nothing. The bishop of the church of Prague also, for the honor of the kingdom, who until then had been a suffragan under the archbishop of Mainz, he raised by apostolic authority to an archbishop, and adorned him with many privileges, subjecting to him suffragans and bishops after the manner of the others. In Bohemia he founded many new churches, and churches founded both by himself and by his predecessors he magnificently honored with relics, ornaments, paintings, jewels, and various gifts. The hall of the village of Ingelheim, not far from Mainz and the Rhine, which the emperor Charles the Great once had as a castle of residence for his majesty [unclear], in which he was born, and where, according to the belief of the inhabitants, he received from heaven the sword sent by an angel when he was about to fight against the Saracens, which is shown yearly at Nuremberg, he changed into a monastery of canons of the rule of St. Augustine, decreeing that no one should be received into religion there unless he had been born in Bohemia. This has been observed up to now, and even to the present day, for no fewer than one hundred forty-four years. He subjected that monastery, however, to the abbot of the monastery of St. Charles in Prague for the time being, to whom its visitation was to belong. He also obtained from the pope, with great indulgences, graces, and remissions of sins, that the feast [unclear] every year on the Friday after the octave of Easter throughout the whole world of Christ [unclear], after a long contest among the brothers over the duchy of Guelders.

*MCCCLXI.*

In the third year of Abbot Wignand, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 1361, on a certain day in the month of February toward evening the sky seemed to burn. That night William, the first duke of Jülich, a warlike and magnanimous man, died.

In the same year Gerhard, count of Mons, and Arnold, count of Blankenheim, laboring under an inveterate hatred against one another, killed each other in the city of Cologne while jousting with sharp lances, each pierced by the other.

In the same year also, after a long contest among the brothers over the duchy of Guelders, after many fires and homicides, at last Edward, coming out victorious, captured his brother Reinald. He kept him committed to custody until the end of his life for ten years, and, having obtained the duchy of Guelders, ruled without anyone’s opposition.

*MCCCLXII.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Wignand, and the year of the Lord 1362, certain brigands coming out of England were roaming through Spain and France, by plundering, fire, and sword inflicting many losses and injuries on the inhabitants of the land in villages, especially where there was no defense. When they had grown together in number and in boldness, they set over themselves a captain, whom in common speech they called the archpriest. That multitude of robbers, however, was called the Great Company or the English Company, having more than 40,000 men going forth to battle. After roaming through Spain and France, they at last entered the territory of Trier, laying waste everything with fire and rapine. On hearing of their arrival, Boemund the archbishop sent against them Kuno of Falkenstein, his coadjutor, Wenceslaus of Luxembourg, duke of Brabant, Walram, count of Spanheim, also the count of Veldenz, and the count of Starckenburg, with many other counts, nobles, and fighters. As soon as they understood that so many princes had assembled against them, they turned their backs and withdrew from the territory of Trier. Entering Alsace, they inflicted many losses on the villagers around Strasbourg, snatching away whatever they found.

Therefore that cursed company came by night to the walls of Strasbourg, stationed itself around the city, and consumed with fire Köningshonen and all the villages situated nearby. The gates of the city, however, were kept closed, and no one was permitted to go out as long as they remained there. Whomever they found afflicted in the field or in hamlets, they butchered with unheard-of tortures, unless it was permitted to pass safely, without their safe-conduct; and whatever safe-conduct they had given to anyone, they preserved with inviolable firmness.
