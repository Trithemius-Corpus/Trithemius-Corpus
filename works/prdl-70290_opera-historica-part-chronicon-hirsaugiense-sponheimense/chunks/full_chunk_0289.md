*1353.*

In the eleventh year of Abbot Wighard, and in the year of the Lord 1353, on the eve of St. Thomas the Apostle, Henry of Virneburg, formerly archbishop of Mainz, long before deposed by Pope Clement VI, as we said above, died. Even after his deposition, as we have often said, he conducted himself as archbishop as long as Emperor Louis IV lived, although Gerlach, promoted and ordained by the pope, had the greater obedience, and very many cities, towns, and castles, in Thuringia Erfurt, and in Hesse almost all, which at first obeyed the apostolic commands. But when Henry died, Gerlach peacefully obtained the church of Mainz, and presided alone for sixteen years.

In the same year, on the day of St. Erasmus, Louis margrave of Brandenburg, Rupert, also called Adolph, the elder count palatine of the Rhine, and John burgrave of Nuremberg, elected for the public advantage, as chosen arbitrators, made a further division of the lands of Upper Bavaria among the dukes, the sons of the late Emperor Louis IV, namely Stephen, William, and Albert, as is clear in the letters. *1354.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Wighard, and in the year of the Lord 1354, Charles, king of the Romans, in the month of January held a solemn assembly of princes at Mainz; there, among many things which [unclear], he treated of [unclear], which happened in the following year.

In the same year, on the kalends of February, Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, died, a man worthy of perpetual remembrance; he amended and corrected the ordinary of the whole breviary for years, restored it to the form in which it now is, and magnificently enlarged his church with many riches and honors beyond all his predecessors. Boemund of Edendorf succeeded him.

[unclear] For that time was clouded and full of the darkness of ignorance, when regular observance perished, not only in this monastery of Hirsau, but also in all the monasteries of our order, almost throughout all Germany; and the monks, given over to the pleasures of the flesh and utterly casting away the study of letters, which had once made our forefathers glorious, thought of nothing less than writing down the deeds of their prelates. Hence there came about the shameful silence concerning the memorable acts of this distinguished monastery also, because there was no one from the time of Abbot Eberhard down to Frederick, the second of that name, for almost two hundred and seventy years, who clearly described the deeds and successions of the abbots, as others had once done. Therefore through all that time we have necessarily passed briefly.

*Concerning Wighard the Second, thirty-third abbot of this monastery, who presided five years, two months, and twenty-one days, and concerning various acts.*

When, however, lord Wighard the First, thirty-second abbot of this monastery, had died, and his poor body had been committed to ecclesiastical burial according to the custom of the order, lest the sheep should long be without a shepherd, or incur some danger to their souls, and lest the dead man’s household property, as often happens, should suffer loss, on the day appointed for electing, as the matter required, a new pastor, the brothers came together in one place in the year noted above, namely on the Sunday of the Nativity, 1354, in the seventh Roman indiction, on the morrow of St. Gregory the pope, which was Friday and the thirteenth day of the month of March.
