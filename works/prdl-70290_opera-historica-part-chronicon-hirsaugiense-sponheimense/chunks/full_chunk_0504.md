*Of Sponheim.*

In the same year, on the Ides of February, Theoderic of Moers, archbishop of Cologne, died in the forty-ninth year of his pontificate and was buried in the greater church of Cologne. After him, in the same year, on the next-to-last day of March, Rupert, son of Louis, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, called “with the Beard,” brother of the magnanimous prince Duke Frederick, guardian of Philip, was unanimously elected archbishop by the chapter; and he presided for sixteen [unclear] years amid many tribulations, as we shall say later.

In the same year also, in the month of November, the above-written agreement between Diether of Isenburg and Adolf of Nassau was made through the mediation of certain princes.

In this same year all victuals increased in abundance and were bought at a low price. Wine was cheap: for in many places one measure of wine was bought for one Hallensian obol, and three malters of rye for one florin.

*1465.*

In the fourth year of our abbot Ulrich, the eighteenth provincial chapter of our order was celebrated at Würzburg, at St. Stephen’s, on the third Sunday after the feast of the Resurrection, with continuation on the following days. There presided in it Berthold, abbot of St. Stephen there, John of St. Michael’s in Hildesheim, Melchior [unclear] of St. Ulrich in Augsburg, and Everhard of Mount St. Michael, abbots of our order; and little was reduced into statutes.

In the same year, on the sixth day before the Kalends of February, Frederick of Nackheim, formerly abbot of this monastery, died; he had resigned the abbacy, with Abbot Conrad succeeding.

In the same year, on the fifth day of the month of May, John of Aldendorf, abbot of St. Burkard’s near Würzburg, of our order, together with his reprobate monks, rashly cast off the holy order and the habit of sacred religion, in which they had been professed and educated, deceiving the pope with false narratives so that they might extort from the Apostolic See permission concerning this; and from reprobate monks they became wicked secular canons, leaving to posterity much example for apostatizing. Our order and the provincial chapter opposed this most impious translation; but, with money and the favor of many who thought against religion being interposed, it accomplished nothing. Those apostates long ago received their reward: they all perished by an evil death, leaving to posterity a manifest judgment of their damnation.

In the same year also, on the eighth day of the month of August, John Nix, bishop of Speyer, who, by waging war imprudently and without cause against Frederick, Count Palatine, brought irrecoverable damage upon his church, resigned the bishopric into the hands of the chapter; and soon, wasting away excessively within himself, he scarcely survived for the space of a year. For besides the evils enumerated above, which Frederick, Count Palatine, inflicted on the church of Speyer because of him, he also, in the first year of our abbot Ulrich, in broad daylight and with no one resisting, burned and utterly destroyed the houses and mills of the priests around the church of St. Germanus outside the walls of the city of Speyer, all of them, both of the vicars and of the canons. On the same day Matthias of Raming, not yet [unclear], chancellor of Frederick, the aforesaid lord Count Palatine, succeeded him in the bishopric by provision of the supreme pontiff Pius II, with the consent of the chapter. He presided for fourteen years, a learned, devout, and magnanimous man, who governed his church very well by virtue and wisdom, and always stoutly defended its rights, privileges, and liberties.

*1465.*

In this year, which was the fourth of our abbot Ulrich, Adolf, Duke of Guelders, seized his father Arnold by night while he was sleeping naked in bed, shut him up in a certain castle, Bobeck, and held him captive for a long time. Having done this, he compelled all the men of the duchy, after abjuring his father, to render him the oath of homage. But [unclear] punished this impious crime in him with fitting vengeance.

In the same year the people of Liège, after many lawsuits and disputes held in the Roman curia with John of Bourbon, their elected and confirmed bishop, following their inborn savagery without reason, while he had neither resigned nor been deposed, took as administrator of their church of Liège Mark, son of James, Margrave of Baden, brother of John, bishop of Trier, and of Charles. Archbishop Charles, brother of the aforesaid, led him from Cologne to Liège with two hundred horses. But not long afterward, offended by their wickedness and malice and placed near destruction, he departed, leaving everything behind, and could scarcely escape their hands.

In this same year the monastery of Liesborn, of our order, in the diocese of Münster, was reformed with the assent and cooperation of the prelate of the same, with Henry, its abbot, being received into union with the chapter of the Bursfeld observance. The monastery in Isenburg likewise was reformed in the same way and united to the chapter.
