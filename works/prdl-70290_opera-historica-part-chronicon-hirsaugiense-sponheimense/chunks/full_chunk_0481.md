This Frederick was born of military stock; having been handed over to the monastery in his youth, he acquired little literary learning, just as nearly all the others of that time, being without education, sang from custom rather than from art. He was a quiet man, unable to endure labors, and for that reason less adequate for the governance of pastoral care. In his time, as far as his own person was concerned, he neither benefited the monastery greatly, nor was he found to have harmed it greatly. He entrusted the care of temporal affairs to stewards, after the manner of those who had preceded him in the abbacy; devoting himself to his own comfort, he involved himself in few matters.

*1440.*

In the first year of our abbot Frederick, the monastery of St. James on the Fair Mountain beside the walls of the city of Mainz was reformed by monks of Bursfelde in Saxony, at the urging and with the cooperation of Theodoric, archbishop of Mainz, after the old, unreformed monks, who had refused to submit to reform, had been removed far away. As the first abbot for maintaining this most holy reform there was appointed Lubbert, a father of venerable religious life, together with other brothers sent from Bursfelde; he lived devoutly and died in holiness.

In the same year John, bishop of Wuerzburg, died. Sigismund, son of the duke of Saxony, immediately succeeded him and presided for barely three years. For before three years had elapsed, he was expelled from the bishopric by the noble lords of the chapter because of his excessive foolishness and sloth. Fleeing to his brothers in Saxony, he was, as a foolish and ruined man, placed by them in custody in a certain castle, with an annual pension intervening for his support from the church of Wuerzburg, according to the agreements, as we shall say below in its proper place.

In this same year, in the middle of Lent, the prince-electors assembled at Frankfurt for the election of a new king. After mature deliberation had first been held, they unanimously elected as King of the Romans Frederick, the third of that name, son of Ernest, duke of Austria, and nephew through his sister of the late King Albert. He ruled for fifty-three years: a mild and peaceable man, a lover of the clergy, patient under injuries, twenty-six years old when he began to reign.

*1441.*

In the second year of our abbot Frederick, on the last day of April, the eleventh provincial chapter of our order was held in the monastery of St. Giles at Nuremberg, with continuation over the following days, as is customary. There again presided Hartung of St. Peter’s at Erfurt, together with George of St. Giles’s at Nuremberg, Nicholas of St. Blaise’s in the Black Forest, and Henry of Blessed Mary ever Virgin at Amorbach, abbots of monasteries of our order. They established many things for beginning and continuing reform, but the necessary executors were lacking. In the same year the winter was hard and very severe, and great snows lay on the face of the earth from the feast of St. Martin until the feast of the Purification of Blessed Mary the Virgin, to such an extent that many poor and needy people died from cold and want.

In the same year also Louis, duke of Bavaria, surnamed the Hunchback, against his father Louis in Ingolstadt, with the help of Margrave Albert of Brandenburg, encircled the town of Neuburg on the banks of the Danube with a siege. When at last it was taken, after the citizens surrendered, he captured his father and left him as booty to the aforesaid margrave; the latter sold him to Henry, duke of Landshut, his bitterest enemy, for a great weight of gold. But divine power did not long tolerate so wicked a crime of a son against his father to go unpunished; instead, in a short time, when he least feared it, removing him from their midst without children, it presented him to its own judgment.

In this year also Theodoric, archbishop of Cologne, having gathered a great army of his men, besieged the Westphalian city of Soest; but since the citizens defended themselves bravely, he could not obtain it, and at last, after an agreement had been made, he returned to his own with loss.

*1442.*

In this year, which was the third of our abbot Frederick, in the month of June, Frederick, the elected King of the Romans, with the princes and a great retinue of nobles, descended by ship along the Rhine as far as Bonn, and from there through Aachen; there, by Theodoric, archbishop of Cologne, he was anointed and crowned King of the Romans with the customary solemnity. But he long delayed being crowned by the pope because of the schism that existed between Eugenius and Felix; at last he was crowned by Nicholas V.

In the same year wine grew in great quantity, very good and pleasant to drink, and a cartload was sold and owed for two florins; throughout the whole land there was abundance of wine. For the summer was very temperate and beautifully disposed toward every growth of provisions, with the result that abundance of wine and grain came forth. But at harvest-time the air was very clear, so that the crops, gathered without any hindrance from moisture, could have been kept for many years without corruption.
