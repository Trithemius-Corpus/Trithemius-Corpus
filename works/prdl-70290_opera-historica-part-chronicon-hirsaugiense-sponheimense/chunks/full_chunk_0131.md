For to some it seemed that it could contribute greatly to the utility and peace of the monastery if they were to have the same man as abbot whom they also had as bishop, who would not only bring upon them none of the troubles of the preceding bishops, but would also by his authority resist those who wished to bring them on. But the others, with sounder consideration, maintained the contrary, saying that to have the bishop as abbot was not a remedy of defense, but rather the beginning of the destruction of the monastery and of its goods and possession, especially because the bishop, surrounded by a multitude of soldiers, coming often by necessity to the monastery for the sake of visitation, or certainly sometimes having quarters in the estates and possessions of that same monastery, would in a short time utterly consume whatever he found laid up by the great care and labor of the brothers. This too, they said, was no small thing to fear: that he might by his power take away the estates of the monastery and confer them as benefices on his friends and stipendiaries, just as they saw had happened to very many monasteries in the region, now almost desolated.

Meanwhile Gebhard secretly caused the minds of the brothers to be solicited and entreated through the advocate Gotfrid and others whom he judged suitable for this purpose, indeed even to be adjured, that they should be mindful of the benefits they had received from him; in view of these he earnestly begged that they should not consent to another abbot being ordained in his place, but should suffer him to be over them at once as bishop and abbot, promising that he would do many good things for the monastery and for each of them. These and similar things he caused to be communicated, not to all indeed, but only to those whom he hoped would agree with him, by letters, by messengers, and through intermediaries. But because at that time there were many very prudent men among the monks of this monastery, who, when they detected Gebhard's hidden schemes, took counsel together, and, so that they might forestall his cunning by cunning, hastened the election of an abbot according to the tenor of the apostolic grant.

*Concerning Bruno, fourteenth abbot of this monastery, who presided for fourteen years, three months, twenty-two days, and concerning his deeds.*

[unclear]

Therefore, after Abbot Gebhard had been taken up to the pontificate of the church of Speyer, as we have said, and was laboring greatly so that he might retain the abbacy of this monastery together with the episcopate, the elders of the convent, wise and most devout men who knew his cunning, lest they be forestalled by him through any scheme, appointed among themselves the day of election according to the privilege granted them by Pope Urban II, namely the last day of the month of November, which was the day of Saint Andrew the Apostle. And so the brothers came together as one, and, after scrutiny had been made among them according to custom, from the votes of the individuals the greater part of them elected Bruno, a monk and venerable old man, as abbot on that same day. There were, however, some among them directing their votes to others, to whom the election of Bruno seemed not to be pleasing because, being rather weighed down by old age, he was less suited to enduring such great labors; nevertheless, without obstinacy and following the majority, being fewer in number, they consented to him without delay. Therefore by the unanimous consent of all, Bruno the elder was elected abbot of this monastery on the day before the Kalends of December, that is, on the day of Saint Andrew the Apostle.

In the year of the Lord 1105, in the thirteenth indiction, he was ordained by Richard, bishop of Ostia and legate of the apostolic see, here in the monastery, on the seventh day before the Kalends of January, that is, on the day of Saint Stephen the Protomartyr, in the presence of very many abbots, monks, and other faithful people. He presided for fourteen years, three months, and twenty-two days. This Bruno was a Swabian by nation, born of noble and most powerful lineage; after the first rudiments of letters, when he had reached manly age, he was made a canon and matricularius of the greater church of Speyer. There, when he saw many going through the precipices of vices into perdition, to whom riches, honors, and the delights of the flesh had offered the occasion, after some years had been spent in that state, and at last, by the mercy of God, having obtained compunction, despising the world with all its vanities, he betook himself to the monastery of Hirsau, and under the most holy Abbot William, converted to God with his whole mind, he assumed the habit together with the monastic purpose. Having lived in it most religiously for several years, at last he was ordained abbot. As far as the composition of his body was concerned, he was great in stature and fittingly proportioned in manly form, somewhat bald, and his head sprinkled with a certain venerable whiteness. And although he appeared fittingly large in body, yet he was not strong and laborious, but through a certain weakness was exceedingly frail and altogether unable to endure labors. Therefore whatever matters in the monastery seemed to need ordering or arranging, he did not do through his own industry, but through that of his subjects [unclear].
