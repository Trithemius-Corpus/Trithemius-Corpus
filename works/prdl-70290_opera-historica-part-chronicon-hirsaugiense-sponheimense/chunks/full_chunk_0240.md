After the celebration [unclear], he sent her after the wedding to Haguenau, in Alsace, and remained there with her for almost the whole winter together with his princes. On the feast of All Saints the emperor held an assembly of princes at Augsburg, where the king of Bohemia was present, receiving from the emperor ten thousand marks for the part of the duchy of Swabia which had devolved by hereditary right to his wife. From there the emperor returned to Haguenau and wintered there. Among other princes there were the count of Toulouse, who received the March from the emperor as a fief, after giving the oath of fidelity, as is the custom; and the count of Provence, in his fiftieth year, whom the emperor made a knight, although he resisted in part, because the counts in his family did not believe they could live long after they had attained the rank of knighthood. Nor would he even then have consented to knighthood, unless the kings of France and England, each of whom had taken his daughters as wives for their sons, had compelled him to this order of knighthood, judging it unworthy that their father-in-law should not be a knight. There were also envoys of the king of Spain, bringing very beautiful destriers and precious gifts to the emperor with greetings.

*1236.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Ernest, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1236, on the kalends of May, Saint Elizabeth was raised from her subterranean tomb at Marburg by the archbishops of Mainz and Trier and the bishop of Hildesheim, apostolic commissioners, in the presence of Emperor Frederick and many princes, bishops, abbots, counts, prelates, nobles and commoners, the number of all of whom was estimated at twelve hundred thousand. There, before all the people, countless miracles occurred at the invocation of her holy name. The emperor lifted the first stone from the sarcophagus, and reverently placed upon the sacred head of the saint the golden crown brought forth from his treasures. Her body was placed in a golden tomb, because she had been canonized by Pope Gregory IX. After this the emperor moved his army into Italy against the Lombards, who for many years past had gone out in rebellion against him.

In the same year, before the emperor’s aforesaid expedition into Italy, the Jews dwelling in the town of Fulda secretly killed certain Christian boys in a mill in order to obtain their blood. When this crime was discovered, many of them were burned. The bodies of the boys, however, were presented at Haguenau for the emperor to inspect, and there were buried with honor as if they were martyrs; and a great persecution of the Jews followed.

In the same year there grew a very great abundance of wine, especially in the regions of Alsace, so that one quartal of wine was bought for one denarius. And so the emperor, coming into Italy with a strong army of his men, in the first encounter took Vicenza, and, setting it on fire, destroyed it for the greater part. Mantua also and Padua, after long rebellion and many dangers, came into his surrender. Meanwhile, while these things were being done in Italy, the king of the Bohemians and the duke of Bavaria, with certain bishops, by the emperor’s command entered the land of the duke of Austria with a strong army, on account of the manifold excesses and grave crimes by which that same duke had been defamed.
