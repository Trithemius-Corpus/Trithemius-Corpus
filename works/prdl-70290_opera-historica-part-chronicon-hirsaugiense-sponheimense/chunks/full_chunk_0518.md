Duke Louis, accepting this appointment, though bound by oath to the Palatine, as was said above, began to act against him.

And so Prince Frederick, compelled by necessity, mustered his soldiers and, as if roaring, went out with his army against Louis. Besieging Stralburg castle and the town of Schriesheim, he took them in eight days; after taking the booty, he had sixteen of the foot-soldiers drowned, while the rest were led into captivity. After this he besieged the duke Louis’s town, called Armsheim, which was sufficiently fortified; and, vigorously assaulting it with engines, bombards, and ditches, he received it in surrender on the day of the Commemoration of St Paul. In that victory the Palatine took eighteen soldiers and forty-one horsemen captive. Having assessed the townsmen for money, and sent forty foot-soldiers to Alzey for custody, he destroyed the surrounding wall, overturned the towers and battlements, and carried off the weapons, bombards, and all the military equipment. The captive foot-soldiers, however, who had been led to Alzey, were almost all drowned, because the prince Palatine had this custom: he let no one live who, being his own man and subject to him in servile condition, had presumed to make enmity against him, unless he had an excusable cause.

Then the Palatine besieged Wachenheim castle, and took it after it had been attacked with great assaults; in it he found a large supply of military equipment. He held this little castle as a garrison for some time, but afterwards nevertheless overthrew it utterly, as is seen today. After these things he also besieged the town of Wachenheim; but when he heard that duke Louis, with the counts of Leiningen-Bitsch, was going to come through Alsace with a strong army, he raised the siege, deposited the war-engines at Deidesheim, and advanced to meet them. When they learned of his coming they withdrew, and did not dare come before his face.

Again after this the Palatine besieged the very strong castle of Ruppertseck, which, after being attacked with the greatest assaults, bombards, and ditches, he at last received in surrender. In it he found provisions, horses, engines, and military equipment in great abundance, and twelve nobles and forty-six horsemen, together with several others, all of whom he captured. Among the noble captives were Reinhard, count Hirsutus, commonly called Raugraf, and Johannes of Landeck, captains of all the rest.

In the year noted above, when Frederick, the illustrious count Palatine, was doing those things which we have mentioned against his enemies, the foot-soldiers who, at Wachenheim, held the garrisons on the side of duke Louis against the Palatine, made a sally on the vigil of the Assumption of blessed Mary ever-virgin and entered the monastery of Limburg, of our order. They violently opened the sacristy and the library, and carried off everything they found, both in the church, except the relics, and outside it throughout the whole monastery; and, after taking away the courts [unclear] and the monks’ horses, they brought the plunder to Wachenheim.

In the same year also Mahomet, emperor of the Turks, with a fleet of forty ships and an army of one hundred thousand horsemen, invaded Euboea, the famous island of the Christians, and then took it on the thirtieth day. He miserably killed many thousands of believers in Christ and utterly overthrew the cities and castles.

In the same year, on the Ides of April, Pope Paul II, amid the sacred solemnities of the masses, created Borso d’Este, count of Ferrara, duke; at that time this seemed to many not to belong to the pope, but rather to the emperor. Otherwise, if it is lawful for the pope to create dukes, it is lawful for the emperor, as formerly, to institute pontiffs and abbots. But Emperor Frederick, as scarcely anyone before him would have done, dissembled this deed, like many others, to the desolation of the empire.

In this year, after an almost four-year vacancy in the priorship of this monastery, Johannes Kolnhausen, the abbot named above, appointed as thirty-first prior Donatus, a certain monk, who did not preside long in that office, but was afterwards transferred to Weissenburg, to a certain convent of nuns; he presided for three years.

1471. In the second year of our abbot Johannes Kolnhausen, Frederick, the illustrious count Palatine, leading in person a strong army of fourteen thousand armed men against Louis, duke and count of Veldenz, again surrounded with a siege his town of Wachenheim, which he had besieged before winter. Pressing it hard with engines, arms, bombards, and ditches in a manly assault, he received it in surrender on the day of St Boniface, bishop and martyr. When the town had been taken, together with eight nobles, thirty-five horsemen, and seventy foot-soldiers, he assessed the townsmen for a fixed sum of money, overturned the walls and towers and levelled them with the ground, filled in the ditches, and carried off all the engines. Of the foot-soldiers, however, who had plundered churches and monasteries, killed the poor, or burned houses, fifty-six were drowned.
