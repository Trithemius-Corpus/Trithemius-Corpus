But if you find any of the monks whom love of the Cistercian order enkindles, you may be able to retain them there, provided that no sinister opinion about them easily arises among good and serious men. The others you shall dispose through the various places of their order in your diocese so cautiously that a diseased sheep may not stain the flock, but rather the lost one may be led back to the sheepfolds; taking more careful provision that the possessions and goods which that same monastery is known once to have had shall not in any way be excluded from the new plantation.

When therefore these letters of the supreme pontiff had been received, Sifrid, archbishop, expelled all the monks of Saint Benedict from the aforesaid monastery of Lorsch against their will, and introduced a convent with an abbot of the Cistercian order, according to the apostolic mandate. This deed greatly displeased the nobles round about, although they entirely [unclear] to withstand so great an authority. The Cistercian monks who had been introduced, as the archbishop handed over the possessions of the monastery that had been left, took possession of them, except for vassals, ministerials, castles, towns, estates, which pertained to the honor of the principality, and turned their fruits to their own use, from which they might live. Meanwhile the former monks, grieving heavily that they had been expelled and that professors of another order had been placed in their monastery, resolved that so great an injury was by no means to be endured without vengeance. Gathering therefore on a certain night, with the aid of certain of their friends, they violently entered their monastery of Lorsch with force; attacking the abbot with the lay brothers and the whole household, they threw them out, captured some and compelled them to swear off the place, wounded others by beating them, and drove them all together from the monastery. The expelled monks of the order brought their complaint to the archbishop of Mainz, declaring that they could not remain amid such dangers. Meanwhile the former monks, possessing the monastery which they had recovered, consulted among their friends about what seemed more useful to them.

But not after much time, when they heard that the archbishop was coming with force, they abandoned the place, not without booty, and withdrew to safer places; [unclear] what he wished to do, [unclear]. Those who had been expelled were, at the persuasion of the bishop, encouraged to return to the monastery of the Lorsch religion, and they continued the manner of living they had begun. And behold, not after much time, when the Cistercians established at Lorsch now believed that they were secure, the old monks, entering the monastery by night with their friends and with force, attacked them, struck them, beat them, and expelled them again, protesting that they would kill them all if they returned again to the monastery of Lorsch. Therefore from that day the Cistercians could no longer be induced to return to Lorsch to remain there.

The monastery therefore remained vacant for some time, since neither these nor those saw that they could safely stay in it. At length Sifrid the archbishop reported the matter for examination to the apostolic see, over which Pope Innocent IV then presided, from whom he received such a reply by letters concerning the ordering of the monastery of Lorsch itself, as follows:

Innocent, bishop, servant of the servants of God, to his venerable brother Sifrid, archbishop of Mainz, greeting and apostolic blessing. Since, as you have taken care to set forth to us, Gregory, pope of most happy memory, our predecessor, having understood that a monastery of the order of Saint Benedict, of the diocese of Mainz, had been diminished not a little in spiritual and temporal matters by the wickedness of those dwelling in it, had given command to you by his letters that, with the monks utterly removed from it, you should reform it from the Cistercian order, you, as a diligent executor of his commands, introduced into it a Cistercian convent. But certain sons of perdition, who, given over to a reprobate mind, persecute the sons of God the more fiercely the more they are held unlike them in their deeds, cast the aforesaid Cistercians out of the said monastery once and a second time by violence; as a result of which, in that very monastery, not without injury to Jesus Christ, given over almost wholly to desolation, the divine voice of praise does not resound in it. But because it is known to pertain to our solicitude that, in churches whose desolation the savagery of the perverse has procured, we come to their aid with swift remedy lest they perish altogether, we grant to you, by the authority of the present letters, free faculty to establish in that same monastery a convent of regulars, or even secular canons, according as your providence shall know to be more expedient for the place and the country, assigning to them the revenues of the former convent. You shall compel gainsayers by ecclesiastical censure, with appeal set aside.
