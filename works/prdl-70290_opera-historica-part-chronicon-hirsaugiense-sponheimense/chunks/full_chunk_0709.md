*Familiar Letters*

I departed from you without consolation, and whatever counsel you gave me, I always received its useful and honorable effect. Whatever had been spoken as your opinion was always and everywhere held firm and stable by the learned and likewise by the unlearned. O, who will now console me, wretched and unhappy, in my tribulations? Who will teach me in my ignorance, or who will instruct me, doubting, toward the truth? To me, and to all who fled to you for refuge, you were accustomed to be the fountain of true wisdom and the inexhaustible ark of the covenant, since you used to disclose to us, in whatever matters we could be in doubt, the clear truth confirmed by the sacred Scriptures. Finally, taught by your instruction, I confess that whatever I know in the sacred Scriptures I have acquired; and if I write in Roman style, it is not my own doing, but the service of your training. Hence I shall always take care, to the best of my ability, to imitate not only your manner of speaking, but also the character of your writing, moved no less by love of you than by the example of my fellow pupils whom you instructed: namely Jacob Trithemius your brother, Johannes Centurianus, Nicolaus Basellius the monk, Adam Pantomoranus, and others doing this same thing.

Would that I could truly imitate you not only in learning, but also in character and in the merit of life; nothing more desirable could ever happen on earth. For I have done what befitted a faithful pupil: I have fixed you as master in my heart, so that from it the memory of you may never depart, and my mouth at all times may utter Trithemian praises with favor. For the Trithemian name is imposed upon me to such a degree that it is sweeter to me than every sweetness, and more honorable than every honor. For I do not blush at the name of my most learned and most innocent teacher, but I shall magnify it and celebrate it with praises as long as breath shall govern these limbs. Let him perish who does not favor, speak well of, and wish well to Trithemius.

Sometimes, most loving teacher, your rivals say to me, whenever I bear your name to the stars: Where now is that most famous Trithemius of yours, the abbot whom the princes of the world and the pontiffs of the churches, and also all men, learned and unlearned, used to admire, to honor with letters and gifts, and greatly to venerate? What prince or pontiff now sends either messengers to him? Where now are the most learned men, who used to flow together to him at Sponheim from every region that is in Europe? Is he himself as honored and glorious before bishops and princes as he was before? To them I am accustomed to answer what is true: You are wrong, fools, you are wrong. For it was not the place that made Trithemius, but rather Trithemius himself who by his learning made the place of Sponheim distinguished. What was Sponheim before Trithemius was in it? Or who heard its name far away? Certainly it was nothing, and its name was obscure, cast down in darkness, [unclear], and unknown. My Trithemius by his learning and lucubrations made the name of Sponheim illustrious, and made it renowned throughout the whole world with immense glory, though before it had been utterly unknown. Princes, bishops, nobles, and learned men venerated him, not the place; they cared to see and address him, not the monastery.

And behold, my Trithemius has left the place which he had made distinguished; he still lives, and has been transferred elsewhere with glory. Where do princes now run? Where do they now send their messengers? To Sponheim, or to Trithemius? Certainly no learned man henceforth now comes to Sponheim; none of the princes or bishops any longer sends his men, knowing that Abbot Trithemius is not at Sponheim. Therefore it is manifest, and needs no proof, that princes venerate not the place but the man; and now, hearing that he has been transferred to Würzburg, they send their messengers to him, as they used to do before. They have no concern for the monastery of Sponheim, since, as I have said, they seek not the place but Trithemius. But I know, informed by a truthful report, that the fame of Trithemius is greater and more glorious today among princes than it ever was before. For as Truth says: A prophet is not accepted in his own country; so Trithemius attains greater glory in a foreign land than he had in his own.

O what a foul face of a monster the monastery of Sponheim is now seen to have, where, because Trithemius is absent, almost everything is perceived to have collapsed. Regular observance has fallen, integrity of morals has been corrupted, and the name of cloistered discipline remains only in the habit of the monks. I know, my teacher, that Duke Johannes already greatly grieves over your resignation, and many with him; and all would wish, except the chancellor, that you would [unclear] at Sponheim, because only now do they recognize how useful a prelate they have lost, and how faithlessly your monks dealt with you. Hence no one favors them, no one feels compassion for them, no one comes to their aid when they are in necessity, but they are despised by almost everyone, and people publicly say of them that the monks of Sponheim are worthy of burning, who did not fear to circumvent and supplant so excellent and distinguished a man with such great faithlessness. The abbot himself, your successor, companion of Judas the betrayer, an inexperienced, lazy, and foolish man, pleases none of the neighbors, is despised by the officials of princes, and is held in contempt by all.
