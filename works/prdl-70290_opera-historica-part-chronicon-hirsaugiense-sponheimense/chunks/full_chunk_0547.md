*Neuwiller likewise.*

*The kingdom of Naples [unclear] by the French.*

Following in their footsteps, the abbot and monks at Neuwiller, in the diocese of Strasbourg, with the consent of the aforesaid pontiff Alexander VI, laid aside their habit together with the order at about the same time, being made from bad monks into worse canons. Woe to the impious.

In the year also noted above, in the month of November, Charles, king of the French, entered Italy with a very great army of armed men; and, passing through Rome to the kingdom of Naples, after its king had fled, he subjected the whole of it to his rule, placing men of his own as guards in all castles, cities, and fortifications. When these things had been accomplished, he returned to Gaul. After his return, the king of Naples, who had previously fled into Spain, returned to his kingdom; and, after the French garrison had been killed or put to flight, he again recovered the whole dominion for himself.

*1496.*

In this year, which was the thirteenth of John our abbot, the twenty-ninth provincial chapter of our order was celebrated in the monastery of Seligenstadt, on the fourth Sunday after the feast of the Lord’s Resurrection. Presiding at it were Hermann of St James near Mainz, Bernhard in Northeim, Conrad of St Ulrich in Augsburg, and John of St Giles in Nuremberg, abbots of monasteries of our order. There John Trithemius delivered that very well-known oration, known to all, which is entitled *On Pastoral Care*.

In the same year, on the fourth day before the Nones of July, there were here in our monastery, to see our library, which fame had everywhere proclaimed precious: John, bishop of Worms, a man most learned in Greek and Latin; John Reuchlin, also called Capnion, doctor of laws, most eloquent in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, secretary of the duke of Württemberg; Franciscus Bononius of Trieste, poet and orator skilled in Greek and Latin, secretary of the queen of the Romans; Heinrich von Bünau, knight, learned in every faculty, secretary of the dukes of Saxony, Frederick and John; and John Vigilius, interpreter of both laws, secretary of Philip the count palatine, patron of all philosophers. All these most learned men, when they had seen so great a multitude of rare volumes in this poor monastery, were exceedingly astonished, praising the order and value of them, and the zeal and industry of Father Trithemius.

In the same year also, on the Kalends of September, died Gerard, abbot of the monastery of St John in the Rheingau, a good man and a lover of monastic discipline, who presided over his monastery well enough and usefully, especially with respect to the substance of temporal goods. He was succeeded by John of Segen, a monk of the same community, an equally good and laborious man, who built the new precinct of his monastery from the foundations in handsome stonework, with windows and vaulting, as it is seen today, aided by alms and by the support of faithful friends.

In the year noted above, the citizens of the town of Kreuznach stirred up a certain sedition among themselves, which turned to the harm of many. For the prefect of the Palatine prince, Albert Goler of Ravensburg, had put a certain citizen in prison because of his offenses; and when certain men had broken it open and led out the captive, and when an assembly had been held in the town, they poured forth words full of insult against the princes. But when the matter had been brought to the knowledge of the princes, the authors of the disturbance were severely punished: some had their fingers cut off, others were branded with iron on the forehead, and others on the cheeks.

In the same year Hermann, archbishop of Cologne, suppressed with force the rebellion of his citizens in Andernach; after certain men who seemed to be the authors of the disobedience had been captured, others punished, and others banished from the country, their contumacy thus ceased.

In the year also noted above, the chapter of the church of Paderborn, because of the old age and continual infirmities of its bishop, took the aforesaid Hermann, bishop of Cologne, as administrator of its church; he administers that same church today.

In these times a certain disease of swelling pustules, passing from the French to the Italians, and from them to the Germans, began to assail and corrupt the human race with a calamity wondrous and hitherto unheard of; most people called this disease [Job?]. It generally begins with a rising ulcer, spreading through the whole body, wretchedly tormenting the sick with continual pain, and contaminating those who come near.

In the same year Philip, duke of Austria and Burgundy, son of Maximilian, emperor of the Romans, took as wife the daughter of Ferdinand, most Christian king of the Spains.
