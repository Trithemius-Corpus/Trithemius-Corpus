He also wrote some sermons and homilies, which for the most part are mistakenly ascribed to another man of the same name, Haymo, formerly a monk and then bishop of Halberstadt, an equally learned man, of whom we made mention much earlier. And so the homilies of both Haymos are found mixed together, so that only a very keen reader can distinguish the sayings of one from those of the other from the very phrasing, and even then with difficulty.

When Gebhard had been ordained abbot, the congregation first [unclear] to the monastery. [unclear] he constructed the cloister. All the buildings necessary for the dwelling of the brothers, which the holy father Wilhelm, overtaken by death, either had not begun or, having begun, had not finished, he completed, with a certain Wigand, a citizen of Mainz, abundantly supplying him with the expenses. He was very capable in managing household affairs, and in all things more shrewd than one would have believed, since, for the sake of advantage, he often feigned one thing while keeping another stored in his mind. He had extremely acute hearing, so that he could very easily perceive by ear things that were said in a place apart.

He was very renowned for his wisdom and eloquence throughout all Germany, not only among religious and spiritual persons, but also among secular men, nobles, and princes; for whoever had once heard him either giving counsel or speaking formally was immediately led into admiration and proclaimed Hirsau blessed, which had deserved to have such a shepherd. He was frequently summoned to the public councils of princes, in all of which he always appeared admirable, since both the honeyed force of his eloquence, by which he surpassed all, displayed in him a wonderful wisdom, and his incredible wisdom made that admirable eloquence fruitful. Under his rule the temporal affairs of the monastery were enlarged in a wonderful fashion: estates were increased, possessions extended, and annual rents and revenues grew in number and value. For many noble and very wealthy men handed over both themselves and their property, for love of the kingdom, to the brothers of the monastery of Hirsau, under the governance of Abbot Gebhard.

In the same year in which this Gebhard was elected abbot, namely three and seventy days before the death of the holy father Wilhelm, in the year of Christian salvation 1091, on the tenth day before the Kalends of May, died that venerable and most holy abbot of the monastery of Brauweiler, named Wolfhelm, a man excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, whose most holy way of life profited many unto salvation. There was a great intimacy between those two most reverend men, Wilhelm of Hirsau and Wolfhelm of Brauweiler, matched by equal holiness, which remained inviolate until the death of both.

The same blessed Abbot Wolfhelm wrote little works not unworthy of reading, but few of them have come to my notice. I have read under his name a small book On the Sacrament of the Altar, full of learning, which he wrote against the errors of Master Berengar of Tours. He also composed poems on Saint Benedict and on various other saints and subjects, together with many sermons. This man was first abbot of Saint Pantaleon in Cologne; afterwards he became abbot in Gladbach, in the diocese of Cologne. From there he was transferred to Brauweiler in the same diocese, and was established there as the third abbot in order.

His life, full of virtues and signs, Conrad, a monk and his disciple, described in a truthful narrative addressed to Everard, abbot of Brauweiler, and Hermann, abbot of Saint Pantaleon. This Conrad, since he was by no means poorly learned in sacred and secular letters, and was excellently imbued in every branch of knowledge by a good and most learned teacher, also composed certain other little works, by which he transmitted the acuteness of his intellect to the knowledge of posterity.

There also flourished in the same times in this monastery of Hirsau, under Abbot Gebhard and certain of his successors, a very learned monk named Conrad, who by his admirable knowledge and learning obtained immortal fame. For he was not only studious and learned in the divine Scriptures, but also most learned in secular letters: the most celebrated philosopher, rhetorician, musician, and poet of all of his time in Germany. He composed many excellent volumes of his intellect, to be read by posterity, in a most ornate style; in them, out of humility, he hid his own name under the name Peregrinus.

Among these there excels that very famous work which, on holy virginity, under the names of Peregrinus the priest and Theodora the nun, dividing it in the manner of a dialogue into eight books, he called The Mirror of Virgins: a work indeed so useful and necessary for the virgins of Christ that it cannot be adequately praised by a mortal man.
