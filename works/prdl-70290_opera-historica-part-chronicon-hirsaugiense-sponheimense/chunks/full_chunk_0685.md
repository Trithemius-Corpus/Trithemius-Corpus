... I myself await pardon for my offenses. Believe me, brothers, as I speak the truth before Christ Jesus, the savior of all: I have always spared you, and I have never brought to light your misdeeds, which you committed against God and me, deeming it unworthy that a pastor should say anything bad, even truthfully, about his own sheep. From this, I know, your boldness increased, and the drunken poor man took up horns against us, so that you invented false things about me, while I always kept silent about true things concerning you. I therefore thought it better to leave the monastery than to accuse your wicked conduct in the chapter of the fathers, by word or in writing. For I am certain and have no doubt whatever that, if I had brought the case as it is to the knowledge of the fathers who held the annual chapter at Mainz at the beginning of September of the present year, my innocence would have been proved, and none of you would thereafter have remained in Spanheim. I willingly bore your disgrace before men, so that I might prove myself a true disciple of Jesus Christ. For I judged it better that one should yield to many than that, on account of one, many should be scattered. I do not doubt that the Lord Jesus, who is to come as judge of the living and the dead, will repay me for these insults and injuries, which I endured innocently, with a reward beyond what I would dare either ask or hope. For the rest, brothers, provide for your consciences according to the fear of God, and choose over yourselves such a pastor, not one who suits wicked morals, but one who by word and example [unclear] you. You have two leaders of factions, each of whom aspires to the abbacy: the one long since encouraged by the prophecy of a certain little fool, whom you all know, the other trusting in his own prudence. They do not seek God, but the comfort of their own flesh, which you can easily prove from the past life of each, and hereafter you will learn it not without danger. Let this warning alone suffice, although I fear I am telling a tale to the deaf. Fear the Lord God, and observe his commandments, lest, piling sin upon sin, you store up for yourselves double wrath on the day of the revelation of the just judge. For the Lord is an avenger, and he compensates for his long-suffering toward those who sin and do not practice repentance by the severity of punishments. Farewell, and at some time be rightly wise.

From Würzburg, on the last day of the month of October, in the year 1506.

*III.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg, to Johannes Brachius, prior of the monastery of St. Matthias the Apostle outside the walls of Trier, his friend: greetings and charity.*

How all mortal things hang under uncertain chance, if you did not know it before, my friend, the Trithemian history now makes plain to you. I am that Trithemius, formerly abbot of Spanheim, whom learned and unlearned alike used to regard as an oracle of Apollo; who, praised in the mouth of all, seemed to be the head and pillar of the Bursfeld congregation. Kings and princes thought me something great; and prelates of churches, drawn together by the fame of our name, wished to see me, to speak with me, and to enjoy my presence. Great and learned men were happy if I had admitted them to my intimacy [unclear]; princes too and pontiffs of churches visited [me] with their letters and gifts. You wonder who could have moved me, such as I was, held in esteem among men far and wide, so that I should leave the place which I had made distinguished by writings and vigils, and also my brothers, whom I had nourished in Christ, together with that most precious store of books, which I had gathered from every variety of writings, and transfer myself to this obscure place, known to very few. We wrote to you in the month of July of the previous year that, because of certain injuries done to us through the envy of the ancient enemy, we had fallen into disgust with our monastery of Spanheim; moved by the recollection of these things, after leaving the monastery for a time we withdrew into the March of Brandenburg. Returning from there to Neumagen, we found such dispositions among our rivals that it seemed better to us to abandon the monastery entirely than, with continual weariness pressing upon us, to live any longer with the ungrateful and treacherous. To the poor, humble abbey of St. James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, offered to me by the most reverend bishop Laurentius, I knowingly and willingly betook myself, and I resigned Spanheim, full of useless and vain occupations. For, born for studies of the Scriptures, I found a place suited to my training, in which the peace of poverty is joined with tranquility, and peace accompanies want.
