But after the brothers had been admitted into possession of the monastery, although it had not yet been completed, they began to labor most diligently so that the church might be finished as quickly as possible, hoping that they would then without doubt obtain the necessary provision of temporal things also, according to the promise of our Savior in the Gospel and of the blessed father of monks in the Rule, since before all else, in the rendering of service to almighty God, they had put forward the duty of obedience owed to him.

The brothers were twelve in number. When the holy woman Countess Wiltrudis saw their fervor and devotion, she strove with all the strength she could before her husband, lest anything should happen to be lacking for the completion of so holy a work. Hence she showed her munificence toward the brothers and her piety toward God by many benefactions; and whatever she understood to be necessary for the support of the servants of the Lord, she generously furnished, now openly, now even secretly. And although the brothers, together with the craftsmen whom the count had hired at his own expense, pressed on strongly and diligently with the work begun, nevertheless they could not bring it so quickly to the desired completion. For over nearly ten years the church was completed, together with certain other dwellings necessary for the manner of life of the monks. Indeed, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1060, as we have said before, the new church, the old one having been taken down in the preceding year, was begun with stone foundations laid; and at last in the year 1070 it was completed, and, as we shall say later, in the seventy-first year it was consecrated by Henry, bishop of Speyer. Thus for ten years labor was spent on the construction of the monastery.

But Abbot Frederick, although he applied an unwearied hand to the work, was not able to see the completion of the church, together with the other dwellings of the monastery, because after three years, having been ensnared by the false accusations of certain men, as we shall say, he was deposed from the office of his dignity, and not long afterward he died. The dedication, transfer, and liberty of this foundation and monastery, although in this place, according to the order of the narrative, we ought to set them forth before we take up writing the deeds of Abbot Frederick, nevertheless, because they occurred after his death under Abbot William, we have judged that they too should be referred to his times.

*Concerning Frederick, the eleventh abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, who presided for three years, and concerning his character and the deeds of his time.*

Thus, when the brother monks came from the monastery of the hermits to Hirsau at the request of Count Adelbert, as we have already said, twelve in number, Frederick was placed over them as the first abbot of this restoration; he was the eleventh abbot from the original foundation of the monastery of Hirsau. He was elected in the year of the Lord 1065, in the third indiction, in the sixty-third year after the abbacy had become vacant, was ordained by Einhard, bishop of Speyer, and presided for three years. Concerning his life and manner of conduct, so far as pertains to the purpose of this work, a few things must first be set down by us.

Therefore Abbot Frederick, of whom we are speaking, was born of freeborn parents, from the people of the Swabians, of an ancient family, not among the lowest according to the nobility of this world. In his youth he was set by them to learn letters, and, as far as his age allowed him, he made sufficient progress in learning over time. But afterward, when he began to have some wisdom and understanding, he turned himself to God, not without fervor of mind, admirable indeed in a boy; and in the monastery of the hermits, namely at the cell of Saint Meinrad, with the support of his parents he sought and obtained a dwelling among the monks. From then on he spent all his time, for not a few years, in monastic exercises and regular discipline, quite quietly, troublesome to no one and burdensome to no one, but walking in simplicity before your face; he thought little about the affairs of this world. For, devoted from youth to the reading of the divine Scriptures and to inward compunction, and well instructed by the solicitude and foresight of his teachers, he learned, as though led in by a certain habit, to have absolutely no dealings with the world.
