No rashness of my own impelled me to withdraw, nor, without the example of the fathers who preceded me, did I dismiss wicked monks, whose ingratitude deserves such a shepherd as I never wished to show myself to be. That Rabanus Maurus, sixth abbot of Fulda, certainly a most learned man, because of the wickedness of his monks abandoned that abbey itself after he had governed it for twenty-four years; fleeing to Louis the Pious, the emperor, he withdrew, and by him, after two years had passed, after the death of Otgar, archbishop of Mainz, he was appointed in his place. Regino also, the very illustrious and likewise most learned abbot of the monastery of Prum, who wrote many useful things, was driven out by the injury of his monks, abandoned the abbey, and fled to the archbishop of Trier. Benno, the first abbot of the monastery of Goseck, harassed with injuries by his brothers through the count, the founder, left the abbey together with the monastery and, for the love of Christ, fled to the wilderness. At length, by the consent of all, he was ordained bishop by the priests of the church of Meissen, and in testimony of his holiness he shines with frequent miracles down to the present day. The writer of the Chronicle of Pegau did not pass over in silence how badly and wickedly his monks thought of him.

Why should I speak of abbots of almost the most recent times, when the prince of monks, our holy father Benedict, left the first abbey to which he had been ordained because of the faithlessness of those subject to him? I am not better than my most holy and most learned fathers, whom almighty God willed to suffer injuries from the father of monks, so that in them he might increase the merit of holiness, or, after the manner of a wise farmer, so that he might produce richer fruit if he transplanted them to a more suitable place. For from reading the ancients we have learned that many most excellent fathers left their abbeys because of the ingratitude of monks; some indeed compelled by fear of tyrants, others by their own free will. After the example of these fathers I dismissed the obstinate monks together with the abbey; the crimes they committed against us I did not think fit, out of shame, to commit to the pen. But just as in these evils I cannot judge all my monks innocent, so neither would I say that all are culpable, although, if they had wished to have me as shepherd for longer, they would have informed otherwise the duke who had been offended by false accusations. For it is quite clear to me that Johannes Nutius, Johannes Cusensis, Johannes Damius, Johannes de Sobernheim, and Nicolaus Sculleteti were always innocent in all the conspiracies of the brothers against me, nor do I affirm that I was harmed by them in any respect. But to name those who armed their teeth against us is of little use, for you know very well who and what sort they are. Among them two heads of factions, Remigius Melantius, the prior, the most ungrateful of all men, and that younger Pingionita, whose malice is surpassed by his folly, having obtained certain supporters of their rashness, both strive with all their efforts for the abbey.

Therefore, so that their faithlessness may at last become better known, we give place and time, taught by long experience how greatly the destructive rashness of monks obstructs the progress of the mind. The abbey offered to us, that of the monastery of Saint James the Greater, Apostle, in the suburb of the city of Wurzburg, through the most reverend bishop Laurentius, by the counsel of friends, we accepted on the fifteenth day of the month of October in the present year; the former one, Spanheim, over which we presided for twenty-three years, two months, and twenty days, we left of our own free will because of the ingratitude of the monks and the persecution of your duke. Farewell now to my rivals, who persecuted a humble and innocent man; farewell to the duke with his envious [Cynonoti?]; farewell to the prior Melantius, lover of iniquity, with all his conspirators. So much, God permitting it, could the envy of rivals do against me, that forgetfulness of my benefactions suddenly arose among my beneficiaries; they returned evil to me, though I did not cease to do them good. But whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord’s. I have set God as my helper, in whom I have placed all my hope; he has transferred me from the midst of a depraved nation and established me in a place of peace and quiet. To him be praise forever. Amen.

Would that those who so greatly desired my contrition might now be armed with zeal for God and with purity, to provide for the desolate monastery such a shepherd as may be willing and able usefully to take thought for its poverty. For I feel compassion for the poor monastery; for the innocent brothers I bear the bowels of compassion, although they seem to have had little regard for me. You have the whole history of my transfer; [unclear], knowing the judge of all to have said: vengeance is mine, I will repay. For I endured all things patiently and withdrew, fleeing from the face of the persecutor; although, had I wished otherwise, neither support nor strength to repel the injuries would have been lacking. Farewell; do not cease to pray to God for me, and stand by the poor monastery of Spanheim devotedly in its need as patron, as you have been accustomed to do up to this point. Farewell again. From Wurzburg, the last day of the month of October, in the year of Christ 1506.
