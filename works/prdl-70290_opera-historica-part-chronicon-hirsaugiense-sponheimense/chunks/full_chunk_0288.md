*222. Of the Monastery of Hirsau, concerning Wighard, the thirty-second abbot of this monastery, who presided for 12 years, and concerning various deeds of that time in Germany.*

After the death, therefore, of the most reverend abbot Sigismund, the conventual brothers of this monastery of Hirsau assembled in the chapter-house, in the aforesaid year of the Lord’s nativity 1342, in the tenth Roman indiction, in order to elect a new shepherd of their souls in the place of the deceased. After the necessary deliberation had first been held, at last they unanimously elected Wighard, a monk of their convent, a Swabian by birth, a man proved in temporal affairs not without experience. Having lived in the monastery under the abbots Henry and Sigismund, he seemed, according to the customs of that time, more suitable and better than the others for the necessity of the place, although he was little fitted for the restoration of regular discipline, which at that time had fallen into ruin in almost all the monasteries of our order throughout Germany. Nevertheless he preserved the manner of living in the monastery which he found there, allowing private property to the monks, as his predecessors had done. Confirmed, however, by Gerard, bishop of the church of Speyer, and solemnly ordained abbot, as is the custom, he presided for 8 years and 3 days, as will be clear to one who calculates.

In his first year he obtained from Emperor Louis IV the confirmation of the royal privileges of his monastery.

In this same year of the Lord’s nativity 1342, while abbot Sigismund was still living, on the 23rd day of the month of January, at the first hour of the night, when a dreadful storm of clouds had arisen, thunder was heard, lightning and flashes appeared, with hail and heavy rain, so that a storm seemed to have arisen in the middle of summer.

In the same year, on the vigil of Saint James the Apostle, the river Main, suddenly rising contrary to its nature and custom, poured into the Rhine so great a force of waters as no one living at that time remembered having seen. For it caused much damage to men and overturned whole villages situated around the Rhine.

*MCCCXIIII.*

While the first year of abbot Wighard was still running, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1343, Reinald, the first duke in Guelders, of whom we have spoken above, died. His son, a boy of ten years, undertook the governance of his duchy, later taking as wife the lady Mary, daughter of John, duke of Brabant; both of them died without children. Some years afterward his brother Edward, led by desire for rule, captured him and kept him in custody for ten years, and [unclear] the duchy of Guelders; concerning his deeds and death, so far as it has become known to us, we shall speak in its proper place.

In the same year, the second of abbot Wighard, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity MCCCXLIII.

In the [unclear] year of abbot Wighard, on the [unclear] day of the month of July, Ulrich, count of Württemberg, son of count Eberhard, of whom we have already spoken above, died, leaving two sons, Ulrich and Eberhard. The first of these died after 26 years without offspring, as we shall say in its proper place. But the second, Eberhard, living for a long time, waged many wars with the cities of the empire and with nobles for almost 46 years; concerning whose deeds and death, so far as [unclear], it has not been customary.

In these times Pope Clement VI, having sent legates into Germany, caused a general passage against the unbelieving Turks to be preached, promising to those contributing in support of the pilgrims from their goods to the chests publicly appointed for this purpose in the churches not only the customary indulgences, but also certain unusual ones: so that he granted to those who had put in their goods, assessed at a fixed amount, the eating of eggs on Fridays and of milk foods, and on all other days outside Lent on which they were permitted; something which until then had not been customary in Germany.

Baldwin of Trier and Walram of Cologne, archbishops, together with certain others, then obtained from the Apostolic See, after a considerable sum of money collected from their people had been spent on the crusade, that all persons established in their dioceses might be allowed in perpetuity, outside Lent, at every time to use milk foods, butter, and eggs; and this is observed so down to the present day. For the people of Trier and Cologne use milk foods and eggs throughout the whole year, with Lent alone excepted, on all vigils of saints and ecclesiastical fasts.

But Henry, archbishop of Mainz, who clung obstinately to the excommunicated Emperor Louis, did not have the favor of the Apostolic See; and therefore he neither wished to contribute anything to the crusade nor cared for the indulgences. Hence it is not lawful, down to the present day, throughout the whole diocese of Mainz, to use eggs at the time of any ecclesiastical fast.

And so Christians, assembled from almost all Europe, were carried in a great fleet against the Turks; and first taking the town of Smyrna, thereafter they fought often, with great slaughter of both peoples.
