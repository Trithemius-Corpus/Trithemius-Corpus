*Chronicle of Trithemius concerning John the Second, ninth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for seven years and eleven months, and concerning various events of this time.*

After the death of lord Peter, the eighth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, in the aforesaid year, which was the year 1300 of the Lord’s Nativity, the brothers, left without a shepherd, came together on 3 May with the intention of appointing a shepherd over themselves. After much counsel had first been taken and mature deliberation held, they unanimously elected as abbot John, a monk and cellarer of this monastery. Not long afterward Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, confirmed him when he was presented to him, and in the same year, on the Sunday after the feast of St Urban the Pope, had him ordained at Mainz by Peter, bishop of Suden, his vicar-general in pontificals. John, elected, confirmed, and ordained as abbot, presided over this monastery, as is customary, for seven years and eleven months. He came from Sobernheim, born of honorable parents of middling fortune; when he was still a boy in his twelfth year he was handed over to the monastery by his own parents. He conducted himself fairly well before the brothers according to the customs of that time; he was appointed cellarer by Abbot Peter, and succeeded him, as we have said, in the governance. According to his capacity he ruled temporal affairs fairly well.

*1291.*

In the first year of his rule Abbot John renewed our inner church with four altars, and had it consecrated anew by Peter of Suden, as we have said, vicar-general in pontificals of Archbishop Gerard, as is clear from his letters.

*Our church was dedicated anew.*

To all who shall see these presents, Peter, by divine mercy bishop of Suden, vicar of the reverend father and lord Gerard, by the grace of God archbishop of Mainz, greeting in the Lord. Since in the year of the Lord 1291, on 8 April, we [unclear] the church of the monastery of Sponheim, of the Order of St Benedict, with four altars for divine worship to be performed perpetually. We granted to all the faithful of Christ one year, [unclear] coming to the altars, forty days, [unclear] in each of the altars, at the time of the dedication, namely on the Sunday nearest after the Nativity of blessed Mary ever Virgin, through all the following octaves, to endure forever; adding also, by special grace, to all benefactors of the aforesaid monastery, after the manner of the venerable father lord Hugh, cardinal and legate of the Apostolic See, an indulgence of forty days, likewise to endure. Given, etc.

But here it must be noted that for reasonable causes the day of the aforesaid dedication of this church, with its indulgences, after forty-six years, at the instance of Abbot Wilhelm, was transferred by Albert, bishop of [unclear], vicar of Henry, bishop of Mainz, to the Sunday nearest after the feast of St Martin.

In the same year King Rudolf of the Romans, holding an assembly of princes at Frankfurt, labored with many entreaties and promises to induce the princes to elect his son Albert, duke of Austria, as king of the Romans. The princes entirely refused to do this, saying that they already had a king, and that the kingdom could not support two, since the commonwealth seemed already sufficiently burdened by one.

*1292.*

In the second year of our Abbot John, on the octave of the Division of the Apostles, Rudolf, count of Habsburg, king of the Romans, died. He was honorably buried in the church of Speyer with his predecessors, kings and emperors. The empire was vacant for one year. In the same year Gerard, bishop of Mainz, held a synod in Aschaffenburg, with his suffragan bishops present: those of Eichstätt, Speyer, Augsburg, Würzburg, Bamberg, and Hildesheim, and many abbots, prelates, and clerics of the province of Mainz. When the council had been dissolved and each man was arranging to return to his own place, Frederick of Bolanden, bishop of Speyer, was captured on the road near the Main, at the village called Kelsterbach, by Gerard, son of Count Eberhard of Katzenelnbogen, provost of the church of Utrecht, and was taken into a fortification; but at last, after an agreement intervened, he was released.

In the same year the princes assembled at Frankfurt around the feast of Philip [and James?] for the election of a new king of the Romans. After many negotiations had first been held, Adolf, count of Nassau, was elected king of the Romans. He ruled for six years, and was deposed by the princes of the empire, as we shall say later. From these things it is concluded that King Rudolf died not in this year but in the preceding year; otherwise the empire would not have been vacant for one year, as the more important chronographers report.
