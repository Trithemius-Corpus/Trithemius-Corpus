Again afterward, in the month of December of the same year, the lion-hearted prince Palatine sent his army to the land called the Odenwald, and besieged Dorsbach and Waltbach, castles of robbers. The first of these he took on the Tuesday after the day of St. Lucy, martyr, and the other on the following day, the inhabitants of each having either escaped by flight, or been captured or killed. Thus the roads through them were made safer for travellers.

In the aforesaid year also, 1471 from the Lord’s nativity, John Kolnhausen, abbot of this monastery, obtained certain altars, namely the chapel of St. George in Dalen, that of St. Anthony here in the monastery, and others; all these, incorporated into this monastery in the reign of Frederick III, emperor, or earlier, were confirmed by Francis, cardinal deacon of the title of St. Eustace of the holy Roman Church and legate of the Apostolic See, as is clear in the letters.

In the same year also, in the month of December and through January of the following year, a dreadful and enormous comet appeared for almost eighty days, fiery in color, glowing red at the beginning of Libra. Stretching out its tail at length, and little by little turning toward the north, it turned everyone to fear and wonder.

*1482.* In the third year of John Kolnhausen, our abbot, a very grave dissension arose between Rupert, archbishop of Cologne, and the chapter of the greater church. This so stirred the bishop’s mind to fury that in various places outside Cologne he seized by force the revenues of the lords of the chapter and applied them to his own uses. This did not turn out to his good, but rather to his loss and everlasting disgrace, as in the next year and those following we shall say more fully in the proper place.

In the same year also three men were appointed and hired at a great price to deliver the town of Neuss into the hands of the aforesaid archbishop of Cologne. One of them, named Basilius, after also receiving money from the people of Neuss, handed over his accomplices, Frederick Schruff and Eberhard Bock, into their hands. As soon as they came to Neuss and disembarked from the boat, they were captured after a signal had been given by this betrayer; confessing the intent of their crime, they were beheaded and divided into four parts. From this came the pontiff’s fury against the people of Neuss; from this all the evil took its beginning.

In the same year also Frederick the Palatine sent Reynard, bishop of Worms, with certain others of his council to Cologne to attempt a settlement between the archbishop and the chapter. But they returned without effect; on the way up, not far from Rodenkirchen, together with the envoys of the aforesaid archbishop, they were captured, despoiled, and led away.

In the aforesaid year, around the feast of St. Martin, Frederick, count Palatine, sent his army to the Bohemian Forest for the destruction of a certain robber-castle, which was called Diffayve; it was taken by them with violent force, the robbers were killed, and the castle was overturned and razed to the ground. The captain was led away captive to Amberg. And thus the road through Bavaria was made safer for travellers.

*1483.* In the fourth year of John Kolnhausen, our abbot, after the monastery of Weissenburg had been brought back to observance after many wars and disturbances, Donatus, the thirty-first prior of this monastery, was sent there. After some years he was sent from there to Lobenhelt, became confessor of the nuns, and at last rested in the Lord.

And it should be noted that before and after him there were some sub-priors, whom they call presidents. For immediately from the beginning of the reform the sub-prior was John of Wesel, a monk of St. James near Mainz, indeed a good and gentle man; but, not remaining here long, he returned to his own monastery. After Donatus, John of Leiningen, the thirty-second prior of this monastery, was appointed, a monk sent here from St. James and established here; he presided in the office of prior for almost eight years, except for a short intervening time in which John of Oppenheim, a monk of St. James, was sub-prior, but he did not remain here long.
