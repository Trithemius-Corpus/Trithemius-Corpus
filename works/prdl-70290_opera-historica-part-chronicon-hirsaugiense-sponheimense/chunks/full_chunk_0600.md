A fourth cause for our rivals against us was supplied by the most atrocious war which the conspiracy of many waged in the previous year against our most mild prince, Philip, Count Palatine. What things were attempted against us by our rivals while that war lasted you know well, since during its whole duration you were always with me in the town of the monastery of Kreuznach for twenty-two weeks.

What if we add also a fifth cause of hatred against us to those already stated, one which the words of our Prior confirm, although we know for certain that it is more certainly fabricated? For he reported to us some time ago, after returning to us at Neumagen from the Cynonoti, that a certain chief secretary, provoked by us in words I know not what, was ill-disposed toward our affairs, and that for this reason he had devised everything against us in the name and by the command of the prince, who had been misled, and had entrusted to the servants of the prefect the office of robbery. We do not remember ever having spoken wrongly against him, not even this very thing of which he seems to accuse us: that he is an unlearned and shifty man, gaping too eagerly after his own advantages, as one who came to the prince as a needy scholar and very poor, and in a few years became very rich by serving.

If I had ever spoken these words, I would indeed have spoken words suited to his conduct, and therefore true, and uttered not against his prince but against the man who had done such things. The character of the Duke seems to me sufficiently clear: he is mild, quickly humane, excessively credulous. Nor do we think it difficult to recover his favor, lost through false accusations, unless a hidden cause should prevent us from being obliged to do so. Too credulous toward his own people, he has unjustly persecuted us. Therefore we shall not seek pardon from him whom we have never injured. We shall try fortune, even when it threatens heavier things, appointing as our protector him by whose nod all things stand.

We would deservedly be ashamed if we seemed to have spent freely so many sweats and labors on the study of letters through thirty continuous years, both before and after our entrance into the monastic religion, only not to be able to command our own mind, surrounded by the strongest defenses of threefold philosophy: both to despise the hatred of the unskilled and to endure with meekness and patience whatever things are adverse to us, for the desire of the heavenly kingdom. For however grave the things which the wicked envy of fortune has prepared for us, they are for that very reason especially to be despised, since, being subject to an end, they cannot continue very long.

The victory of Christ’s holy martyrs teaches us by a most illustrious example how laughable the adversities of this world are to a wise man. For the love of their redeemer they patiently endured torments lasting for a very short time; for these they now enjoy without end the eternal joy of the angels. Christ has wiped away every tear from their eyes, for whose testimony they received adversities in this life; and now there will be among them no more crying, nor fear, nor mourning, nor pain, since all these things have passed away like smoke in this age. But they are in peace, and their names live for everlasting ages.

Let us too endure, most loving brother, for the love of Christ, with patience and for the remission of our sins, those adversities which befall us, knowing that Theophilus spoke faithfully: that through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of heaven. Let us attend to our head, our creator, JESUS CHRIST, God and man, with what meekness, long-suffering, and patience he endured for us the adversities of this life, the loss of innocent reputation, mockeries, contempt, hunger, thirst, cold and heat, and immeasurable labors for our salvation; and finally a passion full of infinite bitterness, horrible torments, and death on the gibbet of the cross, his side pierced by a spear, exceedingly bitter.

And so if our prince, Lord, and master first had to suffer such things and thus enter into his glory, how shall we Christians, we monks, finally we unjust men, and by the very necessity of nature mortal and corruptible, promise ourselves, so to speak, another’s glory, without labor, without adversities and the manifold endurance of sufferings? No one will reach the glory of our prince who has not persevered in his charity by FAITH AND WORK, living Christianly unto death. But what it is to persevere in Christ by living piously, holy Christophilus did not pass over in silence, saying: All who wish to live piously in Christ will suffer persecution. A Christian cannot live without adversity, since the master himself says to us: If they have persecuted me, they will persecute you also. The servant is not greater than his lord, nor the disciple above the master, nor the Christian better than Christ.
