*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius*

The other matters with which he had disturbed the monastery he left uncorrected. Therefore, according to the aforesaid warning of the holy father Wilhelm, scarcely anything thereafter prospered for him. For after a few years, removed from the governance of the monastery and invested with the episcopal mitre at Speyer, he stirred up the hatred of everyone against himself by his foolish conduct, and not long afterwards, consumed by a miserable illness, he died, as we shall say later.

In these times a certain young nobleman seized the possessions of the monastery in the village of Almendingen, which had once been conveyed to this monastery by lawful donation by a certain Winther, a convert, under Abbot Wilhelm, invading them after the death of both as though he were the nearer heir. When one day, under pretext of that inheritance, he was carrying away from there two cartloads of wine and certain other things with reckless audacity, suddenly the foot of one of the oxen that were drawing the booty was broken. The plunderer himself also suddenly fell from the horse on which he was sitting, lost his speech, and for three whole [days?] fell into madness. Then, lifted up by the hands of others, when after some days he returned to his senses and feared the scourge of God, he had himself carried to the monastery of Hirsau; and there, changing his secular habit together with his mind, he faithfully bound himself to God. After a few years he faithfully fell asleep in holy rest.

In the same year Henry, count of Northeim, founder of the monastery of Bursfelde of our order, of whom mention was made above, died and was buried in the same monastery. In the same year Burchard, monk of Hirsau, was made abbot at Erfurt on the sixth ides of February; immediately in that same year he began to found the new church of the holy apostles Peter and Paul on the same mountain.

*1103.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Gebhard, which was the year 1103 of the Lord’s nativity, Henry IV, emperor, celebrated the Lord’s Nativity at Mainz. Entrusting the whole of his affairs to his son Henry, he promised that he would go to Jerusalem, but he did not do so. But the younger Henry was consecrated emperor at Rome by Pope Paschal.

In the thirteenth year of Abbot Gebhard, which was the year 1104 of the Lord’s nativity, Emperor Henry celebrated the Lord’s Nativity at Regensburg.

*1104.*

In this same year, in the bishopric of Speyer, blood was seen to have flowed from loaves of bread, and it was also found prodigiously in a dish of lentils. It was conjectured, according to the likeness of ancient Roman history, that this portended civil, indeed intestine, war.

This year, around the summer solstice, the weather having changed into a storm, it is said that in the district of Würzburg ice of such great size fell among the hailstones that, when divided into four parts, it seemed impossible for as many men to carry them.

*1105.*

In the fourteenth year of Gebhard, and the year 1105 of the Lord’s nativity, Henry, son of Emperor Henry IV, seized the kingdom and began to reign while his father was still living. The elder Henry wished to invade Saxony with a hostile force, because all the Saxons, as true Catholics, were well disposed toward the pontiffs of the Roman Church; but he could not, because his son detested him, as did all the faithful, since they declared that he had been excommunicated by three prelates of the Apostolic See, namely Gregory VII, Urban II, and likewise Paschal II. In that expedition in which he was attempting to enter Saxony, his son fled one night from his father’s army with a few men. When his father learned this, he was saddened and abandoned the expedition. The son, going to Swabia, was absolved from the sentence of excommunication by Gebhard, bishop of Constance, legate of the Apostolic See.

Then, aided by the counsel and help of Margrave Diepold, of Count Berengar, of Otto [unclear], of Franconia, and of the other neighboring men, of the most powerful duke and of all the princes, bishops, and abbots of Saxony, Swabia, Bavaria, and [unclear], he began to order the kingdom most prudently, and he pleased all the peoples in a wondrous way.

First, in the ninth year after Ruthard, archbishop of Mainz, had been driven by his father into Thuringia, he restored him to his own see. He also restored many other bishops of churches who had been driven out by his father on account of obedience to the Apostolic See, and deposed those who had been introduced by his father’s violence. He also announced a general council of bishops and abbots at the royal estate of Nordhausen, on the fourth kalends of June, where the discipline of ecclesiastical institution, now excessively corrupted, was to be treated. In this council, after the decrees of the fathers had first been read concerning the pressing judgments, whatever matters could be corrected praiseworthily were corrected for the present; certain matters that seemed more serious were referred to apostolic obedience.
