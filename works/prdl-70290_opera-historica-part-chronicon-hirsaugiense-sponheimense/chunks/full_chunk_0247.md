In the thirteenth year of Abbot Volpold, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1257, after the death of William, king of the Romans, the princes came together to appoint a new king, lest, if they neglected the matter too long, they should suffer some impairment of their right. But, being at variance with one another, they elected two men.

For Conrad, archbishop of Cologne, and Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, elected Richard, count of Cornwall, brother of the king of England, as king. Arnold, electing the king of Castile as king of the Romans, sent formal envoys to him, asking that he would deign to come as quickly as possible to the regions of the Rhine to take up the government of the kingdom, promising that he would provide counsel and aid to the best of his powers.

But King Alfonso, either despising or not caring for the Roman Empire, sent the envoys back with honor, after bestowing great gifts on them, promising that at a suitable time he would follow with an army of his men. Yet, content with his own kingdom, and because he was a most excellent philosopher and astronomer, he disdained to come to Germany, nor did he involve himself in any way in governing the Roman kingdom, except for the title alone, which, out of regard for those who had elected him, he retained for a time in his letters.

Moreover, the aforesaid Count Richard, summoned from England by Conrad, archbishop of Cologne, came into Germany; elected at Frankfurt, he willingly assumed the empire and was consecrated as king by the same archbishop. Then, by the favor and support of his princes, he was received as true king with great honor by the people of Cologne, Boppard, Wesel, Mainz, Frankfurt, Worms, Speyer, and many other cities, and was led around these parts with great pomp. And when, going up through all the cities of the Rhine, he made his way as far as Basel, and the money spent far too lavishly for acquiring the kingdom was now on the point of running out, all the princes left him and returned to their own places, silently indicating that up to that point they had followed the booty, not the king. He [entrusted?] the administration of the Wetterau to [unclear] of Falkenstein, whose sister he had married because of her extraordinary beauty; to Philip of Hohenfels [he entrusted?] Boppard, Wesel, and the other adjacent cities of the empire. Then, taking his wife with him, he returned to England and did not come to Germany again.

In the same year Richard, count of Cornwall, who had been elected in the preceding year by Conrad, archbishop of Cologne, was crowned at Aachen as king of the Romans, in the presence of Gerard, archbishop of Mainz, and certain other princes. After his coronation, as we have already said, he was led by them through all the cities of the empire along the Rhine, began to conduct the business of the kingdom, received the obedience of the citizens, and held assemblies of the princes, until at last he was deserted by them.
