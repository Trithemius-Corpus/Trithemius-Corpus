At the house of the most reverend archbishop of Cologne, Hermann, we rested for some days in Bonn; he relieved us of the cost of lodging at his own expense and gave us a most welcome gift. We also mentioned you more than once in conversation, as you will hear when you come, [with the highest regard for you?]. The bearer of this letter will make clear at what lodging I am staying. Farewell, best father. From Cologne, on the 10th day of the month of July, in the year of the Christians 1505.

*XVIII.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, sends greetings to Jacob de Maseck, physician, wise man, and friend.*

Yesterday evening you sent us a gift most welcome indeed, namely the books of Kyranus, written on parchment in an ancient and very beautiful script; although we had searched for them through almost all Germany, we had nowhere been able to find them complete. We give you immense thanks, and, if ever it is permitted us, we shall give you the greatest possible thanks for this kindness of yours toward us. For you have honored us more with such a gift than if you had offered us much money, because the reading of so great an author can contribute somewhat to the perfection of our understanding, whereas money contributes nothing. Money is easily consumed, but wisdom, illuminating our understanding, suffers no loss.

For what use to a philosopher professing the religion of Christ Jesus, and striving through poverty toward the riches of paradise, is the money of this world, which is the cause to human beings of all evils and crimes? For money is acquired with labor and with no small danger to the soul; once acquired, it is kept with fear; and often it is lost with the gravest pain of mind. Add that the satirist sang, not unwisely, about avaricious men, saying:

*Meanwhile, while the money-bag swells with full mouth,*
*Love of coin grows as the money itself grows,*
*And he who lacks this resource desires it less.*

And perhaps we despise it the more easily the less we have; we would have it even if we did not despise it. But why [unclear] part, but requires the whole man. Avarice, however, and the boundless love of possessing, are so contrary to wisdom that, once they have begun to possess someone in part, they do not allow him any longer to be free, but subject him wholly to their most restless tyranny. The mind in each person is one, and cannot be open to two contrary loves, as our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ testifies in the holy Gospel: [unclear]. And in another place he says: “See and beware of all avarice, because a person’s life does not consist in the abundance of the things he possesses.” And in the Gospel of Mark: “Little children, how difficult it is for those who trust in riches to enter into the kingdom of God. It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of heaven.”

Our most learned teacher Christophilus, too, wishing us to be fortified against avarice, says: “The root of all evils is desire; some, seeking it, have strayed from the faith and have pierced themselves with many sorrows. But you, O man of God, flee these things. We brought nothing into this world; there is no doubt that we cannot carry anything away from here. Therefore, having food, and garments with which we may be covered, let us be content with these. For those who wish to become rich fall into temptation and into the snare of the devil, and into many useless and harmful desires, which plunge human beings into ruin and destruction.”

For he who serves money is both bound by the fetters of present evils and made ready for future and everlasting evils. For it is difficult for one who has riches not to set his heart on them, not to desire more, not to be anxious, not [unclear] from the belief in acquiring wealth.
