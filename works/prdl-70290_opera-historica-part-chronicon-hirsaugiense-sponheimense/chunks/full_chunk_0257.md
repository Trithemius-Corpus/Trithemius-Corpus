In the same year Rudolf, king of the Romans, driven by money, committed a shameful deed for a prince: he sent his chancellor into Italy, who, after receiving money, was to make peoples free, especially those who redeemed themselves by payment. The Lucchesi paid twelve thousand gold coins on this account, but the Florentines six thousand. Having immediately been made wholly free, they created the magistracy which they call the priors of the arts, with the addition of the standard-bearer of justice. In this way most of the other cities also, with money intervening, brought themselves into liberty.

*MCCLXXXVII.*

In the fifth year of Abbot Crato, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1287, the Jews stealthily seized a certain Christian boy, born from the village of Wamraid, named Werner, and brought him with them to the town of Wesel by the Rhine; and, in order to receive his blood, on the thirteenth day before the Kalends of May, piercing him with needles and knives, after a long martyrdom they cruelly killed him. His little body, buried in the town of Bacharach, is shown incorrupt in a certain chapel up to the present day, and is venerated by the inhabitants of the place as a martyr, and is said to have been renowned for many signs.

In the year noted above Pope Honorius IV died, and the Apostolic See was vacant for ten months. He, after changing the striped mantles of the Carmelites into white ones, confirmed the Carmelite order, not sufficiently approved in the council, and the order of the hermits of Saint Augustine, which had been disapproved at Paris. He was buried at Rome in the church of Saint Peter.

*MCCLXXXVIII.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Crato, and the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1288, on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of April, brother Henry of the Order of Minors, archbishop of Mainz, died and was buried in the major church at Mainz. After him two men were again elected in dissension: Gerard of Epstein, canon of Mainz and archdeacon of Trier, whom we have said was elected before Henry against Peter, and Emeric of Schoneck, scholasticus of Mainz. But while these things were happening at Mainz, the Apostolic See, Pope Honorius having died, as we said, was vacant; for this reason their case could not be concluded either in those regions or at Rome.

Meanwhile Henry, archbishop of Trier, died, and the aforesaid Gerard was postulated as archbishop by one part of the chapter against Bohemund. At length, after Nicholas IV had been created pope, Gerard went to Rome with a double election, and was ordained archbishop of Mainz by the pope himself against Emeric of Mainz and Bohemund of Trier. He presided for sixteen years, a prudent and magnanimous man, who ruled his church well and usefully and did many things vigorously in peace and war. Bohemund, however, having been ordained archbishop of Trier, presided for eleven years; he too did many things uprightly and defended the rights of the church.
