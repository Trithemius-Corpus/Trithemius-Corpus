Hartwig was by birth a Frank; as a young man he was set to the study of letters and became sufficiently learned and educated, not only in divine but also in secular letters. When at length his years increased, he was made a canon of the greater church of Speyer, where he conducted himself so wisely and religiously that by the consent of all he was elected first chamberlain and then provost of the same church. He was a man of good conscience, of gentle heart, and of most honorable conduct; although he abounded in riches and earthly honors, yet with his mind always raised upward to God he devoted himself to good and holy studies. For though he had abundant wealth, he by no means set his heart upon it, but opened his hand to the needy and most readily relieved the necessities of the poor as far as he could. Among other works of piety, while he was still living a secular life, he was bound by great affection to the brothers of this monastery of Hirsau, and bestowed many benefits on them. He often visited them, and whenever anyone from the monastery of Hirsau happened to come to Speyer on any business, it was permitted to find lodging nowhere but in the provost’s house. His house, like a monastery, was furnished with a most honorable household, into which no dishonorable person at all had entrance. By his good and God-fearing morals he was held honorable by all; in his presence no one dared to speak or do base or dishonorable things. Being also inwardly bound in devotion to almighty God, he allowed no idle time to pass in which he did not add some good work to the due measure of his service. He was frequent in prayers and devoted to the reading of the holy Scriptures, as one who had learned by daily meditation the fruit and sweetness of spiritual exercises.

When, however, he saw that many of his fellow canons were going through the steep paths of vices, he applied the medicine of fraternal correction, both by reason of his office and by obedience to the command, and showed, by manifold witness of the Scriptures, how the ministers of the Lord ought to live. But when he perceived that his admonitions profited little among most of them, it wearied him to live in the office of prelacy, in which he saw that he was uselessly set over the rest. Therefore, after mature deliberation had first been held, he resolved for the love of God to abandon all worldly pomp and honor and to bind himself wholly to almighty God by the monastic [unclear]. To undertake this holy and arduous purpose he was especially moved by the sermons and miracles of the holy father performed at Speyer, at which he had been present, and which, as long as he lived, he was accustomed to recount to everyone with the greatest devotion.

He also brought with him to the monastery many things in gold, silver, precious vessels, and various furnishings; all these he resigned into the hands of Abbot Volmar before the whole congregation. Receiving the habit of holy religion from the hands of Abbot Volmar, he thereafter gave himself wholly to God, and was always intent, either in mind or in body, upon his service. To be present at psalmody and at the hours prescribed by the rule, both by night and by day, was always pleasant and most sweet to him; and he groaned that he had lost all the time which he had not devoted to the service of the Creator. He took care to bring his body into servitude to the spirit by abstinence and fastings, and to show himself imitable to all in good works. In all his conduct among the brothers he was peaceful, tranquil, and quiet, and so abounding in charity toward all that he deservedly was called the mother of the congregation. No one ever saw him disturbed; no one heard him murmuring; but walking before his face in true humility, he caused absolutely no one sorrow.
