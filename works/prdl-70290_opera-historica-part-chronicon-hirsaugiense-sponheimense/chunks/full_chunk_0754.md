*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

... befell the abbot of Fulda, who, although they were suitable and excellent shepherds of souls, incurred the hatred of their own monks because of justice; and those whom they ought to have revered with the highest honor they persecuted with envious plots. But those men, soon leaving behind the sheep, not to say the most ungrateful and perverse goats, transferred themselves to other places. At last, led by late repentance, they lamented that they had lost the best shepherds, whom, blinded beforehand by their depraved ways, they had not recognized.

I do not doubt that a similar thing will shortly happen to the monks left at Spanheim: despising and contemning the pillar of the whole order, now living under the barren, foolish fig tree, that is, under the stupid prior whom they set over themselves as abbot. One day they will confess, with sorrowful heart and deep sighs, that they have gravely done wrong and sinned against their father and most gentle shepherd, who was troublesome to no one contrary to equity, burdensome to no one. Then without doubt they will say, perhaps indeed they already say it, making an incomparable comparison between the condition of this monastery in which they now live and that in which they shone under your rule: “We, senseless and the most wretched of all men, why did we provoke to indignation, by our injuries and insults, so useful, distinguished, and most humane a shepherd? Why did we believe the detractors and rivals of so great a man? Behold how we now sit cast down, despised, and contemned under a foolish and inexperienced shepherd, while he is considered great before kings and princes among learned men of both ways, and is honored with various gifts.”

Let your reverend paternity believe me, since soon they will undoubtedly grieve that they sinned against your person, when there shall come upon them, by the just judgment of God, the vengeance which they have deserved, and they will bring back the fitting reward of their ingratitude.

As far as my own condition is concerned, let your paternity know that, last year, after I departed from you from Speyer with the letter of permission of my abbot of Wissenburg, I came to Fulda, and there, in the greater monastery, having now been professed and established, I promised to remain henceforth. The fathers of the place at once entrusted to me the business of the station of St. Boniface in the lands of the Thuringians and Buchonians to manage; by reason of this I trust that I shall shortly come to Hamelburg, and from there, if it can in any way be done, I shall approach your paternity. Concerning the rest, [unclear] more fully.

I have always found you most humane in all tribulations, both mine and those of my former monastery of Wissenburg, in which we were for fourteen years, when that most impious tyrant John of Drade, brother of the bishop of Merseburg, marshal of the count palatine, unworthy of the name of knighthood, raged against us, together with the satellites of Acheron who adhered to his rash undertakings. Our abbot, avoiding the cruelty of the tyrants, was contending for the affairs of the monastery at the Apostolic See in Rome; our rivals, meanwhile, exercised so many and such great cruelties against us miserable monks in our homeland that we were nowhere safe.

Meanwhile almost all the fathers of the order, especially those of the Bursfeld observance, withdrew their hands through fear of the tyrants. Since through the extreme poverty of the monastery it was necessary for us to be dispersed, scarcely anyone dared receive any of us in lodging, especially the nearer neighbors, who feared the burning of their farms more than the violation of brotherly charity. But your paternity feared God more than mortal men; although our enemies were very near to you, nevertheless, like another Tobias, you treated us most humanely, not only receiving us in hospitality, but also admitting several of us into your convent for a long time. Among them, finally admitting me kindly, you kept me for a fourth year, supplying me freely with all necessities, and, what is greatest of all, you took care to imbue me in the studies of good letters. For this I give undying thanks to your paternity, nor shall I cease to give them as long as I am in the flesh.

For you have always been an advocate and patron not only to me but also to all desolate and wretched monks; you have led many back to the way of truth, nor has anyone ever gone away without consolation who implored your counsel. Because of this, as I do not doubt at all, the merciful God, who permitted you to be troubled for a little time, afterward appointed for you, by provident dispensation, a more quiet place, suitable for the study of the Scriptures.

May your paternity pardon me for my lengthy and foolish writing. May you live long in the health of both men; to you I most humbly commend myself. Given from Fulda on the twenty-eighth day of the month of August, in the year of the Lord one thousand five hundred seven.
