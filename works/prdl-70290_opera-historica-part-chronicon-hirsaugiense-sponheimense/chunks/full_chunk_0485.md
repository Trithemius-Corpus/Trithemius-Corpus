At Nuremberg he held [unclear], in which many measures were concluded for the commonwealth, and especially concerning the expedition against the Turks; but either no effect or only a small effect followed from them. For at that time the Turk invaded the borders of the Christians with a great multitude. For on the day of St Martin, in the year noted above, a most savage battle, such as one scarcely reads of any similar one having taken place in Europe, was fought between the Christians and the Turks. In it the struggle was long contested with doubtful issue, but the Turks at last prevailed by their numbers. In this battle more than eighty thousand men were killed. There Ladislaus, king of Poland, an outstanding young man, who was also seeking the kingdom of Hungary by arms, and Julian, cardinal of St Angelo and legate of the apostolic see, perished with many praiseworthy men.

In the same year the citizens of the town of Soest in Westphalia, having rejected their lord, Theodoric, archbishop of Cologne, submitted themselves to the lord of Cleves. From this there followed among them many evils, plunderings, burnings, and wars, to the very great disadvantage of both parties. For the archbishop, having gathered a very great army from Bohemia against the men of Cleves and Soest, and having added his own men, entered Westphalia with great power, laying waste everything by fire and plunder, and utterly destroyed many castles, villages, and fortifications around Soest; and he carried off with him many persons, noble as well as non-noble.

In the same year also Frederick, king of the Romans, brought the Armagnacs from France into Alsace, more than eighty thousand of them, against the Swiss; and they did many evils in the land. In the same year a great dispute arose between the duke of Lorraine and the men of Metz, which grew to such a point that the duke, proceeding against them with a great army of his own men and of Armagnacs from France, in a short time took all the castles and fortifications of the men of Metz, and compelled them to seek peace, with great disadvantage and loss to them.

In the same year also a war was waged between Arnold, duke of Guelders, and Gerard, duke of Jülich, in which the men of Jülich, few in number, put to flight a great multitude of the men of Guelders; some were also killed. At last peace was made. 1445.

In this year, which was the sixth of Frederick our abbot, Frederick, bishop of Worms, died. After expelling the nuns of the Cistercian order from the monastery which is called the Garden of Mary, in the vernacular Kirschgarten, outside the walls of Worms, he introduced the reformed canons of Windesheim under the rule of St Augustine. Louis of Ast, doctor of both laws, provost of the major church, and secretary of Louis, the most serene prince palatine, a good man and one fearing God, succeeded him in the bishopric by election of the chapter; he presided for only forty days. For since he was a prudent man and a lover of God, he considered how dangerous the government of souls is, and therefore, having used the counsel of certain friends, on the fortieth day after his election he resigned the bishopric into the hands of the electors, preferring to remain at rest in holy humility rather than undertake, to his own peril, the care of governing souls. After him Reinhard of Sickingen, a good and peaceable man, was elected bishop of the church of Worms; he presided over his church sufficiently and energetically for almost thirty-six years.

In the same year, while the Council of Basel was still continuing, John, bishop of Lausanne, William, archdeacon of Metz, Rudolph of Rüdesheim, provost of St Paul’s at Worms, and John of Bachenstein, archdeacon of Zagreb, entrusted to Abbot John, for they were auditors of cases of the aforesaid Council of Basel, that he should correct the order of divine services and the manner for our union and annual chapter, reject apocryphal material, and cut away superfluities, as is clear from the letters drawn up concerning this, whose date is the year of the Lord 1445, the seventeenth day of the month of July.

In this year Louis, count palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria and prince-elector, the son of the late Louis the Bearded, concerning whom we have said more above, took as wife Margaret, daughter of the duke of Savoy, as we have said, by whom he begot Prince Philip. In the same year Frederick, abbot of this monastery, perceiving that because of the poverty of the place and the malice of the times he could make no progress either in spiritual or in temporal matters, and overcome by weariness, resigned the abbacy into the hands of the convent on the thirteenth day before the Kalends of March, with a yearly pension assigned to him by the convent for suitable support during life; and afterward he survived for eighteen years.
