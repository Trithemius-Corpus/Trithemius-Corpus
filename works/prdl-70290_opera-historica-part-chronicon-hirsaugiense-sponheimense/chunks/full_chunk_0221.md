*MCCXII.*

In the seventh year of Abbot Lutfrid, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1212, Otto the Emperor, around the feast of Pentecost, held an assembly of princes at Nuremberg, setting forth to the princes the cause of the dissension between himself and the pope. After this he launched an expedition into Thuringia against Hermann the Landgrave, who opposed him, where he destroyed two very strong fortifications, Rotenberg and Saltzungen. Advancing from there, he surrounded the town of Wizense with a siege, which he also took, apart from the citadel. There then for the first time began to be used the war engine which is called a Tribock.

In the same year Liège, on the day of the Lord’s Ascension, was captured by Henry, duke of Brabant, after Bishop Huard had been put to flight; and very great booty and inestimable treasures of riches were plundered by the soldiers and [unclear], to the harm of many. In this same year a very great multitude of people of both sexes and every age, from all Germany and Gaul, and also Burgundy, set out on the road toward Italy: old men and young, boys and adolescents, married women and virgins, poor and rich, without order, without a leader, without authority, led by their own presumption, mutually marking themselves with the cross, saying that they wished to cross the sea against the Saracens and recover the Holy Land. But when they came to the sea, some perished from hunger and want, others were seized by pirates and sold, and the rest returned home with great confusion, many carrying back the reward of their own corruption.

In the same year an expedition of those marked with the cross was made by the duke of Austria and certain barons of the land, and other men of diverse condition, from Austria, Saxony, Westphalia, Frisia, and all Germany, in aid of the count of Montfort, to attack the Albigensian heretics, who were also called Ruptarii or Coterelli, from the land of Saint-Gilles, at the pope’s command. When they heard that so great an army was coming against them, some abandoned their cities and castles and fled; others, having returned to the unity of the Catholic faith, promised obedience under oath to the cardinal sent for this by the pope. When these things had been done, the aforesaid duke of Austria, Leopold, with a great army of Christians, proceeded into Spain against the Saracens, sent by ship by Massamutus, king of Maroth. There, when battle had been joined with those same faithful, the Christians gained the victory, an infinite multitude of Saracens being slain and the rest drowned in the sea.

In the same year also Otto the Emperor celebrated his wedding at Nordhausen, taking as wife the daughter of King Philip, previously betrothed to him, as we said; on the fourth day after this she departed from human affairs. Meanwhile Pope Innocent, seeing the stubbornness of Otto, whom he had formerly always favored against Philip and raised on high, since Otto labored neither for absolution nor for any settlement, sent into Sicily and brought out from there the above-mentioned Frederick, a son of the former Emperor Henry, and sent him into Germany with letters and commands, instructing and commanding the princes of the empire that, Otto being excommunicated, deposed, and annulled, they should elect him emperor, receive him, uphold him before the others, and stand by him manfully in all things, and that each in his own place should honorably provide safe-conduct for him as he passed through their lands. And this was done. When Otto learned of Frederick’s arrival, abandoning his attack on the Landgrave, he set out with an army to meet him. When they had come together in the regions of Swabia, Otto with his men was compelled to give way and could not prevail, because many were leaving him on account of his obstinacy. Yet, still having confidence in the duke of Zähringen alone, he went down as far as Breisach, a royal town, wishing to await the outcome of the matter, to see whether in some way he could have the means to resist. While he was staying there in garrison, and his retainers, provoking the townsmen with robberies and injuries, were violently plundering wine, fodder, and whatever pertained to provisions, the citizens gathered as one and drove him with all his men from the town, some being wounded and mutilated in their limbs. Departing from there, he returned into Saxony, certain nobles of that land escorting him.

After this, since Frederick was being received by all as king, while the citizens of the imperial town of Hagenau still persisted in loyalty to Otto, he surrounded the city with a siege and received its surrender.
