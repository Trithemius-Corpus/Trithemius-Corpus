*432 The Chronicle of Trithemius*

*Four of the Order of Preachers burned at Bern.*

[unclear] and burned to ashes among the Bernese in Switzerland were four brothers of the Order of Preachers, namely the prior, the lector, the subprior, and the custodian, on account of certain deceitful and impious contrivances which, chiefly against the immaculate conception of the blessed ever-virgin Mary, to the scandal of many, they had devised with most wicked and damnable presumption. Since the history of this deed, together with the proceedings held against them, has been published everywhere by the art of printing, there is no need for it to be rewritten here in every detail.

In the aforesaid year, in the month of August, William, provincial count of Hesse, whom our people call landgrave, died after a wretched and prolonged illness, leaving an infant son and the government of the county in the hands of guardians.

In the same year died John, count palatine of the Rhine, duke of Bavaria, count in Sponheim, whose father, Duke Frederick, once reformed the present monastery, with the consent and cooperation of the other Frederick, then indeed the guardian of his uncle Philip, count palatine, of whose death I spoke a little before. This John was a most excellent prince, devout, a lover of peace, just, upright, and most humane; he restored peace and concord wherever he could among those at variance, and for that purpose spared neither expense nor labor. Finally, he governed his subjects, poor and rich alike, with such prudence, and protected them with such security, that he had no equal in the whole Empire. He heard the subjects who came to him with the greatest kindness, and always bestowed counsel and aid on each with great liberality. He never burdened his poor in anything, but did not allow those whom he could at times, even justly, have drained of goods or services to be burdened by anyone.

He was always a pious defender and protector of monasteries and religious persons, whom he never burdened with requests, services, or exactions. By nature he was most gentle, and therefore was loved by all. Accordingly, we and our successors rightly ought continually to pour out most devout prayers to almighty God for his soul.

He left behind him one son, called by his father’s name, John, happily succeeding his father in the principality; we trust that he will be not only equal to his father in every adornment of virtue, but we also experience, perceive, and plainly see that he is daily becoming better than the best man. May the compassion of piety long preserve him unharmed.

Also in the aforesaid year, on the sixteenth day of the month of September, Macharius, abbot of the burned monastery of Limburg, died. He is buried in the parish church of the village of Wachenheim, which he had incorporated into his abbey for the construction of the monastery; he was a good man of most honorable conduct, who spent his life amid many tribulations. Werner succeeded him in the abbacy, formerly a monk of this monastery of ours at Sponheim, very unlike his predecessor.

In the same year also, John Damius Cortensius of Cologne, a monk of this monastery, whom John Trithemius, once our abbot, almost twelve years earlier had advanced, to please John Dalberg, the most learned bishop of Worms, as confessor of the nuns in the monastery of Neuburg near Heidelberg, was driven out through the envy of certain rivals, not without the greatest injury, embittered contrary to the faith promised him, and returned to us. How true that distich of Gerard Groote is, he now experiences too late:

No favor is lost which is only the favor of sisters.
Thus it was, is, and will be: ingratitude at the end of labors.

Also in the aforesaid year, Jacob, abbot of the monastery of Gottesaue, of the diocese of Speyer, while residing in the Roman curia, rather imprudently began a lawsuit against Christopher, margrave of Baden, concerning certain escheated property; through it a grave danger was feared to be threatening his monastery. Hence John Trithemius, abbot of Saint James the Apostle, and Conrad of Saint Stephen’s at Würzburg, by commission of the provincial chapter of the order, summoned him from the Roman curia by letters to Würzburg, promising that they would devote their diligence to his peace and advantage. Acquiescing in their counsel and writings, he came to Würzburg, where, in the presence of the procurators of his convent, sent for this purpose with full mandate, on the fourteenth day of November of the year written above, the aforesaid abbot resigned his prelacy of his own free will, and received for his support an annual pension of thirty florins for the days of his life.
