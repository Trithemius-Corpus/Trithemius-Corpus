*V.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, to Master Jacob Trithemius, his brother, greetings.*

I betook myself some time ago to the city of Speyer, not for pleasure but from necessity, lest I burden anyone by my illness; preferring to be with acquaintances and friends, and especially with those on whom I formerly conferred benefits, rather than with strangers in Neuenburg, where I remained ill for fourteen days. I have been received in hospitality at the house of the Abbot of Limburg, and I have not yet sufficiently recovered from all my illnesses. We do not wish you to remain longer in Spanheim, lest our rivals, who we understand bear our absence heavily, perhaps devise some evil against you. For he fears, as we gather by conjecture, that perhaps their wicked counsels may be brought by us to the notice of very many princes; and this indeed they fear not without cause, since, with their own conscience bearing witness against them, they understand how presumptuously they have inflicted insult on us because of the prince’s clemency toward us. But what will they say to this: that, though never harmed by us in anything, they persecute us with hatred because we are well disposed toward the most serene prince of [unclear], who has always been most gracious to us? O envy, always blind, harmful to no one more than to yourself, whom you think you are persecuting. The earth is the Lord’s, and its fullness; for love of him the whole world is my fatherland, and the loss of one monastery is easy. But these things, brother, must be covered in silence between you and me, because the time has not yet come for the hidden counsel of my mind to be set forth in public. I follow a hidden divine will. After you have taken your books and clothing with you, hasten to come to us at Neamatricum, that is, Speyer, with brother Theoderic as soon as possible. We have prepared for you a place, not the last, among the household of the Abbot of Limburg, by whom you will be taken on as secretary, until the light shut off from us shines again. Once our books and goods, [unclear Greek], have been duly arranged according to the order and manner of our will, which that brother will tell you, hand over all the keys of our abbatial house into the hands of the prior. Bring with you our Greek little books of devotion, which we cannot be without for longer. But put away in the appointed place all the other things necessary for the more sacred philosophy, until our [unclear] reach their end. Farewell, my only and sweetest brother. From Speyer, on the last day of the month of April, in the year of the Christians 1505.

*VI.*

*Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim, to Roger Sicamber, most constant of friends, greetings.*

We have read the list of your lucubrations, which you sent to us through Laurentius, our courier, the day before we wrote to you, and we cannot sufficiently admire their multitude. It is plainly worthy of admiration that you, a single man, always most occupied day and night in the observance of regular discipline, have completed, in a decade, one hundred and thirty-six works, both in verse and in prose, and not commonplace ones; even their titles alone delighted us in a wonderful way. I beg you, sweetest Roger, take care that as soon as possible we may be refreshed by the reading of so many works of yours. For I know that it does not escape your prudence by how many injuries our rivals have shaken us, and by what adversities we are oppressed. Against all their assaults one consolation has been left to us; one impenetrable shield remains: the confidence of our mind in God, and zeal for sacred reading.
