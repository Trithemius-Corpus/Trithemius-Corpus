Certain priests in the town reported to me that, in the presence of many people, he said that he had such knowledge and memory of all wisdom that, if all the volumes of Plato and Aristotle, together with their whole philosophy, had utterly perished from human memory, he himself, by his own genius, like another Hebrew, would be able to restore everything with greater elegance. Later, while I was at [Neometum?], he came to Wurzburg, and, driven by the same vanity, is said to have declared in the presence of very many people that the miracles of Christ the Savior are not to be wondered at, and that he too could do everything that Christ did, as often and whenever he wished. In the last Lent of this year he also came to Stauronesus, and, boastful of himself with similar folly, promised great things, saying that in alchemy he was the most perfect of all who had ever existed, and that he knew and could do whatever people might wish. Meanwhile the scholastic office of teaching in the aforesaid town was vacant; to this he was appointed through the promotion of Franz von Sickingen, bailiff of your prince, a man very eager for mystical matters. He soon began, namely with boys, in a most unspeakable kind of [fornication?]; when this was immediately brought to light, he escaped the prepared punishment by flight. These are the things that are established for me by most certain testimony concerning that man, whose coming you await with such great desire. When he comes to you, you will find not a philosopher, but a foolish man driven by excessive rashness. Farewell, mindful of me when there is a princely opportunity. From Wurzburg, on the twentieth day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1507.

*XLIX.*

*To the most reverend father in Christ, and most illustrious prince and lord, Lord Hermann, Archbishop of the Church of Cologne, Duke of Angaria and Westphalia, administrator of the Church of Paderborn, archchancellor of the Holy Empire throughout Italy, prince elector, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James of Wurzburg, wishes happiness.*

Most reverend Prince, in the last letter that I sent to Your Reverend Paternity at the end of the month of June, I had promised that I would come around the feast of Saint Michael the Archangel; and this I would most gladly have done, had not the plague of epidemic disease meanwhile begun to rage too greatly in almost all the places on this side of the Rhine through which I was to descend, and had it not continued up to this very day. For this reason it was necessary for me to remain at home, knowing that the presence of any man who has passed through places where pestilence is strong is usually distasteful to princes. Therefore, fearing lest this very thing should happen with Your Reverend Paternity, since today this evil is raging in almost all places around the regions of the Rhine, I have decided, on necessary consideration, to defer my coming to another time, unless Your Serenity shall judge that this plan of mine should be changed. Meanwhile I shall see to it, as far as I can, that what remains to be added to the things prepared for that same Serenity of yours may be completed as abundantly as possible. May almighty God long preserve your most reverend Paternity unharmed; to whom I commend myself as humbly as possible. From Wurzburg, on the twenty-fourth day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1507.

*L.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Saint James the Apostle in the suburb of Wurzburg, to Jodocus Beifelius, most perceptive jurisconsult, greetings.*

I believe, Jodocus, that you have been informed by our common friends Rogerius, Herbenus, and Kymolanus, and by others, of the change in my affairs according to the genesis of circumstances, so that the prediction of the stars might be fulfilled, if it is to be believed to have any force, which the astronomer of King Philip prophesied twenty years ago. Often, I confess, I myself in the meantime, after making the calculation, found the figures threatening; but, persuaded by the counsel of Saint Augustine, I treated the prophecies of the stars as laughable. At last there happened to me what they seemed to promise, and an occasion has been given to me by which experience may necessarily make me more cautious hereafter. The envy of rivals furnished a place for the prophecy; and, so that the prediction of the astrologer should not falter, some malignant demon, no one doubts, stirred it up. For if God permits, demons gladly cooperate with human prophecies, by which they may catch, on the path of error, minds too credulous of vanities. The rashness of rivals, driven without doubt by the instigation of a demon, prevailed against me; and although the stars may have foretold that it would happen, nevertheless it came about by the freedom of human will.
