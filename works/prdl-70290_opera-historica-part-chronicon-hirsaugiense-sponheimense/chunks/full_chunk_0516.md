*Sponheim.*

...bearing a bitter mind toward the reformed monks, he secretly labored to harm them, or even to kill them if he could. Coming therefore to the abbot and brothers with a feigned intention, he asked to be admitted to the reform, promising that he wished to obey the commands of the elders in all things, and he hid the lurking poison under the sweet modulation of his words. Why delay? Received for probation, the wolf was joined to the sheep. But when he had learned everything for the sake of which he had come to spy it out, namely the entrance and exit to the dormitory, also the cells of the individual brothers, the arrangement of the beds, and the other things that would serve his purpose, having found an opportunity he secretly fled from the monastery. After two or three months, by night during matins, he entered the brothers’ cells, stole whatever he found pleasing to himself, and threatened that he would kill certain of them. At that time the brothers’ dormitory was on the western side of the cloister, where the dormitory of the donors now is.

Therefore on a certain night he came with a sword into the dormitory of the sleeping monks, and silently approaching the bed of Bruno, whom he pursued with greater hatred, thinking to thrust the sword into his throat, he wounded his foot or shin; for that night Bruno had turned his feet toward the place where he had previously been accustomed to put his head, I do not know whether by design or by chance. But as soon as he felt the wound of the one stabbing him, he awoke and cried out; whereupon the terrified thief fled.

Afterwards, when the harvest had already been gathered, returning one night, he set fire to one granary with its crops, burned more than forty malders of grain, and, having taken a horse from the stable, fled through the gate that leads to the vineyards. At length he came to Hornbach, and there, received for a time among the monks in the guise of a guest, he could not long hide his wickedness; but, having stolen money, jewels, and garments in the church from the little chests of the townsmen, placed there because of the immunity of the place, he was detained and put in public custody. Since there he was fed only on bread and water, he wrote to John Kolnhausen, our abbot, whose monk he was not, and begged him more urgently to rescue him from the prison of hard affliction. That good and exceedingly merciful man, although he could have left him, as one subject to him by no profession, utterly expelled by right from the monastery as incorrigible, caught in his iniquity, was moved by mercy; after paying what he had furtively taken in the church, together with the expenses incurred in prison, he freed him from there. He came, therefore, free from prison, but a slave of iniquity; he sought pardon and was received again, but he did not in the least amend his depraved morals. Finally, when with excessive rashness he had troubled everyone, he fled again, perpetrated many wicked and unjust deeds everywhere, until at last, in the third year of the aforesaid Abbot John, captured by the horsemen of Duke Frederick, he was placed here in the monastery in perpetual imprisonment. This is the twenty-ninth year of his imprisonment.

The other old monks, however, attempted nothing of this sort against the reform. The eldest of them, Peter of Blenich, came to the monastery of Sunsheim; there he died in his deformation. Otto, once abbot, having become, as we have said, confessor of the nuns on Mount St. Rupert near Bingen, rested there in the Lord, buried there in the chapel of the blessed Mary ever virgin. The other three young men returned to observance; two of them died shortly afterwards. The third, John Nutigin of Kirchberg, was confessor of the nuns for more than twenty years in Bibelis in the diocese of Strasbourg, until at last, compelled to withdraw because of the impropriety of the nuns, he was again appointed confessor of the nuns at the monastery of Schmerlenbach in the diocese of Mainz by John Trithemius, abbot of this monastery, where he still lives, an old and infirm man.

In the aforesaid year of the Lord 1470, the citizens of the old town of Weissenburg, who in the preceding year had sworn in the words of Count Frederick that they wished with all their strength to maintain the reform introduced by that prince, as we said above, in the monastery of our order there, moved by I know not what reason, changing their decision against the oath they had made, secretly admitted the old deformed monks in women’s clothing; and, after the reformed monks had been driven out with disgrace, they handed the monastery over to the old monks. They also received and fostered with hospitality enemies and foes of the Palatine prince, such as Wirich Hoffart with certain others, and they treated the prince’s prefect in Weissenburg, captured and subjected to exaction, with insults. Whereupon the magnanimous prince, moved to anger, besieged the town of Weissenburg throughout the whole harsh winter with a very strong army of mercenaries, and, after placing a garrison in the deserted provostry of the four towers, began to assault the city vigorously. Meanwhile the people of Strasbourg, together with certain cities in Alsace, were laboring for concord. Peace was concluded on condition that satisfaction be made to the prince for the insult, and the siege was lifted. But the people of Weissenburg did not remain in the agreement they had accepted; for, after sending envoys to the emperor, they accused the Palatine of persecuting imperial subjects, asking that Duke Louis, surnamed the Black, Count of Veldenz, be appointed for them as captain in the emperor’s name against his attack. The emperor did what he had been asked, [unclear] against the Palatine.
