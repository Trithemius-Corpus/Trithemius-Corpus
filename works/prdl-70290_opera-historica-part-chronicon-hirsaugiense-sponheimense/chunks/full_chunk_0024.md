Since he was threatening many injuries, the brothers, who knew the man’s falsehood, began to implore divine aid, and entrusted to Saint Aurelius the defense of the cause of his monastery against the tyrant’s wickedness. And behold, one night, when the day of the plea had been fixed for the morrow by both parties, the holy bishop Aurelius appeared to the tyrant in dreams, saying: “Impious tyrant, what have you to do with me and my brothers, whom you are planning unjustly to despoil of their goods? It will not go unpunished for you.” When these words had been said, the holy man, raising the pastoral staff which he carried in his hand, struck the tyrant in his sleep, saying, “Receive in me the first-fruits of your deserts; you will shortly receive heavier things, unless you quickly take care to make satisfaction to me and to my brothers.” But the tyrant Edwinus, awakened both by fear and by the blow, filled the whole house with so great a cry that all were turned to amazement. On the next day, lying in bed, he called to himself the brothers of Saint Aurelius and, in the presence of his wife and sons, sought pardon for his injuries. He asked them to pray to the Lord for him, promising that he would do great good to the monastery if, through their intercession, he recovered. Not many days later he recovered, and, with many gifts offered, he strove to render Saint Aurelius, the bishop of Christ, favorable to himself.

*886.*

In the first year of Reginbodo, Bertolphus, archbishop of Trier, a monk of our order, died, as we have already said above; Ratbodo, abbot of the abbey of Mettlach, a learned man and most prudent in affairs, succeeded him in the archbishopric. He acquired many gifts and possessions from the emperors for his church of Trier. He presided in the archbishopric for almost 36 years and did many good things.

*887.*

In the second year of Abbot Reginbodo, Ansbaldus, the most reverend abbot of Prum, died on the fourth day before the Ides of July, a man of the highest sanctity and religion, who happily passed to the heavenly fatherland. Farabertus, a venerable father, succeeded him in the abbacy on the eighth day before the Ides of August.

In these times Reinibodus, formerly a monk of the monastery of Corvey in Saxony, flourished as archbishop of Bremen, a man most learned in divine Scriptures and secular letters, who for many years presided gloriously by teaching publicly in the school of the monks in the aforesaid monastery. He ruled the church of Bremen for 9 years, and at his death was entered into the catalogue of the saints; his feast is observed on the day before the Nones of February. He was buried in the church of Bremen, shining with many miracles.

Almannus also flourished around these times, a monk of Alt[u]itar[um?], a man most learned on every side, who left many splendid monuments of his talent; of these we have made mention elsewhere.

Adrevaldus, formerly a monk, [unclear] bishop. The archbishop of Bremen, a man learned in knowledge of all things, who himself also displayed the liveliness of his talent by a manifold publication of books, is held famous among the monks in these same times. Remigius also, a monk of Auxerre, later presided over a certain church, as they say, as bishop, a man in divine writings [unclear] left behind. After him Harderadus, a monk, a Frank by nation, received the office of teaching, [unclear] was enrolled; a man excellently learned in the knowledge of secular letters, he illuminated these times by teaching and writing, and in the knowledge of secular [letters] [unclear]; of whose little works we have made mention in the second book of the illustrious men of our order.

*889.*

In the fourth year of Reginbodo, which was the year of the Lord 889, in the seventh indiction, Richbodo, a monk of this monastery, died. He had presided over the public school at Saint Aurelius after the master Ruthardus for almost 24 years, and left several disciples well instructed in every branch of knowledge. After him Harderadius, a monk, [unclear].

Arnulphus, son of Carloman, ruled in his place for 12 years. But Charles, dying not long afterward, was buried in the monastery of Reichenau on the day before the Ides of June.

In the same year also Lintbertus, archbishop of Mainz, died; Saint Sunderoldus, a monk of Fulda, succeeded him, a man conspicuous for every sanctity and religion, whom the Northmen killed a little later. In these times, after Emperor Charles had died, the Italians divided the kingdom: one part of them chose as king Berengarius, son of Eberhardus, who held the duchy of Friuli; the other chose Guido, son of Lambertus, duke of Spoleto.
