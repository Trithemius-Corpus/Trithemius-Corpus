This Rupert, king of the Romans, had as wife Margaret, daughter of the Burgrave of Nuremberg, who bore him six sons and three daughters: Rupert called Pipan, who fought against the Turks, as we said above; Frederick of Amberg; Louis the Bearded, who after his father was Count Palatine and Elector of the Empire; John of Neumarkt; Stephen of Zweibrucken; Otto of Mosbach; Agnes, Margaret, and Elizabeth. The firstborn, Rupert Pipan, died without children and was buried in Amberg; Frederick likewise died before his father, without children. Of the other sons, however, we shall speak in their proper place.

*1401.*

In the tenth year of Bernard our abbot, Gerard, bishop of Wurzburg, died, leaving the church weak, exhausted, and very much impoverished. For almost everything had been pledged: castles, towns, villages, together with the revenues of the church. He was succeeded by John of Egloffstein, through the promotion of King Rupert, and he presided for eleven years, a good and peaceable man, faithful to his church and in all respects sufficiently useful.

In the same year, on the feast of the Epiphany of the Lord, Rupert, elected king of the Romans, was consecrated in the greater church of Cologne by Frederick, archbishop of that city. But the citizens of Aachen, because they were bound by oath to the deposed King Wenceslaus, refused to admit the coronation of the new king in their city, having hope in the duke of Julich, who had promised them assistance against Rupert. But this did not turn out prosperously for them. For King Rupert, crowned, as we have said, in Cologne, took action against the people of Aachen and subjected them to the imperial ban, until at last, after six years, they were compelled to swear to his authority.

*1402.*

In the eleventh year of Bernard our abbot, Rupert, king of the Romans, celebrated splendid nuptials for his son Louis in Cologne in the month of May, giving him as wife the daughter of Henry, king of England, named Blanche; she bore him a son named Rupert, who died in the twentieth year of his age without children. But the wife herself also died after a few years, and then he took another, Matilda, daughter of the duke of Savoy, of whom we shall speak below in her proper place at the appropriate time.

In the same year Rupert, king of the Romans, set out for Italy, both to obtain the crown of the Empire and also to bring aid to the Florentines against the Milanese.

*1403.*

In this year, which was the twelfth of Bernard our abbot, on the feast of St. Hippolytus the martyr, a very great fire arose by chance in the town of Bingen and inflicted many losses on people; for scarcely a fourth part of the town remained unburned. The devouring flame consumed the parish church, towers, houses, and the chapels of Saints Christopher, Nicholas, Giles, and of the Holy Spirit, with everything that it seized; and so powerful was the fire that it could be restrained by no one. It laid everything waste, consumed everything, and in one hour made many of the rich extremely poor.

In the same year, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December, Rudolph, prior of this monastery, died, the twenty-fourth in the order of priors; a man according to the manners of that time, when they cared little for the discipline of regular observance. Through him, even after himself, he transmitted the monks of this community to posterity as possessors of private property, incontinent, and secular rather than religious. After Rudolph’s death, Abbot Bernard appointed as the twenty-fifth prior of this place a man named Conrad, a professed monk of this community, who presided in the office of prior for almost eighteen years; he was indeed, according to common human estimation, a man of honorable conduct, who, because he had been brought up from childhood among the deformed, trusted that living by the morals he had found would suffice him for salvation.

In the same year also, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of August, John, parish priest of our church of Sponheim, died; he had formerly been rector of the scholars here in the monastery, a singular benefactor.

In the same year the duke of Berg placed his captured father in custody in the castle that is called Nuvvenburg, and usurped his father’s dominion for himself, making little of his father’s prayers and despising his mother’s tears, poured out without ceasing. But in the same year, through Henry of Oeyr, who brought help to Archbishop Frederick, he was secretly freed from custody with great cunning and taken away in secret.
