For a certain woman in the city of Cologne, moved by the reputation for holiness of the monks of Hirsau, named Liberadis, gave to the men of Hirsau, before the citizens and the council, the house she owned in Cologne, of great price and value, to be possessed forever for the remedy of her soul. Volmar himself also, the abbot, since he was a prudent man, most experienced both in secular affairs and in spiritual actions, by acquiring many estates, goods, revenues, and rents through his own industry, won them for the use of the brethren. Hence the resources of the monastery under his rule, by the Lord's bounty, were sufficiently enlarged, and the brethren serving Christ deservedly increased in number.

For he used great industry, and looked after everything so diligently that, if you had seen his zeal for spiritual exercises, you would have believed that he had had no care at all for temporal affairs. Again, if you had considered the labor and care that he expended in procuring and arranging temporal matters for the peace of the brethren, you would altogether have thought it impossible that he could have had any solicitude for spiritual exercises. For thus, as the proverb says of Aod, he was ambidextrous, so that he could reasonably attend to one without detriment to the other.

*1122.*

When his first year was completed, which was the one thousand one hundred twenty-second year of the Lord's Nativity, Pope Calixtus II sent Lambert, bishop of Ostia, as legate into Germany. He absolved all from the general excommunication.

Cuno, bishop of Strasbourg, was deposed in the same year; another, by the name of Bruno, succeeded him. Gebhard was appointed bishop of Wurzburg.

In the same year Frederick, archbishop of Cologne, with the people of Cologne bringing aid, besieged, captured, and destroyed the emperor's castle, named Carpena.

In these times flourished Hugh, formerly abbot of Saint Germanus of our order, bishop of Auxerre, nephew of Saint Hugh, abbot of Cluny, a man distinguished in learning and character and to be celebrated among posterity in eternal memory. He greatly loved the Cistercian order, then very new, did many good things for it according to his ability in his diocese, and supplied its necessities.

*1123.*

In the second year of Abbot Volmar, which was the 1123rd year of the Lord's Nativity, the foundation of the monastery of our order at Odenheim in the diocese of Speyer, by Bruno, archbishop of Trier, and his brother Poppo, count of Bretheim, as we said above, was completed. There, after a solemn endowment and confirmation had been made by Emperor Henry V, monks were introduced, twelve in number, sent from this monastery of Hirsau at the request of the aforesaid archbishop and count. Eberhard, a monk of this Hirsau community, a man renowned in life and conduct, who was sufficiently experienced and useful both in spiritual and in temporal matters, was placed over them as first abbot. Almost three years ago this monastery, after the monks with their abbot apostatized from the order, was deformed into a secular church; how this happened we shall tell in its own time.

In this same year Bruno, bishop of Speyer, died and was buried in the monastery of Limburg before the steps of the presbytery. Concerning him Rupert, abbot of Limburg, relates wonderful visions, saying that he [unclear] incurred a grave sentence before the inner judge because he permitted him to be buried in the church, and that he scarcely escaped the danger of death through the intercessions of the saints.

In the same year, when the servant of God Norbert came to Cologne to seek relics of the saints, after he had proclaimed a fast to his followers and they were devoting themselves to prayer, one of the number of the eleven thousand virgins appeared and disclosed her name and the place where she lay. On the next day she was solemnly raised and, together with other relics of saints, was transferred by him to the monastery of Premontre. On the following day, spending the night at Saint Gereon's in Cologne, when morning came, in the middle of the church, where no sign of burial appeared, he ordered digging to be done. He found the body of that same Saint Gereon whole except for part of the head; it was raised from the earth with great joy of the people, and a part of it was given to the man of God.
