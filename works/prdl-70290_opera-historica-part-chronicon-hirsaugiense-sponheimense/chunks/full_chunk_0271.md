The aforesaid Brother Bernard, however, had many illustrious men as witnesses of his innocence, and likewise letters of John the king, son of the Emperor, and of archbishops and bishops who were present at the Emperor's death and excused him in a clear voice, namely those of Milan, Genoa, Perugia, Lucca, and Strasbourg.

*The opinion of [Genosus?] is justly refuted.*

Nevertheless Henry, count of Flanders, and the other noble courtiers, on the contrary, steadfastly affirmed that Bernard was guilty of the Emperor's death, and that he had poisoned him in the sacrament of the body of Christ. Hence a great persecution of the Order of Preachers arose, with too little justice, even if that brother had not been innocent of the charge laid against him, since the crime of one man should not have been referred to the whole order. In these times there was a great famine everywhere in the land, and a great pestilence followed, and in both dangers many people perished.

*1314.*

In the fourteenth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year 1314 of the Lord's nativity, Pope Clement V died, in the ninth year of his pontificate not yet completed; and the see of Peter was vacant for two years, three months, and seventeen days, because of discord among the brethren.

In the same year, on the day before the Ides of January, Sibodo of Lichtenberg, bishop of Speyer, died. Emicho of Liningen succeeded him and presided for fourteen years, not without dissension among his citizens, as is their custom.

In the same year also, on the fourth day before the Ides of February, Emericus, bishop of Worms, died. Henry, son of the Wildgrave of Dune, provost of the house, succeeded him and presided for five years.

In the same year also, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of November, the princes of the empire assembled at Frankfurt for the election of a new king; and when the votes were divided in different directions, Peter, archbishop of Mainz, Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, Volmar, margrave of Brandenburg, and John, king of Bohemia, elected Louis, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, as king of the Romans. Against him Henry, archbishop of Cologne, [unclear], and it lasted eight years; then at last Louis prevailed and reigned alone, captured Frederick, and forced him to surrender. His own brother Rudolf, count palatine of the Rhine, who obstinately took Frederick's side, he proscribed from his homeland and banished to England until the end of his life; there, living in great poverty, he finally died. Thus he held the Palatine county, with the other things that had belonged to Rudolf, for some time, but all [unclear]. This Louis had as wife Margaret, daughter of William, count of Holland; she bore him six sons: Louis, margrave of Brandenburg; Stephen, duke of Bavaria, dwelling in Landshut; Albert in Straubing; William, duke of Holland, afterwards count; Louis called the Roman, because he had been born at Rome; and Otto, duke, who was called the Brandenburger.

In the year noted above, three moons appeared at once in the sky. A brightness also, which they call a comet, shone in the sky for three months.

*1315.*

In the fifteenth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year 1315 of the Lord's nativity, there was a very great famine throughout all Germany, in which many thousands of people perished from want; and it lasted for two years. Afterwards an immense pestilence among people followed everywhere.

In the same year a battle took place between Louis, king of the Romans, and Frederick, duke of Austria, his adversary, near the city of Speyer, where Leopold, Frederick's brother, burned several villages round about with fire and inflicted much damage on the poor. Yet Louis came out victorious, and compelled Frederick his adversary, with his men, to flee shamefully. The famine, as we have said, was very great; but through the provision of Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, nothing necessary was lacking to the army.

In the same year also, in the city of Krems in the diocese of Passau, in the duchy of Austria, several most abominable heretics, investigated by the brothers Preachers, were found and burned. The first of the articles of their heresy was this: they said that Masses were vanity, and of no reverence or usefulness. The second article was: they said that Lucifer, with his demons, had been driven from heaven unjustly and was at last to be restored with all his own to blessedness; that Michael and all the angels were to be assigned to eternal torments; and that human beings outside their sect were likewise to be damned. Hence, when they greeted one another, they said: May he who suffered injury greet you, that is, Lucifer.
