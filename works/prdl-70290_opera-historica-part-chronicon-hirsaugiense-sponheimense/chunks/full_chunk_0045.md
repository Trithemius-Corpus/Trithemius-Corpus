*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius*

*DCCCCLXV.*

In the thirteenth year of Abbot Sigert, which was A.D. 965, the aforesaid Bruno, archbishop of Cologne of holy memory, died, and was buried in the monastery of St. Pantaleon, which he had begun the preceding year and, when at the point of death, ordered to be completed. He was buried in the twelfth year of his pontificate. He did many wondrous things in his time, and governed the duchy entrusted to him by the emperor his brother for the peace of the faithful so prudently that he did not on that account neglect the office of bishop, as many do. Wolmar succeeded him in the archbishopric for four years.

*Bruno the archbishop dies.*

*DCCCCLXVII.*

In the fifteenth year of Abbot Siger, Otto the Second, at Rome, at the request of Pope John and in the presence of his father, was consecrated emperor. In the following year William, son of the emperor and archbishop of Mainz, died; in his place Hatto, abbot of Fulda, the second of this name, was ordained archbishop. He is said, in a time of famine, to have burned a great multitude of poor beggars together in one barn, as though they themselves were bringing famine upon his region by their begging and idleness, wishing in this to take counsel both for their want and for the common advantage. Hence they say that, before the third year of his archbishopric had yet been completed, the vengeance of divine correction proceeded against him and punished him harshly enough with a wondrous kind of torment, unheard of before by many. For so great a swarm of mice, bursting forth upon him from every side, is said to have rushed upon him in a mass that, wherever he turned, he was oppressed by the deaths of beasts of this kind. If he was in a high place, or in any more elevated place, the mice climbed up the walls; but if he was shut in, they entered through the cracks and, overwhelming him with an infinite multitude, bit him, tore him, and utterly strove to devour him. So great was their importunity that the more forcefully they were driven back, the more violently they attacked; and when he saw that he was nowhere safe on land, the now pitiable pontiff surrounded himself with the protection of waters, and, carried by boat, ascended a tower near the town of Bingen, in the middle of the Rhine, formerly constructed for the defense of the country, hoping that the mice would by no means come to that place through the rushing of the wavy river. But there is no place where divine power is prevented from exercising its vengeance: swimming through the waters in infinite multitude, the mice again climbed the tower by the walls, entered, harassed the pontiff, gnawed him, assailed him, and at last, when he had been overwhelmed by their excessive multitude, miserably killed him. To this tale we neither give authority nor withdraw it, since we read it as described by the ancients down to the present day, and we know that it is told by the inhabitants of the land to their descendants as something received by hearsay from their forefathers. The tower, however, standing in the middle of the river Rhine, retaining its name from the event, is called the Mouse Tower, in which the pontiff Hatto is said to have been consumed by those little beasts.

*Otto, son of the emperor, is crowned emperor at Rome.*

*Hatto, from abbot, becomes archbishop of Mainz.*

*Hatto burned the poor in a barn.*

*The unheard-of vengeance for Hatto’s crimes.*

*The bishop is surrounded by mice in every place.*

*The bishop is consumed by mice near Bingen in the Rhine.*

*The Mouse Tower is still so called.*

In this same year Meginrad, a monk and most learned scholasticus of this monastery, died in a good old age, a man in whom the discipline of Hirsau had been established with complete perfection in every kind of learning.

*Meginrad, monk and scholasticus, died.*

*DCCCCLXVIII.*

He died on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of November, A.D. 968, in the eleventh indiction. Reinhard, a monk, succeeded him in the governance of the monastic school, a man indeed sufficiently learned, but he did not attain the measure of his teacher; nor was he moved by so great a zeal for teaching the monks what he knew that he might be hoped to leave learned disciples after him. Nevertheless, from the instruction of Meginrad the scholasticus there were very many learned monks in the present monastery, of whom one, named Eginard, was made bishop in a certain church of Saxony. Werner was appointed abbot at St. Alban’s in Mainz, and Bernolph at the monastery of St. Michael.

*Werner and Bernolph, abbots.*

*DCCCCLXX.*

In the eighteenth year of Abbot Sigert, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 970, Theodoric, bishop of Metz, cousin of Emperor Otto, founded a monastery of our order on an island near the city of the bishopric of Metz, in honor of the holy martyr and Levite Vincent, and richly endowed it with many estates and ornaments.

*The monastery of St. Vincent at Metz is built.*

At this time the Bulgarians were ruled by two sons of Simeon the monk, Peter and Baianus. Baianus was most learned in the magical art, and through it did many wondrous things. As often and whenever he wished, he changed himself into a wolf, or into the form of any other beast, or so that he was seen by no one at all; and he both could do and did other wondrous things, by which he was accustomed to bring men into great amazement.

*Baianus is renowned as most learned in the magical art.*

In these times Richard, formerly a monk of St. Germanus of Auxerre and bishop, flourished: a man of excessive simplicity and of less practical capacity than these times seemed to demand, yet very venerable for the merit of his virtue and his zeal for religion.
