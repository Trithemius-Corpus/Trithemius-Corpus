*Chronicle of Trithemius*

At the same time also the monastery of Deutz, of our order, which the people of Cologne had destroyed ten years earlier together with the parish church, was rebuilt and consecrated anew.

In this year also there was war between the duchess of Brabant, Joanna, widow of Duke Wenceslaus, and William, duke of Guelders, who emerged victorious against her; and at length an agreement was made between them through Frederick, archbishop of Cologne.

*1388.*

In the fourteenth year of [Crafto?], our abbot, Adolf of Nassau, archbishop of Mainz, died. Conrad of Weinsberg, canon of the cathedral church, succeeded him by election and presided for seven years, a good and peaceable man.

In the same year a general school, or university, was established in the city of Cologne by the council there, and at its request was confirmed by Pope Urban VI; elsewhere we read that Boniface IX, who succeeded Urban, confirmed it.

In the same year also the citizens of Mainz, Worms, and Speyer, with certain other confederates against the princes, entered the land of lord Rupert, count palatine and duke of Bavaria, with six hundred armed men, and began to lay everything waste with fire and plunder. The duke himself came against them with his army not far from Worms, and, when battle was joined, emerged victorious: two hundred of them were cut down and three hundred captured, so that scarcely one hundred of their whole army remained after escaping by flight. Among the captives were sixty wretched plunderers who, roaming about by night, had oppressed the poor with fire and robbery. The duke ordered them to be thrown into a fiercely burning limekiln, saying: “You burned up my land with hidden fire in the nighttime, and I shall make you burn in broad day, with a very great crowd of people looking on.” The rest were compelled to ransom themselves from captivity with money. These things took place on the Friday before the feast of Saint Martin in the year noted above, not far from Worms. And thereafter the confederate citizens did not dare to attack the princes’ lands in war, except those very nearby, nor did they afterward have peace until they satisfied the princes with money according to their will, and dissolved the league of their confederation among themselves.

In the same year also Cuno of Falkenstein, archbishop of Trier, burdened by old age, with the will and consent of his chapter resigned the archbishopric to Werner of Falkenstein, his kinsman. In May they unanimously received him as bishop, canonically elected and confirmed by Pope Urban VI, assigning to him all the possessions of the church, towns, castles, and villages, with no one opposing. Cuno, however, keeping with him his very great treasures in gold and silver, began to live a private life, taking up residence in the castle called Wellmich. He did not long survive there, but died in the same year, in the month of June, and was buried in the church of Saint Castor at Koblenz. Werner performed solemn obsequies for him, with Frederick, archbishop of Cologne, and Rupert, also called Adolf, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, standing by, together with many other princes. Werner, his successor, found with him an immense treasure, the church free of debts, and most abundantly supplied with every kind of provision.

In the same year the people of Frankfurt led an army against the lords of Kronenberg to fight. The men of Kronenberg, though fewer in number, hastened boldly to meet them; and when battle was joined they were victorious, killed one hundred of the Frankfurt men, and led away six hundred captives; the rest fled in disgrace. For the liberation of the captives, however, the nobles of Kronenberg received seventy thousand florins from the people of Frankfurt, and compelled them to accept conditions of peace.

In the year noted above also the confederate citizens in Swabia took up arms against the count of Württemberg. Against them those counts, supported by the aid of other princes, advanced to battle near the town of Weil in Swabia; after a long contest they became victors, killed many of the confederate citizens, and, utterly dissolving the league of cities, which had lasted almost ten years, reduced it to nothing. All these evils occurred in the land while Wenceslaus was reigning and imprudently looking the other way.

In this year, in the town of Boppard, in the diocese of Trier, an infant was born who was double from the navel upward: he had two heads, four hands, and all the other things required for the perfection of a human being in double form. But from the navel downward all the members were arranged in the form of one human being. This monstrosity did not live long, for within the space of one year both died.
