*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius*

*1363.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Wignand, which was the year of the Lord 1364, Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, having been accused before the apostolic see of being a squanderer of ecclesiastical property and of possessing nothing worthy of the pontificate, resigned the archbishopric, not by force or fear, but voluntarily.

*Adolf, archbishop of Cologne, is deposed. Engelbert becomes archbishop of Cologne.*

With the consent of Pope Urban V, provision was newly made, as was then the custom, for Engelbert of the Mark, bishop of Liege, his paternal uncle, to the church of Cologne; he presided for four years, not very usefully. Adolf, after resigning the pontificate, not without loss to the church of Cologne, married a certain girl, the sister of the count of Mark, through love of whom he hoped to obtain the county of Cleves; although he held the bishopric of Munster for five years, and, relying on the presumed confidence of [unclear], he at length obtained with his wife the county he had desired, so that both counties, namely Cleves and Mark, have remained joined even to the present day, although Cleves was afterwards, as we shall say in its place, gloriously raised to a duchy.

*1365.*

In the seventh year of Abbot Wignand, and the year of the Lord’s nativity 1365, Emperor Charles IV again entered Italy; and, after finding Pope Urban in Rome and holding with him a discussion of certain secrets, he returned safe to Germany with his nobles.

*The emperor returns from Italy [unclear]. Severe winter, famine, and pestilence.*

There was famine everywhere and a great pestilence in Germany, and many people died, some from cold, others from hunger. John of Rupescissa, led astray by I know not what spirit, unless his own, foretold many things soon to come concerning Antichrist, the general trampling-down of the clergy, and the reduction of the whole world to the faith [unclear].

*John of Rupescissa, of the order of Saint Francis, on Antichrist.*

He wrote many and various things about these matters to a certain cardinal, which are extant; but whether they have held true as he predicted, let those who have read his books, learned judges of histories, decide.

In these times a great and very firm confederation was made among the imperial and free cities, which even to the most powerful princes seemed formidable at its beginning.

*The Worms contention against John [unclear], bishop. Great confederation of cities.*

Among the people of Worms there was great disagreement over the establishment of the council and the office of Schultheiss, and over certain other matters belonging to jurisdiction; this was at last brought back to peace by Rupert, count palatine of the Rhine, who was also Adolf, and by the councils of Mainz and Speyer, on the day of the Conversion of Saint Paul in the aforesaid year. We often read that such confederations, which they commonly called leagues, “Bund,” were sworn and confirmed not only among cities but also among princes, and likewise we see it every day; but human instability preserves nothing inviolate for long. Hence the fitting proverb is:

The fickle crowd is always moved along with its prince.

*1366.*

In the eighth year of Abbot Wignand, but the year of the Lord’s nativity 1356, between the bishop and the citizens [unclear].

*Bohemond, archbishop, dies.*

Cuno, archbishop of Trier, was buried in the greater church of Trier. After him Cuno himself of Falkenstein, lord of Minzenberg, born of a mother who was the daughter of the count of Saarwerden, while Bohemond was still alive, as we have said, was ordained archbishop and presided very usefully for twenty-one years.

*Boppard and Wesel are pledged.*

This Archbishop Cuno, for the services rendered in many ways to Emperor Charles IV, obtained Boppard and Wesel, with half of Sterrenberg, which Baldwin, the former archbishop, had obtained in pledge from his brother Emperor Henry on account of the expenses of the empire incurred in Italy, by a more artful pledging, for the sum of [unclear] of eternal goodness, indeed a very great one, so that the amount of the later pledge was greater than that of the earlier. For Cuno was a very prudent man, whom nature had made for the rule even of many churches, nor was there readily found anyone like him.
