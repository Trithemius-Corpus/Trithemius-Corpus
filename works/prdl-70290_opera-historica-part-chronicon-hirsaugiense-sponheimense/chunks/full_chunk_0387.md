*Sponheimense.*

The Apostolic See was vacant for ten months, so that the matter of this election could not easily be settled either in those parts or at Rome. Meanwhile Henry, archbishop of Trier, died, and the aforesaid Gerard, against Bohemond, was also postulated as archbishop by one party of the canons of Trier. At length, when he had gone to Rome with a double election, he was ordained archbishop of Mainz by Pope Nicholas IV, and Bohemond, succeeding Henry, presided as archbishop of Trier for sixteen years: a magnanimous and most prudent man, since he vigorously accomplished many things for the advantage and benefit of his church.

In the same year the duke of Limburg died, and [there was a dispute with?] Reinald, count of Guelders, over that same duchy of Limburg, which each of them labored exceedingly to claim for himself by hereditary right, as the nearer legitimate heir. At last it came to war, and the duke of Brabant had with him his brother Godfrey, Adolf count of Berg, Henry count of Windeck, Walram count of Julich, his brother Gerard, Eberhard count of the Mark with his brother, also the bishop of Liege, the count of Looz, the citizens of Cologne, and many other noble and very brave men. With Reinald, count of Guelders, were Siegfried archbishop of Cologne, Henry count of Luxembourg and his brother Walram, John lord in Limburg, Henry count of Westerburg, likewise the count of Falkenburg, and several others. When battle was joined, the duke of Brabant obtained the victory, captured the count of Guelders, and secured many castles and lordships for his release. Siegfried archbishop of Cologne was also captured, in the custody of the count of Berg. Since he did not wish to redeem himself with money, he was detained for seven years, until at last, compelled by necessity, he redeemed himself from captivity at a great price.

*1289.*

In the twenty-fifth year of our abbot Peter, King Rudolf again moved an army into Burgundy. First he captured the city of Payerne, enclosed by a siege. Then he powerfully subjected to his power the count of Burgundy himself, also the count of Montbeliard and the count of Savoy, and made them obedient to him in all things.

In the same year there died Emicho count of Leiningen, Diether son of Eberhard count of Katzenelnbogen, Walther count of Geroldseck, Gerlach the elder lord of Limburg, the count of Uechingen, and the count of Reineck by the Black Forest; at one and the same time they all perished miserably together.

In the same year also Siegfried archbishop of Cologne, as we have said, was held in the chains of Adolf count of Berg, and for seven years was treated quite miserably.

*1290.*

In this year Peter, the eighth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, after he had presided for twenty-five years and four months, died on the sixth day before the Nones of May, at an advanced age, being more than sixty-eight years old, and was buried in the cloister of the monastery before the entrance to the church. He was a very active man, but less concerned with inward spiritual matters. He was the first to allow the monks of this monastery portions of revenues, although later he wished to revoke this, but did not succeed. When he was about to die, however, after making a most devout confession, he said that he feared the future judgment of God for nothing more than that, by his agency, the greatest occasion for private property seemed to have been left to the monks of this monastery. Nevertheless, as much as lay in him, even when already placed at the point of death, he revoked with constant perseverance all that had been done concerning the division of revenues, contrary to the Rule of our Holy Father Benedict; but he accomplished nothing. For the monks, having once obtained permission for private possessions, so to speak, paid no heed to the words of a dying man.

In these times a great dissension arose for a slight cause between John count of Sponheim and Henry count of Veldenz.

For the count of Sponheim had caused a certain man from the county of Veldenz to be hanged, who had stolen a considerable sum of money in the town of Kreuznach and, carrying it away secretly, had hidden it in a small village of the aforesaid count of Veldenz. When the money was disclosed by torture, the count of Sponheim wished it to be returned to the man from whom it had been taken; on the contrary, the count of Veldenz asserted that it belonged to his jurisdiction and was in no way to be returned to anyone, because it had been found in his domain. And so, when dissension arose, they were close to attacking each other in war, had not Gerard archbishop of Mainz intervened and restored peace, adjudging that the money ought to be restored to the injured party.
