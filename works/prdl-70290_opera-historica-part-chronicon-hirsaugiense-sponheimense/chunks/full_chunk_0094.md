*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

By these letters and counsels of blessed Anselm, the holy abbot William, strengthened in the Lord and wholly kindled with divine love, and no less stirred by zeal for souls, throughout all that time during which the wretched schism against the Church of God endured, while the impious King Henry raged against the vicar of Christ, encouraged with salutary counsel, with all his strength, all whom he could to preserve and protect the unity of the Church. And because with a pure and simple eye he pressed on in so holy and so necessary a matter, whatever he did was pleasing to the minds of all religious; whatever he said soothed the hearts of all good men with the inward affection of love. Together with dove-like simplicity, serpentine prudence always flourished in him; and because in all things he sought not his own glory but God’s, God gave him his grace, so that he acted prosperously in all matters, such that he seemed to surpass all the prudent men of this world in prudence, and was honored without envy by those who professed divine wisdom.

In these times there also flourished in England Gilbert, abbot of our monastery which is called Westminster, a disciple of blessed Anselm, a man excellently learned both in divine Scriptures and in secular letters. Among the other monuments of his talent he wrote, against the Jews, a fine disputation in no inelegant style.

In these times too there flourished Diemo, an abbot of our order in the city of Salzburg, who, because of the excommunication of King Henry IV, was expelled from his monastery, since, siding with the holy Roman Church, he refused to communicate with the excommunicated. Therefore, drawn by the fame of Saint Abbot William to the institution of the monasteries, he came to Hirsau; and, living humbly among the brothers, when he had seen the laudable ordering of their life, he resolved to remain with them in the Lord forever. And so, made a monk anew, he lived religiously under William, giving thanks to God that he had deserved to be enrolled in so holy a congregation. Then, after some years, he was raised to be archbishop of the church of Salzburg, and, compelled by obedience, was unwillingly forced to withdraw from the monastery. He presided over the aforesaid church vigorously in word and example, offering many inducements to virtues. At length, inflamed by divine love, he resolved to go with many others to the Holy Land and to visit the Lord’s sepulchre; and on the way, captured by pagans, he was crowned with martyrdom along with most of the other companions of his pilgrimage. His deeds are extant.

Around these times Rupert, formerly abbot of Reichenau, was deposed by Pope Gregory VII, the mandate being carried out by Saint Hugh, abbot of Cluny, and every exercise of ecclesiastical order was forbidden him except psalmody, because, though summoned to Rome a third time to clear himself of the charges brought against him, he refused to go. Later, despising the prohibition of the supreme pontiff, he received from King Henry the monastery at Gengenbach, where, while according to his custom he devoted himself too immoderately to temporal gains, he was killed with another monk of the aforesaid monastery, of by no means hopeless character, against whom, after advancing upon the possessions of the monastery and his own right, he himself wished to defend himself by warding off force. In these times Pope Gregory VII, for ecclesiastical causes, sent as legate into Germany and Gaul a venerable man, outstanding in learning in the Scriptures and in [unclear], [unclear] to this our monastery of Hirsau, subject to the archbishop. From this the most reverend abbot William, with the permission of [unclear], most holy abbot of Cluny, arranged in two books, in a fair order, the statutes and customs transmitted to him, with certain things reasonably to be changed according to consideration of place, region, and climate, and handed them down to his brothers and successors to be observed for the preservation of monastic life.

*1079.*

In the tenth year of Abbot William, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1079, Hidolph, archbishop of Cologne, died, and Sigeuinus succeeded him. In this same year King Henry fought fiercely against the Saxons, of whom many perished.
