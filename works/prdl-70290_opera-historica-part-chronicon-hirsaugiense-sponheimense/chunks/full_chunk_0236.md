*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius, 181*

[unclear] enclosed women, townsmen, citizens, and peasants, in various places of Germany, were condemned to punishment by fire under the name of [Luciferians?] by a certain friar of Marburg of the Order of Preachers, who had been appointed general inquisitor of heretics by the apostolic see, with too hasty a sentence, as it then seemed to many. For, as someone writes, on the same day on which a person was accused, whether justly or unjustly, with no refuge of appeal or defense availing him, he was condemned and cast into the cruel flames.

Among the many who were then accused, the count of Sayn was denounced to him, falsely defamed for heresy; for his purgation or defense King Henry held an assembly of many princes at Mainz. For this reason Brother Conrad, the minister and author of this persecution among the Germans, fell into the hatred of many nobles and commoners.

Departing from Mainz with an army, he entered Bavaria, having in his host six thousand fighting men, whom he brought out to battle against the duke of Bavaria in the fields of the river Lech near Augsburg of the Raetians; in a short time he received the duke himself, with all Bavaria, in surrender. Meanwhile, while these things were being done in Bavaria, Theoderic, count of Montbeliard, was killed on the road with arms near the borders of Alsace. His body lies buried at Marburg.

At the same time King Henry, leaving Mainz with the princes, began to depart with a strong hand, after certain men had been captured together with booty; when behold, a clamor arose, and the people of the land pursued those fleeing. The count was again captured with many others and handed over to the custody of the bishop of Strasbourg. The bishop, returning from the Bavarian expedition, received security and permitted him to go free.

In the same year also the bishop of Chur was killed by certain wicked men. In the year noted above Berenger, bishop of Speyer, also died; Conrad of Thann succeeded him, and was the forty-fifth in the order of the bishops of Speyer. He presided for almost four years usefully enough.

In this same year, on the third day before the Kalends of March, a concord was made between the bishop and the citizens of Worms in the town of Frankfurt, through Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz, Conrad the aforesaid bishop of Speyer, and the margrave of Baden, by the order and command of King Henry, son of Emperor Frederick.

*1234.* In the third year of Abbot Ernest, which was the year 1234 of the Lord's nativity, Pope Gregory IX caused the cross to be preached against the Stedingen peoples, situated on the borders of Frisia and Saxony, surrounded by roads, marshes, and rivers. Because of disobedience, their offenses, and the withholding of tithes, they had for many years been excommunicated and placed under interdict by the apostolic see, and had shown themselves despisers of the ecclesiastical keys; fighting often with bishops, counts, and other neighboring nobles, they were frequently victors and rarely defeated. But when an army of crusaders came against them, they were overcome and defeated; more than two thousand of them were slain, and the rest fled to their neighbors. Their confederation against the Church came to an end.

In the same year the city of Worms, set alight by an accidental fire, was burned in great part, not without irrecoverable loss to many citizens. This fire began on the tenth day before the Kalends of July, at night, and lasted two days.

In the same year also, on the day before the Ides of September, Henry of Saarbrucken, bishop of Worms, died and was buried with honor in the middle of the choir of his cathedral church. Landolph succeeded him and presided for three years. He expelled the nuns of our order from the monastery commonly called Nonnenmunster, in the suburb of the city, because of their disorderly life, and introduced others of the Cistercian order there; they inhabit that place down to the present day, as we see.

In this year also King Henry summoned an assembly of princes at Boppard, where, after accepting the counsel of wicked men, he made a conspiracy against his father, Frederick II, to his own destruction and the danger of very many. From that day onward he began to solicit whomever he could, with threats, entreaties, and promises, to leave his father and follow him, and he gained many. But the Worms men and the others who, because of their oath, refused to defect from the father, he troubled for a long time with many injuries. Seeing this, the margrave of Baden, faithful to the emperor, set out for Sicily to the emperor, urging him to return to Germany as quickly as possible; otherwise his native land would be [unclear], and he himself would lose [unclear].
