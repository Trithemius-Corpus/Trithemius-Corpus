*Johannes Trithemius.*

...than to our sons. Nor was it enough to have mocked even the little works that we composed at the fathers’ command, unless you also attacked them before the unlearned common people in your discussions. You have always lived in opposition to fatherly exhortations. Often you contrived many conspiracies against us, and, after an oath had been imposed in chapter, under penalty of losing the voice, that no one should reveal to us the secrets of your conspiracy, you fabricated against us falsehoods about debts; these, through the goldsmith of Worms, who was said to be a hidden creditor, we showed before you all in the chapter room to be fabricated and false. Both witnesses are alive today: Gerlacus, abbot of Deutz, and Johannes, abbot of Schönau, then appointed visitors, who by their entreaties prevented us from taking due vengeance upon you.

Likewise, heavily oppressed throughout the whole time of our rule by care and anxiety over temporal and spiritual affairs, we could never find anyone among you who either felt compassion for our labors or truly grieved over the condition of the poor monastery. How many cloister priors did we appoint for you? Apart from one, Nicolaus of Kreuznach, and another, Johannes Damius of [Curtius?], we could never find any zealot for regular discipline. We are ashamed to say with how much hatred you always persecuted those who tried to persuade you to accept the discipline of monastic observance. But how much diligence in household affairs was shown by those whom we took as coadjutors to ourselves, we have learned by painful experience; it cannot be hidden from any of you. O with what groans and sighs we have until now borne among you the burden, full as it is, of pastoral care, having no faithful helper from among you either in spiritual or in temporal matters. You have proved useless to us in everything, having no zeal for uprightness or for the common good; you sought nothing but the comforts of the flesh. Among you there was no one who stretched out helping hands to us; whenever we took some outsider as helper in temporal care, you at once tore him apart with the bites of slander. For four years we were compelled in our own person to collect and renew all the rents of the monastery, bereft of every comfort from you; as often as we appointed one of you cellarer, so often we suffered serious loss in the monastery’s affairs. But we, who often had to obey the command of the fathers of the order, and for twenty years have for the most part spent ourselves by visiting through all the neighboring provinces, carried [unclear]; with us absent and you neglecting everything, indeed seeking your own comforts, at last a by no means fair appearance of affairs in each estate emerged.

Yet, through the intervention of our greatest solicitude, you never felt any lack of the things necessary for sustaining life, until at last, in the year of the Christians one thousand five hundred and four, when war was raging against the most illustrious prince Palatine, most dear to us, the monastery’s farms, from which on every side we used to live, were either burned or laid waste by the Hessians and the Alexandrines. Nevertheless we had a sure hope of recovering everything, but your folly, not to say treachery, put itself in the way; with presumptuous rashness you spoiled every plan of ours, which, mindful of no wrong, we had prepared for your good. We know that for twelve years the seeds of discords had preceded, from which it is clear that every evil arose. Certain men among you, by their hidden machinations, rendered the duke of the [Cynonoti?], otherwise a most pious prince, hostile amid common chatterers, bringing forward as a cause, so that the reason might seem sufficient, fabricated charges, as though we had judged the side of the prince Palatine, with some great attachment, to be preferred to the rights of that duke. For what if this fabrication of yours had even been true? Were we on that account to be torn apart by your slanders, or justly liable to the duke’s indignation? What did our attachment contribute to the Palatine? What did it profit him, or what advantage did he gain from the matter? Or what harm did it do the Duke that we favored the Palatine’s affairs? We frankly confess that we have always truly favored the most serene prince Palatine, most gracious and munificent to us, and today we favor his honor, good fortune, and prosperous affairs, although this favor of ours has contributed nothing to his majesty. Nor should we therefore be thought to have hated the Duke, or to have ill-favored his affairs; even if we had wished to do so, what loss would we have brought upon his affairs? If he believed your false denunciations, I am inclined to cry out against the duke and his men, with Aristophanes, who says quite finely in a certain passage: *at the beginning of Plutus*.

Concerning all these things we would indeed gladly persuade ourselves that the prince was innocent, were not manifest indications of a seduced mind seen to stand in the way. We speak truths; we write what is known about a powerful man, fearing no proscription of his, since he will not be able to proscribe us, for we are in no way subject to his command. Yet we refrain, lest it seem a kind of revenge to have written the truth. We report to you things known and manifest; contradict them if you dare, indeed if you can.
