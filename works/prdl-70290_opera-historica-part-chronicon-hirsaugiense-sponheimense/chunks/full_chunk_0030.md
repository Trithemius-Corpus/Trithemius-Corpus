*DCCCCXIII*

In the twenty-fourth year of Haderad our abbot, and in the nine hundred and fourteenth year of our Lord Jesus Christ, the second of Conrad’s empire, the Hungarians again devastate all Germany with sword and fire, coming as far as Fulda, Alsace, and Basel.

In these times Betto, a monk of our order, provost of Saint Columba, became bishop of Auxerre, a learned and religious man, who from his boyhood had been excellently instructed in monastic disciplines.

In the twenty-second year of Abbot Haderad the archbishop of Mainz died; Heriger succeeded him.

Betto.

Hatto dies.

*[DCCCCXVII?]*

In the twenty-seventh year of Abbot Haderad, Saint Ratbod, bishop of Utrecht, passed to the Lord on the third day before the Kalends of December. He was a learned and most holy man, full of the spirit of prophecy, and also wrote many small works in praise of the saints. He rests at Deventer, exalted by miracles.

The monastery of Gembloux is founded.

Ratbod the bishop dies.

Around these times Wipertus flourished, a noble man, rich and powerful in the world, who founded a monastery of our order in the diocese of Liege, named Gembloux. When he had completed its construction, and monks and an abbot had been introduced, he himself laid aside secular dress and, becoming a monk in the same monastery, shone forth as renowned in life and conduct.

In these days also Wiberadis, a consecrated woman, flourished at the monastery of Saint Gall. Enclosed for the love of God, and devoting herself day and night to prayers, fasts, and holy meditations, she deserved to be enlightened by the spirit of prophecy. She foretold to Saint Udalric, then living among the monks at the head of Saint Gall, that he would become bishop in the church of Augsburg; afterward the outcome of the matter proved this true.

*[DCCCCXVIII?]*

In the year of the Lord 918, in the sixth indiction, Harderadius, the fourth abbot of this monastery, died on the fourth day before the Ides of August, in the twenty-eighth year of his rule. Rudolphus, a monk, succeeded him in the abbacy. He presided over the monastery and the brothers for seven years, as we shall say: a man advanced in age, not very useful for governance.

*Concerning Rudolphus, fifth abbot of this monastery, who presided for seven years, with certain deeds of that time in Germany.*

After the death of the venerable Abbot Haderad, as we have said, in August, and after he had been honorably buried according to custom, Rudolphus, a certain monk advanced in age, was substituted in his place. He presided over the perfection [unclear] of the brothers for seven years; under his rule regular discipline did not greatly prosper. For he was a pious man, excessively liberal, and I will not say prodigal, who took pleasure in drinking-cups and banquets. Hence he neglected temporal affairs and by no means preserved spiritual ones. Therefore, when after years the brothers wished to depose him, they could not, because of the support of friends, many of whom among secular persons he was said to have gained for himself by gifts and presents.

In the same year in which the venerable Abbot Haderad died, Conrad, the first emperor, died on the tenth day before the Kalends of January, and was buried in the monastery of Fulda beside the altar of the Holy Cross. Before he died, lying on his bed, when he was asked by the princes whom he advised them to elect as his successor, he replied: “I know no one better than Henry, duke of the Saxons, who is an excellent man, prudent and renowned, through whom the empire can easily be restored to its former condition. Elect him with confidence, because he is powerful to defend you and to recover what has been lost.”

Henry, first emperor, dies.

*[DCCCCXIX?]*

And so Henry, duke of the Saxons, elected to the empire on Conrad’s advice, ruled for eighteen years. He began in the first year of Abbot Rudolphus, namely in the year of the Lord’s nativity 919: a peaceful man, a cultivator of justice, magnificent, and a most vigorous extirpator of the wicked.

In the third year of Abbot Rudolphus, when the brothers themselves wished to depose him from his office because of his disorderly way of life, they could not, because of the force and threats of his friends, whom partly by blood and partly by gift he had made and had as defenders for himself. Therefore, compelled, they bore it patiently, until the Lord God should arrange to dispose the matter otherwise.
