*Chronicle of Johann Trithemius.*

1317.

In the seventeenth and last year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1317, Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, with King Louis's consent, redeemed with money the imperial castle of Sterrenberg, which had been pledged, and joined it to his church of Trier. For he was a magnificent man, and always obedient and pleasing to the emperors, which greatly benefited the church of Trier.

In the same year, on the fourth day before the Kalends of June, Guido, bishop of Utrecht, died. Frederick of Sierck, provost of the greater church, succeeded him and presided for five years, afflicted by many adversities.

Also in the same year Henry, the thirtieth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, by order of Louis IV, emperor, was deposed from his abbatial dignity and recalled to private life. For, as we remember saying at the beginning, he was a man given to leisure and idleness, who, indulging too much in banquets and the comforts of the flesh, shamefully neglected the whole state of the monastery in temporal and spiritual matters. He sold off many movable and immovable goods of the monastery, alienated [unclear], and spent all the money badly. Therefore, when he disdained to amend his ways, after often being [unclear] by the more prudent men, the matter was brought by the brothers to the bishop, and through him to the king, and by his mandate he was deposed.

*Concerning Sigismund, the thirty-first abbot of this monastery, who presided for twenty-four years, and concerning the various events of that time.*

After Abbot Henry had been deposed and recalled, as we have said, to private life, there followed among the brothers a great examination of merits, lest, if they chose someone as pastor with too little deliberation and with hasty counsel, they should be subject to similar, or certainly graver, dangers and evils, and should inconveniently reduce both states of the monastery to extremes. Therefore, after mature deliberation had first been held for the election of a new pastor, at last they proceeded and directed their votes toward two men. One party, stronger in number and in maturity of counsel, strove to set over [unclear] Sigismund, formerly prior. When both elected men had first come to the judgment of the bishop of Speyer, and then to the audience of Louis, king of the Romans, after the process of the election had been heard, Sigismund, by the sentence of the doctors, was confirmed in the abbacy. Having been ordained according to custom, he presided vigorously over this monastery for twenty-four years, and in spiritual matters governed all things well. He was a Swabian by nation, born of noble stock, of seemly stature, neither short nor misshapen by excessive height. In manners he was humane, in disposition gentle and subtle, prompt in counsel and circumspect with singular prudence, eloquent in speech, and venerable through a certain severity. He managed the affairs of the monastery prudently and orderly, and restored to the monastery's possession many things alienated by his predecessor, Abbot Henry.

1318.

In his first year, which was the year of the Lord's Nativity 1318, Andrew, bishop of Wurzburg, died. Godfrey of Hohenlohe succeeded him and presided for nine years; after many disputes held with his citizens, he finally obtained victory and compelled them to obedience.

In the same year the people of Cologne, at the command of Emperor Louis, called to their aid John, king of Bohemia, who was then at Luxembourg with a great army, William, count of Holland, John his brother, count in Hainaut, Gerhard, count of Julich, Adolf, count of Berg, and John, count of Sayn, with certain others. Proceeding to war against Henry, archbishop of Cologne, who was violating the pacts and treaties of peace and siding with Frederick, duke of Austria, they inflicted much damage on him; and among other things, they besieged the castle called Bruhl for the space of four months. But at last, peace having been interposed and satisfaction made, they withdrew.

1319.

In the second year of Abbot Sigismund, the year of the Lord's Nativity 1319, Henry de Dune, from the count of Wildberg, bishop of Worms, died in the fifth year of his pontificate and was buried in the greater church; and the bishopric was vacant for two months and eight days. Afterward Cuno of Schoneck was elected, a son [unclear] of Worms. Some, however, think that he presided longer, but they have been found by us, after very diligent examination of the sequence of time, to be in error by ten years.
