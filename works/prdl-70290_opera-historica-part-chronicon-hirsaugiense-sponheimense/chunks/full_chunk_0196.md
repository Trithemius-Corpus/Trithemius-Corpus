There, then, Rainald, elect of Cologne, was first ordained priest on Ember Saturday, together with many others newly elected. Afterwards, on the sixth day before the Nones of October (October 2), he was elected archbishop of Cologne by Philip, bishop of Osnabruck, Emperor Frederick being present together with his empress.

In the same year also, on the day of Peter in Chains, which is the Kalends of August (August 1), after midday, Manegold, the seventeenth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, in the sixty-ninth year of his age, and in the eighth of his abbacy, with four months and six days completed, departed from this life to the Lord, and was buried before the altar of St. Michael the Archangel. He was certainly a man worthy of remembrance: although he sometimes endured the murmurings of his brothers, as we have said before, nevertheless the cause of the displeasures arose not from evil, but rather from a good zeal for regular discipline. For he was a most fervent lover of the monastic life; and when he corrected delinquent monks rather harshly according to the monastic rule, he provoked the hatred of the weak against himself. But after he was once admonished by St. Hildegard, he changed severity into the greatest kindness, and supreme peace between him and the brothers prevailed until his death.

*Concerning Rupert, the eighteenth abbot of this monastery, who presided for eleven years, and concerning his way of life and certain deeds of that time.*

In the year of the Lord's nativity 1163, on the second day after the feast of Peter in Chains, which was the fourth day before the Nones of August (August 2), after Manegold, the venerable abbot, had died and been buried, as we have said, the brothers of this monastery came together as one to provide themselves with a new shepherd. After many necessary discussions among themselves, and after humble invocation of the grace of the Holy Spirit, they elected as abbot the venerable man Rupert, at that time prior in [Schonrain?]. But once elected, only with the greatest difficulty could he at last scarcely be induced by the brothers to consent, since, taught by experience, he had learned that there is more danger than honor in the prelacy of cloistered men. For he had previously been abbot in Gottesaue, as we shall say; and from this he had learned very fully in many matters how much the prelate of monks must necessarily suffer. He therefore refused to accept the abbacy of this great and distinguished monastery, [unclear]. At last, overcome by the prayers of the brothers, after he had long resisted, he gave his consent, and for the love of God humbly accepted the burden of pastoral care to be borne; in it he served the Lord strenuously for eleven years. The office of blessing, which he had once received in the abbey of Gottesaue, sufficed also in this one.

He was born of honorable parents in Eastern Francia, and, having been set to the study of letters in boyhood, advanced diligently, as his years increased, in good morals and in knowledge of the Scriptures; in a short time he surpassed many of his contemporaries in both. Then, as his years increased, touched by divine inspiration, he became a monk in this monastery under Abbot Volmar, where he gave himself wholly to spiritual exercise, so that as a young man he became an example of virtues even to many elders. As far as bodily constitution is concerned, he was [unclear]; he was resonant [of voice?], mature and eloquent in speech. In morals he was composed and gentle, ready and cautious in counsel, and circumspect in all his actions. He was of the mildest and most tranquil conduct among the brothers, and never offended anyone by injury. Finally, he applied himself with such diligence to serving almighty God that he always entered the choir among the first and left among the last; and day and night he devoted himself almost continually to the reading of Scripture, holy meditations, and prayers. At length, when the fame of his virtues spread everywhere, even to outsiders, the brothers of the monastery of Gottesaue, which is four miles distant from Hirsau, moved by his name and reputation, when they were bereft of a shepherd, sought him as abbot over them by unanimous election and obtained him. After a humble excuse, at last, constrained by the command of his abbot, he accepted that burden of pastoral care, and was solemnly ordained according to custom by the bishop of Speyer. Once ordained abbot, he applied all his zeal to [unclear], and made no small progress in the restoration of temporal affairs. But in spiritual matters he could not make progress to the same degree, because the insolence of certain brothers stood in the way not a little.
