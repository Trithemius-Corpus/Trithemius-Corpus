*Rudolph, Count Palatine of the Rhine.* Rudolph, the firstborn of Rudolph, brother of Emperor Louis and Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, a man of no great wisdom, had as wife the sister of the king of Hungary. By her he begot a single daughter, Margaret by name; Charles, king of Bohemia and afterward emperor, took her as wife. By her he received Wenceslaus, after his father king of the Romans; John, king of Bohemia; Sigismund, king of Hungary and afterward Roman emperor; Helen, who married the king of England; and Margaret, wife of the margrave of the Old March.

*Adolf, Count Palatine of the Rhine.* Adolf, the second-born son of the elder Rudolph Palatine, from whom the succession of the Counts Palatine of the Rhine is continued, took as wife the daughter of the count of Oettingen. She bore him Rupert, or, as very many maintain, Adolf, father of Rupert, afterward king of the Romans, by Beatrice, daughter of the king of Sicily; concerning him we shall speak more fully in his own place, when we come to him.

*Rupert, Count Palatine.* Rupert, however, the third-born son of the elder Rudolph, Count Palatine of the Rhine, died without children, although he had taken two wives. The first of them was Mechtild, duchess of Brabant; the second Beatrice, daughter of the duke of [Montenfis?].

*The University of Heidelberg is founded.* This Rupert first founded the universal school of Heidelberg, which Pope Benedict XII confirmed. Likewise afterward Pope Urban VI confirmed it. Its first director and moderator was Marsilius of Inghen, doctor of sacred theology, canon of Paris and treasurer of the church of St. Andrew in Cologne, as I remember that we said some time ago in the book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*. He also raised up from its foundations, founded, built, and endowed the collegiate church in the New City of the diocese of Speyer, with beautiful stone vaulting; in it he was afterward buried.

*1340.*

In the twenty-third year of Abbot Sigismund, and the year of the Lord 1340, there was a very great pestilence throughout all Germany, and it struck down many thousands of people. There was also a great famine [unclear], having gloriously triumphed over the captives, bringing no small booty to the English.

In this same year, on the sixth day before the Kalends of May, Henry of Kreuznach, the twelfth abbot of my monastery of Sponheim, died. Willichin II of Sponheim succeeded him and presided for only one year, three months, and four days.

In the same year also, on the twelfth day of the month of February, Rupert, Count Palatine of the Rhine, died and was buried in the choir of the church of the New City, which he had raised from its foundations.

*1341.*

In the twenty-fourth year of Abbot Sigismund, and the year of the Lord 1341, a certain count in Isenburg, at the fair of St. Remigius in Cologne, in a friendly joust, publicly killed against his will Stephen, knight of Oersbeck, who, his neck broken, expired at once, nevertheless remaining seated on his horse as if alive, until he was found and taken down by the hands of servants, in all respects good and magnificent.

In this same year also, John, duke of Brabant, and Engelbert, bishop of Liège, [unclear] Pope XII, whom Clement VI succeeded, presiding for eleven years, a man full of piety and clemency; and [unclear], the rest being turned to flight, until at length they accepted terms of peace.

In the same year, on the eighth day before the Kalends of July, Sigismund, the thirty-first abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, died in the twenty-fifth year of his governance, an excellent man and to be honored with every honor; he presided in both states usefully enough and did many good things.
