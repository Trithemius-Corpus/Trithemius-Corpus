*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

...they reverently celebrate the feasts of the saints, and most tenaciously observe the fasts imposed upon them; and they are the more fervent in the worship of God inasmuch as they are known to have been converted to the faith of Christ among the newest peoples of Germany. Only excess in drinking does not have the name of a vice, although many abstainers are also found among them; the immigrant Franconians and Swabians, as we have often experienced, exceed more in drinking than the natives of the land. But you, Roger, pray to God for us, that at last, departing from here in safety, we may return to our homeland: not because we are weary of the March, for it pleases us greatly, but so that, having returned to the Rhine, we may bring our affairs to their due conclusion. For [unclear] is easy for us for Christ’s sake, since wherever we shall have been in the Lord’s will, we think ourselves to be in our homeland. Before the feast of Easter we shall not see the Rhine, because we must please the most serene prince, who insisted that we remain longer than had been our purpose. We wished you to know these things. If you have anything, write back. Farewell, mindful of us. From Berlin, on the 20th day of the month of October, in the year of Christians 1505.

*XLV. Johannes Trithemius to Johannes Vigilius Sunszhemius, doctor of both laws, greetings.*

*We have sent both my letter and that of the prince Margrave, excusing my rather prolonged stay in the March, to the most serene prince Philip, Count Palatine, by the hands of our chaplain Theodoric, very well known to you; we ask that it become your duty to present them to the prince.* You will learn from the bearer what is being done concerning me; receive in these few words how it pleases us here. The land is indeed good and very fruitful, but it needs hard-working cultivators, since it is itself vast, broad in extent, and the peasants are few and very lazy, given more to drinking and leisure than to labors. Of them we may say: among the March-men, feast days hasten leisure and poverty, fasts hasten illnesses, and drinking hastens death, because in these three things they seem to exceed the other nations of the Germans. For they are men born, as it were, by nature for leisure; they are forbidden to work on many feasts of the saints, and are oppressed by constant want, especially those who live in the countryside. Yet of all whom we have seen they are most observant of fasts, and in this one thing they are rightly to be judged praiseworthy before many. But the greater multitude of them, too much given to drinking, shamefully stains the merit of fasting, to such a degree that here living is almost nothing other than eating and drinking. These things concerning the customs of the people.

There is nothing new here, nothing unusual is being done, except that very serious quarrels are arising between the Duke of Mecklenburg and the Lübeckers; unless they are extinguished by the counsel of friends, they will certainly run together into war. But my prince, the most serene Margrave, whose sister the duke is to have as wife, has interposed his offices and has labored much for concord. Through his mediation truces have been made until Easter. Meanwhile there is peace; yet sometimes confidence in peace is never [secure]. We desire to know from you the good health of our most serene prince Palatine, before whose majesty our necessary delay has been suitably excused. Farewell. From Berlin, on the 20th day of the month of October, in the year of Christians [1505?].

*XLVI. Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, to Johannes Damius, monk, greetings.*

We wish you to know, most loving brother, that, by God’s mercy, first of all we are well, and are being kept in good condition with the most serene prince Margrave, and most abundantly provided with all necessities. Assenting to his will, we have promised to remain here until Easter. Moreover we have written to our brother M. Jacobus Trithemius, instructing that he betake himself to Neuenburg, to remain there with you until we come. Meanwhile let him remain with you, by the kindness of the mistress, lest he be compelled any longer, with salutary studies interrupted, to live fruitlessly, as up to now, with unsettled brains lacking memory. For it is most troublesome to serve the ungrateful; it is too grievous that an obedient man should be deprived of his fruit, and that one to whom a reward ought to have been repaid should be forced to carry off disadvantage. The men dwelling in the land of Nayth are exceedingly ungrateful, as we have experienced because, for many benefits often shown to them not without our loss, [unclear]; therefore not without cause do we forbid the brother to serve the ungrateful. You will make us more certain what our brothers are doing, if you have ascertained the [unclear] of the Cynonoti. Deceit will assuredly be checked by deceit, if they have not changed their plan of impiety. We have no concern about their machinations, because we desire neither favor nor hatred...
