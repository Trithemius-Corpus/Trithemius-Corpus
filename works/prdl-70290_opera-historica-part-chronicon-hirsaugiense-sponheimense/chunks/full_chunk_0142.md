He wrote many volumes, by which he made himself useful to those then present and to those who would live long afterward. Among these I have found an outstanding volume of histories of past time; also one book *On the Illustrious Men of Our Order*. He described the life of Saint Theodoric, bishop of Metz, in heroic verse; also the life of King Sigebert. He composed the life and passion of Saint Lucy the virgin in elegiac verse. He wove together a history of the translation of her body to the city of Metz. He also wrote, in heroic verse, the passion of the Theban martyrs; also the life of Saint Guibert the monk, founder of Gembloux. [He wrote] a chronicle [unclear] against those who slandered the masses of priests who had concubines. At the request of the same persons he replied to the people of Trier concerning the fasts of the Four Times. Likewise he published a little book on famous writers, brief indeed, but not useless. On Ecclesiastes, too, he composed a threefold exposition in heroic verse. He also wrote many letters to various persons; sermons, homilies, antiphons, responsories, concerning various saints. This Sigebert, summoned from his own monastery to the monastery of Saint Vincent at Metz, of our order, on account of his learning, presided there publicly for some time over the school of the monks. Afterwards, recalled to his own monastery, he spent all the time of his life in the study of letters, learning, writing, and teaching.

Rudolph also, of our order, abbot of the monastery of Saint Trudo in the diocese of Liege, a man excellently learned both in the divine Scriptures and in secular letters, practiced in verse and prose, in these times, among other monuments of his talent, wrote an outstanding volume against the simoniacs, which he divided into seven books. He also compiled a short chronicle of the deeds of past time. But he is said to have written many other things, which we have not yet seen.

In the fifteenth current year of Bruno, which was the one thousand one hundred twenty-first year of the Lord’s Nativity, Count Werner, the first founder and endower of the monastery at Breitenau, died, a man wholly devoted to God. Frederick, bishop of Liege, also died; in his place Adelbero, brother of Duke Godfrey, was appointed.

In the same year also Godebald, bishop of Utrecht, founded a monastery of our order not far from the city of Utrecht itself, in a marshy solitude, in honor of Saint Lawrence the martyr; it is called Oesborch down to the present day. In it he established an abbot and monks, for whom he provided the necessary things abundantly. It is reported that there was a very large congregation of monks in that place, who lived in such strictness of monastic conduct that the place itself, long afterward, was called by the inhabitants of the land “the prison of religion.” For those who could not be corrected in other monasteries were sent there to do penance. He also joined to the previously established monastery of men a monastery of nuns, or women religious, in which he placed many virgins serving Christ, and for them also he provided necessities.

In the same aforesaid year of the Lord’s Nativity, the one thousand one hundred twenty-first, in the fourteenth indiction, Bruno, the venerable abbot of this monastery, after many labors undergone in holy religion, and weighed down by age, began to be ill; desiring Christ with his whole longing, he at last happily passed from this misery.

In that year, on the tenth day before the Kalends of April, after the fourteenth year of his rule had been completed, with three months and twenty-two days, he was buried in the church of Saints Peter and Paul, before the altar of Saint Lawrence the martyr; a man certainly worthy of remembrance, [unclear] for the people: a rule of right conduct, who from that day on which he once took up the habit of holy conversion in the present monastery, always advancing toward what lay ahead, did not look back. In prayer he was constant; in the offering of the body and blood of the Lord, daily. For from that time, out of love for God, he despised [unclear], began to be a monk, so wholly betook himself to inward things, so despised the cares and all the business of the world, and so cast himself down, for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ, in such great humility and simplicity, that to those who did not know him he seemed the simplest of all. Thereafter no one ever saw him idle; but always occupied in the mental exercise of the spirit, either he burned with tears in prayer, or was intent on the reading of the divine Scriptures, or in meditation.
