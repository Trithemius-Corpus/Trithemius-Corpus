*Of Johannes Trithemius.*

*XXXIII.*

*Germanus de Ganay, to the most illustrious man Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, splendor of our age, greetings.*

[unclear]

Most worthy sir, the radiance of the sun cannot be hidden when it strikes our hemisphere, nor withdrawn from human sight; rather, it spreads the most brilliant rays of its light into the eyes of all, and draws them into admiration of its brightness. So too the most luminous splendor of your learning can by no means lie concealed, but sends forth the ray of its light to all, however far removed by distance of place. Hence among foreigners your name is held most celebrated and is exalted with wondrous praises; stirred by the fame of your singular learning, they desire nothing more ardently than to be illuminated by the ray of your distinguished knowledge.

And I too, relying on confidence in your kindness, have not feared to send you this letter, both to congratulate your dignity and to explain my desire to you. A letter from you came into my hands, sent to a certain Johannes Steinenmoelen, whom, in the very opening of the salutation, after the expression of your name, you call a man of desires in sacred matters. It certainly contains a rare and admirable philosophy, both concerning numbers and concerning elements, yet so veiled in enigmas and hidden in secret words that understanding of it is only partly open to me, and far exceeds the grasp of my mind.

Therefore, if there were with you some interpretation of it which you would not refuse to share with me, I myself should wish to read nothing more gladly. For my mind conjectures that a very worthy and lofty meaning lies beneath those words, and that from your same workshop other works have come forth, which go beyond the accustomed mode of philosophizing and contain a secret and sacred knowledge. If, by your assistance, I should attain these, things more desirable than Attalic wealth and the riches of Croesus would fall to me. I beseech you not to refuse to impart them to me, a most observant devotee of your name.

Our Carolus Bovillus, who in former years, when he was traveling through Germany, came to visit your excellence, has often mentioned you to me, and, to tell the truth, has wholly led me to become an observer and admirer of your virtues. Finally, the bearer of this letter, known and friendly to your excellence, Master Narcissus, will explain more fully the greater wishes of my mind, which I could not easily include in a brief letter. Farewell, glory of our age and unique exemplar of learning. From Paris, the third day before the Kalends of August, in the year 1505.

*XXXIV.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Spanheim, to Germanus de Ganay, a most learned man, greetings.*

[unclear]

Your envoy Master Narcissus came to us at Neumetum on the fifteenth day of the month of August, bringing your letter, and at the same time welcome little gifts. We detained him with us for almost twelve days, so that he should not seem to have undertaken so great a journey in vain. For the matters which you indicated to us through him are great, and it was not within our power to give him an answer without mature deliberation. We have fully understood your mind from his report, and, as far as it has now been permitted to us, we have taken care that you should understand that you have not been disappointed in your desire.

Yet we have by no means been able to satisfy you in all things on this occasion, because from the first day of April of this year we have not been in our monastery; the reasons for this matter we have entrusted fully to Narcissus himself to report to you. For the understanding of our letter which we wrote in the previous year to Johannes Steinenmoelen, we have given Narcissus some entrance, from whom you will understand the foundation of the hidden matters for knowledge of which you are striving. The ternary must altogether be reduced to unity if the mind wishes to attain a perfect understanding of these matters. For the unit is not a number, and from it every number arises. Let the binary be rejected, and the ternary will be convertible to unity.

But, O Germanus, as Hermes says, without falsehood, certain, and most true by kinship with unity. Narcissus is not capable of all things. That which is above is like that which is below, and that which is below is like that which is above, because every number consists of units alone, to perpetuate the many wonders of one thing. Do not all things flow from one thing by the goodness of the one? And whatever is joined to unity cannot be diverse, but bears fruit by the simplicity and fittingness of the one. What is born from unity? Is it not the ternary? Understand: the unit is simple, the binary composite, but the ternary is reduced to the simplicity of unity. I am not Trithemius of a triple mind, but rejoicing in one mind in the ternary number, which truly brings forth a wonderful deed. Its father is the sun, its mother the moon. The wind carried the seed in its womb; the earth nourished it. This is the father of all perfection of the whole world. Its power is complete and immense.
