Therefore, when the man of Württemberg was one day in the natural bath which we commonly call Wildbad, the count of Eberstein came secretly with an army and encircled the place with a siege, hoping that he would capture the count inside. But vain confidence deceived him. For, led by a certain peasant by night through the woods to safer places, he escaped his hands without injury; and very serious wars, burnings, and plunderings followed between them. For almost all the nobles, conspiring with the aforesaid count of Eberstein against the man of Württemberg, except only the citizens in Esslingen, who sided with Count Eberhard, took up arms and stirred up battles.

In the same year also Engelbert, archbishop of Cologne, weighed down by very great burdens of debt, and also not moderately disabled by paralysis, feeling that on this account he was less equal to the government of the pontificate, with the consent of his chapter took as coadjutor Cuno, the above-mentioned archbishop of Trier, to whom, with Pope Urban V approving, he committed the whole administration of the church in spiritual and temporal matters. Archbishop Engelbert, however, after this provision had been completed, withdrew to the tranquillity of private life, retaining certain revenues for the use of his life; he did not long survive.

*1368.*

In the tenth year of Abbot Wignand, and in the year of the Lord one thousand three hundred sixty-eight, Count Eberhard of Württemberg died, leaving two sons, namely Ulrich and Eberhard, who afterwards waged many battles against him [unclear].

In the same year, on the twenty-first day of the month of August, Engelbert, archbishop of Cologne, died; after his death the archiepiscopate was vacant for two years. For Cuno, archbishop of Trier, although he was postulated by the chapter, did not wish to be archbishop; but by order of Pope Urban, under the name of administrator, he governed that church together with his own Trier church quite strenuously and usefully. In the aforementioned year, in Lent, a comet appeared from heaven.

In these times also Cuno, the often-mentioned archbishop of Trier, built quite handsomely the castle Engers with its fortification beside the Rhine, between Koblenz and Andernach, and wished it to be called, from his own name, Cunenergers, although few attach his name to it.

*1369.*

In the eleventh year of Abbot Wignand, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1369, around the feast of Easter, Emperor Charles IV, after a very great army of his men had been assembled, entered Lombardy to fight against the Milanese; ranging about here and there until the feast of Saint Michael the Archangel, he could not defeat those who were in fortifications. For he wished to reduce the lordship of Milan to the empire by arms, and to avenge the wrongs of Pope Urban V, which he had once received from the Visconti, when he was still in minor orders: they had forced him to eat the apostolic letters brought against them. But when plague had violently invaded the army, after receiving a great sum of money as a sign of the superiority of the empire, he returned to his own lands, not without great displeasure on the pope’s part.

In this same year the citizens of Cologne, having attempted to infringe the privileges and liberties of the clergy, were subjected to an ecclesiastical interdict for two years; the clergy, leaving the city by order of Cuno, administrator of the church of Cologne, remained outside for eighteen months.

In the same year also Gottfried, count of Arnsberg, with the consent of his wife, handed over his county of Arnsberg, with all its appurtenances, castles, villages, and revenues, to the church of Cologne to be possessed forever, just as it is also possessed today by the archbishop.

*1370.*

In the twelfth year of Abbot Wignand, and in the year of the Lord one thousand three hundred seventy, a great battle took place on the vigil of Saint Bartholomew the Apostle between Wenceslaus, duke of Brabant and Luxembourg, brother of Emperor Charles, and William, duke of Jülich, near Baesweiler; more than eight thousand men were slain on both sides. The duke of Jülich himself emerged as victor and led away Duke Wenceslaus captive with many other nobles; at length, after eleven months, out of fear of the emperor, he released him freely without exaction. But from the others he extorted an almost boundless amount of money for their liberation.
