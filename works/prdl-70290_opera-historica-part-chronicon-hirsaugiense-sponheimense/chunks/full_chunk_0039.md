*The Monastery of Hirsau*

In the same year Frederick, archbishop of Mainz, died. He was succeeded by William, son of Otto the First, the emperor, who writes thus about him in the Chronicle: “In the year of the Lord’s incarnation 954, in the twelfth indiction, lord Frederick of blessed memory, archbishop of the holy church of Mainz, died on the eighth day before the Kalends of November.

“In the same year I, William, unworthy of so great a succession, was elected in his place, with the consent of the clergy and people of that holy see, on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of January, at the place called Arnsteden, and was ordained at Mainz on the ninth day before the Kalends of January.” These are William’s words.

At these times there flourished in Saxony Widukind, monk of St. Vitus at Corvey, a man learned in the divine Scriptures and most highly educated in secular writings. As we said above, he wrote to Mechthild, daughter of Otto the First, the emperor, namely the abbess at Quedlinburg, a history of the deeds of Henry the First, of Otto the First, and of the origin of the Saxons, arranged in three little books. He also wrote the Life of Saint Paul the First Hermit and the Passion of Saint Thecla the Virgin in a double style, that is, in prose and in verse, and also many other works, of which elsewhere we have already made mention. For many years he publicly presided over the school of monks in the aforesaid monastery of Corvey, and left after him several most learned disciples, many of whom were taken up into various bishoprics and abbacies. In that little book which he wrote on the studies of the ancients, he admits that, stirred by the reputation of Meginrad the scholastic and of the monks of St. Aurelius, he came to Hirsau and had conversation with them for many days, and that he saw there such study and fervor among the monks for the knowledge of the Scriptures, and found men so learned, that he could not marvel enough; and he extols that same Meginrad the scholastic with particular praises, saying that he was another Jerome and inferior to none of the ancients in learning.

Around these times there flourished Gregory, a priest, son of the king of England, a pilgrim for Christ, and Everard, a monk, both solitaries in the Black Forest beside the cell of St. Meginrad, the martyr of Reichenau. The first of these, Gregory, though he was the son of a king, despising his father, kingdom, fatherland, and all the pomp of the world for love of the heavenly kingdom, and fleeing secretly, remained unknown to men in great holiness in the Lord in the aforesaid place until his death.

The other, Everard, although he was dean of the church of Strasbourg, by divine inspiration despised the world and, when an angel of God revealed the aforesaid place, lived there a most holy life with St. Gregory the pilgrim until death. This most holy Everard was very familiar with and beloved by blessed Ulrich, bishop of Augsburg, by whom he was often accustomed to be visited. He was of such merit before God that, living and dead, he shone forth with many miracles, and he foretold his own death to the aforesaid holy bishop Ulrich long beforehand. From these two most holy hermits, that same place in which the monastery of our order now stands, built not much later, has from then until the present day been called “At the Hermits”; to it a great concourse has been accustomed to be made for many years past, although the monks dwelling there in these times are of a most dissolute life.

In these days Saint Ulrich, bishop of Augsburg, also flourished, a man renowned before God, who shone forth, illustrious with many miracles in life and after death. While he was still a young man he was brought up in the monastery of St. Gall and instructed in the necessary Scriptures. At length he was made bishop of Augsburg, and founded a monastery of our order in the same city, in honor of St. Afra the martyr, in which he himself at last became a monk.

He died, moreover, and was buried there, in the year of the Lord 973, which was the twenty-first year of Abbot Siger, in the first indiction, on the fourth day before the Nones of July, in the eighty-third year of his age and the fiftieth of his having assumed the episcopate, under Otto the Second, emperor.

Saint Maiolus also, the fourth abbot of the Cluniac monastery of our order, is held to have been renowned in these same times for learning and holiness. In the sixth year of his conversion, when St. Ademar yielded because of weakness of body and failing of the eyes, he was appointed shepherd of the Lord’s flock and shone forth, illustrious with many virtues. His feast day is kept on the fifth day before the Ides of May.

In the year of Abbot Siger, Otto the Great, emperor, overthrew the Hungarians with great slaughter, with great danger to himself and his men, in the place called Lechfeld, not far from Augsburg; in that battle Duke Conrad fell, with many others. Henry also, duke of Bavaria, the emperor’s brother, died in the same year.
