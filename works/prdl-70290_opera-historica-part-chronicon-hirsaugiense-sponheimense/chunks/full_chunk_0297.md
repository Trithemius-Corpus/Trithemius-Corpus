Now, after Wighard, the second of this name, the thirty-third abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, as we have said, had entered upon the way of all flesh and had been buried with due solemnity, as the custom then was, the prior and brothers, bereft of their spiritual shepherd, soon in the same year, on the last day of the month of May, which was the Friday after Urban’s day, came together in the chapter-place for the election of a new father. After the necessary counsels and discussions had meanwhile first been held, they set over them as thirty-fourth abbot of this monastery Wignand, a monk from their own company. Confirmed and ordained according to custom by Gerhard von Ehrenberg, bishop of Speyer, he presided in the abbacy for twenty years, ten months, and twenty days. Of his deeds, because of the sloth and idleness of the monks, we can bring down either nothing or little to the knowledge of posterity, since they were done beyond the memory of those who live today, and were committed to writing by no one, as ought to have been done. For after love and zeal for letters began to fail among monks, together with the discipline of regular observance, it is not surprising if no one either wished or was able to write the chronicle of the monastery for the instruction of posterity. Shameful to say, I am compelled to state what many abbots, who profess the holy observance of regular life with their monks, and who are ignorant of the usefulness of history, object against us. For they say: “What advantage can it bring us to know the antiquity of the foundations of monasteries? What does it contribute to recall to memory either the successions or the deeds of abbots, who sleep in the dust of death and are not soothed by any praises?” O you already buried, idle men though alive, whose memory will perish with a noise: how asinine and crude in wit you are, you openly show, since you despise the good of histories, which you cannot attain. History is written for the living, both present and future, not for the dead; therefore, if you lived in understanding, you would certainly love histories. For if history were taken away, neither would the memory of the most holy Gospel nor the martyrdoms and passions of the saints have come down to our knowledge. Let men alive yet buried in ignorance therefore fare well, and let them live for themselves alone, content with their own idleness, useful to none, and imitable by none in good actions or zeal. But let us pursue our own subject.

Around these times Albert, duke of Bavaria, count of Holland and Zeeland, and lord of Hainaut and Frisia, captured the town of Holland named Delft because of the disobedience and rebellion of its citizens. After taking it he overthrew its walls and gates down to the ground. He also utterly destroyed the castle of Heemskerk.

In the second current year of Abbot Wignand, in the year of the Lord [1360?], the emperor Charles summoned the princes of the empire to the Swabian town of Esslingen for certain affairs of the empire. There, when one day in the refectory of the Friars Minor he was discussing his counsels with them, a disturbance of the citizens arose against the emperor and princes, and a tumult was stirred up. Soon withdrawing from the town, he entrusted it to Eberhard, count of Württemberg, to make compensation for so great an affront inflicted upon him. He, after an army of his own men and of the imperial cities had been gathered together, encircled Esslingen with a siege and began to attack it vigorously. But when the citizens saw that they were placed in an evil situation and could not resist so great a multitude, they accepted peace through the intervention of money, paying to the emperor sixty thousand gold pieces in satisfaction, and thirty thousand to Count Eberhard for the expenses incurred. And thus the disturbance of the tumult, purchased for one hundred thousand, came to rest.

At these times serious disagreements had arisen between Boemund, archbishop of Trier, and the counts of Sponheim and Starkenburg, over certain castles and lordships. But through the intervention of friends, Count Walram of Sponheim soon returned into concord with the archbishop. The count of Starkenburg, however, began to lay waste grievously with sword and fire the fields, villages, and possessions of the church of Trier, and inflicted much damage. But the archbishop, having gathered an army, [attacked?] Enkirch, a village of the count situated on the bank of the Moselle, together with the fortification itself.
