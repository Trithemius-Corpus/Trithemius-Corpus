For you know that I can find no other abbot nearer to us who is fit to preserve in us the holy institution of regular discipline, which your reverend paternity planted with great labor and has laudably continued up to the present. Therefore we beg again and again, by the most innocent death of our Lord Jesus Christ: hear us in this petition of ours, if it cannot be done for another, since we seek things just and holy, salutary for us, and not impossible for you. We send with these letters our procurator, through whom we unanimously request and await the response of your reverend paternity. May almighty God long preserve you, sweetest father, for our advantage and that of many. From Seebach, on the twenty-first day of September, in the year 1506.

*LXXVII.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to his sweetest daughters in Christ, the abbess and convent in Seebach: sincere greeting and charity in the Lord.*

*The letters* of your charities we received at Neumarkt, in which you signify your tears and sighs for us. We do indeed grieve with you from the heart, but it cannot be otherwise. Sponheim, as we have resolved, we shall abandon because of the ingratitude of the monks, not caring for the words of the prior, whom we know to be deceitful. For he says one thing and ponders another in his heart. We do not heed the mind of the duke, nor do we care for the wishes of those who are with him, since, if either must be, we have resolved at all times rather to beg than henceforth to be subject to his rule. We know and have learned by the report of many that he is greatly pained at what has been done, that he desires us to return to the monastery, and that he promises faithfully to maintain us; but we do not seek the favor of one whom we have experienced as provoking us so ill-advisedly, unjustly, and rashly.

As for the commission long ago made to us concerning you through Ludovicus the bishop, we shall gladly do what you unanimously request: namely, that we retain the name of visitor together with the office, until better provision can be made for you. For although I shall by no means be able to come to you personally as often as hitherto, nevertheless I shall come in writing, until at last it may someday happen that I come in person. But while the affairs of the Limpurgers are set in turmoil, no abbot or monk of our order will dare to approach your Seebach because of the savagery of your count, much less I, who am charged with having always adhered to the just causes of the Limpurgers against the count. Hence you will necessarily remain without visitation for some time, until concord has been restored between the abbot and the count himself.

Therefore we ask you in the Lord, dearest daughters, to attend diligently to your calling in all charity and patience, living humbly according to the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, so that, advancing daily in the peace and love of God, you may have the Holy Spirit as visitor, who is accustomed to rest upon the humble, quiet person who keeps the admonitions of God. Do not sadden the mother who presides over you in solicitude, but imitate her fervor in the service of God, obeying humbly in all things. For she herself, as you perceive by word and example, always precedes you first in the choir in all holiness, so diligent and careful in divine service that she seems to have no occupation in temporal matters; yet so occupied in external matters that no time for prayer can seem to remain.

Before all things preserve mutual charity among yourselves, since where charity is not, there can be no justice. For love of neighbor works no evil. Charity, indeed, the bond of perfection and the fount and origin of all virtues, covers a multitude of sins; without it no one will be able to have true contrition of heart, no one will obtain pardon for crimes. Love of neighbor, conformed to divine love, is a wave of peace, a shower of charity, the seed of concord, the merit of eternal happiness. Therefore almighty God commands each one of us, saying: *You shall love your neighbor as yourself.* This neighbor is to be considered not by nearness of blood but by fellowship of reason, in which all human beings are companions. For this reason you have been gathered in a monastery, that by the mutual aids of charity you may protect your weakness; therefore always keep the mutual observance of love, so that you may satisfy the monastic institution, just as the holy Apostle commanded: bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ.
