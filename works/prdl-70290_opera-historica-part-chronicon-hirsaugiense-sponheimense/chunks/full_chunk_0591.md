Also in the year noted above, after the assembly of the princes at Augsburg had been dissolved, the Venetians, considering that on account of their deserts they were despised both by the king of the French and by Caesar and the princes, and that they could not obtain what they had wished, took counsel among themselves and sent an embassy to Pope Julius. Up to that point he had agreed with the kings, was an adversary of the Venetians, and, as was said above, had excommunicated them as rebels. They asked mercy from him; finally, by letters and by the persuasions of their ambassadors, they at length brought it about that the pontiff, moved by mercy and judging that the bosom of mother Church should not be closed to the penitent, received them back into grace and absolved them from the bond of excommunication by which they had been bound. How much this deed displeased Caesar Maximilian and Louis, king of the French, will become manifest to posterity not by my words, but by their deeds. For the pontiff seems to be especially blameworthy in this, not only to those kings themselves but also to many others: that he summoned Caesar and the French king of the French by his commands against the Venetians, and now, when the affair had come to its final crisis, he himself grants pardon to the enemies without their consent, and, after the treaty and compact which he had made with the princes had been concluded, he breaks and violates the conditions of the war which he himself, first establishing them, had commanded. From this point the pope stands for the Venetians against Emperor Maximilian and at the same time King Louis of the French; he wages wars, leads an army, and for several months now the pontiff himself has learned to live in tents. We await the end of this matter.

Likewise, in the year so often noted above, the abbot and monks of the monastery of St. Januarius the martyr in Murrhardt, in the parts of Swabia, of the diocese of Wuerzburg, consenting to the malignant machinations of Satan and wishing, after the example of Camberg, to apostatize from the Order of St. Benedict, which they had professed, sent their prior to the Roman curia with letters, money, and false narratives, so that by whatever rashness they could they might obtain permission to withdraw from the order. When he came to Rome, he obtained letters of consent and an indult for transferring the monastery into a secular church. Yet they have by no means been able to bring this into effect, nor can they up to the present. For Ulrich, duke of Wuerttemberg, in whose dominion Murrhardt is situated, hearing that the bull of transfer had been obtained by the prior not as he wished but as he could, and that it had been deposited with merchants in a bank at Augsburg, sent a messenger and intercepted it, and did not allow it to come into the hands of those who desired to apostatize. And so the thoughts of the impious were mocked, and they did not come to their end. They spent much money for the same useless bull, and on that account fell into great want. But the prior, having returned to the monastery, because he had exceeded the limits of the mandate entrusted to him and had spent a huge sum of money for a bull, not of the kind he ought to have had but such as he could obtain, was seized by order of the duke and with the consent of the abbot, and was consigned to prison in a certain very harsh castle; in it he is now paying the deserved penalties of his rashness for a second year. For the mandate ran thus: that if the pope consented that all presentations to the benefices of the aforesaid monastery should pass to the one who desired it, I do not say who he was, there is a reason, he should receive the bull, the money being paid through bankers; if he did not consent, he should by no means presume to do so. But in that transfer the pope did not wish to remit anything either of his own right or of that of the bishop of Wuerzburg, nor did he wish entirely to grant to those who desired to have it the presentation to the benefices. The prior, however, caring little who should have the presentations to the benefices, provided that he himself might be allowed to put off the monastic habit and become a secular canon, and desiring all too much to apostatize from the order, spent two thousand florins and more for the aforesaid bull of transfer, and transgressed the law of the mandate imposed on him by those who had sent him.

Therefore in the same year, in Advent, Lawrence, bishop of Wuerzburg, ordinary of the place, greatly indignant at the rashness of the abbot and monks of Murrhardt, took counsel and sent his councillors there, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James, and Conrad, abbot of St. Stephen, with certain others, to visit the place and the monks; the abbot of Lorch, with certain others, agreed with the bishop in the same matter. When the visitation had been carried out, and a summary reckoning of the debts received, they placed certain brethren there for the reform, and also established for them an order of life. Further, for the fuller execution, they appointed another day to meet there again, at Lent after the second Sunday of the following year.
