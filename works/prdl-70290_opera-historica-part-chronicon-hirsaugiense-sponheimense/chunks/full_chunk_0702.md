If our Veldicus has any of his works that he wishes to be printed, let him send it to us polished, and we shall take care that it is printed as soon as possible. Concerning your little works also, send whatever you wish to have printed, and you will find our diligence. Roger, you have written many little works in a few years, by day and by night, while nevertheless carrying the burdens of canonical religious life; at this quite a number, indeed, are amazed. I praise and commend the diligence of your studies; I would praise it more if, leaving secular matters aside, you turned yourself to spiritual ones. You have already composed almost one hundred and forty little works, among which there are very few (yet I do not know whether there are any) whose reading either builds up faith or advances the mind into devotion to spiritual exercise. I beg you, my Roger, change your purpose in writing, and you who until now have written many things for the sons of the world, from now on bring forth something for the worshippers of God. [Boys'?] songs do not always befit [a man?]; studies must be matched to one's age, and, according to the [unclear] of Saint [unclear], when one is a man one must put away those things which seem to belong to children. You are strong in talent and at the same time in facility of writing; it is not enough unless you write such things as may benefit readers either in knowledge or in love of the highest good, which is God, and may prepare a reward for you in the future. Farewell, praying to God for us. From Würzburg, on the eighteenth day of the month of December, in the year of the Christians 1506.

*XIII. Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, sends greeting to Johannes Damio Curiesto, confessor of the nuns at Neuburg near Heidelberg.*

From your letter, dearest brother, I have now been made more certain both what the form of the election was, and who has been elected abbot of the monastery of Sponheim in my place. Did I not long ago predict that Melantius the prior was panting after the abbacy, and that for this reason he had earnestly devised all methods and ways by which he might induce, or at least provoke, me to resign? What he ambitiously coveted he has obtained; let him himself see by what means, since the Lord God is just, who gives grace to the humble and is wont to resist the proud and swollen. This must be imputed to the foolish and inexperienced visitors, who badly arranged the difficult business of the election. And so that you may recognize what seems to me to have been wrongly done in an election of this kind, we shall recount in order what we noted from your letter. First, indeed, the visitors left a bad precedent to posterity, in that they summoned laymen to the monastery for counsel at the time of an election, with such great display, as a bad example; this had not been done before, nor was it then necessary that it be done, unless perhaps in the [council of the brothers?] they feared an outbreak of schism. Then, since they could clearly have known that on that occasion none among the brothers then present at the election was fit for the charge of pastoral solicitude, why did they not postpone the day of election until [the absent ones?] were compelled to be present? Why did they not elect some fit man from wherever? Only five conventual monks were present at the election; the rest, and likewise you too, were absent out of indignation at what had been done; and, after a conspiracy had been made among them, they swore that they would receive no one as abbot unless he was contained in their number of five. Once this conspiracy had been allowed by the visitors, there could be no doubt that no one except Melantius would be abbot. What sort of election of monks is this, in which the way is barred to the Holy Spirit, and the votes of all the electors are maliciously restricted contrary to the norm of equity? In such a manner the prior was elected abbot, who [unclear] of our father Benedict [unclear]. For they elected as abbot one consenting to their own vices: sluggish, afflicted with acedia, and having no zeal for regular discipline, unless perhaps from now on, by God's mercy, he rises to better things; that this may happen, we do not cease to pray to God with continual prayers. For, to confess the truth, we do not hate the man, many evils though we have received from him, but we rightly detest his vices enough; unless he corrects them, he will be a useless shepherd.
