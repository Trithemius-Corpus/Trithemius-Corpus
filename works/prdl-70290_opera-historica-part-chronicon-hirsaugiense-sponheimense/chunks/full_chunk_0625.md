*Familiar Letters*

...to kindle with all one’s powers the observance of righteousness and the inward love of God. But this cannot be done otherwise than by the doctrine of Christ, because the holy Spirit of God does not pour in the gifts of his grace where he finds not the words of Christ but the folly of the Gentiles. We do not read that any of our people, from a discourse supported by testimonies of Gentile philosophers, received the grace of the Holy Spirit or ever felt the sighs of inward compunction. For the Spirit of God is accustomed to make fruitful his own seeds, not those of another; the Son of God confirms by the infusion of virtue his own words, not the empty babblings of Gentile philosophers.

O golden ages of the fathers of old, when the Spirit of the saints spoke through them his own words to the people, not words proper to them or alien ones; for then he bestowed his grace upon the minds of the hearers when he recognized in them his own things sown and cast forth, not those of the Gentiles. For the holy preachers, burning with the inspiration of the divine Spirit, spoke fiery words of God to the people with humble sincerity; and they, kindled by their ardor, were rendered willing and most steadfast for every observance of Christ’s commandments. At that time the words of the preachers, kindled by the grace of the Spirit, prepared the minds and hearts of the peoples aflame; and then the mercy of God worked many wonders among Christians, granting to both alike, that is, to preachers and hearers together, increases of all virtues. Sinners were suddenly converted to repentance; an incredible grace of compunction imbued the minds of the hearers; and the fervent and glowing love of God sweetly set all hearts on fire, and, inducing contempt for earthly things, armed them bravely for the endurance of every tribulation for the love of Christ.

For the discourse of all teachers then was Christian, simple, pure, and whole: not defiled by the opinion of the Gentiles, not [unclear] by a confused citation of [unclear], nor interrupted by fables and words provoking laughter, nor swollen with curious and useless quibbles, nor devised for the vain esteem of the preachers. It is fitting for you, Kymolanus, to be an imitator of these fathers, who, as lovers of true wisdom, sought in all things not their own glory but Christ’s, and the advantage of their hearers. Beware of becoming an imitator of those who do not preach Christ in a Christian manner, who seek either glory from men or the advantage of temporal property.

You have girded yourself for the true philosophy, which professes, preaches, loves, praises, and imitates Christ, the power and wisdom of God; in love of it you ought to remain faithfully, so that you may deserve to receive the fruit of everlasting blessedness. But those who imitate false wisdom cannot come to true wisdom. And what is false wisdom, except that which seems great to the lovers of this world? This is the wisdom of the world, with Gregory as divine witness: to cover the heart with machinations, to veil meaning with words, to show as true the things that are false, to demonstrate as false the things that are true. Those who know this wisdom despise the rest in pride; those who do not know it, being subject and fearful, admire it in others. It commands those who obey it to seek the heights of temporal goods, to rejoice once the vanity of temporal glory has been obtained, to repay evil for injuries, and to love the advantages of this life more vehemently.

But our wisdom, O Kymolanus, is to feign nothing for ostentation, to disclose meaning by words, to love true things as they are, to avoid false things, to offer good things freely, to endure evils more willingly than to do them, to seek no vengeance for injury, and to reckon insult for the truth as gain. The fear of the Lord, as Scripture says, is the beginning of wisdom. Blessed is the man who finds true wisdom, and to whom Christian prudence has flowed abundantly. Its acquisition is better than the trade in gold and silver. Its first and purest fruits [unclear], because it is more precious than all riches, and all things that are desired cannot be compared with it.

For true wisdom is that by whose splendors the mind, once illumined, both understands the truth which it loves and loves the good which it knows. Let us take an example of wisdom from the sun, so that we may be able to distinguish between knowledge and love. The sun does not warm all those to whom it gives light; and wisdom indeed teaches many what is to be done, yet it does not at once set all on fire so that they wish to fulfill in deed the good that they know. For just as it is one thing to know many riches, another to possess them, and knowledge does not make a man rich, but the possession of riches does, so to know many things and to understand wisely does not make a truly wise man if the display of works does not follow. For all our wisdom consists in this one thing: that we know God, and, worshiping him devoutly above all things, because he is the highest good, love him with all our powers. This is the philosophy of Christians, this the certain wisdom of the faithful, to which all other sciences are chiefly to be referred. This is consummate wisdom, which all the philosophers of the Gentiles sought throughout their whole life and were never able to find, comprehend, or hold, because they either retained a depraved religion or utterly abolished every religion. But whoever has deserved to obtain familiarity with this perfect wisdom is truly wise, and to him thereafter whatever of talent and labor
