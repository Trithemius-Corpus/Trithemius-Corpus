*1293.*

In the second year of Crafto, and in the year of the Lord’s Nativity [1293], a great part was again consumed by a terrible fire. In the same year also, on the day of Saint Othmar the abbot, Simon, bishop of Worms, died and was buried in the monastery of Schönau. After his death Gerhard, son of the count [Hirluti?], commonly called Raugraf, was elected as his successor; before he could be confirmed, while the Apostolic See was vacant, he was overtaken by an untimely death. After him, in the following year, Eberhard of Strahlenberg was elected; he presided for only ten months, and died.

In the same year also, Adolf, the new king of the Romans, led an army against the margraves of Meissen, and made that very march subject to the Roman Empire and obedient, together with its princes. Between Philip, king of the French, and Edward, king of the English, in this same year there began a war which lasted for some years, with great loss to both, not without the bloodshed of many.

In these times also Eberhard, count of Württemberg, siding with Albert, duke of Austria, persecuted Adolf, king of the Romans, inflicting many losses upon him and receiving many in return. This Count Eberhard, a man impatient of peace, a lover of war, always in commotion, always restless, had disputes with all the kings who in his time governed the Roman Empire; he was hostile to all, rebellious and opposed to all. Allied with Rudolf the First, he rebelled with fourteen counts, as we said above. Later, in persecuting Adolf, he clung most firmly to Duke Albert. Then, after Adolf’s death, he was no less opposed to Albert than he had, with all his strength, persecuted the other kings before him.

In the year noted above Crafto, the twenty-eighth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, died, having completed the second year of his rule; he was indeed a good man in his time, but by no means to be compared with the ancient fathers in zeal and fervor for monastic integrity, since he found the observance of regular discipline fallen into ruin and left it so.

*Concerning Godfrey, Twenty-Ninth Abbot of This Monastery, who presided for seven years; and concerning various deeds of that time in Germany.*

When the venerable Abbot Crafto had died and been buried, the brothers of this monastery came together in the chapter-place for the election of a new shepherd; after prayers had first been offered for counsel, they began to hold mature discussion among themselves. At length, after many deliberations, they unanimously elected Godfrey, a venerable old man, who had for a long time held the office of custodian, a Swabian by nation, born of honorable parents according to [unclear], who, confirmed by Frederick, bishop of Speyer, and blessed according to custom, was the twenty-ninth in the order of abbots of this monastery and presided for seven years. He was a man of great stature and elegant appearance, born of a [unclear] family, prompt and capable in carrying out affairs, subtle in intellect, cautious and prudent in counsel, eloquent in speech, outstanding in action, mature in life and conduct. He would gladly have restored the fallen observance of regular discipline to the former state of its integrity; but, with the monks, long since accustomed to laxity, opposing him, and the malice of the times resisting, he could not bring the holy purpose of reform to its due [unclear]. He bore their vexations [unclear]; and since he was a prudent man, he easily surpassed them all in counsel.

*1293.*

In the course of his first year, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1294, after the Apostolic See had been vacant for two years and almost four months, by divine ordinance at last, as was believed, Celestine V was elected pope: a hermit, professed under the rule of our holy father Benedict, from whom the order of Celestines took its beginning; a man of most holy conduct, called Peter before his pontificate. He presided for only five months and renounced the papacy.
