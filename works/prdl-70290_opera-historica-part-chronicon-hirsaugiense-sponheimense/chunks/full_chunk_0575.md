On the same day, after the departure of the Palatine garrison, certain petty robbers of the adversaries’ party assembled, entered armed into the monastery of Limburg, now empty of people, stripped everything, plundering it, and carried off to their own places whatever they could find. Finally, when all its goods had been carried away, they set fire to the monastery, burned it, and utterly destroyed it. In that fire all the monks’ dwellings, and also the houses, barns, stables, and all the buildings, together with the notable church, were entirely consumed and reduced to ash, so that apart from stones absolutely nothing remained. This fire lasted for twelve continuous days, the ministers of impiety returning daily and kindling the fire anew, lest anything remain intact.

The church of the aforesaid monastery was great and very beautiful, built sumptuously in honor of the Mother of God, the inviolate Virgin Mary, and of the Holy Cross, by Conrad II, emperor, in the year of the Lord 1030. Its two inner parts were supported by twenty columns of solid stone, each of which was twenty feet in length and two geometrical cubits, that is, at least twelve feet, in thickness. The length of the church itself, from the outer western door to the high altar, contained 450 feet; its breadth, 140. In it there were twenty altars, six bells, and very many altar ornaments, panel-works, and images on the altar, all of which, as we have said, were either first carried off by the enemies of the cross of Christ or consumed by fire.

The count palatine, protector of the monastery, had in time made the abbot more cautious, so that whatever he wished to be safe he should have carried to the cities. Being so advised, he ordered the ornaments of the church, the sacred vessels, the books, and whatever could be transported to be brought to the city of Speyer, where at that time he lay ill. The monks and all the donati, through fear of the enemies, had previously betaken themselves to safer places. For because of the cruel threat of the enemies, who were staying nearby in the castle of Duroburg, no one remained in the monastery except one old man, wholly unfit for flight. When he saw the monastery on fire, rising from the place in which he was hiding, he entered the church crawling as best he could; hiding himself in the [unclear], he was burned. So violent was that burning of the church that it consumed not only all the timber, but by the violence of its heat broke almost all the stone columns, solid, large, and very strong, of which we have spoken. Nothing in that whole monastery remained sound and intact except the single tower of the church. Indeed, as we afterwards saw the place restored, it would more easily have seemed possible to build a new monastery from the foundations in a known place than to restore that one again after the fire.

This savage and bloody war of almost the whole empire against the aforesaid Philip, count palatine, lasted about five months. On its account many, almost infinite evils were committed against God and men: many mortals were killed, others were driven into the utmost poverty; monasteries were either burned or plundered, churches burned or overturned, villages utterly laid waste by fire. The monastery called Waldsassen in Bavaria, not far from the town of Eger, situated at the entrance to Bohemia, of the Cistercian order, was burned in the same year and war by certain satellites of Frederick, margrave of Brandenburg, in a similar fury of the impious; but not long afterward two principal authors of the burning paid the penalty, dying shamefully.

In the year noted above, around the feast of St. Michael the Archangel, the renowned King Maximilian, after he saw that the palatine had been afflicted harshly enough by his rivals, was moved by mercy and applied the hand of piety. Forbidding to all the power of making war, he established peace for the humbled prince and those set in motion [unclear]; by edict he appointed a day for pleading at Cologne, on the first day of July in the following year, to which he summoned all.

In the year prescribed above, on the vigil of St. Andrew the Apostle, Johannes Trithemius of Sponheim returned to the monastery, after he had necessarily absented himself from the monastery for five months and twenty days, withdrawing because of fear of the enemies, nor did he judge it safe to return sooner, since the peace was not yet sufficiently established. On account of this war our monastery [suffered] very heavy [unclear]. For our estate in Bosenheim perished utterly by fire; our rents and revenues, especially of provisions, were for the greater part either consumed by the enemies or, because of the malice of the times and of men, did not come at all to our use. For all the goods of the household property by which we should have lived perished badly.
