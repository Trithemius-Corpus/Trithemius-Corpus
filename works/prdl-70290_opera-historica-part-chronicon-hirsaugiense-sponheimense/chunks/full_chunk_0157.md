Many bishops were present there: St Otto, bishop of Bamberg; Adalbert, archbishop of Mainz; Buggo of Worms; Sigfrid of Speyer; Embricho of Würzburg; the bishops of Augsburg, Eichstätt, Hildesheim, and many others. To this council also Wolmar, abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, summoned by the archbishop of Mainz, came together with many other abbots.

In the same year died Frederick, archbishop of Cologne, founder of the monastery of nuns of our order on the island of Roland, a man magnificent in every respect; by his generosity and authority that most reverend abbot of Deutz, the great Rupert, composed almost innumerable works. He died in his castle, which he himself had newly built on the highest summit of a mountain, and for that reason called Wolenberg, and was buried in the monastery of Siegburg of our order, in the chapter-place. In the archbishopric he was succeeded by Bruno, dean of St Gereon, provost, son of Count Adolf of Althene, ordained by the judgment of Emperor Lothar, the princes, and three cardinals or legates of the Apostolic See then present at Cologne, against the will of many.

In the same year also, the tenth of Abbot Wolmar, namely the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1131, King Lothar set an expedition in motion against the Danes and Slavs, and subdued them. Eckebert, bishop of Münster, formerly dean of the greater church of Cologne, died at Cologne at the time when the aforesaid election of Archbishop Bruno was being held, and, carried back to his own see, was buried. Werner succeeded him.

About these times, a little before these years, namely in the year of the Lord 1100 or thereabouts, that noble abbey of our order, Reichenbach in Bavaria, was founded by the count of Vohburg, who enriched it with many estates, revenues, and ornaments, and raised it up splendidly.

In the year of the Lord 1106 also, a certain rich and powerful count of Montbéliard began to found an abbey of our order in honor of St Walpurga the virgin, situated in the diocese of Strasbourg, not far from Haguenau; he endowed it with many goods for the support of the monks serving God.

The same count of Montbéliard also, in the same wood near the village called Bibelis, at a cannon-shot from the aforesaid monastery of St Walpurga, founded another monastery of nuns of our order. In it he appointed as first abbess his daughter, named Gunthera, a holy virgin. Renowned for miracles, she was at last buried in the middle of its dark church. In these times also Count Otto of Scheyern in Bavaria, of the stock of Emperor Charles the Great, from whom all the dukes of Bavaria who now exist have descended, converted his castle Scheyern, almost collapsed from excessive age, into a monastery and abbey of our order in honor of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin. Cooperating in this were Count Conrad, Count Otto of [Tochauie?], the other Otto, count palatine in Wittelsbach, and Count Otto, with seven other counts, all of whom bore the names and arms of the aforesaid castle of Scheyern, although at that time [unclear] lived there.

All these, with Agnes, wife of the aforesaid count, with the greatest devotion offered forever to the Blessed Virgin every right which they had in that castle, building a distinguished monastery in which they appointed burial for themselves and their posterity. But there was one among them, Count Arnold of Scheyern, [unclear] to offer to the Lord God; and when he perceived that one man could hinder many, he threw his glove into the air and before everyone offered his share to the devil, saying: “You lords of the devil, take my share among yourselves!” And immediately invisible demons visibly carried off the glove, which never afterwards appeared anywhere.

*1132.*

In the twelfth year also of Abbot Wolmar, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1132, died the venerable [Drutwin?] of Hirsau, of whom we made mention above under lord Abbot Bruno, in the year of the Lord 1119. He presided for thirteen years, a man magnificent in every respect. In the governance of the abbacy of the aforesaid monastery of Breitenau, Henry succeeded this Drutwin: a monk of this monastery of Hirsau, in all his time most learned in divine Scriptures, who wrote many and varied works, both in verse and in prose.
