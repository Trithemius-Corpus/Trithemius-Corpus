In the same year Hermann, abbot in Bleidenstadt of our order, with the consent of his convent, handed over to the canons of the greater church of Mainz, for the increase of their prebends, the parish church in Kemde, with all its revenues and appurtenances, under the witness of letters secured with his seal and that of the aforesaid convent. I think it superfluous to insert them here, because they were written in the same form as the letter of the abbot of Saint Disibod concerning the donation of the church in Osternach, which we placed above. Henry also, abbot of Fulda, with the consent of his convent, in the same year conferred on the church of Mainz, for the increase of the prebends, the church in Berstatt, with all its rents, revenues, and appurtenances, as is clear in the letters written on the matter, dated at Fulda on the Nones of June, that is, on the vigil of Saint Boniface, in the year 1255.

Starkardus also, abbot of Seligenstadt of our order, together with his convent, handed over to the same canons of Mainz the parish church in Nuhem, with all its revenues and profits, reserving to himself and his successors forever the right of conferring one vicarage, to be established from these revenues in the greater church of Mainz. Done on the sixth day before the Kalends of May, in the year of the Lord 1255.

Gerard, bishop of Mainz, together with the canons of his chapter, greatly troubling our abbot John and the convent of this monastery, pressed them with pleas and threats for the church in Gentzingen, which they desired by every means for the increase of their prebends. But Abbot John would not consent, and with steadfast mind despised both their threats and their pleas. But after his death, at last Abbot Peter gave the church itself to them when they asked for it, as we shall say below in its place.

In the same year William, when he was preparing to go to Rome for the crown of the empire, and had first wished to subdue the rebellious Frisians, led an army against them on the fifth day before the Kalends of January. Going ahead of the army over the ice, to reconnoiter an entrance and passable road against his adversaries, he was sitting on his horse; behold, the ice broke, and in a marshy ford the horse itself was gradually submerged with the king. The king, having none of his servants with him, was almost suffocated. Seeing this, the Frisians who were hiding in the reeds ran together and, not knowing that he was the king, killed him before the army arrived.

*1257.*

In this year, which was the fifth of our Abbot John, the princes of the empire assembled to establish a new king, lest, if they delayed longer, they should lose the right of election. But being at variance among themselves, Conrad of Cologne, Gerard bishop of Mainz, and Louis count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria elected Richard, count of Cornwall, brother of the king of England, as king of the Romans. But Arnold, archbishop of Trier, with those adhering to him, elected Alfonso king of Castile as king of the Romans and sent solemn envoys to him, promising counsel and aid. But King Alfonso [unclear], promising that he would follow with an army; but, content with his own kingdom, because he was a philosopher and a very great astronomer, he did not come, nor did he involve himself in any respect in the kingdom of the Romans, except for the title alone, which, apart from the honor of those who had elected him, he retained for a time in his letters. Further, Richard, the aforesaid count of Cornwall, summoned from England by Conrad archbishop of Cologne, assumed the title and government of the empire of the Romans, and, with great rejoicing of his men, was consecrated king by the same archbishop in the church of the blessed Mary ever virgin at Aachen. Afterwards, by the favor and votes of his princes, he was received as true king of the Romans by the people of Cologne, Boppard, Wesel, Mainz, Frankfurt, and many other cities. Going on farther, he received all into his dominion and spent much money to obtain obedience. And when he had come as far as Basel, and the money too lavishly spent was now failing, the princes, withdrawing from him, left him alone and abandoned, tacitly making clear that they had followed plunder, not the man. Therefore, seeing himself deprived of the princes’ aid and unable to make any further progress, he entrusted the kingdom of the Wetterau to Philip of Falkenstein, whose sister he had taken as wife because of her great beauty; Alsace to Werner, bishop of Strasbourg; and Boppard and Wesel with their appurtenances to Philip of Hochenfels.
