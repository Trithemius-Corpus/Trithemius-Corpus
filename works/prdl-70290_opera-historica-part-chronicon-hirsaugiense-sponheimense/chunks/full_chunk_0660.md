But we have resolved to dissemble everything, because we have no thought at all of returning to Sponheim. We shall not easily change our plan in this matter, unless perhaps, God forbid, we are compelled by the authority of our superiors. What hope of safety, what confidence of peace could there be for us with such men, who, forgetful of benefits, neither fear God nor reverence men, nor even consider the account of their own salvation? These things for now, most beloved brother, until the day comes for carrying out the plan. Farewell, mindful of us before God in your prayers. From Budori, on the fourteenth day of the month of August, in the year of Christians 1506.

*LXVIII.*

*To the reverend fathers in Christ, the presidents, definitors, and the other abbots celebrating the annual council of the Order of St. Benedict at Mainz, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, reverence and honor.*

I had determined, most beloved fathers, to come in person to the sacred convent of the order which you are now celebrating, on account of causes and business of no small importance that concern me, as you will understand partly from this letter of mine, partly from its bearer, Johannes Damius, my professed monk. But fortune, raging too fiercely against me, prevents it. For that most wicked tyrant, the count of Leiningen, who last year utterly destroyed the monastery of Limburg by fire, has occupied with robbers all the roads leading to Mainz, so that he may capture either me or the abbot of the said monastery, as we have been informed by many, both secretly and openly. For he lays snares for the aforesaid abbot because he accused him before the royal majesty and the princes concerning the plundering and burning of the monastery. He persecutes me for the same reason, because not only at Cologne but also in other places I always favored and advised the abbot's just cause, and was often present at many discussions against the tyrant. Offended by this, he pursues us both with grave hatred, and if we fall into his hands, we could not escape without danger.

Recently too, when I had returned from the March of Brandenburg with my whole household, which was then with me, the retainers of the same tyrant took me while I was staying in the monastery of Seebach, which has been committed to me, seized lodgings in the house, and led me to his neighboring castle, the tyrant himself being then absent. When he returned the next day and learned what had been done, he severely rebuked his men because they had let me go, and now he plots against me with even greater madness.

As for my affairs, I believe none of you is unaware how severely all of us who are placed under the protection of the most serene prince palatine were afflicted last year by the war and cruelty of the [Pinaleones?] Hessians, who, running through everything around us, plundered it, consuming churches along with villages by burning. From fear of them, since they did not even spare religion, I withdrew into the nearest town, and remained there until that whole disturbance had subsided. At length, returning to the monastery, I discovered that many things had been wrongly done in my absence, and that the morals of certain brothers had terribly degenerated from regular observance, and that they had committed such things as did not seem to be passed over entirely in silence, but rather deserved to be corrected according to the form of regular discipline.

But the authors of these crimes, fearing the punishment which they knew they had deserved, began by hidden machinations to defame and carry about on their tongues certain members of our household, about whom they suspected that I knew all the things that had been done from their report, first to the officials of Duke Johann, and then to certain others, laying upon them I know not what insult. Meanwhile it happened that I was summoned to Heidelberg in the case of the despoiled monastery of Limburg. When I had been there for several days, the officials of the aforementioned duke, by whose command I do not know, seized certain members of our household and put them in chains, at the insistence, as report has it, of the abbot of Mainz, who preferred to give credence to one fugitive monk rather than to me and my whole convent of Sponheim.

When news of this deed was brought to me, I seemed to myself not a little offended, and indeed not undeservedly, especially because, while I was absent and without first being warned, they presumed such things contrary to the order of law. For I feared what did happen: that the common crowd, otherwise too ready to believe evil, might suspect something unfavorable about me, as though those arrested were being detained on my account. Soon many things were being said by the people in varying opinions, and so great a rumor arose round about that I resolved not to return to the monastery before the rashness of those doing the injury should be punished with fitting satisfaction. I referred the whole affair to the prince palatine, with whose counsel and assent.
