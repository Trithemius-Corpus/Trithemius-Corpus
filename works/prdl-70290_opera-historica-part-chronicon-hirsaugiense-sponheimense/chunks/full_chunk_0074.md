For in the disposition of his body, no less than of his mind, he was venerable with a certain majesty of dignity: of middling stature, so that he was considered neither very short nor excessively tall; full and rounded in the face, strong and robust, and yet not notably fat; with black hair sprinkled here and there with gray, and not stripped by any baldness. When he had taken up the name and dignity of pastoral care, he omitted either nothing or very little of his former holy discipline, but, devoting himself day and night to prayers, readings of the Scriptures, holy meditations, and contemplation of inward things, he did not readily admit the distractions of secular business. He also did not love wandering excursions away from the monastery; afflicting his body continually with vigils and fasts for the love of God, he strove to offer himself to the Lord as a living and spotless victim. Toward the poor he was very merciful, and, as far as he could, for the love of God [unclear]; for if he found a poor man even somewhat hungry, immediately and without delay, as far as he could, he relieved that man’s need both in clothing and in food. For, according to his name, he was in all things peaceable, gentle, sweet, and quiet, full of the charity of God and brotherly love, serious in [unclear], sweet in speech, and adorned with a beautiful gravity of conduct. Indeed he had learned so to humble himself before God that even in his outward manner of life a certain brightness of spiritual grace shone forth, openly showing to all the inward humility of his heart. For among the other scribes, an office which among the old monks was very commonly in use, he was not ashamed to take his place with the brethren, where, in turns, when free from prayer, he might usefully occupy himself. In his dress he differed in nothing from the monks subject to him, who at that time, content in accordance with the rule with every usefulness and utmost plainness, wore cowls underneath and cloaks above. Thus, carefully preserving his life in this humility and innocence, Abbot Frederick nevertheless could not escape the rashness of the wicked, which from the beginning of the world has always been hostile to good men; but he lay exposed even to the outrages of evil men to such a degree that at last, as we shall say later, he was most unjustly deposed from the office of his dignity.

1066. In the first year of Abbot Frederick, Sigfrid, archbishop of Mainz, returned from the lands across the sea; and of the seven thousand whom he had led away with him, he brought back scarcely two thousand. In the same year the kingdom of England was miserably afflicted by William, bastard of the Normans, who killed its king Harold and reigned in his place. He soon sent almost all the bishops of that same kingdom into exile; the nobles and princes were killed; men of middling rank he handed over into servitude to his soldiers; he joined the wives of the natives in marriage to his newcomers; and in the city of York he cruelly killed more than one hundred thousand priests. Also at the Easter feasts in this same year a comet appeared in the sky for about fourteen nights.
