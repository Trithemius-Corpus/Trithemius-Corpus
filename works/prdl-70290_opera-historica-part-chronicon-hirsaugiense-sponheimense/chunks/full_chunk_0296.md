*Chronicle of Johannes Trithemius*

229

...was raised to a [duchy?], although among the common people, and among many even today through ignorance, the name of county remains. The same Charles, having summoned an assembly of the princes for the advantage of the commonwealth, caused the so-called Golden Bull, so called because it is strengthened with a golden seal, concerning the election of the Roman emperors, to be written and confirmed with the consent of all the electors and of the realm. When these matters had been duly completed and the assembly dissolved, he passed with his wife from city to city, from monastery to monastery, everywhere most diligently seeking out the relics of saints; and from those he found he carried many into Bohemia, by entreaties which no one could refuse, for the glory and adornment of the churches of his nation.

*1357.*

In the fourth year of Abbot Wighard, and the year of the Lord 1357, Wilhelm, Count Palatine of the Rhine and Duke of Bavaria, Count of Holland, Hainaut, and Zeeland, and lord of Frisia, son of Louis, formerly the fourth emperor, about whom we have said much above in its proper places, after he had governed those lands for almost twelve years, not without tyranny, having taken poison, by the contrivance of I do not know whom, as was said, became mad. Committed to custody, he remained thus for twenty years until his death; he died mad in prison in the year from the birth of the Lord 1377, as we shall say below. And because he had no sons, Albert his brother, taking up after him the counties and lordships of Holland, Hainaut, and Frisia, governed them vigorously for forty-seven years; and he was twenty-first in the order of succession of the counts of Holland. He had as wife Margaret, daughter of the Duke of Brieg, who bore him two sons: Wilhelm, who succeeded his father in the aforesaid counties, and John, bishop of Liège, of whom mention will be made in their proper places; and one daughter named Catherine. She was first betrothed to Edward, Duke of Guelders, who was the brother of Duke Reinhard the captive. But before the wedding was celebrated, he was killed in a certain war, in the year of the Lord 1371, as we shall say there. When he had died, his betrothed, still a virgin and now a widow, thereafter agreed to be joined in marriage to no one unless he was of the blood of the dukes of Guelders. Therefore at length she married Wilhelm, son of the Duke of Jülich, by Sophia, daughter of the illustrious man Florentius of Mechlin, first wife of Reinhard, first Duke of Guelders, father of Edward her former betrothed, about whom we spoke above.

*1358.*

In the [fifth?] year of Abbot Wighard, which was the year of the Lord 1358, Albert, Duke of Austria, son of Albert formerly King of the Romans, died, leaving after him Leopold his son, duke, from whom the genealogy of Maximilian our king, who rules today, is continued, as will appear in order in what follows; also Albert, Duke of Austria, who founded the University of Vienna in Austria; also Rudolf, duke, who founded the provostship of St. Stephen the protomartyr in Vienna and joined the county of Tyrol to the duchy of Austria, and who had as wife Catherine, daughter of Emperor Charles IV; Frederick, duke, who died without children. He also left two daughters: Margaret, Margravine of Brandenburg, and Catherine, abbess of St. Clare in Vienna.

In the same year there was a great pestilence from the beginning of August until the Nativity of the Lord throughout almost all Germany; many thousands of people perished from this disease in a very short time.

Also in the year noted above, very serious wars arose between Louis, King of Hungary, and the Venetians over the kingdom of Dalmatia, which the Venetians had possessed for many years while the kings of Hungary protested. And so King Louis, having gathered a great army both of his own men and of Germans, entered Dalmatia in strength. First he obtained the city of Zadar, then the maritime cities, and finally all Dalmatia. Thereafter he also entered the borders of the Venetians, and after some of their fortifications had been captured, he struck great terror into them. Hence, compelled by necessity, they sought peace. The king not only granted it to them, but also restored their fortifications, which he had taken by right of war, except for the kingdom of Dalmatia itself, after receiving an oath from them that they would in no way harm or punish anyone in those lands which he had restored to them because of loyalty previously shown to the king; nevertheless they by no means kept this.

*1359.*

In the sixth year of Abbot Wighard, and the year of the Lord 1359, Edward, King of England, released John, King of France, and his son Philip, whom he had captured in battle the previous year, under certain conditions. When these were not observed, in the following year, with more than one hundred and fifty ships armed, he led a huge army into Gaul and, besieging Paris, afflicted the whole province with great slaughter, devastating everything with fire and sword.
