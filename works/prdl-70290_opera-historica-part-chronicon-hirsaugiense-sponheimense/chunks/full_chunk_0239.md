*On Volpold, Twenty-First Abbot of the Monastery, Who Presided Twenty Years and as Many Days; on the Various Events of That Time.*

When Ernest, the venerable abbot of this monastery, had died, as we have said, and had been buried with the reverence of his sons, as is customary, in the year and on the day prescribed above, the brothers, bereft of a shepherd, came together on the first suitable day following in the chapter place and began to take counsel concerning the election of a new abbot. Therefore, after the customary prayers had first been said, at length, confirmed by Conrad, bishop of Speyer, and, as is customary, ordained with great solemnity in the greater church of the said city of Speyer, he presided over this monastery of Hirsau for twenty years and as many days. He was a man of middling stature, inclining more to corpulence than to slenderness of body. He was a Swabian by nation, born of honorable parents, and, according to the custom of that time, had been adequately instructed in the Scriptures for necessity. Having become a monk, he lived quietly and peacefully among the brothers at all times, walking without reproach. He is not found to have alienated anything. Moreover, the fervor of monastic life at that time had grown not a little lukewarm in this monastery, as in almost all others; he could not easily revive it, nor did he labor greatly on that account.

In the course of his first year, the year prescribed above, which was, as we have said, 1245 from the Lord’s nativity, in the month of November, Conrad of Eberstein, the forty-sixth bishop of Speyer, died. Henry, son of the count of Leiningen, chancellor of the imperial court, succeeded him and presided for twenty-seven years. This Henry, elected but not yet confirmed, besieged and took by armed force the castle called Starkenberg, which pertains to the principality of the monastery of Lorsch, after driving out from it the guardians of the church of Mainz. From this a great dissension arose between him and Siegfried, archbishop of Mainz. At length Pope Innocent, at the instance of the aforesaid archbishop, entrusted the case to certain judges for determination and restored peace.

In the same year, on the vigil of Saint Andrew the Apostle, at night, Otto, the first of the dukes of Bavaria to be count palatine of the Rhine, died. At the beginning of his rule he was a pious, just, and most merciful prince. Afterwards, when he sided with the excommunicated and deposed Emperor Frederick, as we have said, he himself was excommunicated by the pope, and he began to persecute the clergy out of hatred for the pope; this did not turn out well for him. For on the aforesaid vigil of Saint Andrew, when he was cheerful and well with his wife, sons, and household, he was found dead suddenly in that very night, excommunicate. He left two sons, Louis and Henry, who governed his principality for ten years in fraternal community. But after ten years, that is, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1255, they divided the principality between themselves. Henry received Landshut with Lower Bavaria as his legitimate portion.
