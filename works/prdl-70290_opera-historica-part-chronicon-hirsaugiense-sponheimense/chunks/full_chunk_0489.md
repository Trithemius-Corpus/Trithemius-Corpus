O how many thereafter, caring nothing for what had been done, were perjurers. The aforesaid legate Nicholas of Cusa made great effort for the reform of our order, and made no small progress. First, after the chapter, he issued a constitution on the manner and form of reform and visitation, in which he treated rather fully of the integrity of monastic discipline and conduct. He also was the first to confirm the observance and union of the reformed Fathers under the designation of the Bursfelders, which at that time was new; nevertheless it was afterward confirmed anew by Pope Pius II, namely in the thirteenth year of Conrad our abbot.

In the same year King Frederick, accompanied by Ladislaus, king of Hungary and Bohemia, by Albert, brother of the Duke of Austria, and by a great host of nobles, entered Italy with his bride Eleanor, daughter of Edward, king of Portugal; and on the sixteenth day of the month of March he received, from the hand of a cardinal appointed for this by the pope, the iron crown, which he had not been able to receive at Milan because of the duke’s obstinacy. Then on the twentieth day of the same month of March, which was Laetare Sunday, in the middle of Lent, Pope Nicholas V solemnly adorned the king with the golden crown in the church of St. Peter, with great honor. Afterward, departing from the City, he turned his route toward Naples, where, after celebrating his wedding with his betrothed and greeting Alfonso, king of Aragon, he returned to his own lands.

In this same year also, as we have said, Pope Nicholas V sent into Germany as legate, with the indulgences of the Jubilee and with great authority, Nicholas of Cusa, a Mosellan by homeland, cardinal priest of the holy Roman Church of the title of St. Peter in Chains, bishop of Brixen, a man most learned in every respect and an outstanding zealot for the Christian faith, to restore peace among the princes of Germany and to stir up the hearts of the faithful for the relief of the Greeks, who were then being attacked by Mehmed, son of Murad, prince of the Turks. The apostolic legate therefore came to Trier, and then to other cities of Germany, and both in his own person and through many other learned men he caused many public sermons to be delivered, exhorting the faithful to the support of Christian fidelity.

In this year, which was the eighth of Conrad our abbot, Frederick, count palatine of the Rhine and duke of Bavaria, brother of Louis [the pious?], of whom we spoke above, on the Ides of January, that is, on the octave of the Epiphany of the Lord, by the consent of all the nobles and counts of the Palatinate, and also by apostolic authority and confirmation, was assumed as guardian and governor, and as father, of the child Philip, his brother’s son, with the title and prerogative of the princely election of the Roman Empire, and at the same time of the Palatinate of the Rhine, so that he was held by all to be, and was, prince-elector of the Empire. The establishment of this prerogative displeased above all Theoderic, archbishop of Mainz, and also Duke Stephen of Zweibrücken, James, margrave of Brandenburg, and his son, and also the counts of Lützelstein, who, conspiring together, devised many things against Duke Frederick. But after many injuries had been done to him by the aforesaid counts of Lützelstein, who were then very powerful, Duke Frederick could no longer dissemble; in the year already noted, with a strong army of his men, he surrounded Lützelstein in a very firm siege for two months and seven days, and at last, on the vigil of St. Martin the bishop, he took the castle together with the town by force. The counts, however, saving themselves by flight, lost everything they had; and so the whole county of Lützelstein has been subject to the Palatinate down to the present day. At that time Schaffhard, count of Lingen, Duke Frederick’s intimate and counselor, was being held captive by the aforesaid counts in the fortress of Lützelstein; when Duke Frederick had obtained the victory, he freed him with great joy.
