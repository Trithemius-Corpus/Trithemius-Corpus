But it is most certain to us that the Palatine had wanted nothing less than either to besiege Bingen or to seize the monastery in advance. For if he had resolved openly to attack the hill, the lords of the Rheingau would not have been able to resist. And so they lay for a long time in the church of the aforesaid convent, in the fortifications which they had made, with the nuns shut up in the inner parts, until at last they realized that they had been deceived, and had uselessly spent much without cause. This rashness of the people of the Rheingau, however, harmed the monastery of Saint Rupert not a little in its destroyed buildings, and gave certain persons through them an occasion for no small sum of money [unclear].

In the same year also Johannes Trithemius, abbot, built a new abbot’s residence, partly from the old one and partly from the foundations, sufficiently costly and adorned, as it is seen today, on the eastern side of the monastery, where once the entrance gate had been. In it he made two chambers for the abbot, a third for the master chaplain, one heated room, two lower closets, and a chapel with an altar, of whose consecration we shall speak in the following year. How necessary the construction of this abbatial house was, all know who saw the earlier abbatial dwelling. For the dwelling was too narrow, unsuitable, and to be feared because of the danger of fire, although because of the little house, bare and empty of all things, there was nothing in it. For on the day on which Trithemius entered the abbey, apart from one loaf, a biretta, and a bed with its necessary furnishings, nothing was found in the abbatial house. For Johannes Kolnhauen, abbot, a lover of simplicity, had always been accustomed here at Seligenstadt to build rooms that were excessively narrow and mean.

When he resigned, there were here in the abbey no cupboards, no chests, no tables, no closed or secure receptacles of any kind in which anything could be stored. Trithemius therefore, compelled by necessity, built the abbot’s residence with work costly for his poverty; he made cupboards, arranged chambers, added locks, set up the necessary small chests, and procured many other things, which we see today, at no small expense.

In the year noted above, Johannes Trithemius, abbot, at the request of Rumold of Laupach, prior of the Carmelites of Frankfurt, a very good man and a most upright guardian of regular observance, wrote the book, today very widely circulated, On the Praises of Saint Anne, mother of the most blessed God-bearer, the Virgin Mary, which is held by many in great esteem. In its seventh chapter he makes mention of the most pure conception of the God-bearer. On this occasion a certain man of the Order of Preachers arose, named Wigand Wirt, preacher of the convent of Frankfurt, a man indeed quite learned, but excessively rash and proud. He wished to attack the work as far as it concerns the purity of the conception, and sent letters against Trithemius, the writer’s name concealed under the designation of Brother Pensans’s hand, to him by an unknown messenger, as though Trithemius had written badly about the conception, and reproved him rather timidly.
