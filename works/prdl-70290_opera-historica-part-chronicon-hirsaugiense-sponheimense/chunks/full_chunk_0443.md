*Concerning Bernhard, eighteenth abbot of this monastery of Sponheim, who presided for 41 years, 3 months, and 9 days; and concerning his various deeds and the events of that time.*

When Philip the Second, seventeenth abbot of this monastery, had died and been buried according to the custom of ecclesiastical tradition, the brothers, having the voice of election for choosing a new pastor over themselves, came together to the chapter place; and, after the grace of the Holy Spirit had been invoked, as is customary, they resolved that the greatest care must be taken not to elect an abbot like the deceased, who would consume the remaining goods of the monastery in empty pomp, but rather to promote a useful and provident man, fearing God and having zeal for the common good. Therefore, after mature deliberation had first been held, they at last unanimously elected as abbot and pastor of this monastery Bernhard of Sponheim, a professed monk of this community, then in the thirty-first year of his age. Confirmed by Conrad, archbishop of Mainz, and blessed by his vicar in pontificals, as is customary, he presided over this monastery for 41 years, 3 months, and 9 days: a steadfast and energetic man, and sufficiently keen and fit for the management of affairs.

He was, moreover, from Sponheim, born of knightly stock. In his boyhood he devoted himself for some time to learning letters; and when he had already begun to show understanding in them, by the will and persuasion of his parents he became a monk, and turned out better than those of his own age. Once ordained abbot, he labored with all the zeal he could so that the goods and possessions unjustly alienated by his predecessors might be restored to the ownership of the monastery. Part he restored; part he was forced to leave in another’s possession, because of the poverty of temporal goods and the wickedness of the times. Finally, he often undertook to restore the observance of regular discipline, which had utterly collapsed in this monastery, to the integrity of monastic conduct, but, with the rashness of wicked men standing in the way, he made little progress. Yet he did bring about this: that the monks, so long as he lived, were compelled to eat together in one refectory and to sleep together in one dormitory; nevertheless, immediately after his death this was again laid aside. However, he did not take away the private property of the monks, the root of all irreligion, nor did he restore private possessions to common use. Nonetheless, because of his zeal for rectitude, he often endured many insolences from his monks, not without loss to the monastery and danger to souls.

In the fifth month of his rule, Philippus and Eberhardus Meysvin, squires of Sponheim, resigned forever to the abbot and convent their right, which they had with the abbot, to the altar constructed in our monastery in honor of Saints Mary Magdalene, Lawrence the martyr, and Margaret, virgin and martyr, so that the same altar should henceforth be served by a monk of this community and not by another, as is evident in the letters of this resignation, which the aforesaid squires handed over to the abbot in the year of the Lord 1391, on the vigil of Lawrence.

*1392.*

In this year, the first of our abbot Bernhard, the University of Erfurt in Thuringia was founded, and was confirmed by Boniface IX, privileges and liberties being granted after the manner of other universities.

In the same year Pope Boniface IX, at the request of Stephen, Frederick, and John, brothers and dukes of Bavaria, granted a jubilee year to the city of Munich, in the diocese of Freising, in Bavaria, from Laetare Sunday until the feast of St. James the Apostle.

Also in the same year, on the Tuesday before the feast of St. Catherine, virgin and martyr, a division was made among the aforesaid three brothers, sons of Duke Stephen, son of Louis IV, emperor, of Bavaria. In this division John received Munich in Upper Bavaria, with its appurtenances, as his lawful portion; Stephen likewise obtained, in Upper [Bavaria?], Baiolzhuett and Rattenberg, with the [50?] things belonging to it; Frederick, however, received Landshut in Lower Bavaria, with its appurtenances, for his portion. Of these, Stephen and Frederick married wives who were daughters of Galeazzo Visconti of Milan, whom Wenceslaus, king of the Romans, after receiving money, created duke. Stephen begot Duke Louis of Ingolstadt, Elizabeth queen of France, and John, bishop of Ratisbon. Frederick begot Henry, duke of Landshut, and two daughters, Magdalena and Elizabeth, of whom the last was taken as wife by Frederick, burgrave of Nuremberg, whom Emperor Sigismund afterwards, at the time of the Council of Constance, made margrave of Brandenburg.
