After Abbot Bernhard had died, the brothers, in accordance with his commission, seeking back those estates, began to mow the hay; but by armed force they were violently driven out by the servants of the men of Coppenstein. And although afterward a complaint was often brought against them by several abbots, as we shall say below, nevertheless they achieved nothing against the violence.

*Concerning Gobelinus, nineteenth abbot of this monastery, who presided for seven years, one month, and fourteen days; concerning various deeds both of his and of that time.*

After the death of the venerable lord Abbot Bernhard, the tribulation which he had foretold to the monks while he was alive came upon them. For as soon as Johannes, count of Sponheim, the last of the line, heard that the abbot was dead, he came in person to the monastery together with the count of Veldenz, and by a strict prohibition forbade the brothers to elect a new abbot without his presence, consent, and will. Perhaps he would not have done this if the death of the deceased abbot had not by chance been reported to his knowledge so suddenly; and it has been handed down to memory that it happened thus. At the very hour when the bells were being rung here in the monastery for the deceased abbot, it happened by chance that the count himself, with his attendants, had begun to continue his journey from Winterburg toward Kreuznach; and during that ringing he met a peasant not far from the monastery. When the count asked him what that ringing of the bells at an unusual hour meant, he replied that the abbot was dead. When the count heard this, immediately turning aside from his journey, he entered the monastery. He made the prohibition which we have mentioned, contrary to justice and the privilege of the monastery, appointing for the brothers the day for the election of a new shepherd at the town of Kreuznach on the third day immediately following, which was the day of the holy martyrs Abdon and Sennen, namely the third day before the Kalends of August.

The count, moreover, had with him a certain monk of the Cistercian order from the monastery of Saint Disibod, named Gobelinus, a shrewd and courtly man, whom he loved greatly; and he was held by him in high esteem, and was first among his secretaries. Therefore, having obtained an opportunity, desiring to honor this monk for his fidelity toward him, he [unclear] to promote him as abbot of this monastery. The brothers, because of necessity and because of their depraved morals and dissoluteness of life, did not dare to oppose him. And so, when the term appointed for the election arrived, the monks of this monastery, compelled by necessity, came to Kreuznach, as had been ordered. When they had been assembled in the count's court, the count of Veldenz himself being present with certain others, the count of Sponheim began in this manner: “Do not suppose, lord brothers, that because I have summoned you to this place for the election of a new abbot, I am either your enemy or an invader of your monastery. For I, as advocate, guardian, and faithful defender of your monastery, especially desire to see your advancement both in spiritual conduct and also in the substantial good of temporal affairs; nor should it be believed that I wish in any way to destroy the monastery which my forefathers built to the honor of God, but rather to advance it in all things toward better things according to my powers. Since, therefore, your monastery of Sponheim has now for many years notably declined in both respects, there is certainly need, if it is to be brought back to its original institution, of a man prudent and experienced in many things, who is willing and able to preside over you usefully. I have such a man with me, known to you as prudent and experienced, lord Gobelinus, whom I advise and ask you to elect as your abbot; for I know that through his wisdom and industry the condition of your monastery will be brought back to a better state.”

When these things had been heard, the brothers were suddenly exceedingly dismayed and were wholly ignorant what they should do. But after much counsel at last, and after many prayers to the contrary had been offered to the count, since they were making no progress at all, they yielded to the advice of the count of Veldenz and of others who knew that the mind of our count was not to be bent from what he had begun. Lest something worse should happen to them, as could be inferred by a likely conjecture from his threats, consenting under injustice, they elected this Gobelinus, in such manner as they could, as their abbot.
