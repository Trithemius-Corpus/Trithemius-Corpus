In the aforementioned year of the Lord’s incarnation, 1462, Charles, margrave of Baden, George, bishop of Metz, his brother, John Nix, bishop of Speyer, and Ulrich, count of Württemberg, gathered together and assembled an army; advancing against Frederick, count palatine, whom they believed to be far absent, they promised themselves all his land as plunder, together with the town of his residence, Heidelberg.

First, therefore, they surrounded the town of Heidelsheim, not far from Bruchsal, with a siege. When this was reported to the Palatine, he personally and secretly entered Heidelsheim with his men; and while the enemies pressed the walls fiercely throughout the day, he himself stoutly defended the town. On the next day, seeing that they could not prevail, they raised the siege and withdrew, moving the army toward the district of the town of Heidelberg, not knowing that the Palatine was in the land. For they thought, having been deceived by certain letters, as is reported, that at that time he was outside his country.

But when the aforesaid princes came by night to the land of the Palatine, not far from Bruchsal, they surrounded [unclear] with a siege. When this was reported to the Palatine, he joined battle with three hundred horsemen. The Palatine men, hiding not far from Seckenheim in a wood, allowed the adversaries, suspecting nothing of their presence, to pass, until, shut in between the Rhine and the Neckar, they could no longer flee. Then the Palatine men burst from the wood and boldly made an attack on the adversaries. The fighting was fierce on both sides, but victory soon fell to the Palatine, and with great honor, by God’s aid, he triumphed over his adversaries.

There were captured by the Palatine Charles, margrave of Baden, together with his brother George, bishop of Metz, John Nix, bishop of Speyer, the count of Württemberg, and five hundred counts, nobles, and knights, all of whom were led bound to Heidelberg. The rest, however, who had not fallen, fled. On the Palatine’s side few fell. These things took place in a very broad field not far from Ladenburg, on the last day of June.

But the aforesaid princes, after they had been held in captivity for some time, since they could do nothing else, redeemed themselves with money. George, bishop of Metz, for his liberation gave the Palatine forty thousand florins, and by letters and oath made himself, with all his soldiery and with three castles, Bergzabern, St. Theobald, and Holy Cross, perpetually bound to the Palatine. Charles, margrave of Baden, for his liberation offered the Palatine [unclear] thousand florins; for these, since the money was not ready to hand, he assigned to that same Palatine in pledge, with usufruct, his share of the county of Sponheim belonging to Kreuznach; likewise he assigned into the hands of the Palatine the towns of [Besigheim?] and Bensheim with the castle, toll, and feudal rights, for the same sum of money. Furthermore, he was compelled wholly to renounce the right of redemption which he claimed to have over Heidelsheim and Eppingen, and also the hunts, waters, and fisheries about which there had previously been a dispute near the Rhine between Germersheim and [unclear]. He also made the town of [Porcesse?] a fief, and made himself and his soldiery bound to the Palatinate by oath and the necessary letters.

Ulrich, count of Württemberg, was assessed at one hundred thousand florins, of which forty thousand were paid, and twenty thousand, on account of the Church of Mainz, were assigned by the superscription of Stratenmont, beyond the one hundred thousand for which, as we said above, it had previously been pledged to the Palatine by Diether, archbishop of Mainz. Then, concerning the remaining forty-one thousand, he promised, with letters and securities furnished, that he would pay a pension of two thousand florins each year. Furthermore he made Marbach a fief of the Palatine, and made himself, with all his soldiery, bound to the Palatine. Once these securities had been provided, the aforesaid princes were released.
