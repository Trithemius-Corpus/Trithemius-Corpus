In the first matter, indeed, I do not deny that I both can and wish to give sufficient satisfaction, since the monastic profession itself, indeed the Christian faith, does not permit me to be a liar, as Sacred Scripture attests when it says, “The mouth that lies slays the soul.” And since I am a lover of truth, no necessity compels me to lie. In the second matter, however, I do not promise that I shall be obedient, unless perhaps a tepid will may suffice, which, moved by brotherly reasoning, neither sufficiently wills nor, if it desires, can do much. For I wished to accommodate myself to those lovers of simplicity for whom I am about to write the history, for their benefit; and, because of my rusticity, as all will judge, I easily could.

There are three kinds of histories. One is called general, embracing the solemn deeds of the kings and princes of the whole world or of many provinces. In this kind of writing there were Titus Livius, Orosius, Eusebius the bishop, Cornelius Tacitus, Cassiodorus, Freculphus the bishop, Vincent of Beauvais, Marianus Scotus, monk of Fulda, Martinus the preacher, Antoninus the archbishop, Hernandus the monk, Rupert the monk, Hugh of Fleury the monk, John de Columna, Jacobus Philippus of Bergamo, Marcus Antonius Sabellicus the Venetian, Sebald Schedel of Nuremberg, and many others.

The second kind is special, which describes the deeds of one kingdom or a few kingdoms, provinces, or a people. In this kind Josephus, Hegesippus the Jew, Bede in the deeds of the English, Gregory of Tours the bishop, Windichinus the monk of Corvey, Saxo, Regino abbot of Prum, Otto bishop of Freising, the aforesaid Marcus Antonius Sabellicus in the history of the Venetians, and many others, whose number is uncertain, made use of it.

The third kind of histories is called topical or local, which narrates the history of one city, family, or place. In this kind there were Bede the monk, Sigebert the monk of Gembloux, Strabus of Fulda, and very many others who wrote the histories of their own monasteries. In this very kind of writing also, out of love for the brothers of Hirsau, I have resolved, driven by their exceeding charity, to weave a history. And although I cannot apply to it the polish of eloquence, I nevertheless promise that I shall observe truth before all, so far as it pertains to my conscience.

The materials have been taken from those whose names are these. From the annalistic records and the letters of privilege of the aforesaid monastery of Hirsau I received the names and successions of the abbots, together with their deeds. I have also obtained many things from various authors, histories, legends of saints, and letters. The authors are Windichinus the monk in the history of the Saxons, another who completed that same history, Regino abbot of Prum in the history of the people of Metz, Berno abbot of Reichenau in the history of the Alemanni, Theoderic the monk in the history of the people of Trier, Golscherus the monk in the deeds of the same, Marianus Scotus, monk of Fulda, in an all-embracing German history, Otto bishop of Freising in his history, Andreas monk of St. Emmeram of Regensburg in the deeds of the dukes of Bavaria, Henry of Erfurt in the history of memorable things, Godfrey of Viterbo in his chronicle Pantheon, Gottfried the monk of St. Pantaleon in the history of the people of Cologne, John of Hildesheim, Carme-[unclear], Hugh the monk of Fleury, John the monk of Kastl in the book on the illustrious men of our order, Everwinus the monk of Segenburg in his chronicle, Bernard the monk of Corvey in his history of the Saxons, and Bertrand the monk in the history of the people of Hersfeld. And from many other various histories of the Bavarians, Swabians, Mainz people, Speyer people, and from the deeds of the counts palatine of the Rhine, described by a certain monk of the monastery of Lorsch, I have inserted here and there, scattered throughout, not without great labor, those things that seemed to me to pertain to the plan of the history.

Finally, many things by digression [unclear], cannot lie hidden in the narrow confines of one monastery, but by the zeal of men, not to say curiosity, had at last been drawn out and had come into the hands of very many to be examined. Therefore, so that it may nowhere proceed empty and may become burdensome to no one, I wished, with a certain moderation, to adorn it with the more memorable clauses of events carried out in Germany.
