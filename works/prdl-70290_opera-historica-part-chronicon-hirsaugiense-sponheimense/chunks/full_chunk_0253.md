*The Monastery of Hirsau*

In the year of the Lord's nativity noted above, John, the twenty-sixth abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, afflicted by the severe trouble of fevers, passed from this mortal life to eternal life on 3 June, after ten years, three months, and four days of his rule had been completed. His body was buried in the greater monastery.

*Concerning Voland, the twenty-seventh abbot of this monastery, who presided for 6 years, 8 months, and 14 days, and concerning various deeds.*

After the death of John, the twenty-sixth abbot of this monastery, which occurred, as we have said, on 3 June in the year of the Lord's nativity 1275, on the same day after his burial the brothers, lacking a shepherd, assembled in the chapter place. After many and various discussions held among themselves, they elected as abbot Voland, a certain monk of the same monastery. He was soon confirmed and ordained by Frederick of Bolanden, bishop of Speyer, and presided over this monastery for 6 years, 8 months, and 14 days: a wholly useless man, a dissipater of the monastery's substance, who pursued pleasures and the comforts of the flesh. He had no care for regular observance, and no thought for the governance of public honesty and advancement. By nation he was an Alsatian, born not far from Strasbourg; from the monastery of Gengenbach, under lord Abbot John, I do not know on what occasion he came to Hirsau, and, received by the brothers into the convent, became one of them. But when Abbot John had died, he was put into his place by cunning and artifice, governed badly, and in a few years disposed of many goods of the monastery by pledging them, sold them, and alienated them.

For in the second year of his rule, as we shall say below, he unjustly alienated a very profitable tithe, without the consent of the diocesan bishop and the brothers.

*1276.*

In the first year of Abbot Voland, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 1276, when Odacharus, king of Bohemia, did not keep the agreements which in the previous year he had promised to Rudolf, king of the Romans, King Rudolf himself again entered Bohemia with a great army, overcame Odacharus in war, killed him, and subjected the whole kingdom to his power.

Moreover, the duchy of Austria, together with Styria and Carinthia, belonging to the empire, as we have said above, he conferred upon his first-born son named Albert; and with the consent of the princes he placed him, from the number of the counts, into the college of dukes. Thus the duchy of the house of Austria came by imperial munificence to the counts of Habsburg, and remains with them down to the present day. From this Albert all the dukes of Austria have descended in order down to Maximilian, who reigns today, and his son Philip, duke of Brabant, as we shall say in what follows.

In the year noted above Pope Innocent V died, of the order of the Friars Preachers, and was buried at Rome in the Lateran church. He was succeeded in the pontificate by Othobonus, Genoese by birth, cardinal deacon of St. Adrian, nephew of Pope Innocent, called Adrian V; he presided for one month and ten days, and died at Viterbo before the office of consecration, and was buried there in the convent of the Friars Minor. This pontiff [unclear] King Rudolf by letters, and [unclear] his son Conradin [unclear] had conferred upon himself. In Rome he did everything according to his own will, God avenging the wrong done to them. But Rudolf, entangled in the Bohemian war, could not satisfy the pontiff. When Adrian also had died within forty days, Peter, bishop of Tusculum, a Lisbonese by birth, succeeded to the pontificate and was called John XXI. He presided for eight months; when he at last died at Viterbo, a new chamber having fallen upon him, the Apostolic See was vacant for six months. He was most learned in medicine and philosophy, and wrote many things. Among them there is extant a certain little summa, which they call the *Treasure of the Poor*, a work indeed pleasant, delightful, and not useless in medicine.

*1277.*

In the second year of Abbot Voland, which was the year of the Lord's nativity 1277, this Abbot Voland, excessively given to banquets and pleasures, spending the substance of the monastery prodigally by distributing it to his friends and kinsmen and other useless persons, began, when money failed, to dispose of and alienate the immovable property of the monastery. And first he sold the tithe, with all its revenues in Elmendingen and the half share in Wyl, to the grave and perpetual loss of this monastery; and he spent that money for the uses of his own people, not for the advantage of posterity, as he ought to have done.
