*Sponheim.*

1293.

In this year the city of Worms, burning by accidental fire, was for the most part consumed. In the same year, on the day of St. Othmar the abbot, Simon, bishop of Worms, died and was buried in the monastery of Schönau. After his death Gerhard, son of the Hirsute Count, was elected; he died before his confirmation. After him Eberhard of Strahlenberg was again elected; he presided over the church of Worms scarcely ten months. When he too had died, Emicho of Badenberg, provost of the major church, succeeded in the episcopate, elected unanimously on the day after St. Thomas the Apostle, and he presided for four years and one month. This Emicho, while he was still provost of the church of Worms, founded a chapel in the suburb of the city in honor of the most blessed virgin Mary, mother of God. In it he established twelve prebendary priests, who were to discharge the hours there in honor of God and the Virgin. Afterwards, when he was made bishop, he confirmed what he had previously done, and joined greater benefices to those already granted. From the first foundation of this same chapel, many miracles and signs are reported to have been performed there by the merits of blessed Mary ever virgin, so that down to the present day there has been a great concourse of faithful peoples to that same church; through their alms and offerings it grew, in the course of time, into the form in which it is now seen.

1294.

In the fourth year of our abbot John, Megenhard, prior of this monastery of Sponheim, the fifteenth in the order of priors from the beginning of its foundation, died in the eighteenth year of his priorate and was buried in the cloister of the monastery with his brothers. In the priorate he was succeeded as sixteenth by John, a monk of this convent, originally from Weinsheim, and he presided in it for nearly five years.

In these times a great quarrel arose between Philip, king of France, and Edward, king of England, which proceeded to a most savage war. The aforesaid king of England hired Adolf, king of the Romans, as an ally for one hundred thousand marks of silver; but Adolf, having received the money which he ought to have divided with the princes of the Empire, kept it alone against their will, and provoked them to the greatest indignation, so that none of them agreed to go with him to the aid promised by him to the king of England. Therefore king Adolf, confounded, was never able to come to the king of the English with the princes, as he had promised, nor alone with his own men at the proper time; and it was a very great disgrace both to that king of the Romans Adolf and to the whole Empire to have received so much money and disgracefully withheld the aid promised for it.

1295.

In the fifth year of our abbot, Albert, duke of Austria, son of the late king Rudolf, rose up strongly against Adolf, king of the Romans, and inflicted many losses on him. When Adolf had tried to depose him from his duchy, he could not, because Albert, favored by many princes and helped, defended himself stoutly.

In the same year Siegfried, archbishop of Cologne, after seven years of his captivity, had at last been released for a high price; [unclear] had detained [unclear], cast into prison and captive, until death, and did not wish to release him for any price, but repeatedly reproaching him said: “Now I shall teach you how you ought to hold an archbishop captive.” And when the count promised that he would return everything that he had extorted from him and would add much greater things if he were released, the archbishop answered: “Saint Peter, even without those things which you promise, can [unclear] his servants; therefore I pay no heed to your promises. You will die a captive.” And this was done. In the same year John, duke of Brabant, died in Hastelitz.

1296.

In the sixth year of our abbot, when the church of the holy martyr George in Dalen, formerly burned down and afterwards begun to be rebuilt, stood unfinished, a certain religious matron Hedwig, wife of the late Hermann, knight of Sponheim, forewarned by divine inspiration, completed it at her own expense and costs, and gave to the same monastery in perpetual possession her court in Winckelsheim with fields, house-sites, meadows, and all its revenues.
