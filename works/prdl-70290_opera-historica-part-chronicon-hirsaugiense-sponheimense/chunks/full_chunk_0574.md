*Sponheim.*

While these things were happening in our lands, very grave wars were being waged throughout all Bavaria against Philip, Count Palatine, and his son Duke Rupert. For Maximilian, most powerful King of the Romans, wishing to humble the proud and recall the rebellious to obedience, took with him certain princes, namely Duke Albert of Munich, Frederick also, Margrave of Brandenburg, who was staying in Ansbach, and also the bishop of Augsburg and the citizens of the towns of Nuremberg, Ulm, Augsburg, and other imperial cities, and devastated almost all Bavaria, and took many fortifications of his adversaries. Amid these events the monastery of Waldsassen, of the Cistercian order, not far from Eger near the entrance to Bohemia, was cruelly plundered and burned by the army of Margrave Frederick, and was utterly destroyed. But King Maximilian, having arranged matters in Bavaria for victory, led part of the army into Alsace, and whatever right the Palatine had around Strasbourg he received in surrender without bloodshed: namely Geroldseck, Offenburg, Schottern, Ortenburg, and Gengenbach, with all things which up to that point seemed to belong there to the Palatine. Finally, on this side of the Rhine likewise he received into his power the whole advocacy of the province, Lutzelstein, Haguenau, and Wissembourg, with all castles, villages, and towns belonging to it, down to the present day.

In the year noted above, when the Hessians seemed to be occupied around Alteburg and the Alexandrians around Biliekheim, Philip, Count Palatine, sent an army of his men from Alzey and Heidelberg into Stauronesum on the nineteenth day of the month of July, and they remained there eight days, awaiting their increase in numbers and the engines necessary for war. But on the twenty-first day of the same month, which was the feast of the most holy mother Anne, leaving Stauronesum in the morning, they directed their battle-line toward the lands of Duke Alexander, adjoining the county of Sponheim, and surrounded with a siege a small town of that duke, Cotirodorum, commonly called Dreckodernheim, which was taken on the following Sunday, that is, on the twenty-eighth day of July. Its walls and towers were thrown down to the ground; except for the people, everything was given to the soldiers as plunder. The Palatines also entered the monastery of the holy bishop Disibod, and, after driving off the garrison which Duke Alexander had placed there, devastated everything and left nothing untouched except the library. For even the country multitude carried off the lead from the roofs and windows, and what it could not carry away, it labored with all its strength to destroy; they also carried off as plunder all the wine and grain, and likewise the livestock. But while these things were being done around the monastery of Saint Disibod, which is only one mile distant from Sponheim, Matthias, abbot of the same, was being held captive by a certain tyrant; at last, through the justice of the Count Palatine, he was released and sent away free. After Cotirodorum had been taken, the Palatine army was led through the Alexandrian lands toward Lutern, and on the way they attacked and plundered certain little villages; they also gave some over to fire. On the eighteenth day of the month of August after this, Wilhelm, Count of Hesse, gathered a great army and set up camp in the mountains of the Rhine opposite the city of Bacharach on that side of the river, and surrounded with a very strong siege a small town of the Palatine called Kaub. He used great force in order to take the little town, but, when the Palatine sent aid to those shut inside, all the efforts of those pressing the siege were broken. For in that village there were men appointed for its defense, many and very strong, whose number was increased daily. The army of the Count Palatine set up camp on the mountain near Bacharach, opposite the Hessians; and so the Rhine, flowing between, separated those men from these. Various devices were devised by the Hessians, but all failed because of the height of the mountains. They tested one another with cannon-shots, and, after some had been killed on both sides, at last on the twenty-fifth day of September the siege was lifted and the prince of Hesse returned to his own lands, not without loss among his men.

In the year written above, on the twentieth day of the month of August, that is, on the feast of Saint Bernard, Duke Rupert died, the son of Philip, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, prince-elector; and after a few days his wife also died, daughter of the late Duke George the Rich.

At the same time afterward Louis, bishop of Speyer, also died, a most excellent man; Philip of Rosenberg, cantor of the church, succeeded him, a man constantly afflicted by illnesses, prudent, and a very vigorous defender of the affairs of the church.

Also in the year noted above, on the penultimate day of the month of August, which was Friday and the second day after the feast of the Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, the garrison of five hundred men, which Philip, Count Palatine, had placed on the mountain for the protection of the monastery of Limburg against the Count of Leiningen, was recalled by him and sent to certain other places of his principality.
