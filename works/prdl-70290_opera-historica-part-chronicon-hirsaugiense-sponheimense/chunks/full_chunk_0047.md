*Of the Monastery of Hirsau*

*Abbo, most learned abbot of Fleury and martyr. Aymonius.*

There also flourished in these times that most learned abbot of Fleury, named Abbo, a man very renowned for his life and zeal for regular discipline; among other little works of his talent he wrote a booklet, not to be rejected, on the calculation of Victorius. At last, preaching the word of God to the peoples in Gascony, he was reddened with martyrdom and ascended to heaven. Aymonius too, a monk of Fleury under the aforesaid Abbo, at the same times adorned this age by learning and conduct. By command of the same abbot, his Abbo, he wrote a quite handsome work on the growth and beginnings of the Gauls; also a booklet on the miracles of our most holy father Benedict, and certain other things, of which we have made mention elsewhere.

*Herigerus.*

Herigerus too, abbot of [Bobiensis?], whom Berno, abbot of Reichenau, mentions in his book on the Advent of the Lord, flourished in the same times. He wrote many things, an account of which we have recorded in the second book of the illustrious men of our order. He was indeed a man most learned in every respect, and very famous both by reason of his way of life and of his doctrine.

*972. Roswitha, nun, most learned.*

There also flourished at this time Roswitha of the monastery of Gandersheim, of our order, in Saxony, a consecrated virgin, very devoted to and learned in the divine scriptures, not ignorant of secular letters either, practiced in verse and prose. She wrote many works not unpleasing; [unclear] a little work to ecclesiastical burial.

In the twentieth year of Abbot Siger, which was the year of the Lord 972, Volimarus, archbishop of Cologne, died. He was succeeded by Gero, son of Count Christian and of Wiburga, sister of the count of Magdeburg. This Archbishop Gero, with his brother Count Diethmar, founded a monastery of our order in Saxony which is called the monks’ new castle, which we commonly call Munchennuenburg by its customary name.

About these times Hermann, duke of Saxony, built the monastery of Luneburg in Saxony in honor of Saint Michael the Archangel. In it he placed monks of our order, and set over them as first abbot a monk taken from the monastery of Saint Pantaleon in Cologne, named Luderic, a man venerable in character and learning.

*973. The first Emperor Otto died. Otto the second.*

In the twenty-second year of Siger, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 974, in the second indiction, Otto the first emperor died, in the thirty-eighth year of his reign and the thirteenth of his empire. His body, with great funeral rites of those mourning, was buried in the church of Magdeburg, which he himself had built. When the first emperor Otto had died, as we said before, his son Otto, the second of this name, succeeded him and ruled as emperor for almost ten years after his father. While his father was still living he had married Theophanu, daughter of John, emperor of the Greeks; by her he begot a son Otto, who after his father ruled as the third emperor. He also begot three daughters: Sophia, who was abbess of our order in Gandersheim; Adelaide, who was also abbess of our order in Quedlinburg; and Jutta, whom Ulrich, duke of Bohemia, took as wife, violently carrying her off from a certain convent of nuns in Regensburg.

In these times too Gero, archbishop of Cologne, founded a distinguished monastery of our order in his diocese, which is named Gladbach, only one mile distant from the town of Neuss, and he enriched it with many estates and ornaments. The first abbot of the same monastery was Sanderadus, a man of most holy life and of regular discipline; after death he was inserted into the catalogue of the saints, and his feast day is kept on the ninth day before the Kalends of September.

About these times also the monastery of Saint Martin in Cologne, of our order, was founded. Its first abbot was Mymborinus, a Scot by nation, a man much venerable for religion and holiness. Hence Scots inhabited that monastery for many years, on account of their incomparable holiness of life, until at last, growing lukewarm from their former fervor and relaxed into every dissolution, they were expelled from that one, as also from certain other monasteries of Germany.
