He was therefore elected in the year of the Lord’s nativity 986, on the same day on which Lupold died, the eighth day before the Kalends of December, and he presided scarcely two years. He was a Swabian by nation, about thirty-six years of age, very finely adorned with monastic morals, by no means poorly learned in all erudition of the Scriptures, handsome in face, and proportionately raised up in manly stature, keen in intellect and eloquent in speech, and such a man as seemed easily able to persuade his hearers of whatever he wished. And indeed, unless untimely death had snatched him from the midst before his time, with the Lord’s help he could have preserved this place from the extreme dangers and evils in which not many years later it was entangled; this one could very readily foretell from the beginning of his rule. For as soon as he was raised up and became abbot, he took care with all his strength that all the monks in the monastery should live according to the purity of the Rule of our most holy father Benedict; and if he found anyone behaving otherwise, he did not tolerate him, but, after he had been admonished once and again and had not amended, he was accustomed to punish him rather severely before everyone according to the norm of the order. And lest anyone could accuse him as he corrected others, he strove to show himself in all things irreproachable, a follower of good works and a living example of virtues to his subjects.

He also transmitted to posterity many lucubrations of his talent.

Engelbertus also flourished in these times, a monk of St. Eucharius at Trier, which today is called by the name of St. Matthias the Apostle, a Mosellan by homeland, a man trained in every respect, very learned in meter and prose. Among the other little works of his distinguished talent he wrote, in verse, the life and passions of the twelve Apostles; he composed certain treatises on music and on the composition of the monochord, and many other things that have not come into our hands. Later he was elected abbot of a monastery whose name I have not been able to find; after some years he rested in the Lord on the twelfth day before the Kalends of March, as is clear in his epitaph set below:

In this tomb reclines the abbot ever memorable,
Engelbertus, rejoicing, dwells in the stars as a spirit;
He died in the month of March, on the twice-sixth Kalends.
He built the church which holds his tomb.

In the second year of Abbot Hardfrid, a very great pestilence, famine, and mortality grew exceedingly strong throughout all Germany, and there was such dryness that almost every kind of grain sown in the earth perished because of excessive drought, since it did not have the moisture by which it could have grown. Meanwhile [unclear] attacked [unclear] faithfully, and, as it appears to us, was the occasion of its entire destruction, which followed a little later. For in a short time, namely four months, sixty monks of this monastery were consumed by so lamentable a calamity; all of them died in this mortality of pestilence. Finally, when so many brothers had died and the end of the danger was thought to be at hand, the shepherd followed the sheep.

*988.*

For in the same year, which was the year of the Lord’s nativity 988, Hardfrid, the ninth abbot of this monastery, died on the third day before the Nones of December, in the thirty-eighth year of his age and the second year after he had assumed rule. After his death there immediately arose, alas, the inextinguishable seedbed of the imminent destruction of this monastery. After the deaths, twelve monks remained surviving in it, in whom rested the chief authority for appointing an abbot in place of the deceased. Would that they had consulted their own affairs more prudently, and had not, through contentions and schisms, handed over a place consecrated to God into the hands of outsiders. The highest virtue of monks is humility, and their greatest vice is pride; for while one strives to surpass another through the lust of ruling, a monastery of the servants of God is changed into a pit of demons. Such an evil befell this monastery in these times after the death of the aforesaid man, since the dissension of the brothers who disagreed in the election utterly destroyed it, so to speak. Whence came this evil to this so celebrated place, Lord God, in which so many most holy men, burning before [unclear] for your name, pleased you?
