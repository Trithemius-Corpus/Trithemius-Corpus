There is no weariness in it; by use it pleases, by neglect it displeases, and the more it is read, the more it is loved. It bestows two most precious gifts on its lovers, since it both sweetly enlightens the understanding by instructing it, and gently inflames above all things, with the ardor of divine love, the mind withdrawn from the vanities of the world.

Commending this, Saint Jerome says: “The divine speech is most rich; it has in itself all delights. For whatever good you wish is born from it, just as manna, when it was eaten, tasted in the mouth according to each one’s will.” Hence Saint Paul, beautifully wishing to express the breadth of the divine Scriptures, says, writing to his disciple Timothy: “All Scripture, divinely inspired, is useful for teaching, for arguing, for correcting, for training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be made perfect, instructed for every good work.” Therefore the effect of sacred Scripture is to teach truth, to refute falsehood, to rescue from evil, to lead toward good, and to raise the mind of the diligent reader to perfection.

Hence divine Scripture is deservedly to be preferred to all sciences, since whatever good is contained in each of them is commended wholly and universally in it. For in it is contained the instruction of all philosophy, since all causes of all natures, as holy Augustine says, are in God the creator, whose holy knowledge Scripture promises and establishes for us. In it also ethics is seen to be surpassed, since a good and honorable life is formed from nowhere else than when those things that are to be loved are loved by us, namely God and our neighbor, which truly divine Scripture alone teaches. Here is logic, since the truth and light of the rational soul is nothing but God; and sacred Scripture itself proclaims this to us. Here is the praiseworthy welfare of the commonwealth, here the laws of princes and the rights of nations. For a citizenry is not best guarded except by the foundation and bond of faith and firm concord, when the common good is loved, the highest and truest good, God, which sacred Scripture alone pours into us.

Therefore men intent with piety upon the sacred writings are to be preferred to all teachers and placed before the students of any sciences whatever, since, as someone says: “Nothing is better for a man than, by reading divine things, to fix his mind there whence life comes to him.” For the study of the divine Scriptures is a ready, beautiful, and pleasant way, which with sweetness leads us into the knowledge of the highest and unchangeable good. When the mind of the studious man has come to this, exulting broadly in the Lord it will cry out: “The king has brought me into the wine-cellar; he has ordered charity in me.” Happy is that conscience in which, besides the love of Christ, who is the wisdom of God the Father, chastity, patience, and justice, no other love has dealings; nor has it sunk back to the lowest things, but with a spirit always desiring heavenly things, it sighs for them.

O mind thrice happy and four times most happy, which, inflamed by zeal for divine love, can say with the most blessed martyr of Christ, Ignatius: “My love has been crucified.” O yoke of holy love, how sweetly you bind the humble mind to the plough of Christ, how gloriously you draw it when ensnared, how gently you press it, delightfully burden it, strongly bind it, prudently instruct it. Your sweetness is known to no one untried, since no one understands what love is who, always slothful, has never truly loved.

The love of God is a most powerful fire, which the Lord Jesus came to cast upon the earth, and he wished it to burn, illuminate, and consume in the hearts of his faithful with continual charity. That fire of divine love makes the mind which it inhabits burn with desire for heavenly goods; and, sweetly illuminating it, leads it into all truth; and, powerfully consuming in it all things of vanity, makes it rich by participation in divine goods. The rational love of the mind is Christ; therefore whoever wishes to taste true affection beforehand should learn to love Christ sweetly. For divine love is an unfailing treasure: whoever has it is rich; whoever lacks it, even if he abounds in all the riches of the world, is truly and rightly to be called most poor.

For whoever loves God with his whole heart, and with his whole soul, and with his whole strength, leaves no place for fault, since the devil does not enter when the soul is full of God. For, as the Lord signifies in the holy Gospel, the devil desires an empty mind, and seeks one stripped bare, swept, and cleaned. But where he finds God present through love, he immediately departs in great confusion. For just as visible vessels of material things, when they are full of some liquid, do not receive the increases of things poured in besides, so, if divine charity has wholly filled us, there will be no place by which crime can enter.
