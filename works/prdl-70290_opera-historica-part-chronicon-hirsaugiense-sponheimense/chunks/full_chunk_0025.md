*The Monastery of Hirsau*

*Salomon.*

Salomon, bishop of the church of Constance, died in this year. He was succeeded by Salomon of the same name, abbot of Saint Gall, a man distinguished not only by nobility but also by prudence and wisdom, who is known to have been the third bishop of that name of the aforesaid city.

In the same year Arnolphus, bishop of Würzburg in Saxony, was killed quite cruelly during the solemnities of the Mass, after he had presided over the aforesaid church for 37 years and 7 months. Arnaldus or Arnulphus, emperor of the Romans after Charles the Fat, had as wife Lucarda, daughter of Welpho, duke of Bavaria, who bore him two sons and one daughter. The first-born of these, Ludovicus, was afterward emperor. The second-born, named Conradus, was duke of Lotharingia; his son, of the same name, was also afterward made emperor. Moreover the daughter, named Lucarda, who was given in marriage to Otto, duke of the Saxons, bore Henry, afterward the first emperor. But the aforesaid Conradus, duke of Lotharingia, son of Arnulphus, [unclear] count of Bamberg, whom Henry, nephew of the emperor, when made emperor, through the deceit of Hatto, archbishop of Mainz, seized and beheaded, as we shall say below in its proper place.

*890.*

In the year of the Lord 890, however, Abbot Reginbodo, weighed down by old age, when he felt that through the occupations of household business he was being drawn back from contemplation of inward sweetness, and rejoiced that he could return to the state of his former quiet, voluntarily resigned his abbacy on the twelfth day before the Kalends of December, when the sixth year of his promotion had not yet been completed. He returned to the state of private life; and all the brothers, not recognizing in him such penitence and austerity of life, greatly wondered at the fervor of the old man. Indeed, the rigor of monastic life, which he had once undertaken, he did not abandon up to death. He was constant in prayer and compunction of mind, and afflicted himself so greatly with vigils and fasts for love of the heavenly homeland that all his elders pitied the aged man. When he was once asked by the brothers why he afflicted himself so greatly, since he had committed no serious sin in religion, he replied: “Even if, with the grace of God protecting me, from the day on which I became a monk I do not remember having committed any serious sin, as you say, nevertheless in the meantime I have negligently omitted many exercises of good work; and especially from that time when I began to be abbot, [unclear]. Therefore, brothers, it is fitting for us, as long as we live, to do penance, and not to cease from good works up to death.” And that venerable old man Reginbodo persevered firm and constant in his holy way of life up to death.

*Concerning Haderadus, the Fourth Venerable Abbot of This Monastery of Hirsau, Who Presided Energetically for Twenty-Eight Years.*

After the resignation of Reginbodo, a most holy man, the brothers came together as one and unanimously elected as abbot Haderadus, a venerable monk, who had been in charge of the school of the brothers. He was young in years, namely about thirty years old, but in conduct and prudence older than many old men. He was elected in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 890, in the eighth indiction, on the very same day, namely the twelfth day before the Kalends of September, on which his predecessor resigned, and he presided over the monastery for twenty-eight years. He was a Frank by nation, born of honorable parents, and had entered the monastic order as a young adolescent under Abbot Gerungus; and he had lived so piously and religiously, ready for every obedience, that he was called by all another Placidus, and not undeservedly. For he was of so gentle a mind that no one ever remembered him disturbed by anger. He was truly so studious in the Scriptures that, after the death of his venerable master Richbodo, he was substituted in his place and energetically governed the public school of the monks in this monastery of Saint Aurelius, which at that time was held a great honor. For a monk most learned not only in the divine Scriptures but also in secular letters was required for this office. In those times in the monasteries of our order--there were no others in Europe, Germany, and Gaul--there was this very celebrated custom: that schools of monks were maintained in almost every monastery. Over these were placed not secular men, but monks most renowned in morals and learning, who were regarded as learned not only in the divine Scriptures but also most deeply instructed in Mathematics, Astronomy, [unclear] sciences.
