*The Monastery of Hirsau* 118

*The oath which the abbot made.*

Offering [the body?] of Christ into the abbot’s hand, before he received it, he ordered him to swear thus: “I, Rupert, by the grace of God abbot of the church of Limpurg, say, affirm, and swear before almighty God, all his saints, and you, lord bishop Arnold, upon my soul and upon this true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, which was born of the Virgin Mary, suffered and was sacrificed on the altar of the cross for us, that these revelations which I have written, in part and in whole, are not invented, not false, not phantasmal, not diabolical, not devised by man, not deceitful, nor coming by human contrivance, but revealed to me from God, true, sober, angelic, divine. So may God, and this body of our Lord Jesus Christ which I hold in my hands, help me, save me, and receive me now in the final hour of my death and on the last day of judgment. And if it shall be otherwise than I have said and sworn, may this venerable body of our Lord Jesus Christ not pass through my throat, but stick in my jaws, choke me, suffocate me, and kill me immediately in an instant. Let there be no place for me in heaven with the elect of God, but let my portion be with Dathan and Abiron in hell, with Judas the betrayer, with Herod and Pilate, who crucified the Lord, with the devil and his angels, in the center of Gehenna, immediately [unclear] my Jesus Christ.”

*Attestation added by the bishop.*

And the bishop added: “If those things which have been read before us have not truly been revealed to you by God, but have come either through you or through another by human means, invented, false, diabolical, deceitful, or phantasmal, may this body of our Lord Jesus Christ not come to you for salvation nor pass through your throat. But may it bring judgment upon you, at once kill you, and may you, by the authority of almighty God and ours, be handed over to Satan for the destruction of body and soul forever, as a proof of the truth, so that all may learn, warned by your destruction, not to mock or deceive the holy church of God.”

*The abbot communes undaunted.*

And when he had received the sacrament of the life-giving body of the Lord without difficulty, with great devotion he made clear to those standing by the trustworthiness of his revelations. Having therefore been dismissed, by the counsel of those standing by he returned with his brothers to his own place, and together with them continued the abstinence he had begun. But Bishop Arnold not only did not believe the revelations of Abbot Rupert, but also considered taking vengeance on him. Hence, after a few months had passed, he came to Limpurg with men like himself, who mocked the abbot’s words as dreams, wishing altogether to forbid any trust being placed in them. Therefore, exasperating the holy man with great arrogance, and receiving from him a repeated proof by oath, though unwillingly, he scorned belief.

Then Abbot Rupert, standing in the presence of all, said to the bishop as if with a certain authority: “Behold, God sends his commands to you; you do not believe. I underwent a dreadful proof; you despise it. And now, since we cannot by any reasoning [unclear] you to this desire, that you place faith in our words, it will be necessary that we leave you to divine judgment. And let this be a sign to you: if you, Arnold, survive this year, God has not spoken in me, but all the things I have said have been invented, and you will condemn me as a deceiver according to your will. But if God has spoken in me, you, bishop, will without doubt die this year, to receive from our Lord Jesus Christ, who sent me, the reward of your unbelief.”

When he heard these things the bishop was afraid, and perhaps would have corrected his error, had he not been deceived in his infancy [unclear] by the counsel of his men, who said: “Far be it from you, lord, that you put faith in this foolish madman and his ravings. It is not fitting that your authority be deceived by the dreams of monks, so that you come into scandal and reproach before all the people, and it be said of our lord that, infatuated by little women, at the voice of one mad torrent he was changed from his purpose.”

*In the same year the bishop dies.*

Strengthened, indeed deceived, by this wicked counsel of his men, the bishop returned to his own affairs and died. After his death, when Rupert steadfastly persisted in his purpose, he was again accused through envy before Arnold’s successor Sigefrid, and for nearly six years was troubled by his rivals with many injuries and persecutions.

At length, by the authority of that bishop of Speyer, he was expelled from the monastery of Limpurg and sent into Hesse, banished to Breitenau, a monastery of our order. Henry, formerly a monk of Hirsau, a learned and holy man, then presided over it as abbot after Drutwin, as we shall say, so that, corrected by his instruction, Rupert might return from his purpose to the common order and practice of living. But since he persevered in his observance, abstaining from everything by which he lived or could have lived, and Henry the abbot could not recall him to the common ways, he wrote letters to the bishop concerning each matter done with him, asking him to recall Rupert to his own monastery of Limpurg, lest he infect the brothers at Breitenau with his singularity.

At length, returning to his monastery of Limpurg, and persevering in his resolve of abstinence together with his brothers, most devout men, until death, he endured many persecutions from his rivals, and not long afterward rested in holy peace.
