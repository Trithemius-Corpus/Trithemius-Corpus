But he who preserves the order, and cares for what is first, even if he neglects what is second, nevertheless through the salvation of the first the second also will be preserved. We must therefore take care that we have a soul sound, beautiful, and unharmed, rather than the body. Now in the face shines forth the appearance of beauty, from which we are accustomed to judge the whole person. But the face of the soul is conscience. Therefore, just as a beautiful face is pleasing in human sight, so a clean conscience is beautiful in the eyes of God. Sometimes a change of place benefits a person for the salvation of the soul, so that a face which did not please those who knew it through familiarity may in another place, by novelty, appear more beautiful. For often, when the place is changed, the disposition of the mind is also changed. And although no fault of my own moves me to change place, but rather another’s envy, still I yield somewhat to it; I am less attached to any particular place. For I know that one Lord reigns everywhere on earth, under whose command I shall serve wherever I shall be.

You praise our letter in friendly terms, which we sent to you almost twelve days ago; and wishing to adorn it with wondrous commendations, you asserted that it had come down from the oracle of Apollo. We understand that by a pagan word you wished to designate our Jesus, who alone with the Father and the Holy Spirit presides in the high heaven; his dignity is so immense, and his majesty incomprehensible, that nothing among all things can be compared with him. Beware henceforth, our friend Capellerius, we pray, lest you attribute to this highest and inaccessible majesty any other name than Christian religion is accustomed to give. For we think it very unworthy to attribute the polluted names of idols, which were not gods, to the highest God, and to address the divine substance with the most filthy terms of the gentiles. Nowhere will you find in the whole sequence of the Scriptures that the true God, whom we worship and adore, has taken for himself the names of false gods, or called himself Jove or Apollo, or anything else belonging to those vanities. We do not lack most honorable and most holy words in the sacred Scriptures, by which with sobriety we can designate in speech that unnameable and superdivine majesty. There is no need, nor is it permitted, nor is it fitting, to express among ourselves the true divinity of the creator of all things by the names of demons and the words for false gods. If we wrote correctly, if we sent a letter worthy of praise, the true God bestowed it, not Apollo; Jesus Christ gave it, and it came from nowhere else. Illumined by truth, we wrote true things, by which we desire to call your mind away from earthly, perishable, and transitory things, so that you may turn yourself to true philosophy, which most befits us Christians, through which alone we shall be able to arrive at the felicity of eternal blessedness, which is the highest good and the final end of the rational creature. For whatever we perceive in the world is corruptible; whatever we possess is transitory; the very body also by which we live is mortal; only our mind, by which we live, is immortal. For the bishop of Le Mans once said:

All mortal things hang beneath uncertain chance,
And by their own instability promise flight.
Whatever you have today may perhaps leave you tomorrow,
Or even now, while you speak, ceases to be yours.

*Matthew 24. Death is not evil.*

Finally, we all die, as sacred Scripture bears witness, and we flow down like water into the earth, which does not return again. For there is one entrance for all into life, and a like departure. Therefore it is fitting that we always be mindful that it has been appointed for human beings to die once; and that death does not delay, but when it is least expected, then it stands at the doors. For this reason our Lord Jesus Christ, wishing us always to be prepared for death, commands in the holy Gospel, saying: Watch, because you do not know at what hour your Lord will come. We must always live in that state in which we desire to die, because that death is not to be thought evil which a good life has preceded. For what follows death does not make death evil; rather, the evil life which preceded death itself makes it evil. And so we, who otherwise must die by necessity, should not be greatly concerned with what happens to bring about our death, but before all else this should rather be our concern: that life be so ordered, adorned in good works with the adornment of virtues, that it does not allow death to be evil. Yet death cannot be evil, since it is nothing other than the abandonment of the body, the laying down of a heavy burden, and the road from toil to rest. But he who has lived well, rooted in the love of the Lord, will never be able to die badly, even if people have judged otherwise of him.
