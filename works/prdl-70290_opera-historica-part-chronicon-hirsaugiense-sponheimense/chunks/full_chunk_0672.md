*The second book of the familiar letters of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James of Würzburg, to Jacob Trithemius, his most dear brother.*

*I.*

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of the holy Apostle James in the suburb of the city of Würzburg, to Master Jacob Trithemius, his only and most dear brother, greetings.*

[unclear] In order that we may be approved by a full brotherly affection toward you, we have thought it proper, for a fine reason, to connect this first book of our Würzburg letters with the last of the Sponheim letters, which we dedicated to you. For in that one you read the miseries of our calamities; in this one, the compositions of a mind rightly and gladly at leisure after quiet had followed. For we wrote the letters of the previous volume as abbot of the monastery of Sponheim; those sent in this book, however, we issued nevertheless as abbot of St. James of Würzburg.

What we did in the first, we promise also in this one: that we have not copied out the forms of all the letters, but only of those which we thought would please you. Also letters of friends sent to us, not all indeed, but a few out of many, which we judged worthy of your reading.

We exhort you, brother: imitate the studies of the wise, and flee the sloth of fools, knowing Solomon’s saying that a wise son makes his father glad, but a foolish son is the sorrow of his mother. While age permits and memory is strong, one must learn, since, as Seneca truly said, studies will make you renowned and noble; thus without studies the mind is sick, with no distinction among its marks. Human understanding is compared to gold, which lies fouled with the dust and mire of ignorance unless it is continually cleansed by the file of exercise; and neither gold attains its beauty, nor understanding its natural knowledge.

There are three things which are often accustomed to obstruct those intent on the study of the Scriptures: namely negligence, imprudence, and fortune. Negligence hinders us whenever we either extravagantly abandon or pursue with too little zeal those things which are profitably to be learned by us. Against this corrupter of salutary study, the venerable company of studious men and of those who continually exercise themselves in the arena of the Scriptures offers the best remedy; it is found in public schools in many places, but in the cloisters of monasteries in few. For today the study of the Scriptures is despised by many cloisters of almost all orders, and seems to flourish in few monasteries.

Imprudence, however, overturns us while we are studying when we do not preserve the proper order in the things we learn. For either we desire to learn things that are not suitable for us, or, sometimes neglecting the more useful, we rashly take up the less useful, or we do not distribute even good and useful things to the proper time. It is highly improper for nobler minds to be dishonored by lesser studies, and for those whom arduous and weightier studies await to be occupied with vain and useless exercises. Lest we fall into this evil while learning, the best teachers must be chosen by us, by whose instruction and likewise example we may attain a suitable order of studies.

Fortune also lies in wait for the studious in many ways, as each one of us experiences daily who strives for the ascent of true philosophy. For by various events, poverty, riches, illness, honors, prosperity and adversity alike, by which at times individually the mind of a studious man is intercepted, he is prevented from apprehending the knowledge he desires. The natural slowness of the intellect, lack of books, and scarcity of suitable teachers have drawn many back from progress in salutary study. Riches have often harmed philosophers; they have recalled more people from study than poverty has. Honor, beauty of body, and prosperity teem with the same poison, and have provided more people with an occasion for ignorance than for wisdom.

But love conquers natural slowness of intellect, if diligent continuation of studies is applied. The ancients could allege lack of books; but abundance has rather made us poor, since the art of printing, invented in our days at Mainz and now spread throughout the whole world, brings to light so many renewals of ancient and new doctors that now, for a small amount of money, anyone can be learned. Nor are teachers of good studies lacking today, but everywhere on earth they abound in every variety of discipline, not only in the Latin language, but also in Greek and Hebrew. These are truly golden times, in which the studies of good letters, neglected for many years, have flourished again.
