Could such a man rightly have been defended by you, and, to the destruction of others, while we were then absent, summon to himself the aid of secular men? He publicly denied the deed which had been proved by the testimony of many; and, lest he should pay the punishment due for his misdeeds, he, against all justice, charged with guilt our innocent familiars, by whose diligence he had been caught in the crime, though we had consented and given aid. Why did you not await our arrival? For all these things were done while we were then at Heidelberg and ignorant of them. With your favor and counsel, by his lies he deceived the abbot of Mount Speciosus, who was too credulous, and certain officers of the duke of the [unclear], and stirred them up so that, to no small contempt, insult, and reproach of us, they seized some and put others to flight: namely, our innocent familiars who, from the eleventh hour until the second, had watched him alone in a dark place of the church, remote from human access, with the manifest signs of his crime, which the greater part of you also saw and knew to be true, had found him, and had reproached him as he came out of the church. We have learned with certainty that all these things were done by your counsel and urgency, and were presumed by ducal ministers in our absence, together with the rest which shame does not allow us to write. When the indignity of this deed was at last reported to us, we resolved to remain outside the monastery for a time, until we should understand more fully your dispositions toward us and have explored the mind of the duke, why and whether he had committed such things. Meanwhile eighteen months passed, in which, apart from the prior, we saw none of you, received no letters from you except one in the March, and lived at others’ expense, sustained by the munificence of princes: understanding the dispositions of your men well enough from the display of their conduct, unless perhaps the prior disdained to be a faithful go-between, which time without doubt will bring to light. What is it, brothers, that you have so often wished to press us with prayers to return to the monastery, as though you did not know our mind, which we long ago made known to you by letters through the prior? If you wished us to return to you, why did you not obey our commands? Why did you not approach your duke, so that he might have restored a gracious disposition toward us? But we have heard that he said to several of his men: Why does the abbot not return to his monastery, when no one has been troublesome to him, no one has disturbed him, no one ordered him to leave, no one forbids him to return? For we do not doubt that the duke would rather that the deed had not been done, which he ordered to be done at the urging of that fugitive monk, and that, led by repentance, he desired that we return; but, after the manner of the princes of this world, he fears that if he wrote his desire to us, he would seem to have acknowledged the injury which he did. Rather, however, he expects humility from us, so that we who have suffered the insult should beg pardon. We cannot deny that this would befit our monastic institution, and we would have done it most gladly for the love of Christ, had not your too long-standing ingratitude toward us stood in the way of our returning. For the duke’s innate clemency is sufficiently known to us, and we do not at all doubt that it could have been obtained even by a single letter. We consider the faults of ingratitude committed by you against us and often repeated; and, unwilling to tolerate their insolence any longer, we accepted, albeit with bitterness, the occasion given to us by which we might be freed from you, knowing that up to this point we had cultivated a barren field. You are exceedingly slothful and simple if you think that I did not foresee your tricks and deceits, when you caused me to be asked repeatedly in words to return, and yet feared nothing more than my return. Do you believe that the plans discussed in the assembly of the malicious at Bingen are hidden from us? Or will you think that the eulogies which the prior, in the name of all of you, contrived at Mount Speciosus against us, causing them to be written by the hand of Peter Schlarp, are not at all known to us, although, intercepted by the diligence of a certain brother at Budoras, they have not yet come into our hands? These manifest signs of your ingratitude and ill will at last reasonably moved us, so that, departing from you, we would no longer endure such great insolence. Would that we had done this earlier, when we were called by many to several abbeys, all of which, with one decision, we despised for love of you. For there were then among you several most religious brothers, distinguished in character and prudence, out of regard for whom we despised the fortune offered to us. But when these were at last either dead or called to other monasteries, you harassed [him?] with so many injuries, insults, and mockeries, until you drove him not only from the priorship but also from the monastery. Likewise the venerable brother and most upright man Johannes Damius Curtesius of Cologne, sufficiently instructed by us in every sort of literature, not only Latin but also Greek and Hebrew, you refused, with equal madness, to endure in the priorship, because, walking according to the purity of the rule, he would not consent to your sins.
