Among the other princes he had with him Reynald, first duke of Guelders, William, count of Holland, his father-in-law, Theodoric, count of Cleves, William, count of Jülich, Theodoric, count of Berg, and many others, all of whom came to his aid at great expense and with a great army.

At the same time, after Matteo Visconti of Milan had died, the emperor Louis, in Italy, as we have said, enfeoffed his son Galeazzo, who was very hostile to Pope John, with the same duchy. This displeased the supreme pontiff more than was fitting, and he said that Louis had not been permitted to do anything in the name of emperor unless he had first been crowned emperor by the Roman pontiff, and that even after coronation he had no right at all in Italy; rather, Italy, indeed the whole world, was subject to the Roman pontiff in spiritual and temporal matters. Since the emperor despised this vanity of the pontiff, at the insistence of Leopold, duke of Austria, the pope, having used counsel not very prudently, excommunicated the emperor himself, and declared that all who called him emperor were excommunicated and that their lands were placed under interdict; and a grave scandal followed in the Church.

In the year noted above Gottfried, bishop of Würzburg, died. Wolfram von Grumbach succeeded him and presided for six years, and compelled the citizens to be subject to his rule.

In the same year also Matthias, archbishop of Mainz, held a synod of abbots and prelates and established many things necessary for ecclesiastical utility.

*1328.*

In the nineteenth year of Wilicho our abbot, on the fourth day before the Ides of September, Matthias, archbishop of Mainz, of the Order of Saint Benedict, died, and he is buried in the church of Mainz. After his death the archbishop of Trier governed the church of Mainz in the name of administrator for almost three years. In these times there was great dissension between the clergy and the citizens of Mainz: the former were contending for liberty, while the citizens were striving to violate everything. And this was perhaps the first reason why the clergy took Baldwin as administrator, whom everyone knew to be a prudent and magnanimous man, through whose strength the pride of the people of Mainz could easily be subdued. Therefore, after he had been made administrator, to repress their rashness he built from the foundations a castle at Eltville near the Rhine, which Heinrich von Virneburg, who later succeeded Matthias, completed.

Meanwhile, while Baldwin had undertaken this administration, at the request of the canons and clergy of Mainz, Pope John XXII sent the aforesaid Heinrich von Virneburg, consecrated as archbishop of Mainz, with mandates to the clergy and citizens that they should receive him without contradiction as their pontiff. But the canons of the aforesaid church of Mainz, alleging defects and many crimes against the aforesaid Heinrich, appealed against him to the apostolic see, committing for a second time the castles, cities, and fortifications, together with the whole diocese, to the aforesaid Baldwin, archbishop of Trier. But after many disputes and lawsuits had taken place before the supreme pontiff between Heinrich and the aforesaid chapter, at last the pope sent two legates to Mainz, instructing the aforesaid Archbishop Baldwin to hand over the administration, with all the castles and fortifications, into their hands, and commanding the canons, clergy, and people, under sentence of excommunication, to receive Heinrich, destined for them as archbishop. Therefore the legates, together with the often-mentioned Archbishop Baldwin, placed the aforesaid Heinrich in the archiepiscopal see with the favor of the citizens.

Meanwhile, while these disputes were being carried on between the church of Mainz and Heinrich von Virneburg, the citizens of Mainz, fearing a siege by Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, took counsel and on a certain day went out armed and miserably destroyed the monasteries of Saint Alban the martyr, Saint James the apostle on the fair mountain, and Saint Victor, together with the castle at Weisenau, as is clearer than daylight especially in the church of Saint Alban, which shows the traces of the destruction down to the present day. For this rashness, when they were later summoned to the imperial chamber by the prelates of the said monasteries, the citizens of Mainz were condemned in a large sum of money. From that time until the destruction of the city, with God avenging the wrong done to the saints, fortune was always adverse to the people of Mainz.

In the same year, on the fourth weekday before the feast of Saint George the martyr, Emicho von Leiningen, bishop of Speyer, died. He was succeeded by Berthold von Bucheck, of the Order of the Teutonic Brethren, and he presided scarcely one year; afterward he was transferred to the church of Strasbourg by Pope John XXII, as we shall say in the account of the following year.
