*The Monastery of Hirsau*

Another Conrad also, a monk of this monastery, at about the same time, with the consent of Abbot Volmar himself, was ordained abbot for the monastery in Schwarzach in the diocese of Strasbourg. Burchard also, a monk of this monastery, was elected abbot at Weingarten, a man shrewd in ability, and in affairs very provident and experienced.

After his death Diethmar, our monk, by the election of the brothers of that place, was made abbot; by his industry he did and acquired many good things for that same monastery.

At the same time when Hartwig, from being provost, became a monk under Abbot Volmar, the cloistral prior was Manegold, a Frank by nation, who was afterwards elected abbot of this monastery, concerning whom we shall speak. He loved Hartwig with great charity, a wise man, magnificent in all his actions. Hence, when promoted to the cellarership of the monastery, he watched with much solicitude over the necessity of the brothers; ministering well in the lower office, he deserved a higher rank.

After him Adelhelm was appointed prior, first at Amöneburg, afterwards abbot at [Mediolacum?] in the diocese of Trier, a man venerable for wisdom and religion. In his place Heymericus was substituted as prior, an old and weak monk; when he died shortly thereafter, Gerlach succeeded him. After him Manegold was again ordained prior, and he remained in that office until the death of Abbot Hartwig.

In this very year of the installation of Abbot Hartwig, which was the year 1157 of the Lord’s Nativity, Adelhelm, as we have said, having been lawfully ordained by Hillin, archbishop of Trier, visited the holy virgin Hildegard at Bingen and returned to Hirsau, where he was received with the greatest joy of the brothers, as a shepherd of most upright charity.

The emperor celebrated Easter at Worms, where almost all the princes of the German kingdom assembled, and swore that in the following year they would go with the king into Lombardy against the rebels of the empire. Afterwards in this same year the emperor, by his great industry, brought it about that abbots, provosts, and indeed many of the better ministerials of the church of Mainz promised into the hands of certain of his friends that, should Archbishop Arnold depart for any reason, they would not elect another without his counsel and assent.

In the same year Arnold, archbishop of Cologne, the second of this name, died, a man acute in ability and most experienced in military affairs, who, having always followed the emperor into Italy, spent much for his empire. He [unclear] of the monasteries of Rindorf near [Bundericus?], the second of that name, in which he was buried; to him Frederick [unclear], was consecrated pope at Rome, and, having received the pallium, [unclear] of Cologne, was consecrated by Pope Adrian at Rome, and, having received the pallium, returned with honor to Cologne. Against him, amid very grave dissension, Gerard, provost of Bonn, was elected; but at Regensburg, by the emperor’s command of the princes, he was compelled to yield to Frederick.

At the same time very grave contentions began to arise from a slight occasion between Pope Adrian and Emperor Frederick. Pope Innocent II had once caused his own image to be painted on a wall at Rome, as though he were sitting on the pontifical seat, with Emperor Lothar before him with folded hands, bent down, receiving the crown of the empire.

When Emperor Frederick saw this while he was at Rome, urged on by his own men, he took it badly. To this affront, if it is right to call it so, there was added another thing very similar, which was a great seedbed of new commotion and dissension.

When the emperor was holding a court with the chief men of the kingdom in the city of Besançon in Burgundy, two cardinals were present, carrying letters of Pope Adrian, in which, among other things, this was contained: “We have conferred on you the benefit of the crown, nor would we be moved by regret if your Excellency had received greater benefits from us.” This word, namely “benefit,” those who were about the emperor interpreted in its customary sense and asserted that it had been put there as an insult to him; they stirred his mind to anger. He cast the cardinals, after they had been subjected to insults, quickly from his sight, saying: “We have the crown from God alone; that is, we attribute the crown of the empire not to the Roman pontiff, but to God alone. We acknowledge the first voice of royal election as belonging to the archbishop of Mainz; we allow the royal consecration to the archbishop of Cologne; we assign the imperial consecration to the Roman pontiff.
