*Familiar Letters*

...has conferred, or what harm has it done to your duke? It certainly benefited the former not at all, and harmed the latter not at all. What will a poor and humble monk be able either to confer on, or to take from, the great princes of this world? Yet we favor, and always shall favor, the most sacred prince Palatine as well as we can; but your duke we favor in proportion as he is proved to have favored us. For thus he caused word to be sent to us through his own men: “Let the abbot return to his monastery, fearing nothing, because we are going to do him neither any good nor any harm.” What a fine promise of a Christian duke, in which the disposition of his mind toward us is made manifest. But let the fickle crowd at last fare well with its prince. Trithemius will not be fed on these beans. I have set God as my helper, to whom I have entrusted the whole cause of my injuries, certain that he does injury to no one, but will repay each one according to his works. For I know that through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of God, just as our Lord Jesus Christ himself deigned to go before us in many sufferings of tribulations, wishing by his example that we be steadfast in adversities. For love of the heavenly fatherland we must patiently endure all adversities, so that before the angels of God we may deserve to find the company of the saints, we who do not avenge the insults of rivals. For, as Hugh of Fouilloy, our monk, writes in the first book *On the Cloister of the Soul*, four things are necessary for a good man.

First, that he make all men his friends. Second, that if he cannot make all men friends, at least he not make enemies. Third, that if he cannot avoid even this, so that enemies are made, he withdraw from them. Fourth, that if anyone pursues him as he withdraws, he not avenge injuries. But I, who have always striven to show myself a good man, desired, so far as lay in me, to have all men as friends, especially those who seemed adorned with virtues and with learning in letters, from whose intimacy, namely, there would arise for me not reproach but a proclamation of praise. And indeed I made many friends by the fellowship of studies, whose faith and constancy I afterward experienced in adversities. One class of men only I could never admit into friendship, because it always showed itself hostile to good studies and never ceased persecuting learned men. These are the swollen and most idle haters of letters, who, proudly despising learned men, hunt after nothing more than money and the vain honors of the world. I never desired their friendship, nor, if I had sought it, could I easily have obtained it, because all their friendship consists in the largess of money and gifts, not in love of Christ or of truth. But they hated me because I devoted myself wholly to virtues and to the sacred study of the Scriptures, of which they always showed themselves despisers, and also because, as a boy, I had been chastised and beaten by the excessive and indiscriminate flogging of teachers. I have said these things not as an insult to the duke, but so that you may recall to memory what you do not fail to know: what sort they are who wished to show themselves enemies to me without cause. But I, following the counsel of a good man, withdrew from them, preferring to have peace in a foreign land rather than continual disturbance in my own.

Perhaps our rivals thought the spacious orb of the earth was confined within the narrowness of one little boot, so that Trithemius could not find anywhere outside Sponheim to remain, and for that reason would be forced to submit himself to their wishes and live as a slave at the command of [unclear], he who has always been accustomed to live in inborn freedom under the service of Christ alone. Yet I seem to myself certain that, if they had foreknown the outcome of the matter, which now, after my withdrawal, they recognize, this [unclear], since not only is there no one to forbid it, but rather all around, together with us, friends and at the same time those who previously seemed enemies, vehemently desire your return? I admit that no one ordered me to leave, no one compelled me, no one forbade my return. Of my own will, not bearing the insult of rivals, I resolved to remain outside, where I was, until the outcome of the affair should be seen. But to return to the monastery of the most ungrateful men would seem to me folly rather than piety, where an insulter stands daily at the doors, but no helper is present. Hence, fearing to be crushed between two millstones, it seemed better to abandon both the abbey and the people in peace than, in the midst of a corrupt nation, continual [unclear] of mind and body...
