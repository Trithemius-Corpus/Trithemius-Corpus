*Familiar Letters, X.*

Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Wurzburg, to Roger Sycamber, Dumetensian canon: greeting and sincere charity in the Lord.

That you rejoice with us over our peace and tranquillity, you do well, and what befits a true friend; nor are we ungrateful, as in duty we must not be, for your wishes and efforts toward us. For the friendship long since formed between us is great, and great it can be, because it did not take its beginning from the vanity of temporal things and the fleeting consolations of the world, but consists wholly in the strong love of Christ and in zeal for the sacred Scriptures.

And this is the height of our philosophy, we who are Christians and cloistered men bound to God: always to love with pure affection the Lord Jesus, who with the Father and the Holy Spirit is one God in a trinity of persons, and, despising all the vanities of the world, to keep the mind continually occupied in the divine Scriptures with sobriety. For this is the true friendship of cloistered men, which divine love binds together among brothers; having God as its beginning and end, and, rejecting the fellowship of vanity, it walks the solitary way of God’s commandments without stumbling, with the companion of its vow.

For the prophet Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke from his youth; he will sit alone, because he has lifted himself above himself.” For the sinew of cloistered friendship is to love solitude for the love of Christ, and at all times to avoid the conversations of men who are carnally wise. The only firm friendship of cloistered men is that which the fellowship of equal vows has joined in the love of heavenly wisdom, without regard for earthly advantage. Here there is no need of perishable gifts, no gold, no silver, because for true friendship this alone is required: that God be loved purely by both.

Love, however, arises from knowledge; knowledge is generated from continual study of the Scriptures; and study is nourished by desired solitude, as Saint Prosper the bishop said:

The quiet may search out the law in whatever way they can,
if hearts, free from the world’s noise, are at leisure.

And again he says:

Wisdom fills a tranquil mind, empty of cares,
and will be the guest of a peaceful breast.

In body, indeed, you are solitary; you dwell in a grove with a few brothers of the same profession under the yoke of silence; you are fervent in sacred study of the Scriptures. If the invisible noises of the world have not penetrated the inmost parts of your heart, you have now prepared an easy way toward knowledge of the highest good, which is God. The mind, too, must be restrained, lest it be shaken from without by disturbances of excited frenzy, or touched by animal passions, but rather be peaceful and tranquil, always fearful before the divine word, because it is written: “Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil.” And the Lord says through the prophet Isaiah: “To whom shall I look, except to the poor little one, contrite of heart, and trembling at my words?”

For because you burn with zeal for saving knowledge, so that you may understand the true wisdom which is from above, first purify your mind from every pleasure of earthly things, and then further [unclear] in the study of the Scriptures; and the more you advance in knowledge, the more [unclear] you will love. And the more ardently you love the Lord your God, the more you will approach true wisdom. For what else is that enjoyment of eternal happiness by the mind after the dissolution of our body, than a certain unfailing exultation of the spirit, which, consisting at once in knowledge and love of the unchangeable good, with the grace of God cooperating, has taken its beginning in this mortal life through holy studies from our free will?

Roger, I do not esteem you highly as a poet, nor do I admire you as a rhetorician, nor do I praise you as a lover of worldly wisdom; but because you are a lover of sacred theosophy, and worship God with a pure mind, and are held to be a despiser of the age, this is what I venerate and praise in you, and at the same time love most strongly. For this alone I have admitted you into friendship, and inscribed you in my heart as a true friend; without it there is no firm friendship among cloistered men.

Thus, Roger, I would wish us to proceed as constant friends by the love of Christ Jesus alone, so that, with our hearts continually purified by readings of the divine Scriptures, and devoted with sobriety to them, we may through the continuation of actual study attain to knowledge of the unchangeable good, and may always love that incomprehensible God, known by faith, with the whole intention of the mind, despising for love of him all the prosperous things of this world, and fearing no adversities. For the Lord Jesus, the wisdom of God the Father, is our philosophy, and the true bond of cloistered friendship; without him you ought to have no friend.
