*Of Johannes Trithemius. 24.*

*To the most reverend father and most serene prince and lord, Lord Hermann, archbishop of the church of Cologne, duke of Angaria and Westphalia, administrator of the church of Paderborn, archchancellor of the Holy Empire throughout Italy, prince-elector, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of St. James at Wurzburg, wishes happiness.*

Most reverend prince, I send to your highness, with the bearer of these presents, your messenger, whom for this reason the most illustrious Prince Joachim sent to me from the March, the book and those things which in the previous year were entrusted to me by the same prince, to be delivered by me in person, as I recently wrote to your most reverend paternity. I had indeed resolved to come down to you and to hand over the remaining matters which had been entrusted to me to be conveyed orally; but, my affairs being in a state of disturbance, as your serenity very well knows, I have necessarily been compelled to defer this intention to another time. I did not receive in writing from the prince mentioned the manner of using the things which I send; yet I shall take care, as soon as I can bring it about, that it too be sent to your highness by a trustworthy messenger. For at present I do not have at hand the written replies which would seem necessary for the things sent. I trust, however, that at some time time and leisure will be granted me to come into your sacred presence, so that I may dispatch the remaining matters which did not seem fit to be entrusted to dead little letters. May almighty God long preserve your most reverend paternity unharmed. From Wurzburg, on the fifth day of the month of June, in the year of the Christians 1507.

*Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of St. James in the suburb of the city of Wurzburg, to Roger Sycamber, canon of Dumetum, greeting.*

The great and continual tribulations of Macharius, abbot of Limburg, I share all the more deeply the longer I have lived with him. Who would not have compassion for one who has suffered so dreadful an injury, unless he were altogether estranged from the faith of Christ, as are those who burned to ashes by fire such a distinguished monastery as Limburg? For unless they had been utterly estranged from the faith, that savage, impious, and wicked Count of Leiningen, and the satellites of impiety who serve him, would not have destroyed the temples of God, would not have violated altars, nor converted to their own uses things consecrated to almighty God. But what are the princes of the earth doing, who, appointed for the punishment of evils, punish their own injuries, even small and sometimes childish ones, so severely, yet do not notice insults to God? It is no wonder, in my judgment, Sycamber, that the Christian faith is today everywhere in danger, devotion perishes among men, charity is extinguished together with religion, and impiety with cruelty rules everywhere. By our deserts, Roger, we suffer evils, because we do not truly love that supreme good. Since today there are no princes to correct the vices and crimes of tyrants, many are found who alone are to be corrected. Therefore the Church is shipwrecked, the empire is diminished, the number of the unbelievers is increased. What once most Christian princes, with devout intention, bestowed on the churches of God, tyrants today seize, and this very often with impunity, sparing neither God himself nor men. Whence the Church of Christ now justly laments, saying: In peace my bitterness is most bitter. But since our discussion of these matters is fruitless, we are compelled to be silent, lest we seem to have set our mouth against heaven. Your letter announces to me that Johannes Cantherius the Frisian physician from Groningen, a learned man, has departed this life. There is nothing new, nothing strange, if one dies who was mortal by nature, since he entered under this law, that at some time he should go out again. Behold, he has now gone out; he has paid the debt owed to nature; he has conquered what is most terrible of all, and has made past for himself what we all fear will be future for us. If he lived well in Christ, he has gone out happily, since, as Saint Augustine says: He cannot die badly who has lived well, and he can scarcely die well who has lived badly. O that we might live with such constant prudence as we desire to have lived when it is necessary to die. Cantherius has gone before us, has overcome death, and has now shaken off human fear. Let us therefore consider, dearest Roger, that we shall soon arrive where we grieve that he has arrived, and let us so live the very small moment that remains that we may suspect any hour at all to be our last. For, as Saint Christophilus says, we have here no abiding city, nor are we more enduring than our fathers, who have all preceded us to death. We also, as the bishop of Le Mans says:

We are all taken from the midst, with the fates pressing on,
And we are drawn where life, once completed, calls us.
