*Chronicle of Trithemius*

Against his onslaught Duke Louis, leading out an army with a strong hand, took the very wealthy village of [unclear], together with almost sixty others in number, of which many belonged to the people of Ulm; these he reduced almost to nothing. These things happened around Shrovetide.

Afterward, on the sixteenth day of the month of July, the aforesaid Margrave, with many nobles and a strong army of his own men, of the Counts of Württemberg, and of the imperial cities, beset with a siege Heidenheim, which Duke Louis had previously taken. When Duke Louis heard this, he moved his army against him, and on the nineteenth day of the month of July drove the Margrave from the siege, seized his camp, killed many of the nobles and soldiers, and captured many. The Margrave escaped with difficulty with two men and withdrew into the castle of Albeck. Duke Louis, however, found much wealth in the Margrave’s camp, in gold, silver, horses, wagons, and arms. Then peace was again made between them.

In the same year also, on the vigil of the apostles Simon and Jude, while Diether of Isenburg, archbishop, and Philip, Count of Katzenelnbogen, were awaiting the aforementioned Frederick, Count Palatine, in that very night, through the treachery of certain citizens, Ortwin and Dudo being the leaders of the treachery, Mainz was handed over into the hands of Adolf of Nassau, while Louis the Black, Duke, and the lord of Königstein, with the men of the Rheingau and others, set the city on fire and plundered it wretchedly, many citizens being cruelly killed. Moreover Diether and the Count of Katzenelnbogen, having been lowered over the wall, escaped by fleeing in a small boat along the Rhine.

The enemy found there the plunder not of one city, but almost of the whole country, because many goods from the surrounding villages and monasteries had previously been carried into the city out of fear of the adversaries. The houses of priests and Jews, despoiled by them, were miserably laid waste, and the priests themselves, together with the Jews, were shamefully captured and led away to Eltville and other places. Of those who were in the city, almost five hundred were killed; beautiful women and virgins were raped, and many things were done contrary to the order of Christian charity and honor.

After this, peace was made at Frankfurt between Adolf and Diether of Isenburg under this settlement: that Diether should relinquish the pontificate of Mainz to Adolf with all its debts and burdens; and that he himself, for the support of his life, as long as he lived, should freely possess the town, castle, and toll of Lahnstein, with all their revenues. John the bishop, while the aforesaid wars were going on, had twice besieged it with a great army against Diether, but, because the townsmen stood faithfully with Diether and defended themselves bravely, he could not take it; indeed he was compelled to withdraw, with no small loss to himself and to his church of Trier. Likewise Höchst, town, castle, and toll, and also the town of Dieburg, all with all their revenues. These Diether obtained, and thus peace was agreed between them.

Moreover, to Frederick, Count Palatine, for the expenses and provisions which he had laid out for Diether, a pension of one thousand florins each year was assigned on the toll of Ehrenfels. And for the sum of nine thousand florins, which those men of the Rheingau were obliged to pay him, as we said above in the fifteenth year of Conrad our abbot, he received Petersheim in pledge from the church of Mainz.

In the same year also, near Mainz, Frederick instituted the Court Judgment, to be held four times in the year, which he adorned with learned men in law and with nobles, to declare to each person what is just. This Court Judgment, quite usefully instituted, the most excellent prince Philip, Count Palatine, has laudably continued down to the present day with increased strength and wisdom; in it the lengthiness of many lawsuits is curtailed.

In the same year also, on the tenth day before the Kalends of May, Ulrich, abbot of this monastery, appointed as the thirtieth prior of this monastery Otto Hawisen of Cologne, whom almost five months earlier he had made cellarer, after the convent had remained for two years without a prior. He held the office of prior for four years, except for twenty-three days; and when Ulrich resigned the abbacy, as we shall say, he himself was elected abbot.

*1463.*

In the second year of Ulrich our abbot, the monastery of Michelsberg near Bamberg was reformed and, as is customary, united to the annual chapter of the union and observance of Bursfelde. Pope Pius II conferred the abbacy of the same monastery on Eberhard of Venlo, abbot of St. James near Mainz, and ordered him to transfer himself there for the preservation of the reform that had been introduced. John of Littstein, abbot of the Mount of St. John in the Rheingau, succeeded him in the abbacy of St. James; John of Lutherbach succeeded him there in the abbacy, and both did not preside long in either place.
