*XXI.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to his most beloved daughter in Christ, Richmod, abbess of the monastery in Sebach, sincere charity in the Lord.*

As soon as an opportunity of speaking with the archbishop of the people of Cologne was given me, which occurred at Bonn on the second day of July, I set forth the matter of your patrimony, which you have with William, knight of Horst, your brother, and at the same time I asked for counsel and assistance. After he heard from me that you were in good health, he rejoiced with you and promised that he would apply all his effort so that you might obtain some part of your paternal estate, as far as possible. But since nothing can be done unless your brother is present in person, we postponed the whole matter at Cologne, where we hoped he would come. And because he did not appear, the prince’s counsel is that you appoint someone at Cologne by letters as procurator, who may suitably pursue the case before him whenever your brother is personally present here in Cologne, as often happens. I have no doubt that the archbishop, out of love for you, will exercise in the case all the diligence that he promises. Therefore appoint before a sworn scribe [unclear], so that [unclear] in Cologne as procurator. Yesterday, invited by the master, I had dinner in your monastery of the Maccabees, and I set forth your case to Master Elias, who also willingly promised that he would cooperate to the best of his ability. He, together with Master Gisbert, would seem to me not unprofitable to be chosen as advocate of the case. The mistress of the convent greets you, and Sister Margaret of Dauerckhusen, formerly prioress in Sebach, and all the other sisters most devoted to you. They are putting off writing letters to you until my departure from Cologne. This servant will make you more certain about my condition and health. Farewell, most devoted virgin of Christ, mindful of us in your holy prayers. From Cologne, on the 16th day of July, in the year 1505.

*XXII.*

*John Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to Johann Gryposius, baker, citizen of Stavelot, a most distinguished friend, greeting.*

Since, sweetest Johann, you have always shown yourself a true friend to us, according to that saying of the wise man: “He who is a friend loves at all times; and a brother is proved in distress,” you strive to persevere to the end. For with letters no less elegant than sweet you console us most affectionately, as though we were cast down in spirit because of that insult raised against us by a rival; you also promise that, to the best of your ability, you will never fail us in body or substance. And although no dejection has penetrated our mind, since we have placed our hopes in safer things, and have been instructed to bear patiently all the assaults of raging fortune in view of the reward to come, nevertheless we give you immense thanks, for you keep inviolate the rights of true friendship. For this is the integrity of perfect friendship: it is not separated from those whom it has held for the sake of advantage. For the brothers of a poor man hate him; moreover, even his friends have withdrawn far from him. But he who takes mercy away from his friend abandons the fear of God. That is not true friendship which deserts a friend when he is placed in necessity. But true intimacy, according to Saint Jerome, is that which is joined by the glue of Christ, which is brought about not by the usefulness of private property, not merely by the presence of bodies, not by deceitful and flattering adulation, but by the fear of God and the study of the divine Scriptures. For in friends one seeks not property but good will, because substance is often supplied even by those who are not friends; but only the charity of friendship grants good will. For to will the same things and not to will the same things among equals and good men is firm friendship; where it remains, nothing is easily believed, nothing is easily received, as Chrysostom says, that could produce distrust.
