In the same year Baldwin, count of Flanders, emperor of Constantinople, died, a most Christian prince, who accomplished many things for the defense of Christians. His brother Henry succeeded him in the empire of the Greeks, and ruled vigorously for eleven years.

In the same year also King Philip again entered the territories of the people of Cologne. Adolf, formerly archbishop, came to meet him, and with counts and a great army he traversed the whole bishopric, subjecting everything to his will. But King Otto and Bruno, who in the same month had been ordained archbishop by Siegfried of Mainz, with two bishops from Britain sent by the king of England and with four hundred horsemen and two thousand foot soldiers, set out from Cologne and resolved to fight with Philip. Therefore, when these two kings joined battle with their armies at Wassenberg, Otto and his men turned their backs, and King Philip obtained the victory. Among many other nobles he also captured Bruno, archbishop of Cologne, whom he took away with him bound in iron manacles, and kept in close custody for more than the space of a year. Meanwhile the people of Cologne, considering that the whole empire was opposed to them and that their efforts were everywhere frustrated, took the counsel and assistance of the duke of Brabant, went to King Philip while he was staying at Boppard, and, asking pardon for their offenses, were received back into his favor at Cologne. They also afterward, when Conrad had been sent [unclear]. King Otto, leaving Cologne, came to Brunswick in Saxony, and there [unclear] came by ship across the sea into England, where he was received with honor by the king of England, namely his uncle, and remained for some time.

In the same year, on the second day before the Kalends of March, at the tenth hour of the day, there was an eclipse of the sun, and many testified that they had seen a human head in the sun.

In the second year of Abbot Lutfried, which was the year 1207 of the Lord’s Nativity, Conrad, bishop of Speyer, granted in perpetuity to the provost in Denkendorf the church of the Holy Sepulchre at Speyer, in which there were nuns, with all its rights and appurtenances, so that, when the nuns who were then in it had died or had been transferred to other [places?], their necessities of life being preserved, he might establish there a convent of men of his own [profession?], which nevertheless would be subject to the jurisdiction of the bishop of Speyer for the time being.

In the same year, around the feast of Saint Andrew the Apostle, King Philip held a great assembly of princes at Augsburg. There two cardinals appeared, namely Hugh, bishop of Ostia, and Leo, priest of [Holy Cross?], sent by the supreme pontiff Innocent to restore peace in the kingdom. Through the king’s intervention, they released Adolf, formerly archbishop of Cologne, from excommunication, and sent him to the examination of the apostolic see for satisfaction. who, after being admitted, drew his sword and killed the king himself, and immediately fled with his men before the deed became known. The body of the slain king was first [buried?] there in the major church; [afterward?], together with his nephew by his brother, they were transferred to Speyer and buried in the major church with the kings. He was killed on the eleventh day before the Kalends of July, and the empire remained vacant until the feast of Saint Martin. Moreover the murderer, Count Otto, became a fugitive and was outlawed from the land, and after a few years was killed by Philip’s marshal.

In the same year, on the feast of Saint Martin the bishop, through the agency of Conrad, bishop of the church of Speyer and chancellor of the slain King Philip, who at that time had the royal insignia in his possession, a very great assembly of princes, nobles, and prelates was held at Frankfurt. There King Otto, formerly Philip’s adversary, was again unanimously elected by all and confirmed in the kingship; he also took the daughter of King Philip as his wife.
