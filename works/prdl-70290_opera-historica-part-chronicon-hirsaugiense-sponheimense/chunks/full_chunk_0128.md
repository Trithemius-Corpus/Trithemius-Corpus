Among the other little works of his talent, in imitation of Boethius and of his predecessor Martianus Capella, for the consolation of monks, he wrote a very beautiful volume in a double style, divided into five little books, which he entitled The Lantern of Monks. He also compiled a brief history of the deeds of his own time, which we once saw in the aforesaid monastery. He was buried in the middle of the church of the same monastery, not without a reputation for sanctity.

In the same year died Egilbert, the seventy-ninth archbishop of Trier, a magnificent restorer of his church and most generous toward the poor. He was succeeded in the archbishopric by Bruno, born in Franconia, which in ancient times was called Eastern Francia, son of Count Arnold and of Countess Adelheid; and he was consecrated at Mainz on the Ides of January. This Archbishop Bruno restored from the foundations, with marvelous stone paneling, the monastery of Saint Eucharius near Trier, of our order, which is now called Saint Matthias, and which through excessive age had almost collapsed. In that restoration the body of Saint Matthias the Apostle was found, and from that time the name of the same Apostle has remained with that same monastery down to the present day. For before that it had been called after Saint Eucharius. The same archbishop also newly built another monastery of the order, in the diocese of Speyer, in the district called Kraichgau, in the county of Bredheim, near the village of Odenheim, in the place Wigoldesberg, which in common speech is called Odenheim, in the twenty-first year of his pontificate. This is contained more fully in the letter of foundation of the same monastery, given by Emperor Henry, the fourth of this name, in the year of the Lord 1122, indiction 15, on the third day before the Nones of March, at Nuhusen. For it had been established by the holy father William, with the common counsel of the brothers, that some of the lay brothers coming for conversion, in the [unclear] of the poor under secular clothing, should receive the cellar and clothing [unclear]. Abbot Gebhard changed this statute, ordering that the servants of the poor should receive the necessities of their life from nowhere other than from that alms itself. From this occasion, when the provision for the poor had been diminished, a lamentable thing occurred: the customary mercy was withdrawn from pilgrims and the needy, to such a degree that, shut out from lodging through the freezing nights of winter, they would very often cry out wretchedly in the street from hunger and cold, and would repeatedly disturb the brothers lying in the dormitory. When, about these matters and similar ones, though often admonished by the elders, he made no correction at all, a certain elder monk of holy conduct saw himself, on a certain night of the Lord’s Ascension, in a vision, present among the glories of the saints; there, among the others, he recognized the holy abbot William, once very familiar to him, shining in countenance and habit, standing between two most splendid bishops, one of whom was Saint Conrad, bishop of Constance, and the other Saint Ulrich of Augsburg.

And after many words had been exchanged between them, Saint William said these things to the monk seeing the vision: “On behalf of the supreme majesty of God I command you to tell the abbot, the prior, and the other brothers of Hirsau that, according as they received from us, they should serve the needy and guests with every affection of charity, and should correct their manners and their life according to the Rule of Saint Benedict. Before all else, casting away anger, hatred, indignation, and detraction, by which they continually offend God, together with all malice, let them preserve sincere and continual love for one another; and unless they observe this, let them know that they will undoubtedly experience divine vengeance.”
