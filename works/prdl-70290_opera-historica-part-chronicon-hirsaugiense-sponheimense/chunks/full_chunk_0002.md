He, a prelate by no means among the lowest in your diocese, but more by the merit of his learning having acquired a most celebrated name (a rare thing in that age, when knowledge even a little above the common level could give rise to slander about magic), among other memorable affairs carried on throughout the Church and Empire, recorded especially the affairs of Mainz with a diligent pen and preserved them for posterity. When, according to custom, I was looking about for a suitable patron for him, I could not reckon whose name it might more justly be inscribed to, and indeed ought to be inscribed to, than yours, Most Reverend Prince-Elector. I also easily commended this plan to your chancellor, a truly noble man, Philipp Wolf von Rosebach (a man whom I count it part of my good fortune to be connected with by some affinity of family, but still more by the union of minds and studies). And this not so much because that author of ours, as I have just said, committed to writing with particular care the affairs of your Church above all others, so that I would venture to say that an almost continuous Chronicle of Mainz is contained in these commentaries, as because he accurately noted the principal moments and changes of the whole German Empire; in which you, by your great merit, hold that dignity joined to so ample and ancient a prelacy, which the Greek author Nicetas Choniates, speaking of some one of your predecessors, calls [unclear Greek], logothete.

For to your care and ordering belong the dispatch, recording, and publication of affairs to be carried on throughout wide-stretching Germany by the Caesars; to yours the preservation of justice and of the imperial tribunal; to yours the ordering of diets and solemn assemblies. To you, therefore, belong archives, record offices, and chartularies. In these, I do not know whether it is of less public concern that monuments of this kind and commentaries on deeds done in every age be preserved than any other class of acts and registers; without them we would surely be involved in great ignorance of very many matters of the highest importance. Certainly we have this Trithemius, just as he had diligently gathered, turned over, and excerpted the writers and all the remains of preceding ages, so too as a most ample witness of the deeds of his own age, on which he chiefly expatiates. But I have always judged, and I do not yet repent of the opinion, that History, which is called the Witness of Times, cannot better give credibility to its words than if it gives the reason for its knowledge from its own proper perception, as the sons of advocates say. For this reason learned men certainly judge that those old writings, of whatever kind, should be religiously preserved, rather than that new ones should continually be hammered out and compiled according to one's own inclination. Would that Trithemius himself, and after him Nauclerus, Aventinus, and others, had done this: indeed, how much more gladly would we have read the authors whom they follow than those men themselves.

But since those authors either lie hidden somewhere, captive or buried, or have even very plainly perished, meanwhile we must be content with this Cato, as Augustus used to say. In the meantime, if I should present this Trithemius just as he came into my hands, with the best faith, sincere and unblemished, I thought that I would have discharged the duty of a faithful editor as properly as the editor is by no means obliged to guarantee the good faith and truth of the writers themselves. For those men are not to be tolerated who wish to be clever in other people's writings, especially writings of antiquity; still less those who wish to be bold; least of all those who permit themselves to alter anything in the authors of history according to their own judgment or feeling. But if it is counted a fault in a historian to have inserted judgment among the things narrated, what of his editor? And let them indeed have written as each one pleased, as it seemed, as it was discovered; since we wish it to be free for us to believe as we please, and to judge about what has been believed. Let it suffice for us to have used such conscientiousness in editing that we did not allow ourselves to change a single little word, but rather checked it carefully against the author's archetypes, manuscripts of venerable antiquity and remarkable elegance, and, as far as possible, left it to its own integrity.
