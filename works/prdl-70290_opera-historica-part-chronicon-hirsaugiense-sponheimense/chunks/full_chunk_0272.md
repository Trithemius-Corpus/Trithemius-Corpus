Thus, having found an opportunity, he ran him through with a sword on the bank of the Rhine, as we have said, not far from Schaffhausen, on the day of Saints Philip and James the Apostles, which was the first day of May. His corpse was brought to Speyer the following year and was buried with honor among the kings in the greater church, on the fourth day before the Kalends of September.

In the same year, in the month of November, the prince-electors assembled at the village called Rhense, not far from Boppard, near the royal seat; and, through the agency of lord Peter, archbishop of Mainz, they unanimously elected as king of the Romans Henry, count of Luxembourg, brother of Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, a good and vigorous man, worthy of imperial honor. They solemnly made public his election at Frankfurt on 27 November; and thereafter, on the feast of the Epiphany of the Lord in the following year, he was anointed and crowned king of the Romans at Aachen by the hands of Henry, archbishop of Cologne. He reigned four years and nine months. Soon after he had been crowned and came from Aachen to Speyer, he caused the bones of Kings Adolf and Albert to be brought to the greater church of Speyer and buried with honor. He had as wife Margaret, daughter of John, duke of Brabant, by whom he begot John, king of Bohemia, of whom we shall speak later.

*John Scotus, the Subtle Doctor, dies.*

In the year already noted, on the sixth day before the Ides of November, John, the Subtle Doctor, of the Order of Minors, died at Cologne and was buried there in his convent at the entrance to the sacristy. How much he wrote, and how subtle and profound in receptive theology, all theologians know, and we have noted it in our book on ecclesiastical writers.

In these times also Peter, archbishop of Mainz, newly constructed from the foundations a monastery of monks of the Carthusian order outside the walls of Mainz, and provided for it magnificently.

*1309.*

In the ninth year of Abbot Henry, the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1309, on the day of Saint Michael the Archangel, Dietlib, the tenth abbot of my monastery of Sponheim, died: a good man and an outstanding lover of regular observance. He presided usefully in both states, for he both took away the private property which his predecessors had allowed the monks, and suitably enlarged the revenues of the monastery. In the abbacy of Sponheim he was succeeded by lord Willich, a conventual monk, son of the count of Westerburg, brother of the countess of Sponheim; and he presided vigorously for 27 years, [unclear] months, and 14 days. Concerning his life, morals, and governance we have arranged a full history according to the sequence of years in the Chronicle of the aforesaid monastery of Sponheim.

In the same year, when the king of Hungary died without children, the nobles of the land and the elders unanimously elected Otto, duke of Bavaria, son of Duke Henry, as king of Hungary, as the nearest heir, because through his mother he was the grandson of King Béla. Summoning him from Bavaria, they received him with great honor. But not long afterward, changing their mind, they drove him out of Hungary. Returning to Bavaria, he died not much later, leaving an only son, Henry, an infant of fourteen days, by his wife, the daughter of the duke of Poland; he was called prince of [Notterberg?].

*1310.*

In the tenth year of Abbot Henry, which was the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1310, Henry, king of the Romans, held a great assembly of princes at Speyer. There Elisabeth, daughter of a former king of Bohemia, appeared in person and sought from the emperor to be enfeoffed with her father’s kingdom. But King Henry, using the counsel of the princes, with the consent of the nobles of Bohemia gave this same daughter, together with the kingdom of Bohemia, as wife to his son John, count of Luxembourg. The wedding was celebrated there with great solemnity, and he sent him with an army into Bohemia, joining to him Peter, archbishop of Mainz, once his physician. When John came into Bohemia, he was received as king with great honor by all the inhabitants of the kingdom, and was solemnly crowned by the same Peter, archbishop of Mainz, to whom by ancient right that office belonged. More than three hundred thousand people were said to have been present at this coronation. When the coronation had been performed, the king, among other small gifts, gave the archbishop a seat adorned with gold and gems, which, after it had been brought into the church of Mainz, the common people have been accustomed to call the seat of Saint Martin down to this day. Meanwhile, while these things were being done in Bohemia, King Henry, moving his army from the assembly at Speyer, directed his way into Italy to receive the imperial crown, having with him, among many other princes, Baldwin, archbishop of Trier, his brother, Rudolph, count palatine of the Rhine, and Leopold, duke of Austria.
