In the thirtieth year of Abbot Gerung, the Northmen, with an inestimable multitude, burned and destroyed Cologne, Traiectum, Tongres, Bonn, with all the villages and castles adjoining them. They also laid waste with sword and fire the palace of Aachen, the monasteries of our order, Stavelot, [Mundarium?], Prum, Saint-Walaric, and Saint-Riquier, sparing no sex, no age, but killing all whom they could find. After this they set Trier on fire, killed the citizens, and carried off the booty. Advancing to the city of Metz, they joined battle with the citizens and emerged victorious; in that battle Walo, bishop of Metz, fell with many others. But Bertolph, archbishop of Trier, slipped away in flight and escaped; nevertheless he died in the following year.

In the same year the emperor Charles the Bald, setting out from Italy into Gaul, died after being given poison. Pope John VIII anointed Louis, his son, as emperor. The Romans, moved by this, ordered the pope to be held under guard as a captive; they chose Charles, surnamed the Fat, as emperor. Together both held the name of emperor for two years, until Louis, son of Charles the Bald, died after taking poison. And this Charles the Fat alone ruled as emperor for twelve years.

*885.*

In the year of the Lord 885, in the third indiction, Gerung, the second abbot of Hirsau, died in the thirty-second year of his rule and the seventy-first of his age, on the first day of the month of August. He was buried in the church of Saint Aurelius, beside Lintbert, his predecessor. He was a man in all things devoted to God, no less circumspect in temporal matters than capable in arranging spiritual ones. Reginbodo succeeded him; in his time the Northmen overthrew Worms, as we shall say.

*Concerning Reginbodo, the third abbot of this monastery of Hirsau, who presided for six years; concerning the deeds of that time in Germany.*

When Gerung, the venerable second abbot of this monastery, had died and been buried, the brothers, whose number was already great, came together in the place appointed for this and elected as abbot an elderly monk named Reginbodo, a quiet, pious, merciful man, who presided over the monastery for six years. He was, as Meginfrid bears witness in the Chronicle, a Bavarian by nation, born of a notable family. When in his youth he had been handed over by his parents to teachers to learn letters, in time he became learned in all knowledge, both human and divine. Then he was made a canon in the church of Constance; there, abounding in many riches, he began also to flow away in spirit. At last, having returned to himself, he began to groan over the vanity of the present life and to consider how brief a man's sojourn in this world is. Finally, after many turnings of fluctuating thoughts, he came to Hirsau, despising all the things which men are accustomed to love in the world, and for the love of God became a monk under the venerable abbot Gerung. Thereafter, turning all his zeal toward piety, always, so far as human weakness permitted, he was intent either on prayer or on reading sacred scripture.
