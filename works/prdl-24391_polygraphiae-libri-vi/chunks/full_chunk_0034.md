I have said this so that, if perhaps at some time a report should reach you that I can do wondrous things, you should not, I say, think me a magician, but a philosopher. For what happened to Albert the Great, that most profound investigator of natural things, namely that because of the marvels he performed by the hidden power of nature he was regarded by the common people as a magician, I am certain can happen similarly to me. If you ask whence I have these things, which no one knows, listen. I did not learn these things from a man nor through a man, but by the revelation of someone I do not know. For when, at a certain time in the present year, I was thinking what new thing I might be able to discover that would be hidden from everyone, I began to consider whether I could devise those wondrous things which I have mentioned. After a long imagining, when I despaired utterly of it as an impossible matter, I retired to sleep that night, laughing to myself at my own foolishness, that I had attempted to seek out such impossibilities. That same night someone stood by me, saying: “They are not vain, O Trithemius, the things which neither you nor another with you will be able to discover.” And I said to him: “If, then, they are possible, tell me, I beg you, how they are done.” And opening his mouth, he taught me each thing in order, showing how the things over which I had thought in vain for many days could easily be done. Behold, before God, I do not lie but speak the truth: I have so far taught these things to no one, although many, promising great things, have often asked me, except one prince to whom I write, whom by a clear proof I made more certain of the possibility of the art. Nor is it fitting that others know these things than princes; otherwise many evils would be done through them: betrayals, fornications, and whatever else evil men might wish. But those who use the art well could do many things through it for the commonwealth. Moreover, I can teach all these things in every language of the whole world that I have heard. I wished to make these things known to you, Bostius, for this reason: that you should not think me idle.

Thus far the words of Trithemius. Finally, there are other immense uses of these books of the *Polygraphia*, which we shall treat in our books on the method of teaching and learning; for whatever these things may seem to be, they nevertheless have more hidden within than they promise on the surface. On the *Steganographia*, whose principles lie concealed in these books, God willing, we shall treat separately.

*The First Book of the Polygraphia.*

There are twenty-four characters of the signs of the Latin language, which, set in order before the same number of significant words, serve most abundantly in all respects, according to the desire of each person working in this art, for secrets. But the paraphrases are so artfully ordered that they produce entirely Latin discourse, if only one is taken from any given order. And it should be noted that, by reason of our German language, the twenty-fourth letter, w, is taken at the end of the alphabet, while all the remaining letters perform the service of both languages. We have placed the signifying elements in order with such skill that, whichever you take from each line, once the deduction of the sum has been made, concord will remain. Yet care is needed, so that from any line of syntax you take only one, and the monad does no injury to the binary. One must also provide that, whenever you approach the cabalistic operation, you first count all the individual minutes of the night and join them in due proportion to the degrees of the following day. But if the minutes, according to the secret ordinance, do not suffice to the degrees of the day right to the end, the windy runner must by counsel be brought back to the prudent chief-measurer. Nor is it an obstacle if by chance night falls back upon the head of day, since a prudent practitioner in the art can, by voluntary addition, recede from the front by as much as he sees night exceed the light of day. And this is to be understood of the individual chapters throughout the whole work of the *Polygraphia*. Yet we call that consideration unity, by which the binary, by a known little sign of return, may be made certain, lest it happen to go astray.
