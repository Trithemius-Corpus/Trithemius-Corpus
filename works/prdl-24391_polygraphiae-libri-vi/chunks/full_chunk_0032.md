Finally, in this place he wraps many things in obscure words, by no means accepted in common usage, lest he provide wicked men an opportunity either to misuse these things or to devise worse ones. He wrote “race,” with the syllables reversed, for “cera,” just as “Roma” for “amor,” “nemilua” for “alumine,” “raphocam” for “camphora,” etc. But since such matters, for the public good, must be treated rather sparingly, and do not belong to our undertaking, let us omit explaining everything. For it is enough if one understands what he intended to signify by this third kind of letters.

“*There were also, as Bede bears witness.*” The sense is: the ancients not only wrote or spoke by means of words, as we do, but also transferred whatever the world contains, and whatever can be grasped by us, by a certain artifice to the things signified. Diodorus Siculus, book 4: “The letters of the Egyptians,” he says, “are very like various living creatures, and the extremities of human beings, and instruments, especially those of craftsmen. For their words are not expressed by the composition of syllables or by letters, but by the form of images, their signification having been handed down to men’s memory by use.” Indeed they write the hawk, the crocodile, the serpent, the human eye, hands, face, and other things of this sort. The hawk denotes a thing done quickly, since this bird is almost the swiftest of all; this notation is transferred to household affairs that are done swiftly. The crocodile signifies evil. The eye is interpreted as the guardian of justice and the watchman of the whole body. [unclear] designates freedom, but the left hand, with [the fingers?] compressed, tenacity and avarice. In the same way also of the other parts of the body, the right hand with fingers spread [unclear] writing, which, preserved in men’s memory by long use and meditation, they recognized at once, from the trace, what those things expressed. Petrus Crinitus too, in book 7, chapter 2, writes that this kind of method of writing existed among the Egyptians. Concerning the letters of the Egyptians, which are called hieroglyphic, there is mention among ancient authors, and this can still be observed on obelisks, on which those figures and images of diverse living creatures, plants, rivers, and trees are carved, signifying the philosophy of that people, as Strabo and Pliny, most weighty authors, testify. For on the obelisks of the kings [Sedotis?] and [Sennosornius?], which were brought from Egypt into the city, the interpretation of the nature of things was marked out by symbols of this sort and by hieroglyphic signs. For in this way the philosophy of the Egyptians is believed first to have been made manifest. This is handed down by the same Pliny and by others. Among other images, however, they were accustomed to mark scarabs, bees, rivers, oxen, and other things of that kind. Further, by the very image of the bee the symbol of a king was indicated, because in fulfilling the duties of life he ought to have no less the sting of justice than the sweet clemency of honey. In the vulture they indicated the genius and majesty of nature, because among these birds females only are found, etc. They also painted many other signs in place of words; on these see Horus Apollo on hieroglyphic letters. See Pliny, book 36, chapter 11, where, according to the more correct reading, “Sesostridis” is found in place of “Sesotidis,” and “Phaeron” in place of “Nuncoreus.” Moreover Strabo, book 17, Cornelius Tacitus, book 15, Caelius Rhodiginus, *Antique Readings*, book 29, chapter 26, and Volaterranus, book 33, mention letters of this kind, although he writes that they have as yet been discovered by no one.

“*There were those who by accidents.*” The meaning is that whatever exists in the whole nature of things, whether perceived by the intellect or by the senses, can be turned to the use of signifying in the same way as words do.
