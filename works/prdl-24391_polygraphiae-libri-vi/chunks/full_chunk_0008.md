*To Emperor Maximilian I.*

At the urging of Philip, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, I, Johannes Trithemius, now abbot of the monastery of St. James at Würzburg, but then of St. Martin the Bishop in Sponheim, in the diocese of Mainz, began to write eight books of mysteries in the Steganography. While I was already continuing my night-work on the third book of the same work, I am going to tell the matter briefly, if I can.

In the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1499, in the second Roman indiction, when Arnold Bostius, a Carmelite of the town of Ghent, asked by letter what new work I was bringing forth, and with what studies I was occupied, on the second day of Palms I wrote back to him through merchants, as to a friend, that I had under the hammer a new and wonderful work of Steganography, and I reported in order what was contained in each of its books. But before my letter had reached Ghent, Bostius, in the days of Easter, passed from life to Christ. The prior of that place received the letter, opened it, read it, and, astonished, as I think, by the novelty of the promises, shared it, at the request of many others, to be read and copied. Hence it happened that my letter itself, within a short time, was spread through almost all Germany and France, and was copied many times by many people; it brought even many most learned men to amazement and wonder, to such a degree that they hesitated as to what, in truth, they ought to think of me. Among these were some who steadfastly asserted that my promises in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, shamelessly devised by me for the sake of empty glory.

*Preface to*

Others said: “Great and wonderful things this abbot Trithemius promises; if he can do them, he can do them only by the service of demons, since they seem to far exceed the bounds of nature. But if he cannot, who will doubt that he is a liar, and deservedly to be refuted by all good and learned men?” But let both be far from me, since I promised natural things.

Of all those who rashly judged what they did not know of mine, the most rash, and finally the most unjust appraiser of things to burst forth in public, was Charles Bovillus, a Picard by nation. After he had traveled through the upper parts of Alsace, he came to me at Sponheim for the sake of hospitality, intending to return to France by way of Trier. I treated him with all the courtesy I could, and, as long as he was with me, showed him all the benefits of hospitality with a cheerful face.

And since, as is usual among friends, especially those devoted to letters, I had displayed everything of mine for my most welcome guest to see, among the other things the aforementioned work of Steganography, not yet completed at that time, also presented itself. Bovillus saw it and read it cursorily, his mind occupied elsewhere; he said that he admired the invention, praised it, and took no care to understand it. Thus, since he did not ask for the key to understanding, he deserved to hear or grasp none of the things contained in that work of ours. After this, having returned to France, he repaid us evil for good and shamefully violated the covenant of Christian friendship. For when he was asked by Lord Germanus de Ganay, our common friend, later bishop of Orléans, what he had found with me and had seen in Sponheim, he wrote back falsehoods for truths, lies for benefits, condemning and rashly judging those things which he was quite unable to comprehend with his understanding.

*To Emperor Maximilian I.*

For since he believed that the palm of learning would be bestowed upon him through envy and falsehood, he wrote back to the aforementioned most learned man a letter full of falsity, lies, many injuries, and insults. In it, making mention of the Steganography which he had not understood, he proclaims me, falsely, mendaciously, and far too injuriously, with rash presumption, to be devoted to wicked arts, a magician and a necromancer. To his most lying injuries and blasphemies, God having mercy, I shall shortly respond in such a way that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bovillus is impious, cruel, rash, and in this matter a liar.

I steadfastly affirm, I truthfully say, and I confidently swear upon my soul, that I have never had any commerce with demons, nor with wicked and pernicious magical or necromantic arts; but that each and all the things which I have either written or promised that I would write are pure, sound, natural, and in no respect at all contrary to the Christian faith. Therefore I do not fear to submit my promises for examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as there is need; and I in no way intend to resist their determination.
