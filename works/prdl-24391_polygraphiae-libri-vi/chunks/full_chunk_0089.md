*The third book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

What, from the beginning of human creation, confused the unity of language? Was it not the division of bodies and the variety of minds? For as Truth declares in the Gospel: out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. For the tongue is the instrument for bringing outward those conceptions which the mind has thought within itself. Yet of this ministry of the mind’s secrets to the senses there are many modes, whose properties vary both by use and by art. Use, introduced at pleasure, is common to many; art, however, to few. Indeed no one will readily estimate how much the very unregulated use of speaking among the Germans alone has varied in six hundred years, who has not rather often reread Otfrid, monk of Weissenburg, in the poems of the German tongue. The sea flows and ebbs; men, once dying, do not return again to the present life. Customs succeed customs, and the vigor of mortal minds always declines with respect to better things.

Who today among the Germans, ancient emulators of the Greeks, possesses the old knowledge of idioms? In the chronicles, the ancient histories of the Franks, Hunibaldus, Doracus, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed many things said in old verse, in the prose of the German speech, into their compositions, which today are understood by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own idiom among the eastern Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhinelanders, Mosellans, Cologne people, Saxons, Thuringians, daily undergoes notable variation? Men are changed in time, customs vary, minds are changed, clothes vary; the simplicity of languages is altered, the ancient property of idioms is varied; nothing stable is found in human affairs. For there is a vicissitude of all things; variety succeeds the simplicity of minds. Who will be able to reduce this perpetual flowing away, so to speak, to the concord of unity?

But the fact that we add a third to the preceding books of the Polygraphy has not lacked a special reason. For, since the wills of men are various, rightly there are given manifold modes for honorable operations. In those, day illuminates night; in these, however, day lacks light. There light leads shadow around in light; here, however, the blind illuminates the blind, and, existing and remaining sight without eyes, ministers in darkness what it does not have. Here night darkens day with perpetual gloom, and one walking in darkness without light provides blind guidance. These paraphrasms serve all men throughout the world, and in every language transmit any secret safely, securely, and without danger to the cabalistic binary. Latinity moves barbarians to laughter, and barbarism itself produces suspicion among Latin-speakers. Yet it matters nothing or little, since however great suspicion may have been, it will nevertheless contain every most secret mystery. Let the diligent operator recall to mind the rules and precepts which we have handed down, lest he labor uselessly and in vain for himself and for others. For understanding is needed; without it the usefulness of no art will stand. Thus in a few things we have shown the way to the greatest matters, and, with a finite mode, we have given the way to infinite things. From things given and received, greater things are easily prepared for friends. Reader, remember that the retrograde mark can be fixed in every place until you take up the mark of rest. Farewell. From Peapoli, on the first day of March, in the year of the Lord 1508.
