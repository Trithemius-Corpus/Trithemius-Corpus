*Now follow the rules or cautions to be observed in the operation of this book.*

The first rule or caution advises you that the secret of your mind, which you wish to announce to an absent friend who knows the art and has the book of the *Polygraphia*, you should write as carefully as you can on a sheet, in the Latin language or the German, or in any other you wish that can be represented by Roman letters. Then place the sheet so written before you, so that for each individual letter of the secret you may be able to fit an appropriate word. For, as we have shown, in this practice of writing a whole word is taken. We gave an example a little earlier in the foregoing.

II. The second caution instructs you that, as has been said, you should not take more than one word at the same time from an alphabet in one narrative of the public Latin discourse; otherwise you would both confuse the open sense and entirely draw away from understanding the mystery the friend to whom you had written.

III. Third, one must take the greatest care not to skip any alphabet through negligence, without taking from it one word fitting the secret. Likewise one must provide that, whenever one or more words occur outside the sequence of the alphabet, you take the whole upper line or the lower one, and not both.

IIII. Fourth, care must be taken that whenever the discourse of the open speech has been brought to a complete sentence, the next should take its beginning from a larger, thickened letter, according to the form of the preceding example, in which, according to the sense of the Latin discourse, we took large letters at the beginning and small ones for the rest, in the common manner. When the secret of your mind is lengthy, so that it exceeds the narrative of the open discourse, if much remains you should begin again from the beginning; if little, let the discourse be recalled from any place at will. For no one even moderately skilled in this technique could fail to know where you have begun again. Nor is there need to fear the consonance of the speech, which will always be varied in the manifest narrative together with the hidden one; and whenever it is begun from the beginning, a new appearance of the discourse will follow. Continuation can also be made from the end of the first book through the second.

V. Fifth, we advise that whenever you wish to write on a difficult matter by this art, prepare for yourself a small booklet in which you write your secret mystery in manifest discourse. Whoever sees or reads this booklet will judge that no secret is hidden, and even if he has a suspicion, he will not be able to find any of the things that you write.

VI. Sixth, it must be noted that if you transpose the order of the words throughout the whole *Polygraphia*, changing it by metathesis in each alphabet, and have written two volumes according to it, one for yourself and the other for any friend, you will write between yourselves with complete security whatever you wish, nor is there anyone living on earth who can penetrate your secrets. This transposition, however, can be done in infinite ways.

VII. Seventh, we forewarn you that one must write polygraphically in one way to a man ignorant of the Latin language, in another to one skilled in it. For the former all words must be written out whole, without abbreviation, distinct, complete, handsome, and entirely legible, without partition, without division, and without confusion, lest, deceived by error, he either think one word to be two or judge two to be one. The things that are set down must be listened to.

VIII. Eighth, provision must be made that the person to whom you write mystically by this art should have a book in every respect like yours; otherwise you would write in vain and fruitlessly to one who does not understand.

IX. Ninth, if you wish this technique to remain forever secret and hidden, write out the *Polygraphia* anew, but do not set the letters of the alphabet before it, and no one will easily suspect the principle of the secret. But so that no difficulty may confront the writer and the reader of the mystery, let the letters of the alphabet be written on a sheet in the order corresponding to the words, and let [a movable one?] be fitted to the heads of the same. The letters can also be engraved on a copper plate, so that they may be moved from one order into another.

X. Tenth, it must be known that although we do not use the Pythagorean T in German and Latin words, we have nevertheless admitted it on account of the Greek language, so that we might have the doubled twelvefold number. Likewise we have accepted the doubled w as the twenty-fourth letter, which is altogether necessary for us in the German language.
