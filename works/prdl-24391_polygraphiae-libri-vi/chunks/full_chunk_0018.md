For if, as we have said, you take one word from the first alphabet, likewise one from the second, and so consequently one from each, and one from those inserted outside the ordered line, as far as the period or the end of the book, a Latin and sufficiently coherent discourse will arise for you. Care must be taken, however, that at one time, all at once and at once, more than one word not be taken from one alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, not skip over some alphabet and confuse the narrative. By “day” understand the open narrative, but by “night” the hidden and secret one, which should not be too long but brief. The examples that follow in the text, concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen, pertain to both narratives, the hidden and the manifest. Whenever “the leaf is moved by the wind” and the rest occur, we advise that you so number the secret narrative against the open one that both may end together at the close of any given period. But if anything remains over from the secret matter, let it be enlarged with such moderation that both narratives end at one point, lest a confusion of eclipse be produced. The other things that follow in the first instruction of the explanation of the *Polygraphia* are also implicitly referred to all the things we have said. But it must be noted that under the order of the alphabets there is no idle word; rather, each one represents the letter placed before it at the head. Since the reader may lack perfect knowledge of this matter, let us explain the meaning by an example.

*Of Book I.*

Let us suppose the case that a wicked man seeks through you to be advanced with some friend of yours; but you, in order to cut off his importunate requests and yet not advance an unworthy man, decide to write more secretly to your friend by means of the very man who is asking, so that he may not understand: “Beware of that man, for he is a thief and a very bad scoundrel.” Go to the first alphabet; write the word *Conditor*, which signifies C; in the second, *clemens*, which gives A; in the third, *decernens*, which represents V; in the fourth, *mundana*, which signifies E. Now you have in the hidden narrative: *caue*. Consequently, also through the other alphabets, [unclear] signifies: “the seraphic pleasantness with all his saints in heaven, amen. The gentleness of the most glorious prince, to be remembered everywhere reverently by the saints, by the best precepts exhorts us, most abject sleepers, because of the reverence of the director of good men, to leave the present things behind, and with every affection, according to our powers, to await the glory of perpetual rest. Therefore receive instruction, O vain mockers, so that you may be able to enter, most happily adorned, into the most blessed dances of the archangels of the rewarder of the orthodox. Rise, therefore, from the most filthy honors of this pilgrimage, since you are about to enter, most holily, into the joys of the kindly savior of the ages, most blessed.” In this public narrative written above there is contained this sentence, hidden in secret, namely: “Beware of that man, for he is a thief, a very bad scoundrel; but keep silent, I beg you.”

*Exposition of Book I.*

The same order must be followed in all other cases whatsoever, beginning from the head as many times as necessary until you have manifestly enclosed the hidden narrative of your secret. But the words that are placed outside the alphabetical order designate no letter and do not serve the hidden narrative, but have been inserted because of a lack of synonyms, so that the sequence of Latin speech might be completed. For we could not always have so many signifying words, especially among pronouns, conjunctions, prepositions, and interjections, that we could have made an alphabet in place of the twenty-four wandering letters, as is clearly evident after the eighth alphabet, where we placed two words outside the order, lest we leave the discourse defective. For thus we placed: “with all,” and thus: “with all together.” The writer may take whichever two of these he wishes, whenever he finds similar ones. Let him do likewise in other cases, which we often need in our German language. For one who is ignorant of Latin speech may write his hidden secret in German beneath a Latin discourse without any difficulty.
