*Of the Polygraphia* ... whatever is outside the line, by the common reckoning of the day, has deserted the day, not the night. For the night, always enclosed within the limits of the minutiae and paraphrases, never goes beyond them. Therefore, whenever you have crossed the straight line of the day and have not fulfilled the purpose of the nocturnal syntax, turn the head back to the tail, and, a tying together having been made, let there be a bond of both with each other. Arrange the shadow so that it does not confuse the sun by similarity of sound. Nor be ashamed if, in the first calculation, the day has not comprehended the shadow; rather, count up the minutiae of the lines until the necessary proportion follows according to your wish. You will also be able to make a leap from the first into the second, knowing that in the first creation of days there was made evening and morning, one day. Therefore, when evening has been completed, bring the day back into the following one, and, when the sign has been given by a point known at once to the binary and the monad, fix the mystery. But so that you may be able to avoid this difficulty, divide the great revolutions of our whole sphere by arithmetical degrees, and where day is repeated in place of night, set the mark of Archimedes upon the flying one, lest an untimely ternary block the return to unity by means of the binary.

*Book I.*

For for this reason we have set minutiae before the several degrees by an artful invention, so that in these we might relieve the operator from the labor of counting the paraphrases. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in those things which we have handed down as the principles of the new art, believe us, you will find greater things hidden than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these matters, know this one, not the least: that by the prudent operation of metathesis you will, even when you wish, make a man most expert in this art ignorant, the seeing blind, and the learned unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, this invention of ours would give to each man of eighteen thousand spheres his own methods for writing and reporting, safely, secretly, and very broadly, whatever hidden things he wished in Latin.

*The second book of the Polygraphia of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.*

The reckoning-system of mortals tries, by art, to imitate nature, which admits no calculation of the monad. Hence, although the instruction of the preceding book of the Polygraphia, through the extension and metathesis of particulars, is as it were sufficient for the universals of this art in infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a certain consideration has moved us to join a second to the first. The condition of light is different in each; but night preserves inviolate, in both, the order of its course. The reader who is prudent, from small things and things not sufficiently brought to exactness, if he is strong in excellent natural talent, will be able to discover more polished things. Whatever visible thing is set in the whole circle of the universe gives ready support for the service of this new art of ours. Air and fire, water and earth,

earth, springs and rivers, trees, fruits, flowers, grasses, words, names, accident, substance, stones, sand, dung, filth, things, terms, honorable things, modest things, wood, metals, hard things, liquid things, existing things and non-existing things, history, fable, beasts, brute animals, human beings, draft animals, each and all things that either speech can express or the soul think, furnish the very broad field of our Polygraphia, and promise that the art, so far as it can stand for mortals, will have to be enlarged to infinity. For just as, from unity through the binary, the ternary generates the perfect number in the quaternary, so the consummation of this invention, standing through the quaternary in the binary as a twice-perfect root, gives us an infinite number beneath the minutiae of the things of the paraphrases of Polygraphia. For although among us all our things are terminable, nevertheless many things often occur which seem infinite to us because of the slowness of our understanding. Who will be able to count the drops of the sea? And who will penetrate the inscrutable heart of man? Just as a finite, circumscribable number can, by progression, be led into infinity, so the instruction of this art is enlarged by varied metathesis into immensity. Whatever is seen anywhere in the whole world is in every respect conformed to our instruction, nor is there anything known to us that is empty of mystery, if the method is understood by a master. But we, persuaded by reason, stand under the service of the binary and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor is there given an occasion by which we might raise our attempt to weightier matters; if it should be offered to us acceptably, we shall not refuse to make more important things manifest to those who desire them.
