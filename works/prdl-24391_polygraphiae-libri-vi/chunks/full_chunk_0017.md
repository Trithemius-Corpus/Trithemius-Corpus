There is also another kind, dactyletron, not of writing, however, but of signs, by which one person can very subtly communicate to another the conception of his mind. This too is varied by so many differences that there is no one in the world who could enumerate all the species of so great a mystery. In the principal method at once, and in many others descending from it, artfully, while sitting at table, or in any other place whatever, very quietly and without anyone’s suspicion. Finally, that you may announce the secret of your mind without letters to one absent, even without the knowledge of the very person who performs the office of messenger, remains assigned to the inventions of Steganography. For indeed, since many affairs arise for kings and princes, of which they sometimes do not trust themselves to give knowledge safely enough by ordinary letters to their deputies far away,

it would be worthwhile for each of them to have certain special methods of writing and reporting, by which they may be able to make their secrets known most safely to friends, especially in these times, in which, with license for sinning prevailing, nowhere in the world is fidelity found safe. The messengers of princes are seized everywhere, are plundered by savage robbers, beaten, and very often killed; nor do the impious plunderers spare even the letters of kings, sealed and closed with rings. Hence it would be necessary for your commonwealth, O most glorious Caesar Maximilian, that you put on the skin of Hercules, wield the club in your hands, and purge for yourself from these savage monsters the world entrusted to you by God. There will never be peace in the kingdom unless [unclear] are removed from its midst. He who disturbs the peace of the kingdom shows himself to be the king’s enemy. *Brief Explanation of Those Things Which Seem More Obscure in the First Book of the Polygraphy.*

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphy, nevertheless, lest its composition perhaps seem profound to simpler persons, we briefly explain those things which are thought more difficult. We have distributed the words of the Latin language in order through nine [unclear] under almost four hundred alphabets, so arranged in sequence that, if from any alphabet one only is taken after another from beginning to end, cohering with one another, they will make a fitting Latin sentence. For it will be a very suitable method of writing, most secretly, whatever we wish, since each word represents the letter placed before it at the head. Nor let it trouble you, reader, that a multitude of words in the open contains a brief sense in secret, since in this childish arrangement of ours there lies hidden a loftier method of writing more broadly and much more secretly whatever you wish, which public reason has forbidden us to make plain to all. But he who is endowed with talent, and applies the powers necessary for inquiry, will find subtler things among the doctrines hidden in these matters, and will clearly understand more wondrous things, marvelously unknown before.
