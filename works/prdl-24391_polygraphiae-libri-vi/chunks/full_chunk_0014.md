But lest an occasion be given to the depraved and perverse, who even more often abuse good inventions, for deceiving the simpler and less cautious, we set forth in it mystical or hidden [methods] of writing, and covered the truth lying hidden in the marrow with certain veils of figures. Yet we by no means doubt that any industrious man, desirous of these matters, will easily grasp everything, if he applies even a little persistence in investigating. For the things that I have transmitted are not so difficult, nor wrapped in such great mysteries, that they cannot be wholly penetrated by a studious man. For everywhere we have so tempered the enigmas of our words that they are easily accessible to learned and studious men, while nevertheless remaining inaccessible to sluggish men of darkness. But after it pleased me to offer the work of the *Polygraphia* itself, dedicated to the most serene and most unconquered Maximilian, Caesar of the Romans, ever august emperor of the world, I thought it would be worth the effort if, with a few brief explanations, I made it bright and open, lest the mind of the sacred Majesty, which is daily stretched by many affairs and cares, be drawn back from understanding it by the obscurity of the mysteries. For unless my opinion deceives me, the work will be the more pleasing and useful to his majesty the briefer and clearer it is. First, therefore, with the Lord granting it, we shall briefly explain the meaning of our intention, which is contained in the Prologue wrapped in various enigmas; then, through the individual books of the *Polygraphia*, we shall succinctly unfold, according to the order, the methods of working, the rules, and the distinctions, and the necessary matters that remain, to the best of our ability. All these things, so that they may more quickly become accessible to those wishing to know, we shall forthwith show by examples, and we shall be briefer insofar as the matter and reason permit.

*Explanation of the Prologue.*

Our whole intention in the entire work of the *Polygraphia* turns upon this purpose: that we should transmit certain methods of writing secretly, by which kings and princes, nobles and commoners, priests and laymen, learned and unlearned alike, may safely, without any suspicion of a hidden secret, communicate to one another, and to any friends, even those far distant from each other, by the service of letters, whatever concept of their minds they need, as often as there is need. And lest we seem to have presumed to be either the first or the only ones to do this, we forewarn the reader that formerly many of the ancients, especially tyrants, princes, and kings, moved by a similar consideration, devised for themselves familiar and various methods and canons of writing, by which [unclear] that which they themselves attempted universally. From their number, for the sake of an example of the matter proposed, we first introduced Caesar Augustus; understand his hidden method of writing, made manifest, in this way. He changed the breathings of the conceived voice from clear signs into obscure ones, whose resistant barbarism of sound, by a certain beautiful invention, through the dance of metathesis, he made suitable to his command. That is: the great Caesar Augustus, so that he might write secretly and safely what he wished, changed the letters of the alphabet by varied transposition, so that he put B for A, C [for B], and so consequently for each letter. And so that the writing might remain more secret by twisted teaching, according to his own judgment he transposed the letters and transferred them through a long [unclear] to whatever order, number, place, and distance.
