*Preface to Maximilian I Caesar*

...when once they were cruelly devastating Gaul, they summoned bubones from Greece and prepared a safe hiding-place for their secrets, which today is known to few.

Our Bede, a monk in England, not ignorant of the deed of the Normans, described many inventions of the ancients similar to these, by which they made a dark road bright with mysteries. He first commended artrology to the memory of posterity, and invented dactylogy. There were those who, by visible accidents, discerned minute things; by their prudence night was turned into day, and without the service of bubones light shone in darkness. Concerning these things, moreover, the illustrious Count Palatine, Prince Elector, once the requester of our steganography, received from us a manifest proof of instruction.

But others, multiplying minutiae by regulated division, after the manner of barbarians overturned the patterns of nature, concealing in darkness the dances of arising shadows, and, where the secret of counsel required it, supplied the cabala to the absent through diapyromata.

Great labor is involved in these things, and [unclear] long exercise of the mind, in which no one suddenly becomes skilled for use. Necessity endures all things. There were also those whom celestial harmony taught to compose foreign words, of which there was a double use for those who understood: while one sense holds in suspense, the other lifts the mind into the ether. God can do all things. Many have tried to seek light in shadow without light, but they were quite unable, because the fire lies buried in ashes. Some practised painters of phantoms divided one wind into two at a point: one of them, a misophos, always lurks in darkness, while the other, a lover of light, a philosophos, hates darkness, loves light, and guards the secret by means of a bubo. Again, with Bede there were some witnesses whose advancing intention bound the wind, invisible to the eyes, to visible things, and compelled many plants, and no less stones and fruits, which lack voices, to speak with a human tongue. Why should I bring in brute creatures, when all things obey art? This secret is rightly preferred to all inventions thus far, through which the hidden mystery will gain strength for the small, and by no means commit pearls to swine. From nothing, nothing is brought back. The known multitude of visible things, if these are prudently adapted to them, the enlightened mind of man would know and behold wonders. But to treat more fully of these matters is not part of the present purpose; since they are greater and weightier, they require another work and another time. Matthias, the most powerful king of Hungary, a hammer harder than adamant of the cross of Christ, victorious, waged battles; with wondrous ingenuity and art he discovered many things, adorned bubones with periphrasmata, kept his own mysteries, secreted them with manifold variety, and by manifest industry destroyed the counsels of his enemies. He preferred wisdom to gold; therefore a wise, sound mind in a well-disciplined body, driven upward by desire for a higher understanding, when by the likeness of the binary it has returned from the ternary into unity, will thereafter easily rise to the denary.

Pythagoras of Samos, that philosopher, warned his disciples that the quaternary must not be forgotten, in which the first perfection of numbering is contained. For thus we count: one, two, three, four. Stop your mind: for you have ten. For the denary is the head of perfection. What are eleven, except the denary and unity? What is twenty? Twice ten. What is thirty? Three times ten. You have a hundred, and you have by no means departed from the quaternary. And what is a hundred, except ten times ten? In these things mysteries lie hidden in abundance, and they are manifest to few. For love of brevity we pass over many in silence, who, aiding nature by art, applied prudence to secrets, and what to many who did not know seemed impossible they brought by shrewd study into easy use. A cultivated mind can do great things, and he who cleaves to God becomes, by understanding, higher than the world. Many are the mysteries of kings, and their counsels are full of cares, in which confidence and faith are required; rare faith harms the believer, and often admits even doubt when it is present. Therefore it would be worth the trouble and risk for all princes to be philosophers, and endowed with singular wisdom: just, gentle, benevolent, and distinguished in munificence, so that they cannot easily be overthrown by others. Justice alone, the companion of prudence, makes a prince glorious. To the aforementioned Prince and lord Elector...
