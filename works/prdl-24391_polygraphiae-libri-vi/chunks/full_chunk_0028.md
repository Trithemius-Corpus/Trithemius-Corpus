On this method of writing one may consult Suetonius Tranquillus in the Life of Augustus, ch. 88, and in the Life of Julius Caesar, ch. 86. “There are extant,” he says, “also to Cicero, likewise to his friends, on private matters” (letters, that is), “in which, if any things had to be conveyed more secretly, he wrote them by signs, that is, with the order of the letters so arranged that no word could be formed; if anyone wished to investigate these, he should substitute the fourth letter of the elements, that is, d for a, and so likewise exchange the rest.” Moreover Isidore also makes mention of a letter of Augustus to his son, in which he writes thus: “Since innumerable matters continually arise which each of us ought to write to the other, and to keep secret, let us have signs between us, if you wish, such that, when anything has to be written by us, for each letter we write the following one, in this way: b for a, c for b, and then the rest in the same manner. But for the letter z one must return to double aa.”

Archimedes of Syracuse.) Similar to this is what is read in Gellius, book 17, chapter 9. When the ancient Lacedaemonians wished to disguise and conceal letters sent publicly to commanders, so that, if they were intercepted by enemies, their plans might not be known, they sent letters made in this fashion. There were two rounded, rather oblong rods, of equal thickness and the same length, smoothed and prepared in like fashion. One was given to the commander as he set out for war; the other the magistrates kept at home, with their authority and with the seal. When there was need of more secret letters, around that rod they wound a long strip of leather of moderate thinness, or as much as was sufficient for the matter, in a simple round coil, so that the edges of the leather that was being folded, joined closely on every side, met together. Then they inscribed the letters on that leather across the transverse edges of the joins, in lines proceeding from the top.

When the strip had thus been written over with letters, they unwound it from the rod and sent it to the commander, who was aware of that device. The unwinding of the leather likewise rendered the letters broken and mutilated, and the strokes and points of their parts were scattered into very different places. Therefore, if that leather fell into the hands of enemies, they could conjecture nothing at all from it. But when the man to whom it had been sent received it, taking the matching rod which he had, he wound it around to the end, just as he knew it ought to be done; and so the letters, coming together along the same circumference of the rod, again met, and supplied an intact and uncorrupted letter, easy to read. The Lacedaemonians call this kind of letter a skytale. See also Plutarch in the Life of Lysander.
