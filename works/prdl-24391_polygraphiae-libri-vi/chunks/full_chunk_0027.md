*The Apology of Johannes Trithemius, Prefixed to the Steganography*

It is the opinion of many that the ancient philosophers and wise men of art and nature, if they had discovered any secrets, concealed them by various methods and figures, lest they should come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews also affirm that Moses, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his account of the creation of heaven and earth, covered ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words. Saint Jerome too, the most learned among our own, says that in the Apocalypse there lie hidden almost as many mysteries as words. I pass over the wise men of the Greeks, men of no small repute and glory among their own people, who, applying themselves to the writing of fables, by prudent invention handed down one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men in the course of a single narrative. Although, because of the slowness of my intellect, I cannot perfectly imitate these most zealous lovers of wisdom, yet I judge it a beautiful and glorious thing to admire them, and, with what diligence I can, to read and venerate them. Thus, when I consider that they, by their own study, discovered great things beyond other men, I may, applying a certain violence to myself, impel myself to some imitation of them, even among the very latest.

*Apology*

Nor, as I think, has my opinion wholly deceived me, since through the continual study of reading I have both learned many things that I had not known before, and opened an entrance for my thoughts to investigate more secret matters. For if I am not of such great learning or industry that I dare profess that I have grasped that method of the ancient sages for concealing mysteries, nevertheless I have devised certain methods, not altogether to be despised, as it seems to me, of writing secretly and concealing [whatever you wish], entirely without any suspicion; and these, at the urgent request of the most serene prince, lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and Prince Elector of the Roman Empire, the wisest Maecenas of philosophers, than whom no one has seemed to me more worthy to have this great secret revealed to him, I have committed to writing and reduced into the present volume, not without immense labor, lest this great secret should come to the ears of common unlearned men or of wicked persons. I beg you, reader, whoever you may be in the future, to remember how many evils could be done in the commonwealth by evil and wanton men through this science, if it came to the knowledge of many: adulteries, fornications, conspiracies, betrayals, homicides, robberies, and countless evils. For just as a good and honorable man can, whenever he wishes, securely and without any suspicion from anyone, for private or common advantage, make known and express the secret of his will to another who knows this art, so any perverse, wanton, malicious person, with every obstacle entirely removed, however shamefully, [unclear] of his pleasure, will be able to communicate his evil desire without any suspicion. Nor could trust among married persons any longer remain safe thereafter, since a woman, though previously ignorant of the Latin language, made most learned through holy and chaste words of any language or idiom, could understand very fully the evil and immodest will of her lover, even while her husband reads and praises the letters, and in the same way could send back to him her own desire, as broadly as she wished and most securely, in quite eloquent speech. Indeed, although this science may seem childish to some, nevertheless, if it were made public, the whole order of the commonwealth would be disturbed, public trust would perish, letters, documents, writings, and finally all the very speech of men would be turned into perpetual suspicion. No one would any longer trust letters, however good, honorable, and holy; no one would put faith in epistles, since, however holy and chaste the words might be, nevertheless deceit, fraud, and evil deception would always be thought to lie beneath them; and men would become fearful in all things, and no less suspicious of mutual conversations than of any letters. Nor can anyone become so learned and experienced in this very science of writing secretly and most hiddenly [unclear] sufficient for all things, which he himself has not yet grasped.
