XII. Twelfth, kings and princes should note that, if they have many deputies in different places, it is not prudent to write to all of them with one kind of hidden narrative. Therefore, so that they may have for each person his own secret mode of writing, let them change the order of the words as many times as they wish to have separate modes. Thus they themselves will possess the modes of writing of all, and none of their subjects will understand the mode of another. For the variable modes of writing more secretly by this art are infinite.

XIII. Thirteenth, it should be noted that we, in order to stop the mouths of those who speak wickedly, [have shown?] the possible truth of this new invention of ours, placing the words in the alphabets of the first [book?] so that they cohere with one another and produce a fitting Latin prayer in the manner of an exhortation and supplication offered to God. Beneath whose hidden narrative [unclear], and to write in such a way to a friend skilled in the art that, if all the men of the whole world came together in one place, they could never find the secret lying hidden beneath the open discourse, provided it had been rightly arranged.

XIIII. Fourteenth, it should be known that in this very invention of our Polygraphy there still lie hidden certain things far better than those written above: namely, how you will be able, in a few days, not to say hours, to instruct a man ignorant of the Latin language so that he knows how to write, read, speak, and understand Latin, not indeed universally in all things, but in a certain manner whatever he needs in his affairs concerning any subject whatever. Likewise, how you may make known to your friend, far absent and knowing the practice of the art, all the conceptions of your mind without writings, and with the messenger unaware, securely, safely, and most secretly, sometimes even without a messenger, naturally and without any superstition, danger, or fear. But let those things remain buried in their hiding places with the rest. The reason has moved us, since it neither pleases me nor is at all advantageous to cast precious stones to the wind and to accomplish difficult things without the fruit of gratitude. Finally, beneath these things there lie hidden no small mysteries of subtlety concerning medicines, herbs, plants, precious stones, rivers, the members of the human body, brute animals, cosmography, astronomy and the seven liberal arts, and also concerning all those things that are in human use, and concerning the universal world, both of visible and invisible things. And, lest we be more prolix than is right, all things that are in the world, by extension of the likeness of the previously shown art of Polygraphy, serve a mystical lesson.

XV. Fifteenth, note that all these things that we have handed down as discovered in the Polygraphy are certain small introductions in comparison with those that still remain to be discovered and written in this very faculty; concerning them we pass over in silence, lest we seem to boast in vain. For we have given to suitable minds an occasion for discovering greater things than we have either written or perhaps been able to discover. Let him who can add, for the completion of this invention, those things that he knows to be useful; but let him who cannot, by no means rashly judge the things he does not understand.

*An explanation of those things which seemed rather obscure in the second book of the Polygraphy.*

In the second book of the Polygraphy, apart from the order and sequence of the words, nothing new is handed down from the first; and therefore one and the same manner of operating will apply in both, and we carefully admonish that all things which we said must be observed in the first must also be observed in the second.
