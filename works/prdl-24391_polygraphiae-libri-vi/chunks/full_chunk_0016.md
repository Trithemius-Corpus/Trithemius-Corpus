*Explanation*

...their kings, from the time when, in the year 430 before the Nativity of Christ, they left the Scythian borders with their people and settled at the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is, waged continual war with the Gauls for almost nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject to themselves, as we wrote in the first volume of our Annals. Of these, the aforesaid King Pharamund, in order to send messengers securely, used not a few but many new alphabets, which no one [understood?] without his voluntary instruction.

*Mystery.* Clodius Crinitus, son of Pharamund and after him king of the Franks, who subdued all Gaul to himself after destroying the Romans whom he found in it, used his father’s inventions in secret writings.

*Explanation.* This king devised alphabets of his own, new ones, and gave one to each of his leaders, while he alone retained knowledge of them all in his own possession.

*Mystery.* Charles, surnamed the Great, the sixty-third king of the Franks, who first among the Franks wore the diadem of empire, and the rest.

*Explanation.* This man, the elder son of King Pippin, is read to have been born duke of Brabant in the county of Namur, adjoining the people of Liege, although some assert that he was born in the village of Ingelheim, not far from Mainz. He too invented various modes of writing, and, with the grammarian Nanno providing assistance, devised different alphabets, by which he communicated secrets to the individual governors of the kingdom, each in a distinct manner.

*In the Prologue.*

*Mystery.* The Normans, who once came out from the borders of Gothia, when they were devastating Gaul in a cruel invasion, summoned owls from Greece and prepared a safe hiding-place for their secrets, and the rest.

*Explanation.* The Normans, wandering through Gaul, as the monk Bede in England bears witness, in order to write their secrets safely among themselves, substituted Greek numerals for Latin letters, from A, the first, as far as the twenty-fourth point, namely w or gu, according to the custom of the Gauls. This mode of writing is altogether most beautiful: in it all the letters are Greek, and according to the Pelasgian order of numbering they designate our Latin letters, as A, 1; B, 3; D, 4; E, 5; [unclear], 6; G, 7; and so consequently as far as w, which is 24. Neither a Greek nor a Latin will understand this writing unless he has first learned the method and the art of the practice.

*Mystery.* Our Bede, a monk in England, not ignorant of the Normans’ deed, described several ancient inventions similar to these, by which they made a way luminous in mysteries obscure. He himself [wrote on] artrology and the rest.

*Explanation.* This monk Bede, in his little book on the inventions of the ancients, reviewed many and various ways of writing and marking secretly, by which they safely announced their secrets to friends and spoke without voice to those present. He was the first of all to commit artrology and dactylogy to writing; by their use one person most securely discloses to another whatever secret he has, which none of those sitting by will notice, and much less understand. Bede himself taught that this same thing can be done in many other ways as well; since we have written nothing about these in the Polygraphy, we rightly decline to say more about them here. We long ago assigned the proper place for these difficult matters in the Steganography, which we do not yet think of publishing. And who could set forth all the methods of secret writing used by the ancients? For some had as familiars in the service of the art set forth above owls called Philophoti; others had Misophoti; others summoned both into practice according to the art. Again, some transposed the known letters of the common alphabet; others, under the form of barbarian letters, devised characters of their own for themselves. Again also, some used the service of visible letters, writing with minium or common encaustic; others formed invisible characters at will according to their pleasure. But the modes of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many and wholly innumerable, and to mention them all is neither within our will nor within our power. Among mortals today, however, three kinds of invisible characters are more commonly practiced. The first is called Dormatic, which is a certain artificial writing made on the back of messengers, visible only through washing with a certain liquid; no one can penetrate it who does not know the secret of the art.
