Alchemy has many household attendants, who guard their mistress with perpetual vigilance and present themselves under her name, so that they may preserve her, untouched for all time, from intercourse with so many importunate lovers. Vanity, fraud, craftiness, deception, sophistry, greed, falsehood, lying confidence, folly, want, poverty, despair, flight, proscription, and beggary are the handmaids of alchemy; pretending that their mistress is beloved, they keep her inviolate, and readily prostitute themselves to those moneyed, avaricious, greedy, and foolish seekers after her.

*On the Notes and the Wondrous, but Excessively Laborious, Method of Writing of M. T. Cicero, and after him of St. Cyprian, Bishop and Martyr.*

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, wrote a book of notes of no small size. St. Cyprian, bishop of the Carthaginians and martyr, enlarged it with many notes for words, adding terms necessary for the uses of Christians, so that the work itself became useful not only to pagans, but much more also to the faithful. The codex is rare; I have found it only once, and bought it for a low price. For when, in the year of the Lord's nativity 1496, I was examining several libraries out of love of books, I found the aforementioned codex in a certain monastery of our order, neglected because of its great age, thrown under dust and despised. I asked the abbot, a doctor of law, at how much he valued it. He answered that he would prefer to it the little works of St. Anselm recently printed. I went to the booksellers, since the matter occurred in a metropolitan city; I bought the requested little works of Anselm for the sixth part of a florin, handed them over to the rejoicing abbot and monks, and rescued the codex, which was already close to perishing. For they had decided, out of love of the parchment, that it should be scraped. Almost two years after this, I went up on horseback to Strasbourg on affairs of my order and, being admitted by Johannes Keisersberg, the distinguished preacher of the place, into the library of the major church, I found an entire Psalter written out in the same notes of Tullius and Cyprian, and most elegantly written with golden chapter initials. But the title had been placed on the outside by someone ignorant of the mystery as follows: “Psalter in the Armenian language.” I brought in a scholar, showed the falsity, and advised that it be rewritten thus: “Psalter written in Ciceronian notes.” Whether he did so or not I am uncertain, since I did not afterwards return to that library.
