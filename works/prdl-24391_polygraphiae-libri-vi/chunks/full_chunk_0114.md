*From the Grammatical Writings of Otfrid, Monk of Weissenburg*

We read that Charles, king of the Franks, surnamed the Great, taking pity on the barbarism of his nation, attempted, with Nanno, Theobald, Albinus, and Berenger as helpers, to regulate the German language. And when, as Turpin bears witness, he had begun so arduous a work, he did not complete it, being delayed by frequent incursions of wars, but, overtaken by death, left it unfinished; nor did another arise after him who would bring the care of this matter back to mind.

But the aforesaid Otfrid, a monk, formerly a hearer and disciple of Rabanus of Fulda, under Louis the Pious, son of that Charles, having acquired that grammar, though imperfectly, wrote many things in our German language, which seem to sound forth the admirable weight of regular instruction. For he composed many poems according to that same standard, in heroic meter and also in elegiac, and in addition composed some works in prose, addressed to Louis, king of Germany, son of Louis the Pious and brother of the emperor Lothar; also to Otgar, archbishop of Mainz, to the monks of Saint Gall, and to others.

Having obtained fragments of this grammar, I too extracted these present characters of the alphabet from many, lest they utterly perish. It is established, moreover, on Otfrid’s testimony, that Charles devised several alphabets of his own, which he might use securely, throughout his very broad kingdom, in secret matters with individual prefects. From these, with Otfrid supplying them, we entrust a few things to these letters.

Charles the Great, most Christian king and emperor, waged war with the Saxons for no fewer than thirty years, whom at last, overcoming them by the sword, he converted to the Christian faith. But fearing that they would again apostatize from the faith, as they had done several times, he appointed certain secret investigators, to whom he granted judicial power, so that, traveling through all Saxony, they should secretly and diligently inquire concerning the faith and morals of the people; and whoever they found to have apostatized from the faith, robbers, adulterers, blasphemers, despisers of the Church and of its priests and commandments, or those disturbing the Christian commonwealth with notorious crimes, or recalling or soliciting the people to paganism, they should without delay, by imperial and royal authority, hang with a noose with impunity, or otherwise kill as they could.

And so that this institution might remain forever unshaken, he gave the same men power also to appoint others, suitable under certain conditions, who, enjoying the aforesaid faculty, should exercise with impunity the office of inquiry and of death against the guilty. Finally, he prescribed for them secret laws and hidden signs, together with a form of oath, by which they might proceed justly in judging and punishing, and, being mutually known to one another, might remain hidden from others, and preserve forever the necessary, more secret judgment in Saxon land. They also used certain alphabets among themselves for a time, all of which at length entirely fell out of use. Nevertheless the office of this sort of inquisition remains down to the present time; its ministers are commonly called Feimeri.

Although these characters do not appear sufficiently beautiful and elegant, nevertheless it pleased me to join them with the aforesaid new inventions of mine, because of honor and reverence for antiquity, since there is no one even a little diligent who could not devise more beautiful ones for himself according to his own judgment. Yet continued practice in writing will be able, with easy compensation, to correct this misshapen crookedness, and, when frequently brought into habit, to make it pleasing. For no one, however learned or skilled in any language, also becomes a good writer unless by long practice he has transformed use into art. Another follows.

If anyone wishes to fashion a new alphabet for himself according to his own judgment, he should know that two things in particular must be observed, which nevertheless I do not always find preserved in the foregoing. The first is that he form characters easy to handle; and the simpler they are, the better they will be. The second is that he arrange the individual letters in such a way that, when joined into words, they present a beautiful and elegant appearance. I add a third, not promised: if you want the writing to remain secret, make some letters of the alphabet double, in the manner of the Hebrews.
