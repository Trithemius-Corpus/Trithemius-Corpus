To the seven Planets we have assigned alphabets singly, from the numbers remaining after the twelve signs. And note: when in speech a letter is doubled, its number is not written twice, but once, and marked above with a point.

The Sixth and last book of our Polygraphy we have, by God's mercy, brought to its conclusion; in which, concluding our discourse with the wandering stars, we forewarn the reader on certain points. First, that as often as he uses the alphabet of any sign or planet, he should prudently add the character of the same, for the sake of a twofold usefulness. For by this the excessive labor is removed from the friendly Reader, [and] the mystery will remain in secret, while anyone ignorant of the art will suppose the table written in numbers to pertain to signs or planets, [and will] not suspect that any mystery at all lies hidden. [...the source here is corrupt...] For the shrewd Reader, who is touched by suspicion of something hidden, if he detects the function of a single number, will easily reckon out the whole alphabet. Secondly, remember—whoever you are that desire to be a shrewd operator in this matter—that [a doubled letter] is written but once and marked above with a point. As, for example: I wish to write "Ioannes" from the alphabet of Aries; I do not double 18, which is n, but write thus: 14, 16, 7, 18, 10, 23; or "abbas": 6, 7, 6, 23. With "Ioannes" [and] "abbas" one must act with like caution; for unless the operator be endowed with diligence, and know how to find greater things from small ones,

he will not be a fit one to whom these least things of ours ought to be opened. Not everywhere are all things to be communicated to all, especially [those] whose use is convertible to good and ill. Moved reasonably by this consideration, we have plainly handed down very much in these six books of Polygraphy to those willing to know, but we have allowed far more secrets to lie hidden beneath what is manifest. Finally we have written a Key unfolding the whole work, so that in each [part] matter for exercising the memory may not be lacking, [while] from the reprobate—who are not willing to use them well—the cause of knowing these things may be taken away. Many mysteries lie hidden in single passages: the greater [things] you find from these, the more ample remain to be found. A way is prepared for beginners, in which, for those who advance, it will not be difficult, in the course of time, to find many rare and very great things. Here he who knew not will learn to write in Latin, [and] prudently to wrap all the mysteries of his will in a foreign tongue. But that he may understand not only his own mysteries which the unlearned man wraps up, but also the open speech of which he was hitherto always ignorant, it is a matter of no great labor, if the paraphrast shall have fully attained our intention in this volume. For beneath the bark lie the kernels of our Steganography, buried by reason, lest they lie open to unworthy babblers. Finally it was worth the labor that the work should not [pre]judge another,

but that to each one his own right should be distributed inviolate. Wearied after a long voyage, here in this sea we fix our anchor under the name of Jesus Christ, Savior of the faithful, on the twenty-first day of the month of March, in the year of the Lord's nativity one thousand five hundred and eight, in the eleventh Roman indiction, at Peapolis, city of the eastern Franks, which they also call Würzburg and Herbipolis. THE END.
