Nor do I think it at all necessary to recall to memory the things that have been recited in the preceding books, since a discerning reader, if he is studious, can, with the Lord’s help, discover even the greater things that lie hidden in these. We have handed over to the wise and studious man both the material and the reason; if he wishes to use them prudently, he will surpass our work by his own talent. Let anyone whom these things delight keep fixed in memory that he must always remain within the prescribed circle; if he passes beyond it in any way, the whole operation vanishes uselessly. There are many names of men, and finally there are almost infinite terms for things, villages, stones, and words, all of which serve our purposes. From these it is clear that a book and a method can be made, not impossible, if talent is at hand with diligence, which may surpass our inventions without limit. But he who is of such dullness that he cannot understand these childish things of ours, when will he penetrate the more subtle matters that remain? *The fifth book of the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, begins.*

Those things which, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have arranged as best we could in the four preceding books for the completion of our *Polygraphy*, reasonably require that in the fifth book we briefly discuss the remaining precepts of this instruction, the methods that still remain. Therefore in it we shall give a sufficiently compendious method by metathesis, that is, the transposition of letters, by which a friend may securely, safely, and most secretly write to a friend whatever he wishes. We read that Caesar Augustus used that method of writing in secret matters. Although this method of sending messages is in many ways secure, nevertheless to observers it is always regarded with suspicion, because as soon as the transposition of letters is seen, anyone who is curious at once suspects that something lies hidden beneath. Now the methods of this invention are almost infinite; from these, for a necessary introduction, we have marked out the following for the reader, reserving the rest to be investigated by his talent. First, therefore, we have placed at the head a canonical table of this practice, beneath which we have set another, reversed. In these two tables the root and foundation of the aforementioned transposition is entirely placed, upon which the structure of the whole building is set. For when these two tables have been properly known and understood, no transposition of letters will be able to escape one who works diligently. To these we have joined twenty-four alphabets in order, so that we might give the reader abundant means of transposing letters. The use of these consists, according to one’s wish, in each individually, or, if you prefer, also in all together. If you have secret correspondence with many people, distribute the individual alphabets to your individual acquaintances, but commit to memory what you have handed to each one. To the first, indeed, let the first alphabet be entrusted, to the second the second, to the third the third, and so let it be done in sequence with the rest. But if, because of a multitude of business or of household members, the alphabets which we have marked should not suffice for everything, or if some of them seem too open and evident, we shall be able to introduce various new transpositions, the number of which may always remain perpetually hidden. Let us set down an example of this matter: let b be a, let f be b, let p be c, let t be d, let z be f, let c be g, let m be h; so consequently through the whole usual alphabet let it be arranged that between any letter assumed and its substitute three idle and transposed letters lie between. Afterwards four, then five, more or fewer, in such a way, however, that the whole alphabet, as if revolved into a circle, meets together, but each letter is placed for another in its own order in the same series.
