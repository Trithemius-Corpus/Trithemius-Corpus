Mystery. Archimedes, that Syracusan, easily the foremost of mathematicians, his white bird, suited to his instructions, bare through head and feet, etc. Explanation. Archimedes had two little rods of one length and thickness shaped, round or square; one he kept for himself, the other he entrusted to his absent friend. And whenever he wished to write something more secret to the other, he wound his white bird, that is, a sheet of papyrus of small breadth but of the length of the rod doubled, around the wood, and fastened it below and above with wax. Afterwards he wrote on the sheet, joined continuously along the sides into a heap, artfully, whatever he wished; then he loosened the sheet from the rod, and at once so great a distance appeared between the letters that not even one word cohered. He turned it; similarly he wrote. Nor was there anyone ignorant of this device who could have discovered the secret. But the man who had the other little rod, when he had received the sheet, attached it and restored the letters to their former order. Mystery. Marcus Tullius Cicero, [unclear]

[unclear] eloquent orator of the Romans, using ingenuity and art, changed the small elements of speech into signs, and the rest. Explanation. He wrote a book of notes for his son, which the blessed martyr Cyprian later enlarged for the use of Christians, namely in the manner of a dictionary, where according to order first the characters are designated, afterwards the words designated by the same characters are set before them. With these notes or characters we once saw the whole Latin Psalter, beautifully written, in the library of the greater church of Strasbourg, to which some ignorant judge of things had placed outside this title: Psalter in the Armenian language. But he was mistaken, since it should rather have been written thus: Latin Psalter written in Tullian characters. For each character represents one Latin word. Nor does my judgment deceive me, since I have in my possession the very dictionary of Cicero, corrected by Saint Cyprian, written complete and uncorrupted in an ancient volume, which I have seen nowhere else. And so great is the abundance of notes or characters, with the words subject to them, that they suffice entirely for writing in the Latin language whatever you wish. Mystery.
