By the service of this alphabet the Normans wrote, in a secure communication, whatever they wished to keep hidden among themselves. No Greek could understand this method of writing, since, although all these characters are Greek, nevertheless they represent Latin letters according to numerical order; nor could it be understood by a Latin who had not first acquired knowledge of this art. This mode of writing can also be varied in many ways, when by transposition the numbers and the letters are changed. This can also be done with the usual Greek letters, at the wish and discretion of any industrious writer. Nor will the manner of forming these characters be very difficult, especially for one who has been somewhat accustomed to it. The characters written above are also sufficiently suited to the German language; with them we can denote everything that occurs to us, in whatever speech we may be skilled. Whenever iota is placed by itself, so that it may be known to denote the letter k, the tenth of the alphabet, it is marked above.

*On the Alphabet and Method of Writing of the Ancient Franks*

The opinion of many holds that the Germans once had letters from the Greeks, and they also steadfastly affirm that they used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. For they say that great consonance exists between the Greek language and our own, although the long interval of time has utterly extinguished that agreement. That most learned former teacher of mine, Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, the most reverend bishop of the church of Worms, had collected several thousand Greek words which signify the same thing in both languages, Greek and German. And Caesar testifies in the Commentaries that the ancient Druids in Gaul, the priests of sacred rites, used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. But as the Romans devastated the world, they at last violently imposed, together with their dominion, the use of their letters upon many nations.

Finally, the remnants of the Trojans, who afterward were called Franks, in the four hundred and fortieth year before the incarnation of the Lord, having gone out from the territories of the Scythians, with King Marcomer providing leadership, came into Germany and received settlements at the mouths of the Rhine from their brothers the Saxons. Thereafter, for nine hundred and fifty years they waged an almost continuous war with the Romans and the Gauls, as Hunibald is the authority, and at last subjected almost all Europe to their empire or kingdom. Yet they did not abolish the letters of the Romans once they had accepted them; rather, mingled among the Germans and Gauls, they themselves learned their language, and preserved their letters and form of writing.

The writer of the histories of this people, Wastbald, wrote in his native tongue books of the deeds of kings, dukes, and princes through almost seven hundred and fifty-eight years, with the characters appended, as Hunibald says, which he mentions in his volume that he published in eighteen books on the origin of the Franks.
