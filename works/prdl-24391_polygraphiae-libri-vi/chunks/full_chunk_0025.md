*Explanation of Book VI.*

We have shown a sufficient method of counting by Latin letters after the manner of the Hebrews and the Greeks, which we have reasonably placed here by way of digression, although it is also found earlier in the sixth book. But by the fact that, as we proceed, we have here brought our number to a close at thirty times one hundred thousand, we plainly declare that it can be carried on to infinity. If this method of numbers displeases anyone, he will easily be able to devise a better one. Also, in imitation of the Northmen, from the aforesaid numerical letters we have arranged a new alphabet, by which you will safely be able to write to an absent friend and report whatever secret you wish. This manner of writing is indeed so secret, as well as new, and seems never to have been seen or heard of before us, that it appears impossible that it could be understood by any servant of the teacher. But now let us set down its alphabet.

Behold, you now have here an alphabet from Roman numerical letters, in imitation of the Northmen, with which you will be able most safely to write and report to an absent friend whatever you wish; and no one lying hidden will be able to penetrate your secret, except the one who knows the secret of the art. For all who see the writing will think that the letters have been transposed, and they will labor in vain in reckoning them. Let us set down an example. laaihid ifeib, ieifeigeicchkib, cakeibid. i kahb gidibid. eigih ieeigigh .kif. iaahiﬁd ibeidaka.eih. iceikai b. The hidden meaning of this writing is as follows: Beware the bearer of these presents, because he is a very bad man, a thief, a robber, a liar, worthless. By the demonstration of this example it is easily given to be understood how safe and secure this manner of writing is universally, in every language known to the writer, in every cause or business, however difficult, secret, great, or lengthy. Nor do I think that there is any man living in the world who, by his own wit and without a teacher, could read this very writing. For all who see it will think nothing other than that the letters have been transposed; and since they have been unable to obtain an understanding of them, at last, wearied by despair, they will cease from investigation, and whatever you have written will remain hidden. For no one, as I think, will weigh out this contrivance of writing secretly by his own powers just as it is, unless he has either the Polygraphy, or this key to it, at hand, or has been instructed by another. From these numerical letters, too, many other alphabets can be given according to the order of counting, when the beginning for writing alphabetically is taken not from one but from any following number. But in that progression you should know that care must be taken lest more than two numerical letters be taken to represent one letter. Hence, when you have come to 1000, that is, ten times one hundred thousand, halt your step and do not wander farther, lest the multitude of letters, by marring it, confuse the beauty of the writing. For after one has been excluded and ten times one hundred thousand letters included, there are enough for almost thirty individual alphabets. Yet the method of the first alphabet, beginning with one, is the more beautiful for wrapping secrets in writing in proportion as it is shorter and simpler. In short, it renders the writing less tortuous.
