*Brief Explanation of the Sixth and Last Book of the Polygraphia.*

In the sixth and last book of the Polygraphia we have gathered together certain characters of the ancients, which they used in place of letters, so that, if anyone wishes to employ from them an alphabet of his own, he may be able to do so, although a moderately skilled person, if he wishes, can devise finer characters for himself. All the synthemata of this book are sufficiently open and clear; therefore they need no explanation, or only a brief one. Yet those things which stand forth as newly invented by us require a definite declaration, lest perhaps they seem to have been set down without reason.

First of all, we made all the letters of the Latins numerical, after the manner of the Hebrews and Greeks, thinking it shameful for us if we should be found inferior to them in this respect. For what of the fact that we doubled certain letters, so that they might suffice for an infinite number, when the Jews long ago did the very same thing, compelled by necessity? And the Greeks, because of the deficiency of letters, admitted certain marks into the number, since they could not double the letters. But we, because we are pressed by a like deficiency of letters, admitted two marks, namely this one, &, which our scribes put for “and”; nor does it matter whether it is written thus, &, or thus, et, provided that it represents six in number. We added another, 9, which our people write indifferently in abbreviation for con and com, and here, in the order of numbers, it represents six hundred.

But by the same necessity we doubled two letters, namely r, z, s, of which r signifies ninety, while the curved z signifies one hundred, the long s two hundred, and the closed s three hundred. Moreover the doubled w, which we placed in the order of the alphabet on account of the nature of the German language, represents the last letter, and in the series of numbers one thousand. Let us set down a tabular example of this new invention of ours for the reckoning of numbers, so that anyone who wishes to know the method may have a definite instruction before his eyes.
