*The second kind, Hyphasmaticon.* The most learned man Theodorus Bibliander has “hyphalmicon,” for this reason, as I suppose: that in this sort of concealment nothing of the letters appears before the paper has been immersed in water, as when we write letters with alum; water poured over it will make the paper darker with the letters, which otherwise could not have been read.

*Letters are likewise written in various ways.* Herodotus, book 7, near the end. After Xerxes determined to undertake an expedition against Greece, Demaratus, who was at Susa, when he had learned this, judged that the Greeks ought to be informed. But since he could not do it otherwise, since it was dangerous lest he be caught, he devised this thing: having taken a pair of writing-tablets, he scraped off the wax from them. Then on their wood he engraved the king’s plan. When this had been engraved in letters, he covered it again with wax, so that the tablets would present no difficulty among the guards on the roads for the man to whom they were being carried. When the Lacedaemonians [unclear] to interpret it, before, as I understand, Gorgo, daughter of Cleomenes and wife of Leonidas, warned them, interpreting the matter itself, bidding them break off the wax, for they would find letters in the wood. Thus, listening to her, the Lacedaemonians found the letters and read them over, and then sent them on to the Greeks.

Gellius, in book 17, chapter 9: “There is also,” he says, “in the records of Greek affairs another deep and unexpected hiding-place, devised by barbarian cunning. There was a man named Histiaeus, born in a place in Asia that was not undistinguished. At that time King Darius held Asia under his rule. When this Histiaeus was in Persia at Darius’s court, he wanted to announce certain secret matters to a certain Aristagoras by a furtive writing. He devised this admirable covering for letters: on his slave, who had for a long time had diseased eyes, [unclear] his head bare, and he fitted it into the forms of letters. With these letters he wrote out what he had wished. Afterwards he kept the man at home until his hair grew back; when this had happened, he ordered him to go to Aristagoras. ‘And when you come to him,’ he said, ‘say that I have instructed him to shave your head, just as I myself recently did.’ The slave came to Aristagoras as he had been ordered and reported his master’s command; and he, thinking that what had been commanded was not without purpose, did what had been ordered. Thus the letters were delivered.” Herodotus writes almost the same thing; and in that author, too, in book 1, it is read: When Astyages was harsh toward the Medes, Harpagus, insinuating himself among each one of them, persuaded the leading men that Astyages ought to be removed from the kingdom and Cyrus chosen. When Harpagus had carried these things through and prepared them, then at last, wishing to disclose his mind to Cyrus, who was among the Persians, since he could not do so otherwise, inasmuch as the roads were guarded, he devised this sort of thing. Having gutted a hare, which he had cleverly obtained, so that nothing at all was cut away, he put in a little book in which he had written what he pleased; and after sewing up the belly, he gave the hare together with nets to a certain huntsman of his household, a most trustworthy man, and, instructing him by his own mouth that when he gave the hare he should tell Cyrus to open it with his own hands, and to do this without witnesses, he sent the man off into Persia. When the messenger had carried this out, Cyrus opened the hare he had received and read the little book that he found inside it. See Justin, book 1. Historians likewise report that doves served as messengers in difficult affairs, so that letters tied to their necks and feet were sent in the siege of Mutina by the consul Hirtius to Decimus Brutus, and by Decimus Brutus to Hirtius, when, with food placed in certain very high spots, he had trained doves, previously shut up in darkness and affected by very great hunger, to fly from one place to another.

Some, to avoid suspicion, write on paper with ink, as if they wished to communicate something to a friend about household matters; afterwards they write what is secret in the spaces with urine. Others use the juice of lemons or onions; others use crushed sal ammoniac with water poured over it. Writings made in letters of this kind remain hidden before they have been brought near fire.

Some follow the poet’s advice: “When she can conceal bound papers in her bosom, and carry flattering notes under her joined foot. The guard will have taken care of these; instead of a knowing paper let her offer her back, and on her own body she will carry the words. Safe too is the letter, and it deceives the eyes, made from fresh milk; touch it with charcoal powder and you will read it. It also deceives, what is made with a moist needle—”
