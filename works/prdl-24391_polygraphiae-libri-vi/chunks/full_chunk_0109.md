In the present Orchema a manifold alphabet is handed down by transposition and leap, both by the rule of order and by the position of the letters. *Johannes Trithemius [Peapolitanus?] to the reader.*

Since all the letters of the Greeks and Hebrews are numerical, I have often wondered that the painstaking Latinity of the ancients did not also make its own letters serve for the reckoning of numbers. The Hebrews established twenty-eight signs of certain duplicated letters, sufficient for every number. The Greeks, however, with like foresight remedied this deficiency of letters by the addition of four characters. We, therefore, having twenty-four letters, why do we not make ours numerical too, since we can likewise find four duplicated ones? Let us give four as an example, and that will be sufficient for every number: m and final iij; likewise r and final 2; likewise long s and final, or closed, s; likewise closed v and open u. When we add these to the twenty-four letters in common use, we have twenty-eight characters sufficient for all numbers.

And it will be no very laborious matter to grasp this method of numbering for those who already know the letters, if they once commit it to memory. For this invention of ours will be able to comprise many thousands upon thousands in number, all of which can be distinguished and understood at first sight, more clearly than light, without any calculation. Many, less experienced in the art of reckoning, think that this cannot be done so easily by the figures of the algorism, especially in large numbers. For as soon as you have inspected with your eye a number, however great, you will be able both to understand it fully and to express it without circumlocution or division of speech. Since this is so easy, it is fitting that we set down the letters themselves reduced to number.

I wrote this on 16 March, in the year 1508.
