But concerning the other ancient men who are said to have handed down special letters to almost every nation and people, letters that remain in use among them down to the present day, it is not my purpose to set down or describe the account. For all the more learned men among us know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: α, ε, γ, δ, ζ, ω, λ, μ, ν, ι, θ, ξ, π, σ, τ, ψ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is reported to have added four: ξ, θ, φ, χ. And Pythagoras afterward added the letter u, so that there would be twenty in all; ζ, κ, ω were subsequently invented by others. Gordanis, bishop of the Goths, gave his people letters of their own. When the unity of language had been confounded, the simplicity of characters too was varied in many ways. Hence it came about that the use of letters in the course of time arrogated to itself such license that not only individual peoples, but also particular men, fashioning new characters for letters at their own discretion, prepared hiding-places for their mysteries. Yet every writing in an unfamiliar character will not be free from suspicion among those who see it. Notwithstanding this, many kings, tyrants, and princes of old devised for themselves a private mode of writing, separate from the common one, caring little for the suspicion of others, provided that what they wished to remain secret was not perceived. Moved by imitation of these, and desiring to bring this work of *Polygraphy*, as far as possible, to a finished end, in this sixth book, which is also the last, I have resolved to recall from the dead certain characters of the ancients, which long ago passed out of human use, lest they perish entirely; and first indeed we shall set down the ancient mode of writing of the Nortmanni.

*On the Alphabet and Wonderful Mode of Writing of the Nortmanni, at the Time When They Were Ravaging [unclear].*

All the letters of the Greeks and Palestinians are numerical, as was said before, whereas of the Latin letters, which we Germans also use, only seven are counted as numerals, namely c, d, i, l, m, y, and x. The Nortmanni, however, together with the Asiatic Dacians, having gone out from the Scandian islands, which are now called Gothic, overran Gaul for more than forty years and laid it waste most grievously. In order to provide for their secret affairs, as Bede the English monk bears witness, they devised a new and elegant mode of writing from the letters of the Greeks, as follows. For they adapted the numerical values of the Greeks, comprised in ten of their twenty-four letters, to Latin characters, and under a foreign appearance prudently concealed our letters, in precisely the way in which we use numbers in place of letters, that is, when for a we put 1, for b 2, for c 3, for d 4, and so on in succession to the end of the alphabet. It is indeed a fine and quite secure invention, which neither a Greek nor a Latin will easily understand. For the whole alphabet is not found in it, but only nine letters and one numerical sign, namely g, which among them signifies six; and in this mode of writing, representing f, the sixth letter of the Latins, and, in addition, deferring to our German language in which we were born, we have put double w as the twenty-fourth letter of our nation at the end, which, according to the Greek number, we have formed thus: [unclear].
