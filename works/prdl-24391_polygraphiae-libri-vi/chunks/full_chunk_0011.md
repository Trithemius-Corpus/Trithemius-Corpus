*An indication of the things that are treated in the second book of the Polygraphy.*

In the second book of the Polygraphy nothing new is composed beyond the first, in which only the paraphrases of the day are varied. Therefore we advise that whatever we said must be observed in that book must also be kept in this one. For the symbols of both books are arranged under the path of light into the same order, and are necessarily changed only in the meaning of the paraphrases. In both, according to the capacity of human wit, manifold progressions can be made, when the words are varied by metathesis. The [unclear] also are changed at will, if at any time the minutes are converted into whole degrees. When this has been done, a marvellous work in every respect will arise in the art, and the world, brought forth in image, will stand in the light, and there will be no fear at all of the Bacuceans in the operator. I could have put a more beautiful face upon these matters, if I had been induced to write them either by some hope of profit or by the use of my own advantage. But since none of these things induced me to write, it seemed enough to have given those who desire it the opportunity and the material to add things more polished to our inventions, and to adorn art by art.

*An indication of the things that are contained in the third book of the Polygraphy.*

In the third book of the Polygraphy, having by the counsel of reason overstepped the prescribed bounds of our nation, we are led to foreign things, and, though set essentially in the midst of the Franks, we wander farther abroad through the images and likenesses of barbarians. For with almost four thousand mythological paraphrases arranged in order by art, we show a very easy way to artificial unity, through which it will communicate symbolic notions to its binary securely at all times. Here the binary is harder than unity, and unless our philologist is sharpened by prudence, he will render the binary contrary to the monad. For only the ternary, which proceeds from unity and the binary, connects both in itself like a palindrome. But if the usefulness of necessary reason is extended beyond the bounds of the proposed matter, let the paraphrases be counted by minutes from the beginning of the day as often as needed, until the minutes establish the degree in which night is equal to day. The face of this image knows perpetual silence and does not fear the deceits of the Bacuceans. It is extended to whatever abundance you wish, and is varied without number.

*A brief index or table of the things that are treated in the fourth book.*

In the fourth book of the Polygraphy we trust in the binary, which, with all obstacles removed, without the labor of difficulty, at first sight we have reduced to unity. Hence here we use a circle in great matters, a line or point in the smallest. Nor does it matter if night exceeds day, or if light does not agree with darkness, because the circle does not receive an end, and fullness does not admit a void. For they are contraries, and whenever they succeed one another, the one is destroyed by the other. A singular mystery conducive to the binary is handed down in this fourth book of the Polygraphy, since the airy wind itself, the composer, is never designated in anything other than the binary. But in that part the lightness of owls is always suspect to us in difficult matters. The construction of this matter makes paraphrases with infinite atoms.

*Index or table of the things that are treated in the fifth book.*

In the fifth book of the Polygraphy we have inscribed in order, on two tables, the letters of the Latins, which we Germans also use; one of these is called the straight table, while the other is named the reversed table. To these we have set beneath windy dances, so that what we seek with labor in foreign things we may see without difficulty in our own. For besides those things that have been set down by us openly, still more secrets also lie hidden within; through understanding of them a path is opened for the operator to greater things, which nevertheless are by no means proposed to all. We have also subjected letters to numbers, by which hidden things may be made manifest according to our will. Hence through the skilled operation of this book all messages are safely delivered to cabalistic persons, although the mysteries are not free from suspicion of the Bacuceans. For every form of signs in metathesis generates suspicion in the beholder, although it brings no harm to the owls. But you, who have attained understanding of this work, will easily penetrate every metathesis of letters among our people and the Latins.
