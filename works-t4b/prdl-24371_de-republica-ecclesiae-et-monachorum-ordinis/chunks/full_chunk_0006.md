Of them that saying of the apostle Paul is most aptly understood: “They went about in sheep-skins and goat-skins, needy, distressed, afflicted, of whom the world was not worthy; wandering in solitary places, in mountains and caves, and in dens of the earth.” The integrity of the monks’ life then deserved to be exalted, when by word and example it converted many from iniquity to justice. But the world is now unworthy of such monks, for it spurns the love of God, rejects truth, and despises knowledge of the Scriptures. The Church too flourished in learning and holiness together with our commonwealth, so long as it received none to the rule of souls except the learned, and admitted none except the devout. For devotion is not sufficient for the rule of the Lord’s flock if knowledge of the Scriptures is lacking. But now our monks and abbots are neither devout nor learned, and for that reason they are judged unworthy of governing churches. What if I were to say that the commonwealth of the Church is, in this tempestuous age, unworthy of learned and holy rulers? For I think there is no one who could rightly be angry with me on that account. For Scripture says of God, the supreme ruler: “He makes a hypocrite reign because of the sins of the people.” Rare in our times are prelates of churches who are at once learned and devout. Why? Because they are chosen not by the Holy Spirit, but by men. And so it is.

But enough of this; shall I proceed to what remains of our subject? I remember often having said, reverend fathers, that two things above all made our order glorious: namely, knowledge of the Scriptures and holiness of conduct. Of holiness we have briefly made mention; it remains now for us to say a few things about learning in the Scriptures. And, to begin from the origin of our commonwealth: from the beginning of our order down to the times of King Henry IV, as I have found from the catalogue of the ancients, many most learned men were distinguished in it. For while Saint Benedict was still living in the flesh, many very noble and very learned young men submitted their necks to his Rule, who were considered most skilled not only in the divine Scriptures but also in secular letters. father, advanced so greatly in regular discipline that, being held by him as especially intimate among many, after Benedict was dead he described his life in clear and elegant meter. Likewise that most renowned senator Cassiodorus, a man most learned in every respect, despised senatorial dignity for the love of God and became a monk in one of the monasteries of the holy father, and finally an abbot.

But what shall I do? The day would fail me if I wished to review by their proper names all those who, while our lawgiver was still living, adorned his order with wonderful learning and holiness. In the time that followed, moreover, the monks had the highest zeal for the Scriptures, and an inestimable love of learning in every branch of knowledge, even secular knowledge. For those who had come learned to conversion supplied the younger brothers with rules for study. From this it came about that they became most learned in every scripture; for they believed it dangerous in morals, and deadly, to learn the Scriptures usefully while beginning apart from humility and amendment of life. So our monks did in ancient times: those who applied zeal to letters also applied unwearied cultivation to the virtues.

Such was Bede, priest and monk in England; such was Alcuin, his disciple, a deacon; such were Boniface the martyr and Rabanus, archbishop of Mainz; such were all monks truly learned. What shall I recount concerning the other pillars of the Church? Gregory the Great, pope; Leander of Seville; Gregory of Tours; John of Girona; Caesarius of Arles; Hincmar of Rheims; Haymo of Halberstadt; Aldhelm of the West Saxons; Radbod of Utrecht; Amalarius of Trier; Ansengisus of Sens; Remigius of Auxerre; Ratherius of Liège; Adalbold of Utrecht; Notger of Liège; Burchard of Worms; Guinemund of Aversa; Alphanus of Salerno; Peter Damian of Ostia; Lanfranc and Anselm of Canterbury, bishops and archbishops, who produced almost innumerable books and were distinguished in our order at different times no less by holiness than by learning in the Scriptures.
