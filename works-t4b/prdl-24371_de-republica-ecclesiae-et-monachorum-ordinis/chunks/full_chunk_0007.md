All of these, learned and most holy, after publishing many books, were also renowned in life for miracles. It would be a long task if I wished to run through even the names alone of those who shone wonderfully in our order both in learning and in holiness.

Great devotion burned in them, and an inestimable zeal for regular discipline, which never allowed them to be idle. In their studies divine love always flourished; conceived from holy reading, it called those keeping vigil to prayer. Those who had exercised themselves more fully in the divine Scriptures, in the hours free from the service of God, devoted themselves to expositions of those same Scriptures. They composed books and various treatises, by which they stirred the studies of their brothers toward the love of God. The other brothers had no leisure for idleness, but after prayers devoutly applied themselves, according to the Rule, to the work of their hands. Those fit for the task wrote books for the adornment of the library, and from sheets brought into fair form works published by others. Some skillfully bound the written codices; others corrected them; others marked them out with red minium. All were eager to share in holy labor.

The younger brothers, moreover, were instructed in scholastic disciplines: some in grammar, some in rhetoric, some in philosophy, some in arithmetic, some in dialectic, and others in other disciplines, each according to the strength of his nature and talent. Once grounded in the elementary sciences, however, they were called to the theological Scriptures. In the monasteries no place was given to idleness, as each applied himself to his own labors and studies with the greatest fervor. They sent the little works they had composed to emperors, kings, princes, and prelates of cathedral churches; by their favor and authority those works came forth more safely into the light for our edification. Thus Alcuin dedicated innumerable little works to Charlemagne; thus Rabanus to Louis; thus Berno of Reichenau to Aribo of Mainz and to Pilgrim, archbishops of this holy church of Cologne. But the venerable abbot Rupert of Deutz also composed, for Frederick, archbishop of this city of Cologne,

most excellent volumes, with incredible zeal burning for the Scriptures. I pass over the rest, whose number is almost infinite, who published brilliant little works for princes.

But where now is such study among monks? Where is the abbots' love for the divine Scriptures? Shameful to say, he is now called great and useful in the order not who cultivates and loves knowledge of the Scriptures, but who grows foul in gathering perishable riches. O fathers, O brothers, our commonwealth did not once flourish in this way; our order did not grow in this way in antiquity. But abbots given over to the world are accustomed to answer, and monks who are friends of vanity excuse themselves from study: We willingly wish to remain in our simplicity, because we see even learned men going through the precipices of vices into perdition. At the last judgment, they say, profound knowledge will not be required of us, but a good life.

Useless and foolish men, you do not hide your sloth; rather, to your greater confusion, you openly show yourselves enemies of the divine Scriptures. It is well said in the proverb that knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man. You lovers of the world, you followers of vanity, who foolishly cast away from yourselves the study of the Scriptures, why do you not love it in others? Or do you not know that every Scripture inspired by God is useful for teaching, for arguing, for rebuking? What will you teach your monks, you who are ignorant of the Scriptures? How will you be able usefully to reprove offenders, you who do not know how to bring forward the testimonies of the Scriptures?

Remain, then, in your simplicity, but not in a right one; remain in asinine humility. Refuse to understand so that you may act well; refuse to attend to the Rule of Saint Benedict; refuse to withdraw from your foolishness. Do you know also what our lawgiver says in the Rule? I know that you know. He says: The abbot ought to be learned in the divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth discourse. An unlearned prelate is a mute dog, an empty vessel; the soul that does not love the Scriptures of God is empty. O you abbots, ignorant men and enemies of saving knowledge, who spend the day in obscene rumors, who drink wine to satiety, who devote yourselves to earthly profits, who stupidly play at the gaming table: what will you answer to almighty God and to your lawgiver Benedict?
