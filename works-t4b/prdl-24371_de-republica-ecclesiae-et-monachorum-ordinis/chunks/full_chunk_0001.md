A Discourse on the Commonwealth of the Church and of the Monks of the Order of the Holy Father Benedict, delivered at Cologne in the annual chapter by the reverend father lord Johannes, abbot of Sponheim, on the first day of the month of September, in the year of the Lord one thousand four hundred ninety-three.

--- Page 007 ---
When I undertook, reverend fathers in Christ, by reason of obedience, to deliver a sermon before your paternities according to praiseworthy custom, and after I had long and much deliberated silently with myself what subject I should bring forward that might more effectively stir your souls to piety toward the order, I was at last persuaded by certain venerable men of your senate that I should speak, by the gift of the Most High, about our commonwealth, for love of which, and for its defense and adornment, you have come together into this assembly from distant regions of the earth.

I confess, O fathers, that it is holy and wholesome to speak about your commonwealth, holy and religious as it is; but it should be done by a learned man, an erudite man, a holy man, and one fit to persuade. I do not know myself to be such, since indeed I am poor in talent, bare of merit, and uncultivated in speech. How, then, should I dare presume to speak about this most excellent commonwealth, since I am slow in invention, unpolished in speaking, and cold in persuasion? Therefore, fathers, another orator ought to have been sought for you, one to whom talent would supply strength for speaking, learning an instructed understanding for teaching, and for persuading no less doctrine than merit.

But why do I strive in vain, since I am compelled to obey? Therefore, although the burden laid upon me far exceeds the powers of my talent, nevertheless, trusting in the mercy of the Almighty and the aid of obedience, I shall undertake the work enjoined upon me, not so much willingly as cheerfully.

For this matter I shall need neither Plato in his inadequate work, nor Aristotle in eight books, nor Tullius in six volumes on the commonwealth, since our Lord Jesus has supplied abundant material for discourse on this subject in today’s Gospel, according to our intention, when he said: “A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers,” etc. Although this parable is principally to be understood of the perdition of the whole human race, nevertheless nothing prevents us, with faith preserved, from interpreting it also with reference to our commonwealth. For the condition of the commonwealth, not only ours but that of all religious, indeed of the whole Church militant, has declined from Jerusalem into Jericho.
