O holy religion, O venerable mother, O commonwealth of monks, once honored, to what misery you have come. Where now is the adornment of your former beauty? Where is holiness of life? Where the saving study of the Scriptures? Where are learned and holy abbots? Where are monks devoted to regular observance? Behold, you retain more than ten thousand monasteries under the order, of which we believe scarcely a thousand have been preserved in any observance of the Rule. For your abbots, having despised regular discipline and neglected the care of souls, are given over to the vanities of the world, intent on earthly riches, submerged in the pleasures of the flesh. For as a certain excellent versifier, still living, says:

Our abbots and the prelates of churches,
neglecting the worship of those above and spurning the command of the Thunderer,
indulge Bacchus and serve Venus.
They furnish sacred altars with gold and coins;
with gold they pour libations of wine; with gold the ceilings gleam.
They cover whores with gems; they set banquets before dogs.
Clothed with the spoils of the poor, and enriched by plunder,
elated by success, they laugh at those above and at Acheron alike.

They have no care for the salvation of souls, no love of God, no reverence for the saints, no practice of virtues. Would that, fathers, our observance had no such abbots; would that our commonwealth knew no such rulers. But I fear, indeed, if it is right to say so, I know, that among our own such men are also found, who are reformed in name but in truth deformed. There are, I say, abbots who glory in the name of reform: ambitious, vain, proud, given over to the pleasures of the flesh, who, impatient of seclusion, consider every occasion suitable by which they may travel here and there at the greatest expense. Behold, that man, despising regular discipline, sits all day as a curious guest among frivolous persons; this one, making little of the meekness of the saints, involves himself in the cases of litigants; that one sits playing at the gaming-table; this stupid fellow dances to the drum. What shall I say about buildings both curious and superfluous, which are liable to no less reproach than expense? The walls are adorned with vain paintings; the windows are marked with monuments of folly; in

height the walls are raised up; the pinnacles of the roof gleam with gold. Shame does not permit me to speak of the other plagues of certain abbots: lust, eating meat, the abuse of baths, games, and hunts. Would that those who confound our observance by their pestilent morals were cut off.

Behold, reverend fathers, we have briefly heard what the condition of our commonwealth is; it remains now for us to think about its remedy. For, descending from Jerusalem to Jericho, it fell away from its former beauty; it fell among robbers, because from the time of Henry IV it lost more than five thousand monasteries from the order. I am not speaking hypocritically, nor do I report uncertainties, but things I found plainly in the writings of the ancients. The robbers, then, who despoiled our commonwealth are, first, the demons, who kill the souls of monks with the wounds of vices; second, greedy men, who plunder for themselves the monasteries of our order. Therefore our commonwealth lies naked and torn, since it is seen to have been despoiled within by its own and without by strangers. The priest and the Levite passed by when they saw its desolation, because, while our affairs are despised, all seek what is their own.

But our Samaritan, the Lord Jesus Christ, seeing the desolation of our order, was moved by mercy and bound up its wounds, when at different times he renewed it to regular observance. On the next day, bringing forth two denarii, namely love of God and of neighbor, or certainly the increase of spiritual and temporal goods, or understanding and action, he gave them to the faithful innkeeper, saying, “Take care of him,” and so forth. The faithful innkeeper was the emperor Charlemagne, who first reformed our collapsed order in Germany. An innkeeper was Berno, abbot of Cluny, under whose reform there were fifteen hundred monasteries. Innkeepers were Richard of Fulda, Wilhelm of Hirsau, and another abbot, of Hirschfeld, who individually at different times were authors of reform for the order. Innkeepers were also the abbots of the Italian reform of Saint Justina, which, persevering down to the present day, has been honored by many privileges from the supreme pontiffs. Innkeepers were Emperor Sigismund with his helpers, who began the Melk reform in Austria. Faithful innkeepers also were those who provided the beginnings of the Kastl reform.
