The people of the Lord has been led into captivity; the empire of Constantinople has been captured by infidels; cities have been destroyed, churches burned, monasteries demolished; Christians, from the child to the elder, have either been slain by a hard death or sold into slavery with a still harder cruelty. Almost all Asia has abandoned the Christian faith; Africa has withdrawn from Christianity; in Europe alone the Catholic faith has remained, though weak. Alas, how narrow is the ecclesiastical estate, which once, with the greatest honor, had been spread through all the earth.

Take up this lament over it, O pontiffs, priests, prelates, and rulers of the Christian commonwealth; and while you bewail the danger of others, fear for your own. For I fear, and I fear greatly, that iniquity long dissembled may at last come to be punished with a heavier chastisement. If almighty God did not spare the sanctified land in which, clothed in human flesh, he lived among us, how will he spare ours? Our desolation is nearer than we believe; our destruction closer than we fear. Foolishly we presume upon security, when we see so many Christian lands and kingdoms captured by infidels.

But now I have said enough about the general commonwealth, lest the too-short hour compel us to leave our own behind. There is also another commonwealth of the laity, which is called temporal; and although it too, in many respects, already seems quite miserable enough, nevertheless since it does not pertain to us, let it also be left out of our discourse this time. Now at last, reverend fathers, after a long discourse on the common commonwealth, which, as I was able, I have poured forth from my elders, let us come to our own commonwealth, which concerns us more.

The commonwealth, therefore, about which we intend to speak, is our most sacred order, which follows the ruin of the ecclesiastical commonwealth, and is compared with it by a likeness that is quite close. For just as that commonwealth, almost in our own times, has lost an empire, kingdoms, and provinces, so also ours, in recent memory, has lost many and very wealthy monasteries in these regions. But before we begin to describe its present state, it is pleasing to set forth a little what kind it once was. And although I have done this several times in the preceding conferences, nevertheless I do not think it will be superfluous if we repeat more often what has been well said. For we never understand the present state of the commonwealth better than if we compare the past with it from the opposite side.

For it has gone with our commonwealth as, we have already said, it has gone with the ecclesiastical one. By as much as it has departed over time from its first institution, by so many steps, as it were, it has descended from Jerusalem to Jericho. At one time the order of our commonwealth was deservedly exalted above all other orders, when it nourished men mature, zealous, and most learned: firm in faith, strong in hope, radiant with twofold charity, pure in mind, outstanding in devotion, and adorned with the honor of all the virtues. They had no care for earthly things, no lure of pleasures repeated upon them; rather, converted to God with their whole mind, they despised the world with its vanities.

From among these, because of their outstanding learning and inestimable sanctity, almost innumerable men are read to have been raised, at various times, to the summit of episcopal dignity; and the higher the place in which they had been set, the more brightly they shone forth in virtues and sanctity. What church would not seek a priest from the order of venerable Benedict? What people devoted to the Christian name would not desire a teacher from the order of monks?

I have said these things not for the empty glory of our present commonwealth, but rather for useful confusion. For just as the life of the saints is set forth for glory to those who imitate them, so it is recited for judgment against the sluggish and idle. Hence I fear especially that, while I have praised the most holy former state of our order, I shall be compelled to censure the present one, although truthfully, more than the patience of many can bear. But let the little know-it-alls be angry with themselves, if they please; let those puffed up with false justice be driven into fury: I would by no means dare to compare the order of our time with the sanctity of the ancients.

For, as I had begun to say, our order once flourished with most holy men, who, living according to the purity of the rule, became renowned for innumerable miracles. Though placed in the flesh, they overcame the vices of the flesh, since, despising the pleasures of the world, they loved God with all their heart. They were always ardent in the love of God, constant in prayer and devotions, profound in meditation, sublime in contemplation, uniform in holiness of morals. In their conduct holiness shone forth; in their action, purity; in their thought, the integrity of the mind.
