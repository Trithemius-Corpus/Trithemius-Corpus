AN INDEX OF THE THINGS THAT ARE TREATED IN THE SECOND BOOK OF THE POLYGRAPHIA.

In the second book of the Polygraphia, nothing new is composed beyond the first; in it only the paraphrases of the day are varied. Therefore we warn that whatever we said must be observed in that book must also be kept in this one. For the symbols of both books are arranged in the same order and sequence under the path of light, and are necessarily changed only in the meaning of the paraphrases. In both books, according to the capacity of human intelligence, multiple progressions can be made, when the words are varied by metathesis. The feronomata also are changed at will, if ever the minutes are converted into whole degrees. When this has been done, a marvelous work will arise in the art in every respect, and the world, brought forth by an image, will stand in the light, and there will be no fear at all of the bacucei in the one performing the operation. I could have given these matters a more beautiful face if I had come to writing them either induced by some hope of profit or by the use of my own advantage. But since none of these things induced me to write, it seemed enough to have given those who desire it the opportunity and the material to add more polished things to our inventions, and to beautify art by art.

AN INDEX OF THE THINGS THAT ARE CONTAINED IN THE THIRD BOOK OF THE POLYGRAPHIA.

In the third book of the Polygraphia, having crossed the boundaries of our prescribed nation by a plan of reason, we are led to foreign things, and, though placed essentially in the midst of the Franks, we wander farther through images and likenesses of barbarians. For with almost four thousand mythological phrasmata arranged in order by art, we show a very easy way to artificial unity, through which it will securely communicate symbolic notions to its binary at all times. Here the binary is harder than unity, and unless our philologist is sharpened by prudence, he will make the binary contrary to the monad. For only the ternary, which proceeds from unity and binary, like a palindrome connects both in itself. But if the necessary usefulness of reason is extended beyond the limits of the proposed matter, let the paraphrases be counted by minutes from the beginning of the day, until the minutes establish the degree in which night is made equal to day. The face of this image knows perpetual silence, nor does it fear the deceits of the bacucei. It is extended to whatever abundance you wish, and is varied without number.

A LITTLE INDEX OR TABLE OF THE THINGS THAT ARE TREATED IN THE FOURTH BOOK.

In the fourth book of the Polygraphia we place much confidence in the binary, which, with all obstacles removed, without laborious difficulty, we have reduced to unity at first sight. Hence here we use a circle in great things, and a line or point in the smallest things. Nor does it matter if night surpasses day, or if light does not agree with darkness, because the circle receives no end, and fullness does not admit emptiness. For they are contraries, and whenever they succeed one another, one is destroyed by the other. A singular mystery conducive to the binary is handed down in this fourth book of the Polygraphia, since the airy wind itself, the composer, is never designated in anything other than the binary. But in that part the lightness of owls is always suspect to us in difficult matters. The construction of this matter makes paraphrases from infinite atoms.

AN INDEX OR TABLE OF THE THINGS THAT ARE TREATED IN THE FIFTH BOOK.

In the fifth book of the Polygraphia, we have written in order, on two tables, the letters of the Latins, which we Germans also use; one of these is called the straight table, while the other is called the reversed table. Under these we have placed windy dances, so that what we seek with labor in foreign things, we may see without difficulty in our own. For besides those things that have been set down by us openly, still more secrets also lie hidden in the inner parts; through understanding them a path is opened for the operator to greater things, which nevertheless are by no means set forth for everyone. We have also subjected letters to numbers, by which hidden things may become manifest according to our will. Hence, through the skillful operation of this book, all messages are safely delivered to cabalists, although the mysteries are not free from suspicion on the part of the bacucei. For every metathesis of the form of signs generates suspicion in the beholder, although it brings no harm to the owls. But you, who have attained understanding of this work, will easily penetrate every metathesis of letters among our own people and among the Latins.
