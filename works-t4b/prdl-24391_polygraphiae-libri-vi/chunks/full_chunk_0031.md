*Archimedes of Syracuse.* Similar to this is what is read in Gellius, book 17, chapter 9. The ancient Lacedaemonians, when they wished to disguise and conceal letters sent officially to their commanders, lest, if they were intercepted by the enemy, their plans should become known, used to send letters made in the following manner. There were two rods, round, rather long, of equal thickness and the same length, scraped smooth and finished alike. One was given to the commander setting out for war; the other the magistrates kept at home, with legal authority and with a seal. When there was need of more secret letters, they wrapped around that rod a thong of moderate thinness and length, or as much as the matter required, in a simple round winding, so that the joined edges of the thong being wrapped would meet everywhere and adhere. Then they wrote the letters on that thong across the transverse edges of the joins, in lines running from top to bottom. When the thong had been written through in this way, they unwound it from the rod and sent it to the commander who knew of that device. The unrolling of the thong also made the letters broken and mutilated, and the parts and tips of them were scattered into the most different places.

Therefore, if that thong had fallen into the hands of the enemy, they could infer nothing at all from it. But when the man to whom it had been sent received it, he took the matching rod which he had, wrapped it around to the end, just as he knew it had to be done, and thus the letters, coming together along the same circuit of the rod, joined again and made the letter whole, uncorrupted, and easy to read. The Lacedaemonians call this kind of letter a *scytale*. See also Plutarch in the *Life of Lysander*.

There is also another no less ingenious method of hiding letters, described by Girolamo Cardano in the book *On Subtleties*. Take two equal sheets of parchment ruled for writing, and in the very lines make small holes scattered in corresponding places in each, yet proportioned to the size or height of the letters. Some holes will contain seven letters, some three, some eight or ten, so that all the holes together will contain one hundred and twenty letters, joining together all that can be written in them. You will give one of these sheets to the person to whom you wish to write. But when there is need, first write your message as briefly as you can, so that the message contains a smaller number of letters than the spaces can hold. Then, on the parchment placed beneath the holes, write that message; and likewise in another and another place. Finally fill the spaces of the first parchment by completing, deleting, and filling out sentences, until the sense

You will complete this second message on the second parchment in such a way that the words and sentences seem to cohere; on the third also you will fit it so that, with the first letters left unharmed, the whole sentence, the number of words, and their size hold together and retain a certain elegance. When these things have been done, place the pattern which you cut out over an equal sheet of parchment, and at the boundaries of the holes mark very fine points as far as the letters which you wish to write extend. Then take a third sheet of parchment and write the letter in continuous words, with a proper order of spaces and size, and also of letters, so that the first sentence and its words are contained within the boundaries of the points. Thus no suspicion of deception then remains: the other person, when he receives it and places the pattern over it, immediately understands the thought of your mind; and for signifying one’s thought to friends in dangerous times, nothing can be found equal to this device, although it is a matter of no slight labor.

*Marcus Tullius.* The meaning is that Cicero devised individual and special characters for individual Latin words, with which, if necessity required, he wrote speech of any length both with very few signs and in the most secret manner. In this way, they say, the speech of Cato, urging that the conspirators be punished, was preserved and committed to writing, when Cicero as consul appointed very rapid writers who could take down spoken words in signs of this sort.
