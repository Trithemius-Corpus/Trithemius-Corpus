A Brief Explanation of Those Things Which Seem Rather Obscure in the First Book of the Polygraphia.

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphia, nevertheless, lest its composition perhaps seem profound to simpler readers, we shall briefly explain the things that are thought more difficult. We have arranged the words of the Latin language in order, more than nine thousand of them, under nearly four hundred alphabets, in such a sequence that if from each alphabet only one word is taken, one after another, from the beginning to the end, the words, mutually cohering, will produce a fitting Latin discourse. For this will be a very convenient method of writing whatever we wish most secretly, since each word represents the letter set before it at its head. And let it not trouble you, reader, that a multitude of words in the open text contains a brief meaning in the hidden one, since in this childish arrangement of ours there lies concealed a higher method of writing, more broadly and much more secretly, whatever you wish; public reason has forbidden us to make it plain to all. But whoever is endowed with intelligence and applies the powers necessary for inquiry will find subtler things among the teachings hidden here, and will plainly understand things marvelously unknown before. the method will suffice for everything, since you can repeat it as many times as you wish, and the repeated narrative will always be varied. For if, as we have said, you take one word from the first alphabet, likewise one from the second, and so consequently one from each, and one from those inserted outside the ordered line, as far as the period or the end of the book, a Latin and sufficiently coherent discourse will arise for you. Care must be taken, however, that at one and the same time more than one word not be taken from one alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, not skip over some alphabet and confuse the narrative. By "day" understand the open narrative, but by "night" the hidden and secret one, which should not be too long but brief. The examples that follow in the text, concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen, pertain to both narratives, the hidden and the manifest. Whenever "the leaf is moved by the wind" and the rest occur, we advise that you so number the secret narrative against the open one that both may end together at the close of any given period. But if anything remains over from the secret matter, let it be enlarged with such moderation that both narratives end at one point, lest a confusion of eclipse be produced. The other things that follow in the first explanatory instruction of the Polygraphia are also implicitly referred to all the things we have said.
