Letters are also written in various ways.) Herodotus, book 7, near the end. After Xerxes had resolved to undertake an expedition against Greece, Demaratus, who was at Susa, when he had learned this, judged that it ought to be reported to the Greeks. But since he could not do so otherwise, because it was dangerous lest he be detected, he devised this stratagem: taking the folding tablets of two writing tablets, he scraped the wax from them. Then on their wood he engraved the king’s plan. Once it had been engraved in letters, he again covered it with wax, so that the tablets, to whomsoever they were carried, would cause no trouble among the guards of the roads. When they had been brought to Lacedaemon, the Lacedaemonians could not interpret them before, as I understand it, Gorgo, daughter of Cleomenes and wife of Leonidas, warned them and interpreted the matter herself, ordering that the wax be broken off, for they would find letters on the wood. Listening to her, the Lacedaemonians found the letters, read them, and then sent them on to the Greeks.

Gellius, book 17, chapter 9: “There is also,” he says, “in the records of Greek affairs a certain deep and unexpected hiding place, devised by barbarian cunning. There was a man named Histiaeus, born in a not insignificant place in the land of Asia. At that time King Darius held Asia under his rule. When this Histiaeus was in Persia with Darius, he wanted by secret writing to report certain hidden matters to a certain Aristagoras. He devised this astonishing concealment of letters: his slave, who for a long time had had diseased eyes, had all the hair shaved from his head, as if for the purpose of treatment, and he marked the smooth head with the forms of letters. With these letters he wrote out what he wished. Afterwards he kept the man at home until his hair had grown back. When this had happened, he ordered him to go to Aristagoras. ‘And when you have come to him,’ he said, ‘tell him that I have ordered him to shave your head, just as I myself lately did.’ The slave came to Aristagoras as he had been ordered and delivered his master’s instruction; and Aristagoras, judging that this had not been ordered in vain, did what had been commanded. Thus the letters were delivered.”

Herodotus writes almost the same things; and in that author, book 1, the following is also read. Since Astyages was harsh toward the Medes, Harpagus, ingratiating himself with each of them individually, persuaded the leading men that Astyages ought to be removed from the throne and Cyrus chosen. When these things had been done and prepared, Harpagus, finally wishing to reveal his view to Cyrus, who was among the Persians, and unable to do so otherwise, since the roads were guarded, devised the following plan. Historians also report that doves served as messengers in difficult affairs: in the siege of Mutina, Hirtius the consul sent letters tied to their necks and feet to Decimus Brutus, and Decimus Brutus sent them to Hirtius. Food had been set out in certain very high places, and he had trained the doves, which had first been shut up in darkness and afflicted with great hunger, to fly back and forth from there.

Some people, to avoid suspicion, write on paper with ink, as if they wished to communicate something to a friend about household matters; afterwards they write what is secret in the spaces with urine. Others use lemon juice or onion juice; others use crushed sal ammoniac with water poured over it. Letters written in this way remain hidden before they have been brought near the fire.

Some follow the poet’s advice: “Since a woman can conceal tied papers on her breast, and carry charming notes beneath her joined foot, let the guard beware of these things; instead of paper, let her provide her knowing back, and she will carry the words on her own body.
