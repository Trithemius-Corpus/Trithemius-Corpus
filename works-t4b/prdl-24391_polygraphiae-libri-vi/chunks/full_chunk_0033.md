With these things so established, let us observe which numbers arise from the addition, multiplication, or division of which numbers among themselves. For example, twelve arises from the addition of one to eleven, of two to ten, of three to nine, of four to eight, of six to six, and of seven to five. In multiplication, twelve is produced from the drawing of four into three, or of two into six. In division we have twelve if we divide twenty-four by two.

Moreover, when the fingers are only raised, this signifies addition; but when we touch one finger with another, multiplication. But if we bend one and raise the other, division must be understood. Therefore, if one wishes to show the letter m, which occupies the twelfth place, I indicate the number twelve either by addition, or by multiplication, or by division. First, by raising the two little fingers of both hands together with the thumb of the left hand: for the thumb signifies one, while one of the other fingers designates five, the other six; and that number shows the letter m. By multiplication in this way: let us touch the third finger from the thumb of the left hand with the index finger of the right, since three multiplied by four makes twelve; or let us touch the little finger of the right hand with the index finger of the left, which is the same thing, for twice six gives twelve.

It could also be done in another way, namely by the contact of the index finger of the right hand and the middle finger of the left, which show six. These again, by the contact of both thumbs, or of the index finger of the right hand and the thumb of the left, signify the multiplication of the former number by two; but twice six makes twelve. By division thus: first we shall indicate twenty-four, the division of which number by two yields twelve. The gesture of which I spoke above represents division by two, namely if the two thumbs are shown, one raised and the other bent; likewise the thumb and little finger of the left hand, and the index finger of the right bent. For they signify the partition of six by three, that is, two; when twenty-four is divided by this, they show twelve.

So much on dactylogy, or the speech of the fingers, which can be handled otherwise in infinitely many ways, by assigning to fixed positions vowels, consonants, semivowels, double letters, mutes, and liquids, according as it has seemed good to each person to treat such matters. Although these things may appear ridiculous, nevertheless they have the greatest use if they are transferred by a certain analogy to other things.
