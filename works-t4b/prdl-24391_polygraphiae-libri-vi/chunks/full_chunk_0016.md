Mystery. Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the chief of mathematicians, used his white bird, suited to his established rules, naked through head and feet, etc.

Explanation. Archimedes had two rods of one length and thickness cut, round or square; he kept one for himself and entrusted the other to his absent friend. And whenever one wished to write something more secret to the other, he took the white bird, that is, a sheet of papyrus of small width but of twice the length of the rod, wrapped it around the wood, and fastened it below and above with wax. Afterwards he wrote on the sheet, joined continuously along the sides into an artful mass, whatever he wished. Then he released the sheet from the rod, and at once so great a distance appeared between the letters that not even one word cohered with another. He turned the sheet over, and, when he wished, wrote similarly on the other side. Nor was there anyone ignorant of this device who could have discovered the secret. But the man who had the other rod, when he had received the sheet, attached it and restored the letters to their former order.

Mystery. Marcus Tullius Cicero,

--- Page 052 ---

eloquent orator of the Romans, using ingenuity and art, changed the small details of phrase into signs, and the rest.

Explanation. He wrote a book of notes for his son, which the blessed martyr Cyprian later enlarged for the use of Christians, in the form, namely, of a dictionary, where the characters of his notation are first placed in order, and afterwards the words designated by those same characters, so that each word is signified by the note set before it. With these notes or characters we once saw the whole Latin Psalter, beautifully written, in the library of the major church of Strasbourg; some ignorant judge of the matter had placed on the outside this title: Psalter in the Armenian Language. But he was wrong, since it should rather have been written thus: Latin Psalter Written in Tullian Characters. For each character represents one complete Latin word. Nor does my judgment deceive me, since I possess, written in an ancient volume, the complete and uncorrupted dictionary of Cicero itself as corrected by St. Cyprian, which I have seen nowhere else. Indeed, so great is the abundance of notes or characters, with the words assigned to them, that they are altogether sufficient for writing in Latin whatever you wish.

Mystery. Pharamund, the forty-third from Marcomer, king of the Franks, when in the year 424 of the Lord’s Nativity he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, and the rest.

Explanation. The kings of the Franks,

from the time when, in the year 430 before the Nativity of Christ, they left the Scythian borders with their people and settled at the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is, waged continual war with the Gauls for almost nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject to themselves, as we wrote in the first volume of our Annals. Of these, the aforesaid King Pharamund, in order to send messengers securely, used not a few but many new alphabets, which no one understood without his voluntary instruction.

Mystery. Clodius Crinitus, son of Pharamund and after him king of the Franks, who subdued all Gaul to himself after destroying the Romans whom he found in it, used his father’s inventions in secret writings.

Explanation. This king devised alphabets of his own, new ones, and gave one to each of his leaders, while he alone retained knowledge of them all in his own possession.

Mystery. Charles, called the Great, the sixty-third king of the Franks, who first among the Franks wore the diadem of empire, and the rest.

Explanation. This man, the elder son of King Pippin, is read to have been born duke of Brabant in the county of Namur, adjoining the people of Liege, although some assert that he was born in the village of Ingelheim, not far from Mainz. He too invented various modes of writing, and, with the grammarian Nanno providing assistance, devised different alphabets, by which he communicated secrets to the governors of the kingdom.
