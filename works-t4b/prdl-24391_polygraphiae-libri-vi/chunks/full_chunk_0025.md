BRIEF EXPLANATION OF THE SIXTH AND LAST BOOK OF THE POLYGRAPHY.

In the sixth and last book of the Polygraphy we have gathered together certain characters of the ancients, which they used in place of letters, so that if anyone wishes to adopt from them an alphabet of his own, he may be able to do so, although a person of moderate skill, if he wishes, could devise more beautiful characters for himself. All the systems of this book are sufficiently open and clear, and therefore they need no explanation, or only a brief one. Yet those things which appear there as newly invented by us require a definite explanation, lest perhaps they seem to have been set down without reason.

First of all, we have made all the letters of the Latins numerical, after the manner of the Hebrews and the Greeks, thinking it shameful for us if we should be found inferior to them in this respect. For what of the fact that we have doubled certain letters, so that they might suffice for an infinite number, when the Jews, driven by the same necessity, long ago did the very same thing? And the Greeks, because of a deficiency of letters, admitted certain signs into their numbering, since they could not double the letters. But since we are pressed by an equal lack of letters, we have admitted two signs, namely this one, &, which our scribes put for “and”; and it does not matter whether it is written thus, &, or thus, et, provided that it represents six in number.

We have added the other sign, 9, which our scribes write in abbreviation for con and com indiscriminately; here, in the order of numbers, it represents six hundred. By the same necessity we have also doubled two letters, namely r, z̃, and s, of which r signifies ninety, while the curved 2 signifies one hundred, long ſ two hundred, and closed s three hundred. Moreover, the doubled w, which we placed in the order of the alphabet because of the nature of the German language, represents the last letter, and in the series of numbers one thousand.
