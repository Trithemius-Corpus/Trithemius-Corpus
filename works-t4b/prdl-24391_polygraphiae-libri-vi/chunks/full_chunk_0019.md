...lest the reader lack complete knowledge of this matter, let us make the meaning clear by an example. Let us suppose the case that a wicked man seeks to be advanced through you with some friend of yours; but you, in order to cut off his importunate requests and yet not advance an unworthy man, decide to write rather secretly to your friend through the very petitioner, so that he may not understand: Beware of this man, because he is a thief and the worst scoundrel. Go to the first alphabet and write the word Conditor, which signifies C; in the second, clemens, which gives A; in the third, decernens, which represents V; in the fourth, mundana, which signifies E. Now in the hidden narrative you have caue, “beware.” Proceed in sequence also through the other alphabets, and such a discourse will result:

The gracious Creator, discerning worldly things, may pour seraphic loveliness upon the inexperienced, with all his saints in the heavens. Amen. The gentleness of the most glorious prince, reverently to be remembered everywhere by the saints, by the best precepts urges us, most abject sleepers, to abandon present things for reverence of the director of good things, and with all affection, according to our powers, to await the glory of perpetual rest. Therefore receive instruction, O vain mockers, so that you may be able, most blessedly adorned, to enter the most happy dances of the archangels of the rewarder of the orthodox. Rise up, therefore, from the most foul honors of this pilgrimage, since you are destined to enter, most blessedly and most holily, into the delights of the benign savior of the ages.

In this prescribed public narrative there is contained this sentence hidden in secret, namely: Beware of this man, because he is a thief and the worst scoundrel, but be silent, I beg you.

56 EXPOSITION OF BOOK I.

The same order is to be followed in all other cases whatsoever, and one must begin from the head as often as necessary, until you have manifestly enclosed the hidden narrative of your mystery. The words, however, that are placed outside the alphabetical order designate no letter and do not serve the hidden narrative, but have been inserted because of the lack of synonyms, so that the sequence of the Latin discourse might be completed. For we could not always have so many words with corresponding meaning, especially among pronouns, conjunctions, prepositions, and interjections, that we could make an alphabet in place of the twenty-four wandering letters, as is clearly evident after the eighth alphabet, where we placed two words outside the order, so that we would not leave the discourse defective. For thus we put: Cum omnibus, and thus: Cum vniuersis. Of these let the writer take two, whichever he wishes, so that the Latin discourse may be completed. Let him do likewise in other cases, whenever he finds similar ones.

We adopted the double W on account of our native speech, since in our German language we frequently need it. For anyone ignorant of the Latin language may write his hidden secret in German beneath a Latin discourse without any difficulty.

NOW FOLLOW THE RULES OR CAUTIONS TO BE OBSERVED IN THE OPERATION OF THIS BOOK.

The first rule or caution advises you that the secret of your mind, which you wish to announce to an absent friend who knows the art and has the book of the Polygraphia, you should write as carefully as you can on a sheet, in Latin or German, or in any other language you wish that can be represented by Roman letters. Then place the sheet so written before you, so that for each individual letter of the secret you may be able to fit an appropriate word. For, as we have shown, in this practice of writing a whole word is taken. We gave an example a little earlier in the foregoing.

II. The second caution instructs you that, as has been said, you should not take more than one word at the same time from an alphabet in a single narrative of the public Latin discourse; otherwise you would both confuse the open sense and entirely draw away from understanding the mystery the friend to whom you had written.

III. Third, one must take the greatest care not to skip any alphabet through negligence, without taking from it one word fitting the secret. Likewise one must provide that, whenever one or more words occur outside the sequence of the alphabet, you take the whole upper line or the lower one, and not both.
