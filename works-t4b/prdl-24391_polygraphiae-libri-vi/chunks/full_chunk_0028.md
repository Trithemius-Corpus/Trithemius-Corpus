76 EXPLANATION

We have shown a sufficient method of numbering by Latin letters, in the manner of the Hebrews and the Greeks, which we have set here, by way of digression, in a reasoned place, although it is also found earlier in the sixth book. And by the fact that, as we proceeded, we have here brought our number to a close at thirty times a hundred times a hundred thousand, we plainly declare that it can be extended to infinity. If this method of numbers seems difficult to anyone, he will easily be able to devise a better one.

Also, in imitation of the Nortmanni, from the numerical letters prescribed above we have arranged a new alphabet, by which you will be able safely to write and announce to an absent friend any secret whatever you wish. This method of writing is indeed as secret as it is new, and before us never seen or heard of; so that it seems to me wholly impossible that it could be understood by anyone without the instruction of a teacher. But now let us set down its alphabet.

laaibid ifeib, ieifeigeicckhib, cakeibid. i et kabb. gidbhid. eigih ieeigigh ibkig. & kif. iaaibifhid ibeicdaka. eih. icei et kaib.

The hidden meaning of this writing is as follows: Beware the bearer of the present letters, because he is a very wicked man, a thief, a robber, a liar, and worthless.

From the demonstration of this example it is easily given to be understood how safe and secure this method of writing is, universally, in every language known to the writer, and in every cause or business, however difficult, secret, important, or lengthy. Nor do I think that there is any person living in the world who, by his own wit and without a teacher, could read this writing. For all who see it will think it nothing other than that the letters have been transposed; and when they cannot attain an understanding of them, finally wearied by despair, they will cease from investigation, and whatever you have written will remain hidden. For no one, as I think, will weigh out this technique of writing secretly by his own powers as it actually is, unless he has either the Polygraphia, or this key to it, at hand, or has been instructed by another.

from any other following number. But in that progression you should know that care must be taken that, to represent one letter, no more than two numerical letters be taken. Hence, when you have arrived at 1000, that is, ten times one hundred thousand, stop your course, and do not wander any further, lest the multitude of characters confuse the beauty of the writing by spoiling it. For, with unity excluded and the characters up to ten times one hundred thousand included, they will suffice for nearly thirty individual alphabets. Nevertheless, the method of the first alphabet, beginning with unity, is more beautiful for wrapping secrets in writing, insofar as it is shorter and simpler. In short, it makes the writing less tortuous.

This writing contains in itself the following sense: Know, friend, that the king has decreed in council that you are to be killed. Therefore flee as quickly as possible into Narbonese Gaul from before his face, lest you perish.

This method of writing too is tortuous and is subject to no regular rule, since it can be varied and changed at the writer’s pleasure by chance transposition as much as anyone wishes.

Next it should be noted that at the end of the sixth book of the Polygraphia we handed down another method of writing by numerical signs, which people commonly call ciphers; under its covering you may safely write to an absent friend whatever you wish, and no one ignorant of the art will be able either to discover or to suspect that a secret lies hidden, especially if the rules which we have given on these matters are observed with diligent caution. This is that the sheet should be formed in the manner of an astronomical table, as though it contained something pertaining to the reckoning of the stars; that is, in such a way that the characters of signs or planets are inserted, so that anyone into whose hands it may perhaps fall by chance, being led on by the likeness of a table, will not think that any secret lies hidden in it, but will suppose that everything pertains to some reckoning of astronomy.
