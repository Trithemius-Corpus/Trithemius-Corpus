They named the third intelligence of Jupiter Zachariel. Taking up dominion over the world after Anael, it made human beings restless with desire for rule, to conduct hunts, make tents, and adorn themselves with various garments. While he ruled, in accordance with the character of Jupiter, who deprived his father of his kingdom, a great division arose between the good and the wicked: the former calling upon God, the latter devoted to the flesh and the world. Under this rule Adam, the first man, died, leaving to all posterity the testament of dying once.

The fourth mover, or angel of Mercury, Raphael, exercising influence after Zachariel upon worldly things, began in the 1055th year of the world, at the end of the month of February, and lasted until the 1410th year of the world. Under him the use of letters is said first to have been invented. The letters at that time, however, as they report, were very different from those which the world has today, devised in the form and likeness of trees, plants, and animals. Yet afterward, with time, they were altered in many ways, and took on an easier manner of formation and a more cultivated adornment. For the fictions of the poets have said that Mercury is the patron of all literature, promising that great secrets of things lie hidden beneath fables. After this, when the human race had multiplied and had spread everywhere over the whole earth, the unity of letters did not long remain; rather, the separation of bodies and minds far from one another discovered diverse and various modes of writing, concerning which the account would be long for us. in the Phaedrus relates that a certain philosopher named Theuth first invented letters and the use of writing, together with the system of weights, numbers, and the game of dice, and taught them to Thamus, king of the Egyptians. But concerning the other ancient men who are said to have handed down special letters to almost individual nations and peoples, which remain in their use down to the present day, it is not my purpose on this occasion to set out or describe the account. For the more learned among our people know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said first to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: α, ε, γ, δ, ζ, ω, λ, μ, ν, ι, θ, ξ, π, σ, τ, ψ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is said to have added four: ξ, θ, φ, χ. And Pythagoras later added the letter υ, so that there would be twenty-one; ζ, κ, and ω were subsequently invented by others. Gordanis, bishop of the Goths, gave his people letters proper to them.

When the unity of language had been confounded, the simplicity of characters also was varied in many ways. From this it came about that the use of letters, in the course of time, claimed such license for itself that not only individual peoples, but even individual persons, fashioning new characters for letters at their own discretion, prepared hiding places for their mysteries. Yet every writing in an unusual character will not be free from the suspicion of those who see it. Nevertheless, many kings, tyrants, and princes of old devised for themselves a private manner of writing, separate from common use, caring little for the suspicion of others,
