*There were also, as Bede bears witness.*) The sense is this: the ancients not only wrote or spoke with words, as we do, but also, with a certain deliberate skill, transferred to the signifying of things whatever the world contains and whatever can be grasped by us. Diodorus Siculus, in Book 4, says: “The letters of the Egyptians are very like various animals, the extremities of human beings, and tools, especially those of craftsmen. For their words are not expressed by the composition of syllables or by letters, but by the form of images, with their meaning handed down to human memory by usage.” They write, for instance, a hawk, a crocodile, a serpent, a human eye, hands, a face, and other things of this sort.

The hawk denotes a thing quickly done, since this bird is almost the swiftest of all; this notation is transferred to domestic matters that are done quickly. The crocodile signifies evil. The eye is interpreted as the guardian of justice and the keeper of the whole body. Among the remaining parts of the body, the right hand with fingers spread out designates freedom, but the left hand with fingers clenched designates tenacity and avarice. In the same way, the figure of the other parts of the body and tools denoted some definite thing; and, once these had been learned by human memory through long use and reflection, people recognized at once what they expressed.

Petrus Crinitus also, Book 7, chapter 2, writes that this manner of writing existed among the Egyptians. Concerning the letters of the Egyptians, which are called hieroglyphic, there is mention among ancient authors; and this can still be observed on obelisks, on which those figures and likenesses of various animals, herbs, rivers, and trees are carved, signifying the philosophy of that nation, as Strabo and Pliny, very weighty authorities, attest. For on the obelisks of the kings Sesostris and Sennosornis, which were brought from Egypt into the city, the interpretation of the nature of things was marked by symbols of this sort and by hieroglyphic signs. Thus the philosophy of the Egyptians is believed first to have been illustrated, as is handed down by the same Pliny and by others. Among other figures,
