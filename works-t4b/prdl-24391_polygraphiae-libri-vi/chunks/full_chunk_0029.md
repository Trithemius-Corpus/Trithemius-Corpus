But whoever wishes to use his own natural industry prudently will find subtler things from ours, and what we have handed down not sufficiently polished he will easily make better. In the numerical orchema great secrets lie hidden; if anyone properly understands it, he will penetrate many mysteries of numbers. For we had neither the time nor the reason on this occasion to pour everything out into public view; but in our judgment what we have done will be enough for the instruction of those to whom our discoveries will someday be pleasing, so that they may have many methods not only for writing secretly whatever they wish to absent partners in their industry, but also a way of producing one method into several better and subtler ones, according to each person’s capacity of understanding.

Likewise, since the numerical orchemata are sufficiently open and clear in their own places, together with their expanded tables, it was not necessary here also to repeat what has already been said, lest excessive length produce weariness for the reader. Nor did I think it necessary to set down an example of more secret writing by means of numerical figures, since there is no difficulty in understanding the method for all who possess even moderate talent. With similar ease the most ancient signs of numbering, which we have brought back into the light, will be able to be understood and grasped; by them the knowledge of numbers is extended to infinity. THE APOLOGY OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS, PREFIXED TO THE STEGANOGRAPHY.

It is the opinion of many that the ancient philosophers and sages of art and nature, if they had discovered any secrets, concealed them by various methods and figures, lest they come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews confirm that Moses also, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, covered ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words in his description of the creation of heaven and earth. Saint Jerome too, one of the most learned among our writers, says that almost as many mysteries lie hidden in the Apocalypse as there are words. I pass over the sages of the Greeks, men of no small reputation and glory among their own people, who, devoting their effort to writing fables, by prudent invention handed down one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men in the sequence of a single narrative. These most zealous lovers of wisdom, even if because of the slowness of my talent I cannot perfectly imitate them, I nevertheless judge it beautiful and glorious to admire, and with whatever diligence I can to read and venerate them.
