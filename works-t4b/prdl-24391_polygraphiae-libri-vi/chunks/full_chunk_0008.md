Pythagoras of Samos, that philosopher, warned his disciples not to forget the quaternary, in which the first perfection of counting is contained. For thus we count: one, two, three, four. Stop your mind there, for you have ten. For the denary is the head of perfection. What is eleven, except the denary and unity? What is twenty? Twice ten. What is thirty? Three times ten. What is forty? Four times ten. You have one hundred, and you have by no means departed from the quaternary. And what is one hundred, except ten times ten? In these things, beyond measure, mysteries lie hidden, and they are manifest to few.

For the sake of brevity we pass over many in silence, who, assisting nature by art, applied prudence to secrets, and by keen study brought into easy use what seemed impossible to many who did not know. A cultivated mind can advance, and one who clings to God becomes, in understanding, higher than the world. Many are the mysteries of kings, and counsels full of cares, in which confidence and faith are required; when faith is rare it harms the one who trusts, and even when doubt is present it often admits him. Therefore it would be worth the effort for all princes to be philosophers, endowed with singular wisdom, just, gentle, benevolent, and distinguished for generosity, so that they themselves would neither wish to deceive others nor easily be overthrown by others. To Emperor Maximilian I.
23

Philip, Count Palatine and Duke of Bavaria, I, Johannes Trithemius, now abbot of the monastery of St. James at Würzburg, but then of St. Martin the Bishop in Sponheim, in the diocese of Mainz, began to write eight books of mysteries in the Steganography. While I was already continuing my night-work on the third book of that same work, I am going to tell the matter briefly, if I can.

In the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1499, in the second Roman indiction, when Arnold Bostius, a Carmelite of the town of Ghent, asked me by letter what new work I was bringing forth, and with what studies I was occupied, on the second day of Palms I wrote back to him through merchants, as to a friend, that I had under the hammer a new and wonderful work of Steganography, and I reported in order what was contained in each of its books. But before my letter had reached Ghent, Bostius, in the days of Easter, passed from life to Christ. The prior of that place received the letter, opened it, read it, and, astonished, as I think, by the novelty of the promises, shared it, at the request of many others, to be read and copied.

Hence it happened that my letter itself, within a short time, was spread through almost all Germany and France, and was copied many times by many people; it brought even many most learned men to amazement and wonder, to such a degree that they hesitated as to what, in truth, they ought to think of me. Among these were some who steadfastly asserted that my promises in the said letter were frivolous, impossible, and false, shamelessly devised by me for the sake of empty glory.
