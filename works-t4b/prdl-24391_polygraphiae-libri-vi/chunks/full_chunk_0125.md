This was the alphabet of Iaimiel, the great king of the Artici, surnamed Megalopius, a most wise man, which he used in secret matters; and this alphabet too can be changed in many ways. To it, out of love for our prince, we have added this alphabet of our own, in which, when it has been used more often over time, a scribe will become more ready.

Quem-

604

HOW
WE MAY SAFELY WRITE
ANY SECRETS BY NUMBERS
AND BY MANY OTHER VARIED METHODS.

These things are childish, I admit, since each person, according to his own capacity and judgment, can devise many alphabets more elegant than those we have recorded in this volume. For that reason we knowingly omit many things which, for a similar reason, we judge to be childish, common, and of little utility. I fear that someday I shall be ashamed to have applied my mind to these trifles while more useful matters were left aside. But I beg my readers to pardon me, since my own will indeed drew me back from these childish studies, not to say at least partly vain ones; yet the insistence of certain friends compelled me to write them, and I could neither refuse nor ought to have refused them without injury to the bond of friendship contracted.

Nevertheless, moved by their request, I also had to attach the following chapter to what has been written before, and to place numbers in place of letters. For the ancients devised various and manifold methods of writing by means of numerical figures, by which, through subtle invention, they safely transmitted their secrets to one another. Persuaded therefore by the prayers of friends, we have placed at the end of this sixth book a few methods out of many, by which a path to knowledge of the others may be open to those who do not know them. For if anyone takes pleasure in knowing these things, he will easily be able, once these are known, to ascend to higher things, and to vary the invented methods at his own discretion by manifold alternation.

As the beginning of this matter, we have placed a table under the orchema, which, when its reading is varied in the form of a cross, will contain in itself ninety-six alphabets.

Whoever rightly understands the table of the orchema will compose from it as many alphabets as he wishes. For example, we have placed the Latin characters transversely, meeting one another from the four corners, so that we might provide a leap of transposition without limit. Writers will need diligence, lest the multitude of numbers confuse the memory or the understanding.
