A LITTLE INDEX, OR TABLE, OF THE THINGS THAT ARE CONTAINED IN THE SIXTH BOOK.

In the sixth book, which is the last book of our Polygraphy, after introducing a mental narration, we have set out in order the minute details of almost all the preceding signs and degrees, and, with arithmetical distributions, have drawn the offices of the letters into the service of cabalistic operation. One thing lies hidden here in the bark, and another is found in the common operation of the signs. For just as the spirit in the blood gives life to the human body, so the vigor hidden in the art commends the operation. Therefore, whoever needs knowledge of this invention should open the eyes of his understanding, so that he may comprehend what is the particular nature of each operation. For we desire everything to be open to good men in such a way that, if it could be done, it would nevertheless never become manifest to the reprobate. Necessity compelled us to fit the letters of the Latins to numbers, lest, if we were too obscure, the intention of our mind should be clear to no one. Yet this novelty, so that it might not be made public without reasonable cause, had to be mystically hidden under unknown signs and marks of numbering. For nocturnal winds are always stronger than daytime winds, and just as night interrupts day, so light shudders at darkness. What nature denies, no one successfully dares. The work is good and natural, and can be turned to a good use or a bad one, according as the desire of each person's will is moved.

A sword is a good instrument, and is turned to a good use whenever it is carried for defense without injury to one's neighbor. But if it is unjustly extended to the harming of anyone, through abuse a good thing is slackened into evil. Therefore, so that good men may have the means by which they may prudently provide for their affairs, the Polygraphy is written by us; and so that no occasion for offending may be offered to the wicked, the kernel is hidden in the bark under various enigmas. He who is good does not abuse a good thing for evil; rather, if he can, he turns evil into good.

WHAT ARE THE CAUSES OF THIS WORK OF OURS, THE POLYGRAPHY, AND AT THE SAME TIME ITS ADVANTAGES.

We undertook the labor of the present work, both rare and difficult, for no other reason than to provide for the advantage of those in need. I say those in need, not of food or drink, but of assistance in preserving mysteries without danger. We therefore judge it worthwhile,

if we briefly note the advantages and benefits that can come to those who use these inventions of ours. Indeed, since in our household affairs there are no matters such that we need these teachings for the concealment of secrets, we have surely labored for those to whose command deference was owed. The first advantage is that, once the understanding of this work has been completed, it gives an easy method by which you may safely, secretly, and without any suspicion from any observers make known the secret of your mind, by the industry of the art, as broadly as there is need, across any distance of places, in regulated speech. What lies hidden in these things the work of Steganography brings forth; it requires neither letters nor a messenger who knows the secrets he carries. It would be otherwise in matters admitting the light, according to the common custom of all our people. Do not understand the matter otherwise than it is. The second advantage is that, by the ministry of this work, in a short space of time, you will teach a man ignorant of the Latin language, provided only that he knows how to read and write letters in his mother tongue, to write in Latin as you wish, ornately or elegantly, and to conceal and hide all the secret mysteries of his will, and to communicate them to one who knows the art, as widely as he pleases, safely, secretly, and without suspicion. Again I warn you beforehand not to understand that we have promised otherwise than we do promise.
