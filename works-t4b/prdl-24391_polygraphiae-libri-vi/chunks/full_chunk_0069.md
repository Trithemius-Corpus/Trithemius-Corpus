For this reason we have placed the minutiae before the individual degrees by an artful invention, so that we might relieve the person working in them from the labor of numbering the paraphrases. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in the matters that we have handed down as the principles of the new art, believe me, you will find greater things hidden there than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these things, you should know one thing, and not the least: that by the prudent operation of metathesis, whenever you wish, you will make even someone highly skilled in this art ignorant, someone seeing blind, and someone learned unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, that there are eighteen thousand worlds for every human being, this invention of ours would provide each person with individual ways of safely and secretly writing and communicating, very widely, whatever secrets they wished in Latin.

The reckoning of mortal beings, by art, attempts to imitate nature, which admits no calculation of the monad. Therefore, although the arrangement of the preceding book of the *Polygraphia*, through the extension and metathesis of particulars, is in a sense sufficient for the universals of this art in the infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a definite consideration has moved us to join one thing to the other. The condition of light is different in the two cases, but night preserves the order of its course inviolate in both. A prudent reader, from these small inventions of ours, not polished with complete exactness, will easily be able to discover greater and more polished ones, if he is vigorous in outstanding talent. We, however, persuaded by reason, remain under the service of the binary and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor, that we might raise our undertaking to weightier matters, is an occasion given; if it is offered to us in an acceptable way, we shall not refuse to make clearer things manifest to those who desire them.

For it is written: Where there is no hearing, never pour out speech. Therefore, adding the second book of the *Polygraphia* to the first, we keep its same form and method, placing 308 paraphrases in the light of day by simple progression, in honorable memory of the most chaste Mother of God. If the time had been suitable, ability could have provided more graceful things, both to add brilliance to the material and to give sufficient advancement to the form. The first inventor of any art transmits greater things to posterity, because, since the beginnings of an art are hard and unfamiliar, no subtle invention is found completed from its origin.
