JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS OF SPANHEIM TO THE READER.

Since all the letters of the Greeks and Hebrews are numerals, I have often wondered that the ingenious Latinity of the ancients did not also make its own letters serve for numbers. The Hebrews, by doubling certain letters, established twenty-eight signs sufficient for every number. The Greeks, by adding four characters, relieved this lack of letters with similar foresight. We therefore, having twenty-four letters, why do we not also make ours numerals, since we can likewise find four duplicated ones? Let us give four as an example, and there will be enough for every number: m and final iij; likewise r and final 2; likewise long s and final or closed s; likewise closed v and open u. When we add these to the twenty-four ordinary letters, we have twenty-eight characters sufficient for all numbers.

And it will be no matter of great labor for those who already know letters to grasp this method of numbering, if they once commit it to memory. For this invention of ours will be able to be useful to many, since it comprises many thousands in number with few letters, all of which can be discerned and understood at first sight, more clearly than light, without any calculation. Many, less experienced in the art of calculation, think that this cannot be done so easily by the figures of algorism, especially in large numbers. For as soon as you inspect with your eye a number, however great, you will be able both to understand it fully and to express it, without circumlocution or division of speech. To show how easy this is, it is fitting that we set down the letters themselves reduced to number.

I wrote this on 16 March, in the year 1508.
