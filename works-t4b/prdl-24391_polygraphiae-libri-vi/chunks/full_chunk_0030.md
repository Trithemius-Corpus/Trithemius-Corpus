Nor, as I think, has my opinion altogether deceived me, since through the continual study of reading I have learned many things that I did not know before, and have opened for my thoughts an entrance to investigating more secret matters. For although I am not of such great learning or industry that I would dare profess that I have grasped that method of the ancient sages for concealing mysteries, nevertheless I have devised certain methods, not wholly to be despised, as it seems to me, of writing and concealing, secretly and entirely without any suspicion, whatever you wish. At the request of the most serene prince, lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and Prince Elector of the Roman Empire, the wisest patron of philosophers, than whom no one seemed to me more worthy for this great secret to be revealed to him, I have committed these methods to writing and brought them together in the following volume, not without immense labor, lest this great secret come to the ears of common charlatans or wicked men.

I ask you, reader, whoever you may be in the future, to remember how many evils in the commonwealth could be committed by wicked and wanton men through this science, if it came to the knowledge of many: adulteries, fornications, conspiracies, betrayals, murders, robberies, and countless evils. For just as a good and honorable man can, whenever he wishes, safely and without any suspicion from anyone, make known and express the secret of his will to another who knows this art, for private or common advantage, so every perverse, wanton, and malicious man, once every obstacle has been entirely removed, will be able to disclose his evil desire, however shamefully suited to his pleasure,

Nor could fidelity between married persons thereafter remain secure, since a woman, although hitherto ignorant of the Latin language, yet made thoroughly learned in effect through holy and chaste words of any language or idiom, could very fully understand the evil and shameless intention of her lover, even while her husband presents and praises the letters, and could in the same way send back to him, quite safely whenever she wished, her own desire at length and in sufficiently eloquent speech.

Indeed, although this science may seem childish to some, nevertheless, if it were made public, the whole order of the commonwealth would be disturbed, public trust would perish, letters, documents, writings, and finally all the very conversations of men would forever be turned into suspicion. No one would any longer trust letters, however good, honorable, and holy; no one would put faith in a letter, because, however holy and chaste the words might be, deceit, fraud, and evil deception would always be thought to lie beneath them. Men would become fearful in all things, and no less suspicious of mutual conversations than of any letters whatever. Nor could anyone ever become so learned and experienced in this science itself that there would not remain infinitely many ways, sufficient for all purposes, of writing secretly and most hiddenly, which he himself had not yet grasped.
