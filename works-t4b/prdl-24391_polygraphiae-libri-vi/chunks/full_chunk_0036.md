Such is catoptrics, which teaches how to present visible signs of things to sight in a moment, through certain images projected far from us outside mirrors into the air, and established for signifying some speech; these are easily understood by one who has knowledge of the signs. But by what method it can come about that all things are transferred to the use of signs is not part of the present undertaking, but belongs to the Steganographia. The very learned man Johannes Trithemius recounts the contents of that work in a certain letter to Bostius in these words, etc.

“I now have in hand a great work which, if it is ever published, which may God forbid, the whole world will marvel at; the title of its first book is Steganographia. There will be four books, and each will be divided into at least one hundred chapters. I began this work at the instance of a certain great prince, whom there is no need to name. You ask what this work teaches? It teaches very great things, astonishing and incredible to all who do not know them, which have never been heard from the beginning of the world. For the first book contains more than one hundred methods of writing secretly whatever you wish, without any suspicion, without transposition of letters,

without any fear, so that there is no person in the world who by natural skill could know, or even suspect, what is contained in my letters, except one who knows the art from me or from someone whom I have taught. The words are plain and familiar, lacking all suspicion, but no one without the art will ever be able to perceive my intention, however learned he may be; and the thing is truly astonishing. The second book contains many more marvelous things. In this art, namely, I can make the conception of my mind known by fire to one who knows my art at any distance whatsoever, at one hundred miles or more, securely, without words, without writings, without signs, through any messenger. If he is apprehended on the road, even if he is questioned under the harshest tortures, he can reveal nothing about my message, because he knows absolutely nothing about it. Whatever may happen, my message will always remain hidden, nor could all the people of the whole world, if they were gathered together, trace it out by natural power. I can also do this without a messenger whenever I wish. I can also indicate my will to one who knows the art while he is sitting in prison, even though I am far absent, however closely he is guarded, even if he were sitting three miles underground. And I can do all these things most broadly and universally, whenever and as often as I wish, naturally, without any superstition or the aid of any spirits whatsoever. These things are marvelous, I admit; but hear things still more marvelous. The third book teaches an art by
