Having shown the possibility of our new invention, briefly and yet sufficiently, I do not think that I myself should be wearied any longer on this same occasion, since the method provides a path without limit, if anyone should need an example for further things. Nor do I think it at all necessary to recall to memory the things that have been recited in the preceding sections, since a shrewd reader, if he is studious, will also be able, with the Lord's help, to discover the greater things that lie hidden in these matters. We have handed over to the wise and studious man both the material and the cause; if he wishes to use these prudently, he will surpass our invention by his own talent.

But whoever desires this path, laid out in a fairer shade, should join hand and mind to talent, and should make for himself a route more beautifully adorned by paraphrase. Let anyone whom these things delight keep firmly in memory that he must always remain within the prescribed circle; if he transgresses it in any way, the whole operation vanishes uselessly. There are many names of human beings; indeed there are almost infinite words for things, villages, cities, provinces, herbs, trees, stones, and verbs, all of which serve our instructions. From these it is established that it is not impossible for a book and a method to be made, if diligence and talent are at hand, which may surpass our inventions without limit. But the man who is of such great dullness that he cannot understand these childish matters of ours: when will he penetrate the subtler things that remain?

We set an end to the fourth book of the Polygraphia, to which we join the fifth in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.

HERE BEGINS THE FIFTH BOOK OF THE POLYGRAPHIA OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS, ABBOT OF SAINT JAMES THE APOSTLE AT WÜRZBURG.

The things which, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have set forth as best we could in the four preceding books for the completion of our Polygraphia, reasonably require that in the fifth book we briefly treat the remaining precepts and methods of this instruction that are still left. Therefore in it we shall give a sufficiently concise method by metathesis, that is, by transposition of letters, by which a friend may write to a friend whatever he wishes securely, safely, and most secretly. We read that Caesar Augustus used this manner of writing in secret matters. Although this method of sending messages is safe in many ways, nevertheless

nevertheless to those who look at it it is regarded as suspect; for as soon as the transposition of letters is seen, anyone who is curious immediately suspects that something secret lies hidden underneath. Now the modes of this invention are almost infinite; from them, for the sake of a necessary introduction, we have marked out the following ones for the reader, reserving the rest to be investigated by his own talent.

First, therefore, we have placed before him the canonical table of this practice, and have set beneath it a second, reversed one. In these two tables the root and foundation of the transposition just mentioned are placed altogether, and on this the structure of the whole building is set. For when these two tables have been duly known and understood, no transposition of letters will be able to escape the diligent worker. To these we have joined twenty-four alphabets in order, so that we might provide the reader with an abundance for transposing letters. The use of these consists as desired in individual ones, or, if you prefer, also in all of them.
