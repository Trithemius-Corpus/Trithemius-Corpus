Preface of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of St. James at Wurzburg, to the Polygraphia, addressed to Maximilian I, Caesar.

We read that many wise men of old, philosophers, kings, and princes, once devised various and manifold methods by which they might safely entrust to their messengers, when they were being sent to more distant places, the secrets of counsel and whatever secret mystery might arise that had to be entrusted, so that they would be fit to carry them and secure from every attack of the wicked, lest either the simplicity of the cabalists disclose the mystery, or the innate curiosity of the Bacucei bring harm upon apostolic affairs. Great Augustus, Caesar of the Romans, among these, wishing in difficult matters to make use of the service of messengers in obscurity, changed the utterances of conceived speech from clear forms into dark ones; and by the dance of metathesis, with a fine invention, he made their barbarousness, though resisting his wishes, accord at his command. And that receptive and, as it were, perpetual order is nevertheless subject to will and to the reckoning of time; although it keeps the messengers safe on the road from the attack of the Bacucei,

Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the foremost mathematician, fastened all around a white bird suited to his designs, bare in head and feet, to a staff cut back into the form of a tetragon; and, when the servants had been arranged in due proportion, the work of the white bird appeared changed. When this had been properly carried out, he loosed with his own hand what had been fastened; and when he had allowed it to fly away, there suddenly came about among the familiars of the inner chambers a great separation of appearances, and at once there arose the previously far too wandering monster of a tortuous monastic image, at the sight of which no one could think what it was. Nor did the dissension of the owls come to rest until the master's image had skillfully bound the magpie again with a similar tetragon. Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator, using both talent and art, changed the minutiae of phrase into signs, and set the diligence, or character, of his will in place of the customary phrase. Indeed, in this very novelty he used such abundance that his invention could suffice for almost all things in the world. To these heralds of mysteries, moreover, the institution of the art gave a remarkable agility, so that, beyond the nature of all things, with solid steps of the feet, and not by minutiae or moments, but by whole degrees, they serve the good will of the master.
