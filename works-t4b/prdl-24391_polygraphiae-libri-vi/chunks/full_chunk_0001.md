OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
ABBOT OF PEAPOLIS,
FORMERLY OF SPONHEIM, TO MAXIMILIAN I, CAESAR:
Six Books of Polygraphy.

To these,
besides the Key and the Observations of Adolf von Glauburg, patrician of Frankfurt,
there has newly been added a booklet by the same author, On the Seven Secundeans, or Intelligences moving the spheres after God.

A work of most hidden knowledge, in which the principles of Steganography also lie concealed, and a method most useful and altogether admirable, both in teaching and in learning, is shown to the ingenious.

STRASBOURG,
at the expense of Lazarus Zetzner, bookseller.

ROYAL LIBRARY
OF MUNICH.

THE PRINTER TO THE READER,
Greeting.

When, at the urging of certain persons, candid Reader, I had resolved to publish anew at my own expense the Polygraphy of Johannes Trithemius, I diligently applied myself to this: that it might come forth both more correct and enlarged by a new addition. And so, having sought out various editions from everywhere, I diligently compared them with the most recent; and besides, I added also a manuscript exemplar of Jacob Trithemius, brother of Johannes, and, after making a collation with the remaining editions, I found it nearly similar to them, apart from a few readings. Secondly, I appended the same author’s booklet on the Secundeans, or Intelligences moving the sphere after God. It is yours, kind Reader, to take in good part this endeavor of ours, such as it is. Farewell.

TO THE DIVINE
MAXIMILIAN I,
CAESAR, THE LETTER
OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
Abbot of St. James at Würzburg,
on the Polygraphy.

I was hesitating, with wavering mind, most invincible Caesar Maximilian, and for some little while I doubted within myself whether I would be doing something worth the labor if I dedicated the volume of our Polygraphy to Your Caesarean Majesty. For among the great you are greatest, among the more powerful most powerful, and among all the wise by far the wisest; for this reason nothing ought to be inscribed to you except what is great, nothing dedicated except what has been worked out with the highest learning. But I, having attained nothing, or little, of literary learning, hesitated for some time, not without reason, whether I ought to bring my trifles before so great a Majesty, since I am neither fit in intellect to elaborate anything, nor adorned with the necessary charm of expression. For I know, and cannot deny, how scanty my equipment is, how unpolished my intellect, and that I have no vein of eloquence for speaking; and for this reason, upon just consideration, I stood back for some time, and as an unfit writer was afraid to appear

before your glorious Majesty. For since after God you are by right Lord of the world, it is no wonder if I, unlearned and the least of monks, fear the judgment of so great a Majesty as yours, and am very afraid to offer those lucubrations of mine which I know are not worthy of your hands. And while I was turning these matters over in my mind, a messenger arrived with letters sent by your Highness, in which you summoned me to Cologne, the expenses necessary for the journey having already some time before been appointed through another. But when I had read again the letters of Your Serenity, confidence soon overcame shame, and consideration of your inborn gentleness drove away fear. For I know your disposition to be most mild: since you are the most modest of all, you are accustomed both to show gentleness to the humble and to resist the proud. You do not know how to be vainly exalted, nor, in the manner of tyrants, to despise the humble who are devoted to Your Highness; for just as you have no way to increase on earth, so no envy will penetrate your mind. You are the greatest, and many things, great things too, you have accomplished no less bravely than wisely, both in peace and in war; by these you have attained an eternal name, and, amid reverence for the highest honors, you are celebrated with the love and favor of all your subjects. These are the riches of princes: the love and favor of the people, and to do justice to each one according to the commandment of the charity of God. Since, therefore,

all men carry your name to the stars with praises, and all proclaim you most mild and most humane, there was henceforth no reason why I should suspect that you would be more severe toward me in particular, especially when by your letter you deign to encourage me to write. Animated at last, therefore, by the weighing of these things, I laid aside all fear and took up great confidence in the gentleness inborn in you. Indeed, since all draw confidence from the fountain of your piety, why should I alone be cruel to myself? Therefore, with that humility which is fitting, which I both owe and can offer, I approach the throne of your most excellent Majesty, prostrate upon the ground; I devotedly offer the aforesaid work of Polygraphy, and humbly commend my littleness to Your Serenity as a servant to his lord. Receive now, then, most clement Caesar, the very slight little gift which I bring, in which may you deign to weigh my mind, most subject to you, not its value. Now in the following system of Polygraphy there are, in certain places, some mysteries wrapped in enigmas, lest the deeper meaning of the art, which lies hidden, become manifest to the understanding of all.
