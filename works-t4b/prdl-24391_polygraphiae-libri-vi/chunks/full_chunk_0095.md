Book III of the Polygraphia of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Peapol, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.

What, from the beginning of human creation, confounded the unity of language? Was it not the division of bodies and the diversity of minds? For as Truth testifies in the Gospel: “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.” Language is the instrument for bringing forth those conceptions which the mind has thought within itself, in its inward parts. Yet there are several modes of this ministry, by which the secrets of the mind are brought to sense; their properties vary both by usage and by art. Usage, introduced at pleasure, is common to many; art, however, to few. Finally, how much the very unregulated usage of speech has varied among the Germans alone in six hundred years, no one will readily judge who has not often reread Otfrid, the monk of Wissembourg, in his poems in the German language. The sea flows and ebbs; human beings, once they have died, do not return again to the present life. Customs succeed

--- Page 466 ---

customs, and the vigor of mortal minds is always declining where better things are concerned. Who today, among the Germans, those ancient emulators of the Greeks, retains knowledge of the old idiom? In the chronicles and old histories of the Franks, Hunibald, Doracus, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed many things said in ancient verse and in the prose of the German tongue into their compositions; these are understood today by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own native idiom among the East Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhinelanders, Moselle people, Cologne people, Saxons, and Thuringians undergoes notable variation every day? Human beings change in time; customs also vary; minds change; clothing also varies. The simplicity of languages is altered, the old property of idioms is varied, and nothing stable is found in human affairs. For there is a vicissitude of all things, and variety succeeds the simplicity of minds. Who will be able to bring this perpetual flowing away, so to speak, back to the concord of unity? For many times pass away, as Scripture says, and knowledge will be manifold. That we add a third book to the two preceding books of the Polygraphia has not lacked a special reason. For since human wills are varied, it is rightly the case that manifold methods are given for honorable operations.

In those, day illuminates night; in these, however, day is without light. There, light leads shadow around in light; here, however, the blind illuminates the blind, and, existing and remaining without eyes, supplies in darkness the sight which it does not have. Here night clouds day over with perpetual darkness, and one walking in darkness provides guidance without light, under a blind leadership. These paraphrases serve all people throughout the world, and in every language they transmit any secret whatever safely, securely, and without danger to the cabalistic binary. Latin raises laughter among barbarians, and barbarism itself creates suspicion among Latin readers. Yet it matters nothing, or little, since whatever the suspicion may be, it will nevertheless contain every most secret mystery. Let the diligent operator recall to mind the rules and precepts that we have handed down, lest he labor uselessly and in vain for himself and for others. Understanding is needed, for without it the usefulness of no art will be established. We have shown in a few words a path to the greatest things, and have given by a finite mode a way to infinite things. From the things given and received, greater things are easily prepared for friends. Reader, remember that the retrograde mark can be fixed in every place until you take hold of the mark of rest. Farewell. From Peapol, on the first day of March, in the year of the Lord 1508.
