For after prayers nothing is more useful to you than sacred reading, which destroys vain and shameful thoughts, instills holy desires, and, according to the saying of Saint Jerome, kills the vices of the flesh.

Alas, what shall I say, not about a few but about many cloistered prelates, who can neither devote themselves to prayers nor give even a little time to readings? Always entangled in external affairs, they neglect spiritual things; they seek the comforts of the flesh; they cannot remain long in the monastery; they often seem to gasp after towns and taverns. To them the monastery is a prison, silence a punishment, and all devotion utterly contrary. Every action of such men is found to be full either of negligence or of vices. Of these, some earnestly direct their subjects, but not themselves, toward monastic observance; others despise regular discipline both in themselves and in their subjects.

About these men I would indeed have abundant material for speaking, but the hour of the present occasion does not allow it. And what does the holy hierarch of monks say about the maimed pastor? “Everything,” he says, “which the abbot has told his monks to be contrary, let him show by his own actions that it must not be done.” An abbot who lives badly and teaches well is the most foolish of all men, because he calls others to heaven, while he himself voluntarily and knowingly descends to hell. But one who lives badly and does not teach well runs with his diseased sheep to the torments of hell. One, however, who lives well but neglects to teach those committed to him entangles himself in condemnation for the faults of his subjects.

Hence the Lord says to Ezekiel: “When I say to the wicked man, ‘You shall die,’ and you do not announce it to him, he indeed will die in his wickedness, but I shall require his blood from your hand.” What a terrible sentence, always to be feared by pastors: even if they are free from personal sin, nevertheless for the uncorrected faults of their subjects they bear the grave judgment of damnation. Woe to negligent pastors, because by their own faults and by others’ they prepare for themselves in hell punishments beyond measure. And oh, how many abbots will burn endlessly in hell for the sins of their monks, who, had they not been abbots, could have been saved.

For the government of souls, a pastor’s good life is not enough if correction of subjects and apostolic teaching are lacking. Take away every excuse, O abbot of monks: no one compels you against your will to preside over cloistered men, if it is clear to you that you are wholly unfit for governance. Now, therefore, since you have once approached the care of souls, you must take up one of two courses, if you wish to avoid the danger of eternal damnation. Either strive to lead back the monks committed to you to the observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict, or, if you are wholly unable to do this, voluntarily resign the abbacy. It is better to flee the fellowship of sinners with Lot than to perish in the fire with those whom you cannot correct.

And I say what I think. It is better for you to die not an abbot than to live as the pastor of those whom you are not able to govern according to the Rule. Let no deceit of flattering fortune deceive you: either you will be saved with the sheep committed to you, or, if you are negligent, you will not be saved without them. Indeed, the powerful will suffer powerful torments, and a very severe judgment will be made upon those who are in charge. Therefore, if you desire to escape the danger of eternal damnation, feed the sheep committed to you by word; feed them by example.

Third, to the pastors of cloistered men it belongs to feed them with nourishment. It belongs to them to nourish their subjects not only by the word of holy exhortation, not only to go before them by the example of good conduct, but also to provide, according to the Rule, for the necessities of the body. This provision for monks is not to be made in money, nor in annual distributions, but in the daily bestowal of necessary things. Here, pastor, consider yourself, and carefully weigh how you ought to feed the Lord’s sheep.

“Woe,” says the supreme judge, “to the pastors who feed themselves, but scorn to feed the sheep of the Lord.” What do you think, fathers? Are there among cloistered pastors any who feed themselves rather than the Lord’s sheep? I fear there are not a few, but very many among abbots, who, with their sheep neglected, spurned, and despised, feed themselves; they seek their own advantages, not the profits of souls; they acknowledge themselves not as fathers of monks but as lords. Why do you destroy yourself, O pastor and idol, you who, placed as ruler of others, do not live rightly?

Behold, you compel your monks by words and blows toward the observance of the Rule.
