For by as many evil thoughts as the mind is inwardly and pleasurably touched, by so many hidden diseases, as it were, the soul is wounded. But if a perverse thought has gone outward into word or deed, then the hidden disease rises up into a swelling; and unless it is treated prudently and without delay, little by little it grows and gains strength to the destruction of the whole body. For these diseases of the mind, a remedy composed according to art is required in various ways; long experience is judged necessary, since whoever approaches a sick person to cure him while unskilled, so far as lies in him, does not heal but kills. And who is so fit that he can discern the hidden diseases of cloistered minds? Many have killed many sick people through lack of discrimination among diseases. Therefore the greatest learning, doctrine, discretion, and experience are required in a shepherd of souls, so that he may know how to apply suitable remedies to individual diseases, lest inexperience destroy knowledge, lest ignorance confuse the remedies. Meanwhile let discretion in the art be present, until experience may learn without danger. In the art of medicine, indeed, many are learned, but few experienced. And among shepherds of souls we can find very many who are learned and instructed in many things; but we find few who are skilled and experienced in this art. And nothing harms infirm souls so much as the shepherd’s inexperienced knowledge. For one who does not know how to distinguish between one disease and another is often accustomed to drive the sick person into graver dangers. The illnesses of cloistered persons are cured in various ways. At one time the prudent physician of souls uses the authority of the Scriptures; now he strives by arguments to persuade the sick person of the things that pertain to health; at another time he brings forward the examples and paradigms of the ancients; now he subtly lays bare the causes of the disease and the roots fixed in the heart. Indeed, as many kinds of illness as he finds, so many kinds of remedies he sets against them. At one time he teaches the sick person how he ought to be freed from the disease; now, lest a putrid member infect the whole body, he cuts it with the iron of harsh rebuke; at another time, with the wine of sharp correction, he drives out the pus formed in the wound; now with the oil of honey-flowing exhortation

he brings the tender and weak person back to health. To be able to do these and similar things belongs to a most learned physician, whom both learning renders venerable to his subjects and experience renders gracious.

What will the rude and unlearned be able to do in these matters, where even the most learned confess themselves insufficient? The experienced physician trembles when he sees the disease; what will the unskilled one do? But O times, O morals! Today rude and unskilled men approach the care of souls; the infirm are appointed physicians of the infirm. While they desire to cure others without knowledge, they kill both themselves and those others alike. O shepherds, O abbots of this time, who understand nothing of the holy Scriptures, who do not know the art of curing souls, who scorn to be taught by others. They present themselves as lords of their brothers, not fathers; as destroyers of souls, not shepherds. Since, through ignorance, they do not know how to discern the quality of a disease, they set before them not remedies but deadly poisons. They do not know how to impart the word of holy exhortation to their subjects; they blush to comfort those who are tempted; they have always learned to command, never to ask. They preach nothing about the formation of morals to the sheep committed to them; they announce nothing about the future joys of the good; they care absolutely nothing about the salvation of souls. The faults of delinquents they either ruinously conceal, or correct indiscreetly and without charity. Some command with austerity over their subjects, and drive the fainthearted to impatience and rebellion; since they do not know how to apply the moderation of discretion, they allow those whom they command to go through depraved conduct into destruction. Others, having zeal for discipline, are so enveloped in the darkness of ignorance that they do not know at all how they ought to exercise that zeal itself for the salvation of souls. While they desire to amend the morals of delinquents, they corrupt the observance of discipline through indiscreet correction. Others, not knowing the differences of temperaments, determine to approach everyone with one kind of exhortation; since they neglect to fit the sermon to the persons, they bring either no usefulness or little. Others, while they strive to bring back to peace the spirits of their subjects when moved by any displeasures, through imprudence make them more disturbed.
