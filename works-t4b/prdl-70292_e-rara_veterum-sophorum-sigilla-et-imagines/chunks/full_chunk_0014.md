But to return to celestial matters: in precisely the same manner and form as with Mercury, the powers and influences of the remaining planets can be drawn down. Thus, for obtaining honors and dignities, and the favor and goodwill of princes and magnates, there is the image of the Sun when he is in Leo and in the tenth house, in trine with Jupiter or the Moon. Yet in this year 1608 the solar influence is impeded because of Mars opposite from Aquarius, as the remarkable and unusual changes of the very air clearly show. Hence, in forming these figures, this alone must be observed: that the planet whose virtue and influence one wishes to draw to oneself be as free as possible from every impediment.

*Demonstration of the Reasons Whence These Magical Images Have Such Great Force and Efficacy.*

*Treatise VII.*

But why and whence rings, images, and figures engraved in a particular metal or stone possess the powers credited to them is not to be assigned to temperament, nor to manifest qualities, nor to the whole substance, but to a divine and magical cause, that is, to some higher and more sublime cause, which some have not inappropriately called a spiritual vehicle and bond. Through this mediation, body and soul, substances mutually distant, are joined together; by this the magnet draws iron, and chicory follows the motion of the Sun.

By this vehicle and bond intervening or coming between, Mercury is effective in quicksilver, in sharpening or weakening the memory and the cogitative faculty, in the alectorius stone, in a mixed color and flavor, and in Egypt, Greece, Flanders, and Silesia.

By this vehicle intervening, the Moon is effective in silver and glass, in the brain, in the left eye

Therefore, to seek the principle of these hidden influences from those primary and manifest qualities, from temperament, or from the whole substance, is not so much absurd as ridiculous, since the actions of those from whom these virtues flow are not corporeal, and are poured in from heaven without a body. For this reason there is no need here for any measure taken from the senses, no ambitious battle of words, and no empty attempt at demonstrations. That the beginning of every philosophical demonstration cannot be sought from the senses and qualities, but must be sought elsewhere, is clear even from this common example: if someone asks about the color of the white rose, namely whence it derives its origin, and wishes to ascribe its cause to a colder temperament, what, I ask, will he say about the red rose? Will you say that species of endive pour out a milky juice because they are cold? Yet many very hot plants, such as the spurges, swell and drip with the same juice. And is aloe bitter for this reason, because it is hot? But in opium, which kills by its deadly coldness, there is a remarkable bitterness. And although it be granted that such things proceed from the property of the whole substance, nevertheless in our mystery a more sublime and more excellent craftsman, engraver, and fashioner must be placed and sought above that substance.
