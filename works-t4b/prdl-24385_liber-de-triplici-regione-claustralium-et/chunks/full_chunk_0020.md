For the lowest region always sends its citizens to the lower world, unless, led by penitence while still living, they have taken care to renounce dead works.

The second dwelling is called purgatory, and this is compared to the second, rational region. For just as purgatory cleanses and perfects souls, and sends them, once cleansed, to the kingdom of heaven, so this second region, by the continual exercise of perseverance within itself, washes its citizens clean from the filth of sins and disposes them for the ascent to the third region, which is called spirit. In this dwelling all are good, but they must nevertheless be cleansed so that they may become better; and in the second region there are no evil people, but they must nevertheless always be exercised so that they may become better. This region sends its citizens who die within it to purgatory, because even if they are all good, they do not yet seem fit for the kingdom without a temporary purification. For indeed those who inhabit this middle region, although they seem very near to the highest, are nevertheless not yet wholly removed from the dust of the lower one.

The third dwelling of separated souls is called the kingdom of heaven, and this is compared to the highest region, that of spirit. For just as in that heavenly homeland there is highest rest and eternal peace, so in the region of spirit there is continual tranquility of mind.

Here ends the first region.

Preface to the second treatise of the first part, which concerns the middle region, namely the rational region, of cloistered persons.

But now let us at last leave behind this dark region of carnal people, in which a whirlwind of wickedness remains and a continual storm of vices. We have given it enough, and have lingered over it more than our purpose had required. Let us therefore leave the dead to bury their dead; let us yield to the darkness; let us seek the light of spiritual life. We must depart from Egypt, from the dark region, and hasten by the middle way of reason to the highest summit of spirit. Therefore let us now at last approach the middle region of the soul, through which one comes successively to the highest. This region of the soul is quite fittingly called rational, since in it there is continual labor in the search for the highest truth.

The citizen of this middle region always strives to raise the fervor of his mind toward higher things, but the weakness of the flesh and the motion of the concupiscent storm draw him back toward the lowest things. Hence continual labor arises; hence sighs and groans arise, because the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation presses down the sense that thinks on many things. This is the narrow way that leads to life, and few walk through it. In this region violence is imposed upon the concupiscence of the flesh until the will is subjected to the spirit at its nod. For the Lord says in the Gospel: “The kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent seize it.” In this region the war of struggle is proclaimed to those who serve God, as the wise man bears witness: “My son,” he says, “when you approach the service of God, stand in fear, and prepare your soul for temptation.”

How great and how many temptations we undergo in this middle region through the difficulty of spiritual exercise we learn daily, while, driven in various ways, we are unable to remain constantly in fervor of spirit. Now the weakness of the flesh calls us back from the zeal for progress; now the temptation of the ancient enemy vexes us; now the deceitfulness of the world hinders us.
