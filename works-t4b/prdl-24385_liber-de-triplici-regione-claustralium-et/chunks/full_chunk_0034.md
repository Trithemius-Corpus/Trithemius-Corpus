Second, it must be considered that this disposition of a good heart and of an ordered will toward God is a sign of the love of God and of the approval of good works. Thus, when the Lord was born in the flesh, the angels sang: “Peace on earth to human beings of good will.”

Third, it must be noted that, just as a good disposition of the heart always orders and urges the mind through holy exercises toward the fear of God and the performance of virtues, so negligence in a badly disposed heart inclines it especially toward vices and sloth. Consideration makes a conjecture concerning this difference.

Fourth, it must be considered that a bad disposition of the heart hinders and empties out the grace of God and merits in a person, because it makes the soul vile, ignoble, and degenerate, and always disposes it toward graver and worst things. Indeed, the root of evils of tongue and deed comes from an evil heart, as is clear in Saul and in many similar cases.

The fifth consideration is that evil of deed and tongue is never properly examined unless the evil of the heart is first truly known. But each person can examine his own heart better than another can, and consider what sort of person lies hidden within himself. Therefore it is enough for the cenobite, to whom care of another has not been entrusted, to inspect his own heart diligently and in no way to occupy himself with scrutinizing another’s heart. But the pastor, or the monk to whom care of souls is entrusted, is also required to know the face of his flock.

Sixth, it must be noted from these things that it is very rash to wish to examine another’s heart and to judge the inward parts of the brothers from words and deeds. The Lord knows those who are his; nor is it granted to a human being to become a rash judge of another’s heart without the reason of office.

Seventh, it must be noted that a small evil of one’s own heart, when known in oneself, harms more than a great evil suspected uncertainly of another’s heart. For the former must be condemned because it is certain and known; the latter must be piously excused because it is unknown.

Eighth, it must be considered that, because each person sees many imperfections in his own heart that he cannot recognize in another’s, for these, insofar as they are obstacles to grace, he ought to esteem himself lower than others. Insofar as they disfigure the soul, he should despise himself as more vile than another, even if he knows that other person’s public crimes. Indeed, since the root of another’s heart is wholly unknown to him, he cannot know how divine election from eternity has been ordained concerning that person. Who would have believed that the thief crucified with God had been chosen from eternity, if he judged him according to his works? All things turn out for good for the elect, because where sin is seen to have abounded, often, by the will of divine election, greater grace is found. For divine love regards and embraces its elect from eternity, and sees them as chosen not according to the misery in which they now are, but according to their future glory. Therefore, cenobite, be cautious in disparaging another; be cautious in judging another, if you desire to escape eternal death. For who are you, to judge another’s servant? He stands to be judged by his God alone.

The affection of this seventh degree, because of that clause “from the inward affection of the heart,” possesses a threefold root of its origin: namely, truth, hope, and charity. For when the faithful cenobite sees that he does not have his being from himself, he truly regards himself, insofar as he is from himself, as nothing; and consequently, with the same regard, he considers his works, insofar as they are from himself who is nothing, to be nothing, since he knows that nothing comes from nothing. Therefore the monk who, in this respect, reckons himself and his works as nothing gives assent to the truth, because he fears to incur the vice of falsehood if he were to believe himself to be something great. And therefore, withdrawing from himself, he fails in himself, and places his being, life, and power in God alone. Second, this affection arises from hope of acquiring greater grace, because when a person, through humility, fails in himself, he comes nearer to the grace of God. Third, the affection of this humility takes its beginning from love of God and receives continual increase in that love. For whoever loves God perfectly seeks nothing other than his honor in all things, nor does he know how to exalt himself in anything, but desires, on account of God, to be subject to all.
