Second, the shepherd of souls becomes disordered toward his subjects if his mind is too boldly occupied with temporal affairs, if he has greater concern for secular business than for the souls committed to him. Saint Father Benedict forbids this excessive care for temporal things in the Rule, and rightly judges souls and rational beings to be preferred to all worldly goods. “Above all,” he says, “let the abbot not neglect or make light of the salvation of the souls committed to him, and let him not have greater concern for transitory, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always consider that he has undertaken to govern souls, for which he will have to render an account.” Consequently, when the pious father gives assurance concerning the necessities of the body, he does so if one worships God with a pure, loving mind. “And lest perhaps he complain of scant resources,” he says, “let him remember what is written: First seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things will be added to you. And again: Nothing is lacking to those who fear God.”

But the disordered monk-shepherd is led astray by the goad of avarice; blinded by the darkness of a mind that does not perceive, he casts away fear of the Lord. With Ahab, sold under sin, he runs to the aid of wickedness; stripped of confidence in the promises made by the holy father about obtaining necessities, he occupies himself with outward affairs more than is fitting for a monk. Then, driven on by the stings of avarice, he despises the commandments of God and of holy mother Church; he does not keep feast days, but is wholly immersed in earthly affairs; he thinks and meditates on almost nothing else. Like a fool, with Saul he transgresses the Lord’s command; foolishly, he thinks he can atone with sacrifices made against obedience. Finally, swollen and broadened by worldly goods or worldly cares, he very often slips into every kind of wantonness and dissolution; he detests the observance of regular discipline; and, because he fears being restrained from evil pursuits by the authority of the chapter, he seeks the dissolution of holy union. Then, having become reprobate with Saul, he invokes the counsel of the pythoness; he calls the obedience of reform in his monastery damnable; he desires to be absolved as quickly as possible from submission to the holy confederation; and to this end he tells many things falsely and deceitfully.
