The fruit of obedience is everlasting and is bounded by no end. Almighty God obeys our prayers only so far as we obey our superiors. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, about persistence in prayer, for Christ does not hear a man praying if that man despises Christ's vicar in the monastery. Humility raises prayer up; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the Fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, and the finder of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, closes the gates of hell, becomes the companion of angels, and is held to be the food of all the saints. But the monk who casts off subjection to obedience runs toward death.

The fourth degree of humility is patience in adversities. Our holy father Benedict speaks of it in the Rule: if a monk under obedience, in hard and contrary things, or even in any injuries inflicted on him, embraces patience with a silent conscience. This degree too takes its beginning from the third, because no one is truly to be called obedient if he has been found impatient. For, as that divine Chrysostom says, one who has been subject to the enemy cannot be acceptable to the king; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been enslaved to passionate, impatient flesh. No one will be able to conquer a fierce enemy unless he has learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, because no virtue is rightly perfected if patience abandons it. For, as blessed Gregory testifies, it is of greater merit to receive adversities patiently for God's sake than to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: Whoever believes that tribulation has come upon him for his sins more fully calls forth God's mercy toward himself for doing good. Therefore let the cenobite learn to set the virtue of patience against all things that happen to him, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. He who endures conquers; he who murmurs is overcome.

patience preserves the purpose of humility in a prudent man who loves God; in the removal of possessions and temporal advantages it receives increase; and in bodily injury, with a silent conscience, it preserves the discipline of virtue. Consider now, faithful soul, how your most loving Savior Jesus most patiently endured these three things for your redemption; and if you believe that you love him, do not be reluctant, for love of him, to tolerate similar things with patience.

From the three degrees immediately preceding we establish one summary of the cenobitic end, which, as seems reasonable to us, we call the threefold degree. For by three degrees, namely the second, third, and fourth, the end of cenobitic formation is accomplished, and the renunciation of pleasure is first brought to completion. For when patience is joined to the earlier renunciation of pleasure and to obedience, the preserver of virtues, every pleasure and concern of the body is mortified. Whoever has been worthy to ascend this threefold degree has fulfilled the Lord's command, where he says in the Gospel: Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, through the second degree of humility; and take up his cross, through the third; and follow me, through the fourth.

The fifth degree of humility is the true confession of sins. The faithful cenobite is said to ascend into it if he confesses to his abbot, with humble trust, all evil thoughts that come into his heart, or any evil things committed in secret. Indeed, if anyone sins in the preceding threefold degree through impatience at the adversary's suggestion, in this degree he is restored through contrition and humble confession. For no one is found so perfect that he does not contract some stain from earthly living, as Scripture says: In many things we all offend. For no one is clean from sin, not even an infant whose life upon the earth is one day. Necessarily, therefore, this degree follows immediately after the fourth, and in it all negligences of the preceding degrees are corrected.
