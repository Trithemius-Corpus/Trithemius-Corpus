And first, indeed, he denies himself to himself who strives to live according to the teaching of eternal wisdom, and not according to his own impulses, and to obey Christ in all things as he counsels humility. Therefore let the Savior say to the cenobite: If you wish to deny yourself and follow me, turn away your eyes so that they may not see harmful things, and your ears so that they may not hear vain things; and in place of the sweet things of this world, take up bitter things. Learn to despise the delights of your body, to love tranquility and peace of heart, to receive all tribulations for love of me patiently and gladly, and to desire to be regarded by all as vile and contemptible. Accordingly learn to break your own wills in all things, to mortify the desires of the flesh, to despise all earthly things, and, as far as it can be done, to conform your life with all your strength to the way of life of your Savior. For whoever says that he remains in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. Whoever has done these things has denied himself well.

Second, the denial of one’s own will is rightly accomplished when the advantages of others are preferred to one’s own. For the holy Apostle says: Not seeking the things that are their own, but those that belong to others. For according to Bernard’s judgment, the soul is laid down for the brothers as often as their advantages are preferred, for love of God, to one’s own utilities.

Third, the resignation of one’s own will receives the fullness of perfection when the cenobite, compunct and wholly bound over to God, desires out of love to serve him freely, without regard for reward. And this most holy resignation of one’s own will makes a person very near to God, and always rests in his love.

But just as this resignation of oneself receives its foundation from the second degree of humility, so it necessarily requires in all things a complete inward resignation to the will of God. For the Lord says in the Gospel: I have not come to do my own will, but that of the Father who sent me. Therefore perfect resignation is that by which the human will is wholly subjected to the divine will and is strengthened by complete love.

For the Lord himself taught us to pray in this way: Your will be done, as in heaven and on earth. And there is that saying of the psalmist: Into your hands I commend my spirit. Nowhere in this world is one lived more securely than where the human will is perfectly subjected to God.

Indeed, that perfect resignation of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his will to God, shows without intermediary the straight road to the highest good. Here there will be the highest tranquility of mind; the greatest security is present in the heart, because the soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will be able to fear no adversities of this world any longer, except sin alone. Whatever adversity rushes in upon it, with the Lord’s help it has learned to overcome; whatever advantage it desires, it understands that it possesses in the Lord alone. It despises all adverse things for love of him; it has placed all good things in his love. For that devout man spoke prudently: Dismiss everything, and you will find everything; abandon desire, and you will find true peace.

Finally, so that this resignation of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it implores, as I have said, the assistance of a threefold support.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance, so that the will, once denied, may never be taken back. For that holy Bernard says to those who despise this support: Why do you again involve yourselves with yourselves, when once, by mature deliberation, you handed over your will to us? For the monk who has once handed his will over to God into the hands of the abbot cannot thereafter take it back without the crime of sacrilege.

The second thing required for the perfect resignation of the will is that it never be slackened. Hence in the Apocalypse the Lord says to the lukewarm soul: I have a few things against you, because you have abandoned your first charity, that is, your fervor.

Third, resignation of the will will be perfected if it is never broken off. For the Savior says: Whoever perseveres to the end will be saved. Therefore, when you see a cenobite restless or moved by anger, know that the whole weight of the temptation proceeds from lack of resignation.
