Therefore the concupiscence of the flesh draws back the mind of the disordered cenobite, so that he cannot despise the world.

Second, they are disordered with respect to despising the age because of the vain concupiscence of the eyes. For the monk who forgets the true honor of faithful souls takes an utterly curious delight in worldly pomp and ornaments. And indeed, the outward attire reveals what sort of person each one is hiding inwardly within himself. To carry rings around on the fingers, corals on the prayer-strings, scent-bottles in the hands, and folds in the cowls does not show a monk, but a soldier; not a humble cenobite, but a proud secular man. Such adornment among monks can please none but the reprobate, and is often found to have displeased even the proud. Woe to you, disordered cloisterers, who through a double concupiscence of the eyes descend into hell: while you wish to see in others what is not expedient, and you display yourselves to others to be seen otherwise than is fitting. What have you to do with the jewels of the age, what with the curiosities of the world? According to Bernard’s testimony, two beauties quickly make a masculine soul effeminate; these forensic refinements quickly unnerve the manly resolve. They are like children in rags who, because of a single reddish binding fastened to a torn sleeve, think themselves very beautiful and well adorned, and insolently mock others standing before them who are excellently clothed, as if they were naked. These pompous cloisterers see others walking in humble clothing, despising the world in a spirit of poverty, and on that account pleasing both God and men; but they themselves are despised by the wise and by princes, and sometimes are not even admitted to conversation. Seeing this, the proud, even if they grieve heavily as though at an injury, nevertheless utterly refuse to lay aside their arrogance.

Third, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered with respect to contempt of the age because of pride of life, for they are not only infected by vain complacencies of the flesh, but are also proud through swelling of mind. For all who follow the desires of the flesh vainly lift themselves above themselves; they despise others, however much better; they do not recognize equals as peers; they do not admit superiors. They rejoice that a great retinue of servants attends them; in

councils they strive to sit first; they desire to appear glorious to men; they walk about pompously and publicly. Inflated by a spirit of pride, they boast that their monasteries possess distinction from their foundation: wealthy in temporal goods, curious in jewels, buildings, and privileges. With uplifted heart they despise poor monks or abbots of humble cenobies; they are not ashamed to prefer themselves to their betters. The more they desire to hunt for glory in external things, the more they withdraw from true glory with the foolish virgins.

How the citizens of this lowest region are disordered with respect to penance.

Article viii.

Since man, born of woman, living for a short time and filled with many miseries, cannot remain long in the same state, it is necessary that, by reforming himself daily with all zeal, he turn himself toward his appointed end; for whoever is sent to live long in this world must present himself as changeable in the direction of virtue. Therefore whoever, placed in this mutability, desires to come to the joys of immutability should humbly recognize his frailty, should always flee after a fall into sin to the remedies of penance, and should refuse to lie in wickedness even for the first moment. For the life of monks must always consist savingly in penance, and must continually await the coming of the Judge with holy fear. For Jerome says: “A monk has the office not of one who teaches, but of one who laments, who either mourns for himself or for the world, and fearfully awaits the Lord’s coming.” But the disordered cloisterers of the lowest region despise this saving remedy; they consume the acceptable time in vanity; and because they do not fear to be badly ordered in all things, they also show themselves very disordered in doing penance.

First, indeed, they are badly ordered for penance by negligence of mind, while they neglect to understand how greatly sin displeases almighty God, whom they do not fear to contradict by the judgment of a depraved will. Just as night is opposed to day, so the sinner, turned away from divine goodness, is its adversary and opponent.
