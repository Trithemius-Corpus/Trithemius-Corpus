We are commanded, therefore, first of all to do everything to the praise of God, and in everything that we do to raise our intention principally toward him, the Good himself. For only a good intention distinguishes works in God; only love makes them fruitful. Behold, nature has provided us with skill for praising God; furthermore, he has given the teachings of salvation through the law; he requires from us praise and love above all things. Therefore let us exercise, to the honor of God, the skill given to us, and through the teaching of the law let the eye of our intention become pure.

Fourth, the merciful Father has granted us the world with its necessary elements; for these he requires from us the due sincere contemplation of himself. Whatever we see in this world ought to lead us to the remembrance of God, and continually to stir us to love our Creator above all things. Hence Bernard, practiced in sacred matters, says: The upright and true lover of God, wherever he turns, has a familiar reminder of his Creator; he uses things as a mirror, and so, thinking from creatures, is lifted up to the Creator. For concerning individual things he says: If those things seem good, beautiful, and sweet which, being created, are changed with time and pass away, what goodness, beauty, and sweetness does their author himself have, God remaining eternal, who has reserved incomparably better things for his own? By this consideration the devout person looks upon all people with the simple eye of the mind as bearing a likeness of the image of God, and does not curiously attend to the form of the body in them, whether pleasing or displeasing. In a human being he considers not what he sees outwardly, but the image of the Creator, which he knows to be a companion of eternal happiness.

Fifth, the Son of God offered himself in torments for us, and by his blood redeemed us, lost as we were, from the destruction of perpetual death. We are commanded to offer to this charity of our Savior a devoted imitation, if we desire to come to his kingdom. For the prince of the apostles says to the faithful: Christ suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his footsteps. What shall I render to you, O good Jesus, for your benefits toward me, so great, so vast, so immeasurable?

I shall suffer with you in heart; I shall suffer with you in deed; I shall imitate you with the affections of my mind, according to my ability.

Sixth, the Creator has granted us time for meriting eternal life, and has given the necessary nourishment of the body. Here too we exist as debtors to ourselves, since we owe to ourselves, by right, the acquisition of merit in this time. Why, then, do we spend the days of salvation so negligently and without the fruit of merit, when we possess nothing more precious than time? Behold, in this present brief time we can merit the joys of eternal happiness, and yet we neglect to do all that is good. Woe to us, foolish and mad, who account nothing in this world more precious than time, and yet are accustomed to spend nothing more cheaply. For now the acceptable time is granted to us; now the days of salvation are bestowed, in which we ought to apply ourselves to holy works with the greatest fervor, and always to foresee the hour of death.

Seventh, God has set before us heaven filled with every joy, and has promised himself, with all things contained in him, as a reward. For this munificence of our Savior we ought to apply ourselves to holy works of mercy, and to show ourselves imitators of God according to our strength. The Lord exhorts us to this in the holy Gospels: Be merciful, just as your Father, God, is merciful. At the end of the world also, when the day of judgment has come, by the promise of the Lord himself the merciful will go into eternal life, since whoever has shown mercy will obtain mercy. And lest someone among monks say to me, “I can show mercy to no one, since by my religion I am not permitted to possess anything at all,” let us know that among the necessary works of mercy, the spiritual are more excellent. If you wish to be a merciful monk, pray to God for the wretched; provide saving teaching to the unlearned.

Therefore the sum of meditation rests upon benefaction and three exercises, in which it is principally wholly contained. In the first, indeed, we ought to attribute all things to God, not to ourselves; in the second, by remembering God’s benefits, to exercise ourselves in the pursuit of love; in the third, always to meditate on his precepts and benefits.
