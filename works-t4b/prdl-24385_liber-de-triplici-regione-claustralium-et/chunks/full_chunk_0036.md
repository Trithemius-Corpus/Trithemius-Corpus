On the Due Adaptation of the Powers for Entry into the Third Region.
Article VI.

When, through the degrees of humility, the powers of the soul, especially of the inner person, have been fundamentally cleansed and reformed, it is now said to be very necessary in monks that they be more devoutly adapted for entry into the third region. This diligence in striving toward higher things is necessary for cenobites, as has been said, because where the effort to make progress is laid aside, acquired virtue is wholly dissipated.

No one is permitted to stand still in this second region; rather, we are all commanded to ascend to the third through continual effort. Indeed, this continual motion casts every slothful and lukewarm person back into the first region of the flesh, but it gently and without violence always impels the humble, who are fervent with desire, toward the third. Therefore whoever has deserved to obtain from the Lord, through humility, the reformation of his powers is already near the third region of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it powerfully, he must arrange the powers of his soul most diligently, adapting them to their several operations. The intellect is then said to be rightly adapted for entry into the third region when it is illuminated, according to its capacity, for knowing the highest truth. The memory is arranged for the same end when it is fully absorbed in holding and cherishing the highest truth. The will, moreover, is well adapted for the entry of the spirit if it is disposed to love the highest good, which is God.

But in order that the cenobite may be able to attain the purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, dying to the world, the mind, pacified from every inner and outer disturbance, should rest, so that it may remain buried within the bosom of divine love and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union. This rest of mind is reached by four degrees, which are rooted in a good will in four ways.

The first degree of a good will lays the beginning of virtue; it is called an upright will.

For the soul which, consenting to the law of God, is subjected to God through an upright will is ordered toward the passage into virtue, even if it still seems weak. With the flesh resisting, virtue is perfected in this, and the will is proved upright by the force of the spirit. Yet a will is often called upright and good even when it is found to be less strong. It cannot always accomplish the good that it loves; the evil that it hates, it grieves to have committed through weakness. Through the weakness of the flesh it is very near to the lowest region, but by uprightness of intention and by the effort to make progress it is continually kindled toward the liberty of the spirit. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things. O who could describe this battle between spirit and flesh, where the spirit itself desires to subject the flesh to itself through an upright will, and the weak flesh always resists the spirit through contrary desires?

The second degree is a will strong in uprightness, so that it both bravely flees the evil it hates against the assault of the flesh, and strongly lays hold of the good it loves by doing it, even with labor.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in every kind of riches. Indeed, the will does not long remain strong if it is not devout in the commandments of God.

The fourth degree is a full will, inasmuch as it does not divide its strength at all, but through the renunciation of all things continually stretches itself wholly toward its God. Three things are necessary for this degree, so that the good will in us may be made full.

The first is complete renunciation of oneself, since the will is shown still to be weak if it is not mortified through total self-renunciation.
