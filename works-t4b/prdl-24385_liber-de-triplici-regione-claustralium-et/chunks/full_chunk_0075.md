Prayer.

I beseech you, most sweet Lord Jesus, by the power of your most holy body and blood which I have received, though unworthy: grant me the sweetness of your love, sincerity of brotherly affection, strength in temptation, purity in heart, and discipline in conduct, so that, by your generosity, I may satisfy my profession and continually advance in your most holy love. At the first signal before choir, before high mass.

When the first signal has sounded for the hour that customarily precedes high mass, the cenobite, immediately setting aside everything he has in his hands, should read the preparatory prayers for choir, just as they are arranged above before matins. Afterward let him go to choir with reverence and maturity, bowing and praying according to the rite set out above.

At high mass.

At the ringing for high mass you may read the appointed Hour of the Holy Cross, and afterward, as time permits, recall to mind something from the preface set before the private masses. For the sum of our salvation is placed in the merit of the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; nor is any good work rightly credited to us unless it is offered in his power. The memory of this Passion is enacted in the celebration of masses, where the innocent Lamb, slain for us, is offered to God the Father in an ineffable mystery. Therefore we truly hope for salvation from our one Savior, our Lord Jesus Christ alone, whom we believe to have suffered for us. And so, whatever the Church asks in the collects, she humbly asks God the Father that it be given to her through him. Therefore let our devout cenobite have this regard for the merit of the Lord’s Passion in his heart; upon it the whole office of the mass is chiefly founded, and to it let him return gently through faith. For every mass, of whatever kind it may be, that is said in God’s holy Church is always referred to the merit of the Lord’s Passion.

On the introit of the mass.

We do not intend to explain the office of the mass point by point, since each of us can find many expositions by others; rather, we desire to give occasion for meditation to devout brothers, whom we know to be inflamed with inward desires. You may relate the first part of the introit and the verse, in meditation, to the Lord’s incarnation; and the repetition with the Gloria to his nativity. Nevertheless, you ought diligently to examine what the introit contains according to its literal sense, so that you may be able to find a fitting spiritual understanding for yourself. For nothing is done in vain in the divine office, but all things are filled with mysteries and sacred actions.

On Kyrieleison.

Kyrieleison is Greek, composed of a noun and a verb; and among them these are two words. The first, Kyrie, is said in the vocative from kyrios, which means “Lord.” The second is from eleeo, which means “I have mercy”; eleeson, “have mercy,” is uttered in the imperative. From this comes kyrieleison, that is, “Lord, have mercy,” by elision from Kyrie eleison, since a vowel before a vowel can be elided for a better sound.

Three times he says Kyrieleison to the Father, so that he who, from exceeding charity, willed his Son to become incarnate of the Virgin and to die for us, may wish to come to our aid in our necessities by his mercy.

To the Son also we say Christeleison three times, in which we pray to him that he may come to our aid.

We also utter Kyrieleison three times to the Holy Spirit, in which we humbly invoke him for our help by his ineffable goodness.
