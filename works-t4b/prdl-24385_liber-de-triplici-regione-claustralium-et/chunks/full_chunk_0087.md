Therefore whoever despises the vicar of any king undoubtedly contemns the king himself. But tell me, good brother: why do you withhold due reverence from your prelate, whom you hear called the vicar of Christ? Perhaps you think him evil and wholly unworthy of such an office, and therefore you suppose that it is permitted you to despise him? Not so, brother, not so. Rather, consider whose place it is in which the vicar of Christ presides, and know that you have not been sent to honor the man. If you wish to be Christ’s disciple, always consider why you came, and you will never deny reverence to the prelate, even if he is unworthy. For why did you come? Surely so that, according to the rule of the Gospel, you may deny yourself for God; so that under another’s command you may serve Christ as a soldier; so that with patience you may await the eternal reward promised to you. Therefore, brother, do not lose the crown of eternal happiness prepared for you in heaven because of one man’s displeasure, a crown you ought not exchange for the whole world.

Remember that you have nothing except from God, to whom you owe all that you are and all that you know, so that you may fulfill his will and live according to his ordinance. But since you do not trust that you can understand the will of God, you must humbly obey your ruler as his vicar, to whom you have submitted yourself; nor must you examine either his life or his command, since it is not permitted you to judge your pastor. Indeed, in your profession you swore that you ought not follow your own will, but that you wished to obey the abbot in all things according to the Rule. Therefore the abbot is lord of your whole will, not by his own assumption, but by the ordinance of God, whose vicar he is. But handling another’s property against the owner’s will is theft, and you know that thieves will have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God. Thus the monk, having made profession according to the Rule to God and to his abbot, no longer has power over his own will. Whoever afterward usurps his own will for himself commits theft and sacrilege. Nor will an obtained permission save the presumptuous man, if it was obtained through entreaties and insults. Your abbot, brother, is the vicar of God; to him you must render the promised obedience, not as to a good man, but as to God,

and show humble reverence to him in the place of God. For whatever is bestowed by subjects upon the ruler is undoubtedly referred to God, whose vicar he is. Since, therefore, you have subjected yourself to your prelate for God alone and for no other reason, take care to present yourself toward him at all times in such a way that he may be able freely to command you to do or leave undone whatever seems expedient to him, and that he should fear no insolence or disturbance of your spirit at all. For if your abbot is compelled to fear you, his monk, the order of equity is violated; and where the disciple strives to be master, he sins against the Gospel.

Beware, then, lest you withhold reverence and honor from prelates; beware lest by saddening them you offend God. Rather, take care to meet them reverently, whatever sort they may be, and to obey their commands in all things according to the Gospel and the Rule. Believe me, brother, that the fault committed by a subject against a prelate is punished most gravely, either in the present life or in the life to come. For it is written: “Do not touch my christs.” Whatever your prelate has commanded you, you must receive with such confidence that you believe God has inspired it in him.

Let the monk’s conduct be charitable toward the brothers.
