First, because of the delicate liveliness of his flesh, which was excellent in its natural constitution, and therefore was more gravely afflicted under the pressure of pain. For delicate flesh suffers more than flesh that is coarse by its natural condition. Since, then, Christ’s flesh was subtle and delicate, in his sweat in the garden he poured forth blood.

Second, because of the character of those who killed him: Christ was not killed by strangers to his own people, but by his own kinsmen and nearest relations, as he had earlier complained through the prophet: “My people, what have I done to you?” For what one suffers from strangers and enemies is more tolerable than what one suffers from one’s own people, from whom at least friends are expected to suffer with one by reason of nearness of blood.

Third, because of the sharpness of the Passion, since he is afflicted especially in those places in which pain is felt more intensely because of the nerves. Raised upon the cross, he is fastened through hands and feet, and the Lord of all is given gall and vinegar to drink. For the psalmist says of him: “They have pierced my hands and my feet; they have numbered all my bones.” O good Jesus, who could conceive in his mind the pain of your most holy heart, when the most cruel nails began so cruelly to pierce your hands and feet? Would that my soul might always show itself pleasing to you, so that the remembrance of your most holy Passion may never depart from my memory. I shall easily be able to obtain from you the effect of my prayers, if you will make me delight without interruption in the exercise of your Passion. Let each person do what he can: I prefer the memory of your Passion to all exercises, through which I trust that in my prayers I shall receive whatever accords with salvation.

Fourth, because of the long duration, Christ’s Passion was most bitter, since from the hour of his captivity until death he was always in most bitter torment. “All day long,” he says, “I went about sorrowful, and there is no health in my flesh.”

Fifth, because of the universality of the Passion, since in whatever ways Christ could suffer, he suffered in a singular way. For he suffers in reputation, since, falsely accused, he is blasphemed by the impious. He suffers also in his possessions, since he is suspended naked and stripped upon the wood:

his garments are divided by lot among the soldiers. He suffers in his soul, because, with the bitterness of the Passion before him, he was sorrowful even unto death. He suffered in his whole body, because from the sole of the foot to the crown of the head there was no soundness in it. He suffers in his friends, because he is betrayed by a disciple, deserted by all, and even publicly denied by Peter. He is afflicted in the compassion of his mother, whose most holy soul is remembered to have been pierced through by the sword of sorrow. He suffered much from ingratitude, because by the death to which, in suffering with all, he submitted himself, he foresaw that what had been lost would remain lost in many reprobates. Finally, he suffered in all the senses, as we are taught from the Gospel. He suffers in the eyes, which, with many tears poured out, are veiled with a cloth. He suffers in hearing, when, mocked with many insults and reproaches, he is blasphemed. He suffers in smell, because he is defiled by the foul spittle of the Jews. He suffers in touch, because he is tormented in all the members of his body. He suffers in taste, because, thirsting, he is given gall and vinegar to drink.

Sixth, because of the causality of the Passion, which was solely the envy of the Jews, since he suffered wholly without fault. For truly, whoever suffers unjustly is tormented still more in his mind. But the innocent Son of God is bruised for our sins and tormented for our crimes, as is read in Isaiah: “Truly he himself bore our sufferings.”

Seventh, because of the lack of every consolation, since Christ found no one to console him in his Passion. For he says in the psalm: “You have put far from me friend and neighbor, and my acquaintances because of misery.” Indeed, his acquaintances stood afar off.

Fourth, Christ’s Passion, with respect to its effect, was most fruitful, and it is to be considered chiefly in three things. First, there was the remission of sins; second, the bestowal of grace; third, the manifestation of glory. And these three things are indicated by the title fixed to the cross, which, as the evangelist says, contained the cause of Jesus’ Passion: “Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews.” For Jesus, saving sinners, refers to the first; Nazarene, that is, conferring grace, refers to the second; King of the Jews, granting the heavenly kingdom to those who confess him, refers to the third.
