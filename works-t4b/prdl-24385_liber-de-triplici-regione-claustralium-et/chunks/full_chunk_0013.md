It is a very sure sign of weakness to examine the deeds of others and, except by reason of one’s office, to busy oneself with others’ faults. For the monk who, according to the well-known definition of his name, is instructed to care for himself alone, does not scrutinize the deeds of others without manifest danger to his own salvation. Those who subject themselves to the vice of curiosity descend to the second degree in the manners of pride. For one sin disposes a man to another, and no single sin reigns alone in proud monks.

The second degree of pride, therefore, is levity of mind and the unstable occupation of a wandering heart. Into this degree of disordered conduct carnal monks fall through the various affections that they have sensually contracted from the vice of curiosity. But the true cenobite cannot remain steadfast in his holy purpose, because, when levity of mind is admitted, the constancy of virtue is scattered in the heart. It is the judgment of a disordered mind to run about among various images of things and always to beget new forms of sensual affections.

The third degree of pride is called empty joy, which is born in a disordered man from levity of mind. Carnal monks compose for themselves various images of affections; when these occur agreeably to sensuality they rejoice, but when it turns out otherwise, they fall into various temptations, conceiving grave indignation. But that is not true joy which takes its beginning from anything other than God, nor can any joy be useful that does not have its source in the love of God. And therefore disordered men, through vain joy, fall into unfit boasting, whereby they mendaciously ascribe great things to themselves.

The fourth degree of pride, as we have said, arises from empty joy and is called foolish boasting. Carnal monks fall into this when they exalt themselves, magnifying themselves before others. If they have done anything good, they boldly bring it forward in public, often mendaciously devising even things they have not done. They desire to seem holy, learned, prudent, and noble; and they invent many things through craftiness of wit, so that outwardly they may appear glorious to men, though inwardly they know they are empty of the merit of virtue. at once they rise up in opposition; they say many vicious things against him, because, while they wish to show themselves alone worthy of praise, they do not allow anyone else to be praised before them.

The fifth degree of pride is understood as singularity, which is generated from the swelling of boasting. Proud cloistered men fall into this degree when, through boasting, they have presumed that they surpass others either in merit or in knowledge. Hence they do not appear similar to the rest in their manner of life, but from pride alone show themselves singular in all things. But this singularity is reprehensible in the monastery, where all are commanded to live in common according to the Rule. For proud monks, as the divine Bernard testifies, while they prefer themselves to others in virtues and wisdom, fall into such rash arrogance that they flatter themselves more over one fast while the rest are eating than if they had fasted seven days more than the others; one little private prayer seems to them far more profitable than the whole psalmody of one night. During meals they often cast their eyes around the table, and if they see someone eating less, they groan; they begin to grieve within themselves that they have been surpassed, cruelly taking away from themselves what they had provided for necessary sustenance. Proud cloistered men are energetic for their singular practices, but sluggish for common ones. They lie awake in bed; in choir and at the regular reading they nod off.

The sixth degree of pride takes its beginning from the fifth and is called the arrogance of the proud man. And indeed, when disordered cloistered men, through the pursuit of singularity, begin outwardly to appear better than others, they burn into a greater madness of arrogance; and whatever is said in their praise by men, these fools think true. O wretched folly of the proud man: although in all other matters they trust no one else more than themselves, about themselves alone they trust themselves less than another. Whatever they hear said in praise of themselves by the unskilled, they ascribe not to the ignorance or goodwill of the one praising them, but to their own merits. But whoever is delightfully fed on human praises is held before God as empty of every virtue and full of arrogance.
