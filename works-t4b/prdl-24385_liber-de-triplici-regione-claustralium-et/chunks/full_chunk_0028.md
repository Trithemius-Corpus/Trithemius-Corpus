The study of sacred reading must not be undertaken for the vanity of the world, nor for the pleasure that seduces, because reading never advances toward compunction when delight in the flesh or in vanity corrupts it. A monk cannot attain devotion through the study of sacred reading unless he strives to change all curiosity into a simple spirit. Therefore whoever desires to advance from the reading of Scripture to compunction, whoever chooses to ascend to humility through the roots of fear, must not choose just any material for reading, but only that which can most greatly stir the mind to the love of God. For this purpose the reading of the conduct of the saints seems to me sufficiently suitable, because the mind of one who lies low is often raised up to the love of God when the life of his servants is recalled to memory. We also gladly admit the miracles and revelations of the torments or joys of separated souls, in conformity with the Catholic faith, by which we have found that sluggish hearts are often stirred to compunction. Finally, the passions of the holy martyrs stir up in no small measure the fervor of devotion; in them, when we bring back to mind the unconquerable love of Christ, we are pierced by the example of such constancy, and, as though touched by a certain fire of love, we sigh over our imperfection.

The material of meditation is taken from reading, and as the former is, so the latter will be. The shameful songs of poets, the curious teachings of philosophers, and the swollen writings of rhetoricians do not stir up in monks the fervor of devout compunction, but rather choke what has been conceived. Therefore, if you wish to persist in good meditations, do not occupy your mind with readings other than holy and devout ones. Indeed, grapes are not gathered from thorns, nor is compunction born from the study of vanity. Let the person who studies for knowledge of this world read those things that suit him for learning; but let the one who seeks compunction gather those things

that kindle the soul to the love of God through fear.

Therefore let frequent reading take its beginning from the last things that await us; then let the deeds of the holy fathers be read, so that the memory of future things may shake the mind into the fear of God, and the exemplary conduct of the saints may kindle the desire to imitate them. For the wise man says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” The conduct of the holy fathers is set before us as an example, and the destruction of the wicked is recounted to us for our instruction, so that virtue, rewarded, may move our desire, and wickedness, punished, may forbid license to sin. Therefore let the devout cenobite recall to memory the examples and virtues of the saints; let him consider not the brief pleasure of the wicked, but their eternal torment, so that by fearing unhappiness he may deserve to escape it, and by devoting himself to virtues obtain that everlasting joy.

The distinct condition of the good and the wicked is no light matter, since in each case it is described as truly enduring, and is ended by no limit. O eternity, always to be feared by all mortals: after this life has been completed, in it there is no fear for the righteous, and no hope of deliverance for the wicked. Let anyone who can think what eternity is, where there is no end, imagine a limit if he dares; and when he finds nothing in eternity except eternity, let him understand that all the time in which he lives in this world is nothing. Indeed, once the spirit has been cast out from the flesh, it is necessarily compelled to enter this abyss of eternity; and the condition which it has once obtained according to its merits it will bear without end, nor will it be changed into another. For the person who dies good in the charity of God will receive eternal life according to his merits. But the person who is evil and has once departed from this life without the reconciliation of repentance will remain with the demons in hell, to be tormented without end. From these meditations servile fear arises in the mind, and through it one gradually comes to love.

So, in order that we may be able to lead the cenobite successively through these roots to charity, it seems good to us to explain briefly, in order, the degrees of humility described in the Rule.
