Before the venerable sacrament.

Hail, living host, truth and life, in whom the sacrifices of old are ended. Through you infinite glory is given to the Father. Through you the Church stands continually fortified. Grant me rewards in eternal life. Amen. Or: O saving host.

Praying in his own place in choir.

When he has come to his customary place of standing in choir, if there is time, let him consider what our holy father Benedict said in the Rule about reverence in prayer and psalmody. Then, considering the subject matter of the psalms to be sung, let him strive to submit his intention to them, and carefully foresee to what he wishes to refer each part. If the matins office is being sung for some saint, let the psalmody be said before the Lord in that saint’s memory. But if the subject matter of the psalms agrees with the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, it should be referred to his memory. Yet if the spirit of compunction is present in the singer, it will teach all things through love. For a mind fervent in the love of God knows very well how to direct its intention to the praise of the Creator, and how suddenly to vary it, according to the spirit of the psalmody, in individual verses. For according to the subject matter of what is sung, it exercises itself in whatever it wishes: now it embraces the humanity of Christ, and now, suddenly, with another verse, it is raised into his divinity. Nevertheless, if it pleases him, our faithful monk can, while his fervor lasts under the same sign, call to mind the holy angels praising God without interruption, and conform himself to them in desire, according to his strength.

Before all the hours.

Since the power of the Lord’s incarnation and Passion incomparably surpasses the merits of all the saints, therefore let the lover of God, before each of the hours, say to God the Father, with attentive mind, either in heart or by mouth, this prayer.

Since you, almighty everlasting God, created us out of nothing when we did not exist, and mercifully redeemed us when we had perished, I ask you, by your immutable majesty: receive these praises and prayers which I am now and always about to say to you, in that union of charity and love in which you sent your only-begotten Son into the womb of the most blessed Virgin Mary, and in which you received him as he suffered and died for us on the cross. Amen.

Note on the preceding prayer.

Do not think this manner of praying frivolous, nor invented by human curiosity; for if you believe that God has been accustomed to teach his ancient servants many things by revelation, you will in no way contradict this most holy form. Rather, read the wondrous works of the Lord which he has shown to those who love him, and you will find what he revealed to Mechthild and to others concerning this manner and form of prayer. Know this as thoroughly persuaded: that the prayer is most pleasing to almighty God which a devout mind has resolved to offer to God the Father in union with the Lord’s Passion. And this offering can be sent forth, in mind or by mouth, not only in the canonical hours, but also in other prayers, masses, and any good exercises or works. But if through forgetfulness it has not been sent forth beforehand, it can be inserted during prayer, or even added after its completion.

How a monk should conduct himself in the hours.

From the beginning of divine praise, the devout monk should apply great diligence, so that his mind does not admit images or thoughts about transitory things; for unless vain thoughts are driven back without delay at the beginning, they will disturb the intention of the heart throughout the whole office. Therefore the faithful worshiper should gather himself wholly into himself, and prefer nothing to the divine service against the rule.

While psalming, let him refer the very subject matter of the psalms to the corresponding focus of his intention; let him prudently attend to the sense of the prayer; let him pronounce the words distinctly and in order; let his mind tend upward.
