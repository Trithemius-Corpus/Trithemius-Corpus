Since in many preceding places of this little work I have briefly set out the way of ascending to this supreme region of the spirit, nevertheless it will not, I think, be useless to repeat blessed things rather often, so that we may be able to hold them more firmly. For it seems to me of little profit to describe the beauty of contemplation unless we also know how to show those who desire it the way of coming to it.

Therefore, one who wishes to ascend by the desire of love to the pleasantness of this supreme region should take care to exercise himself diligently in the labors of the middle region; for unless he has there been purified from sensual passions by the practice of holy compunction, he approaches this region not without danger. For the completion of this exercise, let him often recall to memory the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; let him weep from delight in divine love; let him learn, with attention, frequently to run through in meditation the text of that passion, and to stir himself to compassion for the Savior.

And because the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, so that the mind may be able to raise itself powerfully in this meditation on the Lord's passion, let it take care, if need be, to present to its thoughts images that are truly perceptible to the senses. Therefore, withdrawing from crowds into silence, let him begin to turn over in memory the order of the passion. Let him see himself with the eyes of the mind as present at each event with the suffering Lord; let him hear Jesus speaking in imagination; let him go with him as he goes through all the places of the passion.

Then, at each article, let him bring his affections from a desire for compassion. Let him gaze with faithful eyes on Jesus suffering, set before him; weeping through anguish of heart, let him grow accustomed to address him in spirit as though bodily present, and through the desire of love to embrace him wounded at the column and on the cross. For the mind will quickly be able to be kindled in the love of the Lord Jesus if, by meditation, it truly accustoms itself to tears of compassion for him. But I remember that I have written elsewhere at greater length about the most effective exercise of the Lord's passion; therefore I have touched on it briefly here. For it is enough to have given beginners the first principles for meditating on it, since those who know have higher things.

I. Therefore, once affection has been purified from every passion and obstructing desire, the mind, with the help of the intellect, begins to think somewhat on the goodness and beauty of the highest good. Then it desires to grasp the immutable good; but when, as the intellect lifts it up, it raises itself by wonder toward knowledge of that good,

by divine working the reasoning of the intellect falls away; nor can reason follow affection any further, since it sees that affection can be raised above all things only alone and utterly naked. But so long as the mind has not been lawfully purified, affection does not reach the true darkness of this mountain.

Contemplation sometimes proceeds by an enlargement of the mind: when the soul, by its own effort, stretches itself more forcefully in holy meditation, meditation is changed into contemplation. And this mode of contemplation is accustomed to rise by three degrees: by art, through which we learn what is to be done by us; by exercise, through which we carry out what has been learned; and by attention, through which we fulfill the duty.

II. Sometimes contemplation, by the lifting up of the mind, raises it into God; for when the mind has been divinely irradiated beyond human effort, it is drawn, as though seized above itself, into the contemplation of divine things. This mode too raises one into the height of contemplation by three degrees. For sometimes understanding, divinely inspired, receives something of the good that wholly exceeds the measure of foreknowledge. The second degree raises the mind above effort, when, divinely illumined, it perceives something to which no human effort reaches. In the third degree the mind is raised on high, when, beyond the limits of human nature and effort, it is divinely illumined in contemplation.

At times also contemplation directs itself, by alienation of the mind, toward the mountain of divine darkness, when the memory of present things has been lulled to sleep and the soul, by illumination from divine operation, passes into a state unknown to human knowledge. And this mode belongs especially to this supreme region of the spirit, in which the mind of a true lover of God is seen to be kindled with so great a fervor of love that it is often proved to be alienated from itself. For sometimes, by the greatness of devotion, it is rapt outside itself; sometimes, through excessive wonder, it is lifted above itself; at times, because of immeasurable exultation, it abandons itself; and by the ray of love it passes into the supreme elevation of contemplation. But no mortal understands the pleasantness of this highest and supreme region unless he has ascended to it purified by the experience of love.

Here ends the third region of cloistered persons.
