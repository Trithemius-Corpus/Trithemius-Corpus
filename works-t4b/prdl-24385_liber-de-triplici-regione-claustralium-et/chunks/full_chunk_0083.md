Whoever has uttered these three truths to the Lord from sincerity of heart, without falsehood in mouth or mind, however much he may previously have sinned, is now secure, if he should die in such a state even without confession, in the absence of a priest; yet in such a way that in purgatory he must make satisfaction for what he did not complete by penitence. But whoever cannot truthfully say those same truths from a pure mind, because the pleasure of sinning, in which he is actually delighting, stands in the way, or because of an evil purpose by which he scorns to avoid the occasions of sins, is in a state of damnation, from which not even the pope can absolve him.

The Indication of Future Caution.

The second thing he prescribes as to be observed by the cenobite in his recollection he calls the indication of future caution. Let him consider what have been for him the occasions of past transgression, and he will more easily guard himself from future danger. For he blocks up the pit of sin better if he avoids the occasion of sinning. And Saint Bernard says: “When night is approaching, an account must indeed be made, distributing one’s exercises according to the rule of the common institution.” In these he duly pays every debt, if the spirit subjects the flesh to God and to the spirit, and if whatever has been omitted, neglected, or imperfect does not remain uncompensated at its proper time and place.

The Request for Suffrages.

The third thing in the recollection of a good cenobite is the devout request for suffrages, since one who understands his own weakness very well asks help from the stronger. Therefore, when he has completed his recollection, he turns to the holy and undivided Trinity to seek mercy; then he invokes the most blessed Mother of God; afterward he implores the prayers and help of all the saints. And here he remembers not only himself, but pours out prayers for the whole order and the Church.

The Blessing for Rest.

The fourth thing in the recollection is the cenobite’s blessing for going to rest, by which he fortifies himself with prayers and commits himself to the divine goodness to be guarded through the night. Therefore,

at the sound of the signal for sleep, or at the appointed time, going to the place of rest, let him fortify himself with the sign of the holy cross and say with devotion:

Blessing for Rest

May the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, the merit and integrity of blessed Mary ever virgin, the sign of the holy cross, the power of the Lord’s passion, and the blessing, guardianship, and merit of all the holy angels and elect of God be between me and all my enemies, visible and invisible, here and forever. Amen.

Prayer

I commend myself this night and at every time into your wounds, most loving Lord Jesus, and I ask you, through the merit of your most holy passion, to have mercy on me and guard me from all the snares and illusions of the enemy, so that with pure mind and clean body I may be able to rise at the appointed hour and praise you with fervor of spirit.

Discretion for Having Rest.

The fifth and last thing to be remembered in recollection is discretion for having rest: that just as the cenobite should never wholly omit this evening recollection, so too he should not wish to prolong it too indiscreetly, to the destruction of his own head. Therefore, when all things have been completed with discretion, let him, fervent in desire for God, raise his mind with tears if he can; let him not pour it out excessively. But when he goes up to his bed, let him carry with him in memory something concerning the Lord’s passion, or concerning the love of Jesus, or surely concerning the glory of the heavenly kingdom, in which, as he sleeps, he may rest sweetly.

For Bernard says: “When you are going to sleep, carry with you something in memory or in thought, in which you may gently fall asleep, which may sometimes also help you to dream, which may also receive you when you wake and restore you to the state of yesterday’s intention. Thus night will be illuminated for you like day, and your night will be your illumination in your delights, so that you may fall asleep calmly, rest in peace, and easily wake; and, when you rise, you will be ready to return to that from which you had not wholly departed.” Yet in all these things discretion must be applied, lest either excessive meditation, impeding sleep, destroy the brain, or sleep taken immoderately interrupt meditation altogether.
