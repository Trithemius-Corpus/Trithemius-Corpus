On the Chapter of Faults.

Since nothing in human actions is altogether perfect, let the devout cenobite rejoice that he has found a remedy for his imperfection, because whatever anyone in a well-reformed monastery commits from human frailty, he corrects daily. For the chapter of faults, continued on individual days, washes away public offenses; sacramental confession, repeated daily through the grace of contrition, remits hidden negligences. We discern four chapters in the monastery, about which we shall speak in order.

The first is the passive chapter, when a brother proclaims himself or is proclaimed by another.

The first is called the passive chapter, in which our devout cenobite proclaims himself for his negligences, or is proclaimed by another. In this chapter the lover of God, who has denied his very self to himself, humbly both accuses his own negligences and permits himself to be accused by others, lest after this life he be judged harshly by the Lord. For the holy apostle of God says: If we judged ourselves, we would certainly not be judged. And the prophet Nahum says: Double affliction shall not rise up. Therefore, he who here, out of love for God, now willingly afflicts himself for his sins is worthy, as one well purified by this medicinal foretaste, to be freed from future affliction. If all cloistered persons knew how much the chapter proclamations, when willingly endured by them, would profit toward the merit of eternal life, they would endure all accusations and injuries for the love of God with inestimable joy. For we merit more when we patiently endure injuries and adversities for the love of God and fraternal charity than if we devote ourselves to good works.

Therefore, in judgment the devout monk should not fear any proclamations, but should desire to be accused by others in remembrance of the Lord’s passion, knowing that the glory of loftiness has been set before him, and is to be bestowed in heaven upon humble patience. O brother, why would you not endure the proclamations of brothers, you who expect the fellowship

of angels? Let all indignation be far from you; let murmuring be far away; let pride withdraw. For you have no cause for impatience. Either you are proclaimed justly or unjustly. If justly, why would you not endure it? It is better to be cleansed here in small matters than after this life in many. And one who sins publicly must be reproved before all. But if you are proclaimed unjustly, remember that you are a monk, and you will find that you are bound to injuries. When you are proclaimed justly, know that you are to be cleansed by humble patience; when unjustly, you show yourself to be a true monk, and you merit more. Therefore, when you are proclaimed, call to mind the fourth and sixth degrees of humility; and if you are a true monk, pray even for those who accuse you. For the true lover of God loves the one proclaiming him all the more, and always, on the same day, either commends him in prayer or performs some special service for him. If you are good and wise, the proclamation will not be able to displease you; but if you are evil and foolish, you are worthy to be proclaimed at all times. For Scripture says of the wise man: Reprove the wise man, and he will love you. But of the evil and foolish the prophet says: They hated the one who reproves in the gate, and they abominated the one who speaks perfectly.

The second chapter is the active one, in which one proclaims another.

We call the second chapter active, in which we no longer proclaim ourselves, but others. For the action of this proclamation, the highest charity is necessary; compassion also no less, and discretion, must be applied. Therefore, when you wish to proclaim your brother by reason of charity, always apply the fear of God; and, mindful of the fellowship of eternal life, say at the beginning: Our beloved brother has committed this or that; I ask that for the Lord’s sake he may wish to amend himself. Whatever must be said, let him present it in few words and with compassion; let him state the deed simply; let him not increase the fault of the one proclaimed by aggravating it; let him more quickly entrust to the president the judgment whether what he did is good or evil. For the one among many who alone aggravates a brother’s fault by passing judgment shows a bitterness of zeal and lack of discretion.
