In a third way a good person becomes spiritually good when, through the desire of love, the spirit is united with God by fixed degrees. These last two things make our cenobite spiritual, and often bring him among the citizens of the third region. Therefore whoever desires to have good confidence from the testimony of his own conscience should humbly strive to exercise himself in the things stated above, because no one prudently presumes upon security when danger is not avoided. And what is so dangerous as always to live in negligence and sins, and to refuse to amend committed errors? The confidence of the just, arising from the testimony of a good conscience, is always excellent, and continually subject to God in humble service. But the impious and sinners, disturbed by savage actions, raise themselves against God, and do not trust in the testimony of conscience, but rather presume vainly.

Here follow the seven roots of divine fear, which are laid fundamentally in the first degree of humility.

Since this second region principally instructs the cenobite, in due order, through the preceding degrees of humility and fear of God, it is necessary for him to know clearly the roots of that fear, if he wishes to reach the love of God by ascending. Therefore, so that we may build this spiritual house to completion, we must place those roots in order in the foundation. We did not set them out in the first degree of humility, lest we confuse the order of what was being said by excessive length. There are, then, seven roots of the fear of God, through which the cenobite is brought to compunction and, by advancing in true humility, is more strongly rooted.

The first root of the fear and love of God is the intent meditation on the incomprehensible divine majesty, which is understood the less the more inquisitively it is investigated by a person. For the prophet says: Truly you are a hidden God. This meditation on divine perfection generates admiration; admiration causes reverence; from reverence arises fear of majesty, and from fear worship is produced. Meditation on the highest goodness, however, divides our purpose into seven traditions, which are not unsuitably fitted to the seven days of the week, or even to the seven canonical hours. And although that highest and most perfect goodness sufficiently provides us with abundant matter for meditation, nevertheless, because it belongs to few to be able to ascend to invisible things without visible ones, we wish to raise the minds of the weak through known things to unknown things. For the holy Apostle says: The invisible things of God are seen, understood through those things that have been made from the creation of the world, his eternal power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.

First, therefore, the marvelous power of God is praiseworthy in the principal work of creation: since he was able to produce all things from nothing, he created things so great, so solid, so varied, and so noble by his will alone. From this consideration admiration of divine power arises, through which a person is moved to the fear of God.

Second, the provident wisdom of God is praiseworthy, by which, knowing all things, he orders the universe and governs by his providence all that he created. He knows all things, understands all things, disposes all things by his counsel, and encloses all things in his empire. As a certain lover of worldly wisdom says of it: God is a sphere whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. He is within all things and is not enclosed; outside all things and is not excluded; below all things and is not weighed down; above all things and is not borne up. He includes all things, because, wherever he is, he is whole, yet he is enclosed in no place; great and immense, he is bounded by no quantity. He orders all things as he wills; stable in himself, always and unmoved, he changes the vicissitudes of things. Good in eternal things, through mercy he mercifully prepares rewards without passion of mind; angry without disturbance, he condemns the unjust. Great without magnitude, immense without quantity, he has set an end to things; governing the world, he disposes all things.

Third, the merciful clemency of God is praiseworthy in our redemption.
