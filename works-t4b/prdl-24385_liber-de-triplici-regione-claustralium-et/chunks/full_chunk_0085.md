The devout and faithful cenobite who truly loves God does not spare himself in labor, but is accustomed to apply himself to the manual tasks imposed on him with such fervor, cheerfulness, and fidelity, as though he alone were compelled by his labor to feed all those established in the monastery. For the most prudent soul knows that it devotes itself wholly to God in whatever it happens to labor at for his servants in bodily necessity. For just as spiritual things cannot long endure in religion without cooperation in temporal things, so I do not hesitate to call the man who, for God’s sake, supplies the needs of the body to those serving God a servant of God. Therefore whatever a good cenobite can acquire for the monastery by his labors with right intention, let him know that he offers all this to the Lord. Nevertheless, a monk should always understand that he must labor in such a way that he in no way omits the monastic prayers, masses, and the other services of holy mother Church.

But manual work taken up out of curiosity and from a desire for self-pleasing is deservedly deprived of reward before the eternal Judge; and work undertaken as if idly, and without the superior’s permission, is held liable to account. But the good cenobite, who desires to please God alone, in every stealing-in of an idle deed, word, or thought, should say within himself that saying of Bernard: If you were about to die now, would you do this in the way you are now acting, thinking, or speaking? Since, if you were now about to die, you would not do what you are doing now, you are very foolish, you who so boldly allow yourself works of death when you will shortly die. But if, since death is necessary, you desire to die well, never do at any time what you would not do if death were already present. For whatever you do now, whether good or evil, you will find it after death.

Let the monk be humble and devout inwardly.

Secondly, during the time of labor the monk ought inwardly to be devout and humble, because if he abandons the inward progress of fervor, nothing helps him that he acquires outwardly through labor.

Therefore, so that the soul may truly make progress while laboring, let it continually recall to memory the steps of good will written in the first region. Let it arrange meditations on the Lord’s Passion with fervor of devotion, in which, as far as possible, it may conform its labors to the labor of Christ. Let it always be humble in heart through denial of self. Let it be devout in affection through the fervor of holy union, so that the labor of the hand may outwardly advance the perfection and usefulness of the community, while the mind does not withdraw from the eternal sweetness of divine love. If labor wearies the working cenobite, if heat burns him, if untimely thirst oppresses him, first let him call to mind how many labors the Lord Jesus endured for us, and he will bear all things lightly. Praying thus, let him say to the Lord:

A prayer for one laboring.

Most sweet and most loving Lord Jesus, King of heaven and earth, who for my redemption were born of the most pure Virgin Mary, and for thirty-three years lived among men as God and man: how great were the labors you had in walking, teaching, and preaching; how for me you voluntarily and patiently endured hunger, thirst, heat, and cold. For all these things I give you thanks. Receive, I beseech you, these little labors of mine, though useless, which I humbly offer to your most holy majesty in memory and honor of all your labors and of your most holy Passion; and may they please you in that union in which your labors most holy pleased God the Father. Amen.

The cenobite should also turn over in his mind, while at labor, if time permits, the principal articles of the Lord’s Passion: first by commemorating them as though present with Christ in the Passion; then by giving thanks at his knees; third, by asking mercy in them according to their likeness.

Let him also recall to memory the lives of the holy fathers or the passions of the martyrs, and consider how great a reward and glory they now receive in their homeland for their labors.

Let the monk be sparing and edifying in speech.

The third thing that must be observed by a monk in labor is that he speak little, and only what is edifying.
