Whoever omits the exercise of daily instruction falls into a depth of torpor, as the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “If you do not hold yourself steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will soon be overthrown,” by which he means a good conscience. For the provision of this exercise, so useful and so necessary, you see that the Rule of our most holy father Benedict is sufficient for a monk, since it is known to contain all the material of exercises, both spiritual and bodily, necessary for monks in guarding regular discipline. And therefore all the exercises of monks must be humbly submitted to the constitutions of the Rule, because whatever either we or others prescribe for the instruction of monks, the whole of it is directed toward the observance of the monastic norm. For the sum of our religion, which is acquired by the continuation of spiritual exercise, is contained in the Rule of monastic tradition as if in a root and compendium. Therefore every spiritual or bodily exercise of monks by which the mind is led, out of love, to humble obedience and self-denial is conformed to the institutions of the holy Rule; and insofar as it is recognized as presenting the monk first to God as good and humble, so far does it oppose the pursuits of proud contempt. The holy mysteries of this exercise direct the mind of their practitioner, by an invisible teaching, to the love of God and to its proper end along a secure path; they wondrously acquire the grace of inward devotion and make the monk despise all earthly things as nothing and burn sweetly at all times in the fervor of the spirit. The animal man does not grasp this salutary exercise of devotion; the vain and curious monk does not understand this study; the proud do not admit this knowledge, to be preferred to all things. Happy is the one who has learned to penetrate the secrets of this exercise; happy too is the one who has deserved to receive from the Lord this gift of holy compunction, for whoever has once perfectly obtained it will fear no adversities of this world. But that exercise which, deprived of its proper end, does not direct the minds of cloistered persons to the observance of the commandments of the Rule does not infuse the holy spirit of discipline, because where the obedience of subjection is despised, no gift is less accepted by almighty God.

For it is written: “Obedience is better than victims, and to listen is better than to offer fat.” And although there are found many little works on spiritual exercise composed by various people, nevertheless not all things suit everyone. It has therefore seemed good to us also to publish something in keeping with the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, by which we may more nearly and intimately stir ourselves to the purity of regular observance. And so we have divided the present little work, collected from the exercises of the excellent father Theoderic, once abbot of the monastery of Bursfelde, into two principal parts: the first is placed in consideration of monastic institution; the second is occupied with the exercise of the spiritual life. In the first it bids you know yourself; in the second, to lift your mind from earthly things to heavenly things. Therefore, approaching the proposed consideration according to our powers, we have divided the first treatise into three parts, according to the threefold state of cloistered persons, whom we may call first sensual, second rational, and third spiritual. From this threefold state of good persons we take a fitting title for this work, which we name On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons. In these three regions the whole crowd of monks is included, as will be said in what follows.

On the first region of the soul, in which carnal monks live with the beasts, disordered by many passions, dwelling sensually in the lowest region.

The first region of the soul, then, is called sensual, lowest, and animal; in it the roots of all evils are contained. In this dark region, men placed among the beasts share in their kind, for they observe, as I might say, bestial habits, since they grant either nothing or very little to reason, but concede everything to sensuality like beasts.
