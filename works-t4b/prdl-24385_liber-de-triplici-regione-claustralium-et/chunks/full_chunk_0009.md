But whoever strives to evade the unity of the chapter imitates the devil. Thus the emperor of the Bulgarians forbids the Christians subject to him, under pain of death, to hold an assembly in church, while nevertheless granting them permission to believe whatever they wish. For that cunning man knows that the sincerity of the Christian faith cannot long subsist if the public office of exhortation happens to be lacking. The disordered and carnal abbot strives, as far as he can, to imitate this adversary of the Christian faith, since he resists the very necessary commands of the chapter. For the authority of the chapter alone maintains the observance of regular discipline in the order; if it is despised, no integrity of monastic life will remain. But the inhabitant of the lowest region, the disordered abbot, invents countless pretexts against regular observance; he falsely claims that the conditions of his monastery are contrary to it; he complains that ancient privileges are being suppressed; he thinks it beneath him for his monastery to be subject to anyone. But woe to the disordered men through whom even one monastery should happen to be lost from this most holy congregation. Finally, this plague of avarice makes the abbot not only disordered toward his subjects, but also opposed to his own self and wholly turned away from God. For covetousness is the root of all evils; taking away a servant of Christ, it makes him a minister of the devil. Therefore the abbot who has subjected his mind to this vice shows himself to be not a shepherd but a hireling. He always gapes after earthly profits; he cares nothing for the advancement of souls; a book of sacred reading is rarely, or never, seen in his hands; the registers of the monastery are turned over more frequently. He joins field to field; he keeps gathering gold and silver to be buried in a chest; and while he excessively desires his monastery to abound in temporal possessions, he prepares booty for plunderers. For the papal treasury often devours the monies of monasteries, which excessive greed brings upon the monks' monastery. Woe to you, greedy pastor of cloistered men, who are more solicitous for brute animals than for the souls entrusted to you.

A horse falls sick and at once you prepare remedies; a soul is endangered and you think no danger threatens anyone. Your ox and ass fall sick and you feel compassion; a brother is swallowed up by sadness and you do not come to his aid. You are therefore a hireling, not a shepherd, who with a stubborn heart despise your brother's infirmities; who do not help one set in bitterness; do not think that you can escape the hand of the Lord, whose sheep you permit to perish so wickedly.

Third, a ruler is disordered toward his subjects when he places outside, not without danger, those whom he ought anxiously to have kept within the enclosures of the monastery. Indeed, when a weak cenobite is exposed to the governance of nuns or to secular churches, the integrity of regular observance is violated, because one who, still dwelling in the lowest region, has not learned to subject the flesh to the spirit does not know how to preside over others without danger to himself. But the disordered pastor who, for the sake of some temporal advantage, is not afraid to expose his monks to so great a danger is guilty of all the souls that perish by such an occasion, and before the eternal Judge he is bound to make restoration for them all. It does not belong to the profession of a monk to govern secular churches; it is not expedient for a weak cenobite to preside over nuns. But what then? Ought a monk to oppose the command of his pastor if that pastor wishes him to reside with nuns or in secular churches? For the Rule commands that nothing is to be preferred to obedience. I truly do not know what I should answer to this question, so as not to incur the danger that is seen to threaten on both sides. For if I say the truth, I shall seem to infringe the obedience of subjection; if I prescribe that monks be exposed to danger, I confess that I sin. Nevertheless, I shall say briefly what I think, although I am not unaware that truth is going to give birth to hatred. Just as a monk, by the necessity of his profession, is bound simply to obey his pastor in all things that are according to the monastic Rule, so a prelate cannot compel a monk, without that monk's own will, to things that are above the Rule, especially when danger to salvation intervenes. But to govern churches, to preside over women, to reside outside the monastery: this is not according to the Rule of obedience.
