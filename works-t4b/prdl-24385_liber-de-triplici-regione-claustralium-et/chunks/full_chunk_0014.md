Bernard says of them: “He who thinks that he surpasses others: how could he not presume more about himself and about others?” He sits first in the assembly; he answers first in counsel; he comes forward without being called; he intrudes without being ordered; he rearranges what has been arranged and rejects what others have done. Whatever he himself has not done or arranged, he considers not rightly done and not well ordered. He judges those who judge, and prejudges those who are about to judge. For the one for whom God does not suffice in the heart seeks comfort in outward things in vain.

The eighth degree of pride is called the defense of sins, and it is always generated from presumption. For a presumptuous monk always thinks himself better than everyone. If he commits some sin, he either stubbornly denies it, or, if he is convicted, excuses it in many ways. For how will he be able humbly to confess his fault, when he does not recognize himself as guilty? If you try to chastise such a man for his offenses, you always make him worse. Therefore whoever, when corrected for any matter, turns aside into words of malice, whoever sets excuses for sins against the one reproving him, falls into this descent of death, opposed to the fifth degree of humility. The presumptuous man tries to excuse his sin in clear ways, because he is found to have fallen into this degree of pride. For either he says, “I did not do it,” or “I did it, indeed, but I did well,” or, “If badly, not very badly,” or, “If very badly, not with bad intention.” And if he has been convicted of malice, he at once adopts another person’s counsel in his own excuse. But he who insolently does not fear to deny or defend even things open and manifest will never reveal hidden things to his abbot according to the Rule. When he comes to confession, he will cloak the truth with a feigned mind, because, so that he himself may escape confusion, he tries to bring the negligences of others into the midst. Inwardly disordered, proud and presumptuous, he conceals his own faults in confession and confesses those of others. Always restless within himself, he examines the deeds of others, condemns everyone with a single sentence, complains that no one lives rightly in the monastery, and thinks that there is no charity among the brothers. is detected by the confessor’s diligence, he immediately seeks another confessor. If the abbot begins to try to oppose him in this, the monk, lifted up into the ninth degree of pride, falls through the restlessness of his mind.

The ninth degree of pride is described as murmuring or impatience, which for the most part rules over carnal monks. For indeed, when a man’s confession is feigned, once touched by a slight rebuke or penance, he immediately rises up into open pride; the deceit that simulated humility is revealed, nor can the false mind hide itself any longer, but it is compelled to bring into public view whatever lies hidden within. From then on, made masterless within himself, the proud man in the monastery murmurs at everything; he sharply attacks the judgment of the shepherd, however just; he gnashes his teeth, is stirred up, becomes angry, and rages without reason like a madman. But the proud monk, given over to murmuring, after despising the fourth degree of humility, falls into the tenth descent of pride, through which he runs toward hell.

The tenth degree of pride follows murmuring and is called rebellion. Proud and carnal monks fall into this, those who, still dwelling in the lowest region, devote themselves to murmurings. Hence Bernard says: unless divine mercy looks upon the murmurer, so that at last, converted, which is very difficult for such men, he silently acquiesces in the judgments of others, he will soon become brazen and shameless, and will fall all the worse into this degree, the more desperately he does not restrain the motion of his mind. He always advances toward worse things; and what earlier, in his arrogance, he had secretly held in contempt among his brothers, now, what is more serious, he openly shows himself to despise his master as well. But the scrutiny of divine judgment does not allow this degree of pride to pass unpunished for long, since monks who rebel against their shepherds are frequently soon consumed by a miserable death. Some, withdrawing from the order, become apostates; others, because of grave crimes, foul the prisons; some, expelled from monasteries, wander in wretched exile; many, horribly struck by the Lord and in despair, hasten to the lower world. For it is written: “He who resists authority opposes the ordinance of God.”
