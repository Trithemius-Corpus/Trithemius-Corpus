The third thing prescribed for the monk to observe in labor is that he speak little, and what is edifying. For Scripture says: “In much speaking sin will not be lacking.” Let a monk never speak without permission, if he truly desires to be good. But once he has permission to speak, let him say only a few useful things, knowing that idle and scurrilous words are condemned everywhere by the Rule. Let him establish for himself, everywhere and at all times, such a manner of speaking, through circumspection, that by speaking he offends no one. Let him listen more willingly than he desires to speak, and let him not say whatever his mind suggests if he fears the reproach of fools. For thus Hugh says to the monk: “Do not pour out everything you know, like a vessel without a lid, which, as soon as it is tilted, pours out whatever it contains, and lies open to all dust and filth.” Take diligent care never to seem, directly or indirectly, to seek your own glory; nor, from a desire to boast, imprudently reveal to anyone else whatever commendable thing lies hidden in you. If you are silent about yourself and hide yourself, you will be more pleasing; if you put yourself forward and commend yourself, you will become vile before all and will be mocked. Beware of idle words, because when we do not notice such things for long, little by little we slip into harmful things; from them are born either disturbances, dissipation, or the grievous gnawings of conscience. Be such in your inner conversation that, from love of spiritual meditation, silence always delights you more than talkativeness.

Let the cenobite be composed and mature in his conduct.

Fourth, the monk, both in labor and outside it, is commanded to compose his conduct in every place, and in all his deeds and words to show himself mature before others. Let him be pure and humble in heart; let him be kindly and useful in speech; let him be modest and grave in his gait; let him be mature and exemplary in his whole manner of life. The whole person, once he has undertaken to be composed according to the norm of discipline, must be held firm at all times in the intention of making progress. For indeed, while the monk desires to advance daily toward better things, he avoids every kind of dissipation and disorderly conduct. For a firm effort

to make progress has this as its own property: it makes the mind of the one striving very circumspect in all things. But he who does not have a firm effort to make progress toward the better cannot truly be called composed, even if he seems mature to others. For feigned maturity quickly dissolves into open dissipation of mind when it does not have the fear of God as the root of discipline. Therefore, so that our cenobite may always be composed within and without, let him strive to be truly rooted in the fear of the Lord. For he who fears God neglects nothing. And the love of God is not idle.

What the monk’s conduct toward other people ought to be.

Consideration XI.

Since we have begun to speak about the maturity and composure that a monk must preserve before others, it seems useful to us now to say briefly how he ought to show himself to particular persons. For he is instructed to show himself in one way to his prelate, and is commanded to present himself in another way to his brothers and to secular people. To his rector, as holding the place of Christ, he ought to show reverence; to a brother, charity; to the hostile, gentleness; to secular people, modest piety. Therefore let the monk render to each what is his, and he will be truly composed before God and human beings. What he owes to God, and what to himself, has been sufficiently stated above; now let us pursue the matters set before us.

Let his conduct toward the prelate, in the place of Christ, be reverential.
