Do not frown if I have expressed what seems right to me; nevertheless, even while I protest, you will do what seems right to you. Yet I beg you: do not expose your monks to danger to their chastity; do not violently compel anyone to the governance of nuns and parish churches, since you can find many who ambitiously desire these offices. Obedience is indeed good, but it must in no way be preferred to truth and the salvation of the soul. For when the danger concerns salvation, it is better to set obedience aside by humbly excusing oneself than to suffer shipwreck of chastity. Woe to monks and prelates who incorporate secular churches into their monasteries, who loosen the fold of desire with the torches of avarice, and who, while they think they are benefiting posterity, damn many by giving occasion.

Fourth, a rector of monks is made disorderly toward his subjects if he is found turbulent and anxious. In a pastor, pious and prudent discretion of mind is very necessary, lest either zeal for discipline be excessive or the measure of compassion be relaxed by too much laxity. But every disorderly rector shows himself a tyrant to the monks through indiscreet fury, or through slack compassion keeps silent, as if insensible, before the gravest crimes. He does not know the just moderation of rule; he does not experience within himself the fervor of fraternal charity; he always thinks himself lord of the monks and does not know himself as a father and humble companion. What is more savage than this rule, what power more wicked, where neither God, nor reason, nor understanding, but a beast holds sway? Truly, where God and understanding govern authority, the judgments of those who preside do not depart from the rectitude of the law of nature. But the disorderly and anxious rector is infected with various bestial passions; he always suspects evil of the monks, and, while he directs the manner of rule wrongly, he is always anxious and, with Herod, fears losing his kingdom.

Fifth, a pastor of cloistered men is said to be disorderly toward the monks if, in dispensing the goods of the monastery, he is held either excessive or obstinate. He must be called excessive if he is prodigal in distributing, or diffuse in indiscreet largess. He is called obstinate if he does not give at the proper time what ought to be given,

or if he grudgingly spends what is necessary for his subjects. He must also be called obstinate who casts aside the fear of the Lord, who trusts too much in his own judgment, and who, in whatever matters are to be done, admits no counsel of the wise. And indeed, among pastors of souls very many are disorderly through this sickness of mind: they either spend the goods of the monastery too improvidently or preserve them with too much tenacity. For their own flatterers at table they prepare the most lavish things, but to others, against whom they are averse, they deny even the smallest things. In the guesthouse or in their own abbey, pastors corrupted by disorderly conduct eat to the point of excess and always drink the best wine; but to the poor monks, who by watching and singing bear the burden and heat of the day, they scarcely grant even meager necessities.

Sixth, a prelate is shown to be very disorderly toward monks if, bold against God, he is lifted up in pride by making light of his judgments. For since a pastor cannot be corrected by his subjects, he is remitted by the holy father Benedict to the judgment of God, as is held in many places of the Rule. “Let the abbot know,” he says, “that he will render an account to God for all his judgments.” And again: “The ordained abbot should always consider what a burden he has undertaken, and to whom he will render an account of his stewardship.” And again he says: “As soon as evils begin to arise, let him cut them off with charity, not with tyranny.” And in another place he warns, saying: “Nevertheless, let the abbot himself do all things with fear of God and observance of the holy Rule, knowing without doubt that he will render an account for all his judgments to God, the most just judge.”

But the disorderly rector of monks, like an asp with stopped ears, scorns to hear this threat of the most holy lawgiver; he despises the patience of God’s long-suffering; he thinks nothing of the future examination, until at last the angry judge says to his face: “You did these things, and I kept silent. Now therefore render an account of your stewardship. Give back to me the sheep of my inheritance, and without delay restore to me the souls once entrusted to you in the body. Return their number whole; for if even one of them has perished through your negligence, your soul shall forever [unclear].”
