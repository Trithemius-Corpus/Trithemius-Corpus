But when the seniors, citizens of this lowest region, withdraw fatherly affection from the juniors, the younger men burn with bitter hatred; and because the youths consider themselves unjustly despised by them, they accuse some of them of wicked habits. Thus weak young men examine the improper conduct of their seniors; and, as the proverb says, as he who considers greater men barking, so too he causes lesser men to dance.

Third, monks are badly ordered toward one another when they cast aside good zeal and mutual obedience, and assume the evil kind of zeal forbidden by the Rule. By words and deeds they commit various injuries against one another. Each strives, through rivalry, to watch the other for evil; one is provoked by another to inflict insult. They mutually reproach one another with their weaknesses; they always pant after revenge. They despise zeal for regular discipline, and the passion of indignation and bitterness rules them. They meet one another with faces turned away, and reveal by open signs how much hatred lies hidden in the heart. Not so the wicked, not so; for whoever persecutes his brother with hatred is shown to be a murderer and unworthy of the kingdom of God. O always wretched condition of carnal monks, which is the worse hell the more alien it has been from the fear of God. They imitate not angels but demons, whoever, through evil zeal, take delight in being discordant in the school of peace. Indeed, just as a brute animal does not abandon the savagery of its nature, so a beastlike monk does not withdraw from the worst zeal.

In What Way Monks, Citizens of the Lowest Region, Are Disordered Toward Themselves.

Article V.

The citizens of the lowest region, carnal monks, are disordered toward nothing more than toward themselves. For they cast aside every consideration of themselves; they do not attend to the end for which they were created; because, while each one pollutes himself with the filth of vices, an evil mind withdraws from the original rectitude of its condition. Nor is it any wonder if the cultivator of the lowest region becomes outwardly disordered toward others, since through the various passions

of the flesh he is inwardly confused and dissipated from himself. Scripture says: “He who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good?” The right order of the human condition established that the flesh should be subject to the spirit, and that the spirit itself should be subject to him from whom it received the form of existing. But among carnal minds the order of reason is overturned, when the flesh rules the spirit, and reason, which according to the force of understanding implanted in it ought to have governed all things, is suffocated by the pleasures of the flesh and does not hasten through the rule of the spirit toward the rectitude of judgment.

Carnal monks, then, are disordered toward themselves through the vice of curiosity, because by spying curiously into another’s life they judge their own, stained with every vice, and do not amend it. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, yet they proudly rebuke others who offend in very small matters. They carefully consider the splinter in a brother’s eye, and the beam clinging in their own eyes they excuse as if it were a speck. Those who subject all their affections to the quality of sense, and therefore live carnally like beasts instead of by the judgment of right reason, are often stirred by the passion of anger; the fury of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With an anxious mind they always demand vengeance against a brother; and often, blinded by excessive fury, they do not carry it out without some loss to themselves. But disordered citizens cannot long remain hidden in secret; for the more they remain unknown to themselves through the pride of the mind, the more, by a blind judgment, they are made manifest to others. For every proud and disordered man is said first to be ignorant of himself, because if he truly knew himself, he would in no way be proud.

Since, then, disorder in oneself takes its beginning from pride, we cannot describe this plague better than by undertaking to disclose its roots through the steps that descend to hell. And just as the summit of the spiritual life is contained in the twelve degrees of humility, so the fountainhead of all monastic disorder is contained in twelve degrees of pride. The first degree of pride, then, is called curiosity. By it monks badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and conduct, and continually inquire into another’s deeds or words. They charge all their brothers with negligence; they rebuke the life of the pastor; because the more curiously they strive to know outwardly the affairs of others, the farther they withdraw from themselves.
