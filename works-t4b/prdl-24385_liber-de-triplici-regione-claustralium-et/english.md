Letter of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the little work On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons.

To the reverend fathers in Christ, the lord presidents, definitors, and the other abbots of the annual chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict, of the Bursfeld union and observance: Brother Johannes Trithemius, called abbot at Sponheim, of the same order and congregation, in the diocese of Mainz, offers reverence and charity.

I have obeyed your commands, most venerable fathers, and, as you instructed me, I undertook to correct the little work on the threefold region of cloistered persons together with the spiritual exercise of monks, recently edited by the venerable father Lord Johannes, abbot of Bursfelde. And indeed, if your authority had not compelled me, I would never have dared to set my hand to the distinguished work of so great a father, who, it is agreed, is far more learned and holier than I, and wrote it excellently. For what was I to correct, where everything is found most approved? That good and devout man wrote no otherwise than as he wished and as was fitting.

Nevertheless, in order to satisfy both your command and the prayers of the author himself, I rendered the whole little work in briefer, though not fewer, sentences, and, while remaining faithful to its sense, I brought it as far as I could into my own manner and style of writing. I preserved the order and matter of the earlier volume, but entirely changed its form and manner of expression. In almost every place I added many things that seemed useful to me; I removed a few; in individual passages I altered the words, not the meaning. Your command gave me the courage to do this, and the insistent request of the aforesaid father fully persuaded me. For you wished me to make a new work out of old material, to add whatever seemed suitable to me, to cut away superfluities, and to alter what needed changing. I did what I could; I obeyed your commands; according to the powers of my intellect I completed, by rewriting, the work assigned to me.

--- Page 011 ---
Now therefore, most beloved fathers, receive a work that is not mine but yours, not composed by me but rather rewritten. If the labor pleases you, praise the talent of the aforesaid father; but if, because of its worthlessness, it displeases you, correct my imperfection. I had excellent material for spiritual edification from another's resources, but through my inexperience I have built an inept structure. If I have been made a fool, you compelled me. Therefore, whether the work pleases or displeases, it has been easier for me, by writing ineptly, to take up your command obediently than, against obedience, to keep silence. Yet I trust, not in my own merits but in another's, that a work brought forth by charity will displease no one who loves God.

I pray that your fatherhoods may remain forever blessed in Christ. From Sponheim, the eighth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord's incarnation 1497.

and renew the condition of our mind to the fervor of the holy purpose. Whoever omits the exercise of daily instruction falls into a depth of torpor, as the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “If you do not hold yourself steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will soon be overthrown,” by which he means a good conscience. For the provision of this exercise, so useful and so necessary, you see that the Rule of our most holy father Benedict is sufficient for a monk, since it is known to contain all the material of exercises, both spiritual and bodily, necessary for monks in guarding regular discipline. And therefore all the exercises of monks must be humbly submitted to the constitutions of the Rule, because whatever either we or others prescribe for the instruction of monks, the whole of it is directed toward the observance of the monastic norm. For the sum of our religion, which is acquired by the continuation of spiritual exercise, is contained in the Rule of monastic tradition as if in a root and compendium. Therefore every spiritual or bodily exercise of monks by which the mind is led, out of love, to humble obedience and self-denial is conformed to the institutions of the holy Rule; and insofar as it is recognized as presenting the monk first to God as good and humble, so far does it oppose the pursuits of proud contempt. The holy mysteries of this exercise direct the mind of their practitioner, by an invisible teaching, to the love of God and to its proper end along a secure path; they wondrously acquire the grace of inward devotion and make the monk despise all earthly things as nothing and burn sweetly at all times in the fervor of the spirit. The animal man does not grasp this salutary exercise of devotion; the vain and curious monk does not understand this study; the proud do not admit this knowledge, to be preferred to all things. Happy is the one who has learned to penetrate the secrets of this exercise; happy too is the one who has deserved to receive from the Lord this gift of holy compunction, for whoever has once perfectly obtained it will fear no adversities of this world. But that exercise which, deprived of its proper end, does not direct the minds of cloistered persons to the observance of the commandments of the Rule does not infuse the holy spirit of discipline, because where the obedience of subjection is despised, no gift is less accepted by almighty God.

For it is written: “Obedience is better than victims, and to listen is better than to offer fat.” And although there are found many little works on spiritual exercise composed by various people, nevertheless not all things suit everyone. It has therefore seemed good to us also to publish something in keeping with the Rule of our most holy father Benedict, by which we may more nearly and intimately stir ourselves to the purity of regular observance. And so we have divided the present little work, collected from the exercises of the excellent father Theoderic, once abbot of the monastery of Bursfelde, into two principal parts: the first is placed in consideration of monastic institution; the second is occupied with the exercise of the spiritual life. In the first it bids you know yourself; in the second, to lift your mind from earthly things to heavenly things. Therefore, approaching the proposed consideration according to our powers, we have divided the first treatise into three parts, according to the threefold state of cloistered persons, whom we may call first sensual, second rational, and third spiritual. From this threefold state of good persons we take a fitting title for this work, which we name On the Threefold Region of Cloistered Persons. In these three regions the whole crowd of monks is included, as will be said in what follows.

On the first region of the soul, in which carnal monks live with the beasts, disordered by many passions, dwelling sensually in the lowest region.

The first region of the soul, then, is called sensual, lowest, and animal; in it the roots of all evils are contained. In this dark region, men placed among the beasts share in their kind, for they observe, as I might say, bestial habits, since they grant either nothing or very little to reason, but concede everything to sensuality like beasts. For in this lowest region three sensual powers of the soul preside: the first, the sensitive power, is diffused about the objects of the five exterior senses; the second, the concupiscible power, delights in carnal things; the third, the irascible power

And this lowest region is compared to the airy heaven, because just as hail, snow, lightning, and various storms are generated in the air, so also in the minds of carnal men inhabiting this dark region various storms of vices follow. Woe to carnal monks who inhabit this dark region, in which the movement of crimes is frequent and the storm of pleasures continual.

These are monks given over to animal passions, who subject reason to sensuality, who live according to the flesh in bestial desires. Now the works of the flesh in one dwelling in this lowest region are manifest: fornication, uncleanness, shamelessness, luxury, service of idols, sorceries, enmities, rivalries, wrath, quarrels, dissensions, sects, envies, murders, drunkenness, feasting, and things like these. Concerning them the holy apostle proclaims that those who do such things will not obtain the kingdom of God.

These are the marks of the works of the lowest region; these are the deadly storms of the minds of carnal monks, who live irreligiously in religion, who despise the traditions of the holy fathers, who contemptuously scorn the precepts handed down by elders. These are the unclean inhabitants of the lowest region, given over to vices and carnal desires, who do not care to have God in their knowledge, who serve the pleasures of the flesh, who stubbornly break the vow of holy religion which they have professed, who, scorning the salvation of their souls, despise following when the grace of God calls.

Therefore he gave them over to a reprobate sense and to false judgment, so that they judge evil to be good, and falsely think that the things which in truth are worst are the best. These are those about whom the holy apostle prophesied: carnal monks, whisperers, detractors, hateful to God, insulting, proud, puffed up, inventors of evils, disobedient to superiors, foolish, disorderly, without affection, without covenant, without mercy, filled with all iniquity, malice, wickedness, contention, deceit, and malignity. Although they know the justice of God, they do not wish to understand that those who do such things are worthy of perpetual death; and not only those who do them, but also those who consent to those who do them, and those who, by reason of their office, in no way oppose evil pursuits.

For indeed the ruler of souls who does not correct his erring subjects entangles himself in damnation for the crimes of others; by silence he becomes the accomplice of those whose faults damnation will follow. For whoever, placed as pastor of souls, neglects to correct sinners according to canonical rule, even if he is free from his own fault, will nevertheless be condemned before the strict judge for another’s guilt.

For the supreme shepherd says to the prophet: “Son of man, I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel; and you shall hear the word from my mouth, and shall announce it to them from me. If, when I say to the wicked man, ‘You shall surely die,’ you do not announce it to him, nor speak so that he may turn from his wicked way and live, that wicked man shall indeed die in his iniquity; but I will require his blood from your hand.” And our blessed father says in the Rule: “Let the abbot know that the fault of the shepherd will fall upon him if the father of the household has been able to find any lack of profit in his sheep.”

O sentence to be feared by all spiritual pastors, by which not only the depraved conduct of subjects is ascribed to the ruler, but he is held guilty before the inner judge even for their lesser progress. O what shall we wretched pastors say in that strict examination, we who neglect to correct according to the rule the monks committed to us when they err in many ways; who do not call those lying in the lowest region to higher things, but, carnal with carnal men, grow sluggish in the shadow of death through pernicious idleness?

Woe to all who remain in this dark region; for unless they do penance, they must be assigned with those who inhabit the outer darkness, where no order dwells, but everlasting horror. Therefore, just as monks who walk according to the rule in the spirit are to be happily crowned without end in heaven with the holy angels, so those who persevere until death in this lowest region of the flesh, contrary to the norm of monastic institution, worse than pagans, are to be eternally tormented in hell with demons.

For that blessed man truly says: “Just as I have never seen any better than those who have made progress in the monastery, so I have never found any worse than those who have failed in the monastery.” For the monastic profession requires the perfection of the spiritual life and tends, as to its proper end, toward the summit of the virtues. But the more anyone slackens from this effort of making progress in monastic life,

How the carnal monks, citizens of this lowest region, are disordered in many articles.

Although the description of this lowest region applies generally to all carnal men who abide in sins and crimes with the moral beasts, nevertheless, so that we may tend to our own affairs, we intend to speak of lukewarm and carnal cloisterers, doing no injury at all to the good and devout. For always and in all things we wish the conduct of good cloisterers to remain untouched and unharmed whenever we discuss the evil pursuits of the wicked. Therefore we rebuke evil cloisterers and corrupt morals, while everywhere honoring and praising the good, as is fitting. And what else but evil can we say about the evil, in whom we find no good things to speak of? They live disorderly in a holy order; they despise the traditions of the elders; they do not admit words of salvation. Indeed, so great is the disorder of the monks of the lowest region that, in my judgment, it would be easier to convert a publican than to recall a disorderly monk to repentance. We comprehend this disorder of carnal monks in eight articles, although we are not unaware that they are disordered in many other matters.

First, the beast-like monks, citizens of this lowest region, are disordered toward God; next, toward their own prelate; third, the prelate toward his subjects; fourth, the members toward one another; again, fifth, they are disordered toward themselves; then toward the goods of the monastery; seventh, toward contempt of the world; and finally toward repentance for sin. These are the principal disorders of the citizens of the lowest region; these are the beast-like affections of carnal monks, who dwell in the lowest things in mind and body, and do not hasten to make progress toward the highest things. Therefore I shall take care to set out briefly, in order, these disordered conditions of the lowest region, and to demonstrate as best I can how far carnal monks are from God. Ps. 118.v. For, as the prophet says, salvation is far from sinners.

How the citizens of this lowest region are disordered toward God. Article 1.

Therefore the carnal monks of this lowest region, its mad inhabitants, are in many ways disordered toward God. They do not feel the sweetness of his love; they admit no compunction of divine fear; beast-like and swollen in their affections, they are at all times unclean in their carnal pleasures.

And first, their disorder toward God takes its origin from ignorance: for, being given over to animal passions, they do not care to have God in their knowledge, nor do they consent to think in their mind about those things that lead to fear of him; instead, blinded by evil desires, they become vain in their thoughts. But what does Scripture say? “He who is ignorant shall be ignored.” They do not wish to understand so that they may act well; they do not fear before the face of the strict judge; without repentance they constantly persist in evil. Indeed, after the manner of beasts they do not fear to rot in their pleasures; they pay no heed to the patience of God calling them to repentance; they seek nothing except the comforts of their own flesh. At all times they walk in the greatest sins without any fear; they stubbornly refuse to know the ways of the Lord. Of the future judgment they think either nothing or little; they do not fear the punishments prepared in hell for sinners; the grace of compunction never pierces their hearts, since the love of God does not inflame their minds. They have no concern for God, and no dread of death. They obey the belly more than God; at all times they desire to live according to the flesh; and while they shamefully neglect their own salvation, they descend to hell with the demons. Indeed, those who, forgetful of God, badly keep the vow of the holy monastic way of life do not reach the rewards of heavenly life to be granted to the just.

Second, carnal monks are disordered toward God by a foolish profession: for they promise to live according to the rule, yet they do not truly care to fulfill this. And what is more disordered than to promise a holy life to the one God and then remain in the filth of one’s former life? Whoever persists in a monastery otherwise than the monastic rule teaches is a mockery of almighty God, not a monk.

We see that many today among cloistered persons live in a worldly manner; we see more of them neglecting the purpose of their profession; they violate the observance of regular discipline in various ways; they knowingly disregard the goodness of God; they refuse to do penance.

Third, many cloistered persons of this lowest region are disordered toward God because of negligence in the service due to him. They serve their own affections and carnal desires in various ways, and they always approach the service of God with a distracted mind. Among them, some run lukewarmly to choir without any devotion; others come unwillingly, compelled by necessity; certain others, scattered by outward thoughts, are present bodily at the divine offices, but in mind they wander outside through various workshops. Others are not afraid to seek false occasions by which they may, as if plausibly, absent themselves from choir. Now they feign a grave headache; now they mendaciously allege this or that, and deceitfully withdraw the service owed to almighty God; they incur the reproach of eternal malediction owed to the negligent.

Fourth, very many are disordered toward God because of the working of habit. Although at times they are free from the malediction of the negligent, nevertheless they never approach the fruit of inward devotion. For they approach the service of God from habit alone, and are not moved to any good work by devotion. Others, placed under grave necessity, obey the commands of their superiors unwillingly enough, and fear temporal punishments rather than the judgment of God. These carry the Lord’s cross with Simon under compulsion, and, like a heifer of Ephraim taught to tread out the grain, they devote themselves to good works by habit alone. But those who approach the service of God with an improper intention do not do good well. Indeed, they perform just deeds without the reward of justice, because in good and just works they do not take up a good will.

Fifth, the citizens of the lowest region are disordered toward God through the vice of ingratitude, which is the greatest crime of a disordered will. Often, indeed, a person who is found ungrateful to the Lord for benefits received is, by his own demerit, exposed to chastisement by various and very grave temptations. And therefore he who neglects to show himself grateful to so great a benefactor deserves to lose the benefit he has received.

--- Page 020 ---

And what greater ingratitude can there be than to live irreligiously in religion, to break one’s vow, shamefully to violate the purpose solemnly assumed? And a monk who entangles himself in carnal vices, who neglects to live according to the Rule he has professed, willingly incurs the greatest vice of ingratitude.

How the monks of this lowest region are disordered toward their prelate.

Article II.

Carnal monks are disordered in many ways toward their prelate. They tear his life apart with constant detractions; with a stubborn heart they disparage his pastoral office; they refuse, according to the Rule, to show the affection of filial love to their pastor. For monks of this lowest region, blinded by various pleasures of the flesh, disdain to consider whose place the abbot holds in the monastery; and the man whom they ought humbly to venerate as Christ’s vicar, they regard, because of weakness, as one like themselves and worthy of no honor. Under the snare of this disordered way of life, the ancient enemy deceives carnal monks, whom he leads in various ways into displeasure with their pastor. For the greatest power of raging against the monastic flock is granted to the devil when the one who is set over it is hated by his subjects. For just as a sick man who refuses to come because he detests the physician cannot be healed, so a monk who, on account of aversion, does not wish to reveal his temptations to his pastor cannot be saved. For how will anyone be able to escape the diseased teeth of the ravening wolf, if he cannot bear the presence of the pastor?

First, therefore, disordered monks disparage the life of their pastor; they stubbornly accuse both his deeds and his words, because, when they see their ruler walking according to the traditions of the holy fathers, they fear being corrected in their own depravity. For among reprobate minds the life of the good is always drawn into suspicion; they are always tormented by envy at the virtues of others; and a monk who voluntarily subjects himself to vices by sinning always hates a pastor of purified conduct.

Second, the citizens of this dark region are disordered toward their prelates when they disparage his office, though they know that he has been ordained by God to preside in the monastery. On slight occasion they murmur against him; they do not observe his commands; and those who had long ago subjected themselves, for the love of God, to the arms of holy obedience now, after the fashion of Sarabaites, despise obedience and usurp for their own wills a dominion ruinous to themselves. They desire to have an abbot suited to their corrupt morals; one holy and useful they reject. If they find a lax and idle pastor, they intoxicate many with the poisons of flattery; they call him the best and most loving father; they subject themselves to him not humbly but gladly, since they fear from him no correction of their depravity.

Third, carnal monks show themselves disordered toward their pastors when they withdraw from them the affection of filial love and despise obedience according to the Rule. Yet since a monk is bound to love his abbot, as Christ’s vicar, with sincere affection, he cannot hate him without the gravest crime. Indeed, whoever does not love his pastor for the love of Christ is no true lover of God. For Christ, who wills even enemies to be loved, how would he permit his vicars to be hated? Therefore look to him who wished your abbot to be in his place for you, and from now on there will be no cause of displeasure against him.

Fourth, the disordered citizens of the shadowy valley detract from the reverence due to their pastor when they not only scorn to render him the honor due by the Rule, but, what is worse, do not even fear to inflict insult on him. For the Rule teaches that the abbot bears Christ’s place in the monastery, and that all monks ought humbly to obey him as Christ himself, because obedience rendered to superiors is shown to Christ. But perverse cloisterers, always ungrateful for the presidency of prelates, do not fear obstinately to oppose the divine ordinance; they scorn to obey the commands of the holy Rule; and by hatred and detraction they dishonor their abbot, to whom they ought to have shown every reverence. Whatever he has done, whatever

he has ordained, whatever he has said, is at once badly done, and with contempt for his conduct and office they seek nothing more than the confusion of their pastor, like diseased sheep. Some of these judge and despise their abbot in secret; others contradict him to his face. And while they neglect to show reverence to their pastor, they confound every observance of discipline. Pride is always familiar to such men, and foulness, the friend of the flesh, because those who voluntarily withdraw themselves from affection for their pastor fall into abominable temptations and the worst crimes. For whoever despises Christ’s vicar in the monastery hands over to the devil the power to rage in him, and confounds the unity of the mystical body.

Fifth, carnal monks are disordered toward their pastor and detract from his authority when they think that nothing is permitted to him in the monastery unless they themselves have consented. With necks raised up, they despise the virtue of humility; they spurn regular subjection; they scorn to yield to their betters. They wish to insert themselves into all business and cases, to usurp for themselves the primacy of counsel, so that the abbot dares to do or arrange nothing unless the business first proceeds with their consent. But what does the blessed lawgiver Benedict say in the Rule? “Having heard,” he says, “the counsel of the brothers, let the abbot consider it within himself, and let him do what he has judged more useful.” And again: “Let the brothers give counsel with all humble subjection, so that they do not presume stubbornly to defend what has seemed good to them; rather, let it depend on the abbot’s judgment, so that all may obey what he has judged more salutary.” And again, concerning the ordering of ranks, he says the opposite: “We think it expedient, for the preservation of charity and peace, that the ordering of his monastery depend on the abbot’s judgment.” This useful ordering concerns the appointment and removal of all the officials of the monastery, which requires not the consent of the brothers but the counsel of those who fear God. Finally, the Rule commands that in all things one act according to the will of the abbot, and that nothing at all be presumed which has not been enjoined by him. Carnal monks, rash usurpers of their own will, attack this authority of his in various ways.

They try in various ways to break his authority, and to disturb the order and charity of the whole monastery, since they think that the ruler should do nothing except what they themselves consider right. But tell me, proud worshipers of the lowest region, who always decide by your own will and usurp another's right for yourselves: is this the virtue of obedience, to refuse to yield to the command of a superior, but stubbornly to cling to one's own judgment? The holy father did not establish this way of living for you in the Rule, for he commanded you to follow the will of the abbot in all things. For obedience is the greatest virtue of a monk, and our holy father wished to commend it so highly that he said a good work lacks merit without the abbot's will. Hence, giving instruction, he concludes thus: “Since therefore all things are to be done with the abbot's will, let no one in the monastery follow the will of his own heart.”

But if the prudence of the ruler seems insufficient for the ordering of the monastery, the blame must be laid on the carnal monks, who voluntarily set over themselves one who was not suitable. Folly always hates prudence, and wicked monks detest a wise abbot. Therefore anyone who knowingly sets a fool over himself must necessarily be subject to his ordering and his will. Indeed, no insufficiency of the pastor ought to extinguish the obedience of subjects, since the Rule commands us to obey even one who is useless. For it says: “Obedience is to be given in all things to the abbot's commands, even if, God forbid, he himself acts otherwise.”

But when we rebuke their disobedience, carnal monks object: “We do these things with a right and good intention, because we see the substance of the monastery perishing through bad governance.” But the Apostle contradicts you, for evil things are not to be done so that good may come; nor, on account of loss of temporal property, is the subjection of humility to be abandoned. For when there arises a necessity of undergoing either the loss of temporal substance or danger to regular obedience, it is better for the substance to perish than for the purity of obedience to be harmed. For God does not require money from our hands, but souls. Truly, one who is a true monk prefers the virtue of obedience to all the things of the world. But the carnal monks, citizens of this lowest region, wound the virtue of obedience in various ways.

--- Page 024 ---
They attack the weapons of holy humility, so that it would seem much easier to instruct monks newly come from the world than to be able to correct the ignorance of those men. But from their fruits we know clearly what sort they are: those who violate peace and charity in the monastery, disturb the concord of unity, sow discord between the brothers and the abbot, and stubbornly refuse obedience to the commands of the Rule. These are the works of evil cloisterers; these are the signs of the lowest citizens: because, as most wicked defenders of their own will, they cast away the weapons of holy obedience, live according to the flesh and not the spirit, defer either nothing or very little to the statutes of the fathers, and do not acquiesce in sound counsels. Their condemnation is just. For just as it is impossible for a blazing fire not to rise upward, so it is impossible for a monk without obedience to penetrate the secrets of heaven.

How the Disorderly Pastors of This Lowest Region Behave

Article III

Since, then, we have shown that very many monks of the lowest region are disorderly toward their pastors, why should we, sparing ourselves, not show what the disorders of prelates are toward their subjects? The name of abbot sanctifies no one, nor does the dignity of pastoral care save the undeserving. Therefore, since many among the prelates dwell on the mountain of the lowest region with the beasts of the earth, they must in no way be passed over by us in silence. For many rulers of cloistered persons show themselves disorderly toward their subjects: not only do they neglect to feed according to the Rule the souls committed to them, but they tear them apart and kill them in various ways. And whenever someone from the lowest region is taken up as pastor, the pursuits of the wicked are strengthened for the worse. Therefore, a good man and one diligent for God should be promoted as abbot: one who has already overcome this lowest region, who has learned to subdue the passions of the flesh, who knows how, and is willing and able, to ascend to that highest region of the spirit. For it is fitting that an abbot of monks be of higher merit than the monks, since whoever is appointed ruler of souls

he must necessarily be adorned with many virtues. For if the shepherd, still weak, dwells with his sheep in the lowest region, by neglecting the feeble he kills them; he does not comfort them. Conditions of the good shepherd.

--- Page 026 ---
Second, the shepherd of souls becomes disordered toward his subjects if his mind is too boldly occupied with temporal affairs, if he has greater concern for secular business than for the souls committed to him. Saint Father Benedict forbids this excessive care for temporal things in the Rule, and rightly judges souls and rational beings to be preferred to all worldly goods. “Above all,” he says, “let the abbot not neglect or make light of the salvation of the souls committed to him, and let him not have greater concern for transitory, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always consider that he has undertaken to govern souls, for which he will have to render an account.” Consequently, when the pious father gives assurance concerning the necessities of the body, he does so if one worships God with a pure, loving mind. “And lest perhaps he complain of scant resources,” he says, “let him remember what is written: First seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things will be added to you. And again: Nothing is lacking to those who fear God.”

But the disordered monk-shepherd is led astray by the goad of avarice; blinded by the darkness of a mind that does not perceive, he casts away fear of the Lord. With Ahab, sold under sin, he runs to the aid of wickedness; stripped of confidence in the promises made by the holy father about obtaining necessities, he occupies himself with outward affairs more than is fitting for a monk. Article V.

The citizens of the lowest region, carnal monks, are disordered toward nothing more than toward themselves. Carnal monks, then, are disordered toward themselves through the vice of curiosity, because by spying curiously into another’s life they judge their own, stained with every vice, and do not amend it. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, yet they proudly rebuke others who offend in very small matters. Those who subject all their affections to the quality of sense, and therefore live carnally like beasts instead of by the judgment of right reason, are often stirred by the passion of anger; the fury of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With an anxious mind they always demand vengeance against a brother; and often, blinded by excessive fury, they do not carry it out without some loss to themselves. But disordered citizens cannot long remain hidden in secret; for the more they remain unknown to themselves through the pride of the mind, the more, by a blind judgment, they are made manifest to others. For every proud and disordered man is said first to be ignorant of himself, because if he truly knew himself, he would in no way be proud.

Since, then, disorder in oneself takes its beginning from pride, we cannot describe this plague better than by undertaking to disclose its roots through the steps that descend to hell. And just as the summit of the spiritual life is contained in the twelve degrees of humility, so the fountainhead of all monastic disorder is contained in twelve degrees of pride. The first degree of pride, then, is called curiosity. By it monks badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and conduct, and continually inquire into another’s deeds or words. They charge all their brothers with negligence; they rebuke the life of the pastor; because the more curiously they strive to know outwardly the affairs of others, the farther they withdraw from themselves. For monks, however, the vice of curiosity

In what way the monks, carnal citizens of the lowest region, are disordered toward the property of the monastery.

Article Six.

Whoever, while established in a monastery, neglects to order his inner affections to the purity of holy conduct often also exceeds enormously in outward matters. For the substance of the monastery, dedicated to God, is commanded to be guarded like the vessels of the holy altar, lest it be dissipated in any way through negligence. But one who forbids the goods of cloistered persons to be spent uselessly would by no means permit them to be bestowed sinfully for shameful uses. What will the monks and rulers of the lowest region answer to the lawgiver in the strict examination, they who do not fear to dissipate the goods of the monastery among parents, relatives, mimes, actors, and shameful people, as an occasion and reward of sin?

Fourth, abbots and carnal monks sin gravely in the goods of the monastery when they heap them up too greedily, to the detriment of divine charity and their own salvation. For, inflamed by the torches of avarice, they think of nothing but profits and money; violating all feast days, they do not observe them; they always desire to increase temporal possessions; they do not notice the losses of charity. They grieve more over a dying beast than over a sinner perishing. Indeed, all disordered cloisterers are so blind that they consider avarice prudence and think riches are reform. They join field to field; they call gain piety; and where they see temporal goods abound, there they say the best discipline is observed. They refuse to spend even a few things for the common good of the order, because they have inwardly lost the order of true charity. They raise useless, curious, and harmful buildings on high; they buy splendid garments and ornaments less necessary for divine services; they clothe sacred things in gold; they leave Christ’s poor naked. This greedy curiosity does not befit monks, since we who are commanded to imitate the holy men of old are ordered to sell these things for the support of the poor. But the covetous monk and the avaricious abbot are as far from the purpose of holy religion as the devil is from heaven.

Fifth, carnal monks sin in the goods of the monastery when they do not repay them with prayers. All gladly receive the alms of the faithful,

--- Page 042 ---

but few render the aid of prayers to those who give. From this many citizens of the lowest region, when they have come to the order, serve not God but the belly. They desire that more food, drink, and clothing be given to them than necessity requires; and those who in the world could scarcely have bread and vegetables for food, now, having become monks, reject eggs and little fish. They importunately demand that large, varied, diverse, and delicate fish be given to them; murmuring with the children of Israel in the desert to their leader on account of the concupiscence of the flesh, they declare that they cannot eat eggs and peas; they always desire more delicate fare.

How the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered with respect to contempt of the world.

Article VII.

Few force their way into the kingdom of God, and the violent seize it. Therefore whoever desires to enter the heavenly kingdom through the weapons of holy obedience, whoever chooses to live for God under regular discipline, must do violence to the pleasures of the flesh and bravely despise the world with all its vanities. But carnal monks, citizens of the lowest region, who are badly ordered both toward God and toward themselves, are also badly ordered with respect to contempt of the world.

And first, those who are always burning with the fire of pleasure are infected by the concupiscence of the flesh. In food and drink they are found superfluous; in beds and baths they are delicate against the rules; they prefer the desires of the flesh to the salvation of the soul; they do not seek the sweetness of inner conversion. They nourish their flesh too delicately, and through this they burn continually with various concupiscences. For no one can truly despise the world who neglects to restrain the appetite of the flesh even in the smallest things. If you eat to satiety, you do not truly despise the world. For the first commandment imposed on those who despise the world is founded on abstinence. But if you have once filled your belly to satiety with food, you immediately fall either into lust or into grave sloth.

Therefore the concupiscence of the flesh draws back the mind of the disordered cenobite, so that he cannot despise the world.

Second, they are disordered with respect to despising the age because of the vain concupiscence of the eyes. For the monk who forgets the true honor of faithful souls takes an utterly curious delight in worldly pomp and ornaments. And indeed, the outward attire reveals what sort of person each one is hiding inwardly within himself. To carry rings around on the fingers, corals on the prayer-strings, scent-bottles in the hands, and folds in the cowls does not show a monk, but a soldier; not a humble cenobite, but a proud secular man. Such adornment among monks can please none but the reprobate, and is often found to have displeased even the proud. Woe to you, disordered cloisterers, who through a double concupiscence of the eyes descend into hell: while you wish to see in others what is not expedient, and you display yourselves to others to be seen otherwise than is fitting. What have you to do with the jewels of the age, what with the curiosities of the world? According to Bernard’s testimony, two beauties quickly make a masculine soul effeminate; these forensic refinements quickly unnerve the manly resolve. They are like children in rags who, because of a single reddish binding fastened to a torn sleeve, think themselves very beautiful and well adorned, and insolently mock others standing before them who are excellently clothed, as if they were naked. These pompous cloisterers see others walking in humble clothing, despising the world in a spirit of poverty, and on that account pleasing both God and men; but they themselves are despised by the wise and by princes, and sometimes are not even admitted to conversation. Seeing this, the proud, even if they grieve heavily as though at an injury, nevertheless utterly refuse to lay aside their arrogance.

Third, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered with respect to contempt of the age because of pride of life, for they are not only infected by vain complacencies of the flesh, but are also proud through swelling of mind. For all who follow the desires of the flesh vainly lift themselves above themselves; they despise others, however much better; they do not recognize equals as peers; they do not admit superiors. They rejoice that a great retinue of servants attends them; in

councils they strive to sit first; they desire to appear glorious to men; they walk about pompously and publicly. Inflated by a spirit of pride, they boast that their monasteries possess distinction from their foundation: wealthy in temporal goods, curious in jewels, buildings, and privileges. With uplifted heart they despise poor monks or abbots of humble cenobies; they are not ashamed to prefer themselves to their betters. The more they desire to hunt for glory in external things, the more they withdraw from true glory with the foolish virgins.

How the citizens of this lowest region are disordered with respect to penance.

Article viii.

Since man, born of woman, living for a short time and filled with many miseries, cannot remain long in the same state, it is necessary that, by reforming himself daily with all zeal, he turn himself toward his appointed end; for whoever is sent to live long in this world must present himself as changeable in the direction of virtue. Therefore whoever, placed in this mutability, desires to come to the joys of immutability should humbly recognize his frailty, should always flee after a fall into sin to the remedies of penance, and should refuse to lie in wickedness even for the first moment. For the life of monks must always consist savingly in penance, and must continually await the coming of the Judge with holy fear. For Jerome says: “A monk has the office not of one who teaches, but of one who laments, who either mourns for himself or for the world, and fearfully awaits the Lord’s coming.” But the disordered cloisterers of the lowest region despise this saving remedy; they consume the acceptable time in vanity; and because they do not fear to be badly ordered in all things, they also show themselves very disordered in doing penance.

First, indeed, they are badly ordered for penance by negligence of mind, while they neglect to understand how greatly sin displeases almighty God, whom they do not fear to contradict by the judgment of a depraved will. Just as night is opposed to day, so the sinner, turned away from divine goodness, is its adversary and opponent.

--- Page 045 ---
What a putrid corpse is wont to do in the sight of human beings, sin does in its own way, incomparably, in the sight of the angels. But carnal monks despise the honor of God, and therefore they do not notice the shamefulness of sin. Those who commit mortal sins almost as if licitly neglect to amend venial ones. For Scripture says: “He who neglects the smallest things will gradually fall.” Hence Bernard’s saying admonishes us: “Let no one, brothers, say in his heart: ‘These things are slight; I do not care to correct them. For no one can avoid greater sins if he has scorned to amend lesser ones.” Nothing is worse than carelessness of mind.

Second, carnal monks, lovers of the lowest region, are too disordered for penitence, because they do not notice the contagion of sin. For just as a healthy person is very often infected by a contagious disease, so a person is inclined from one sin into another. For one sin is always said to dispose a person to another, unless it is amended as quickly as possible through contrition. For the prophet says: “Jerusalem sinned a sin; therefore she has been made unstable.” And elsewhere: “He who is in filth, let him be filthy still.” We have shown sufficiently the contagion of this disease in the degrees of pride: for just as we have said that one always arises from another, so we perceive that through carelessness a person becomes liable to slip from one sin into another.

Third, carnal monks, disordered for penitence, contract a most wretched sickness through the habit of sin, when they fall into a pernicious blindness of mind. Every vice, when often committed, is held cheap, and through habit is judged lighter. Just as grace illuminates the mind, so the frequency of sin blinds reason. For it is written: “Blind the heart of this people and make their ears heavy, so that seeing they may not see and hearing they may not understand, lest they be converted and I heal them, says the Lord.” For when the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he is contemptuous. No sin is so great that through habit it does not seem slight and small.

Fourth, disordered cloisterers, blinded by the habit of evil,

--- Page 046 ---
do not come to the grace of true compunction. They approach confession only superficially, from habit; they do not abandon their depraved will; they do not truly take up the purpose of amendment. Woe to those who, buried in the habit of sin, do not fear the censure of a strict examination; who do not withdraw from blindness of mind through the remedy of contrition. For just as now they despise the long-suffering of God’s goodness, so in that terrible hour they will undergo the sentence of eternal death. What benefit can confession of the mouth have where there is no true contrition of the heart? Without contrition there is no saving confession, nor can there be contrition without the purpose of amendment. The sign of contrition is to be unwilling to sin any more.

Fifth, the disordered citizens of the lowest region, made despisers of almighty God through the habit of sinning, always fall into graver dangers of soul, since they always approach badly the most sacred mysteries of the body and blood of the Lord. They make a superficial confession from habit; they allow great beasts of vices to run about in their consciences. Then indeed, as if allied with hell, they do not fear God; they despise the paths of amendment of life; they think themselves well ordered; and they do not care with how many vices they are inwardly filled. Sometimes, sensibly affected by a fantastic devotion, they think the Holy Spirit is now present, and declare themselves beloved by God and holy. They also impudently thrust themselves upon the divine mysteries, and, not discerning the body and blood of the Lord, receive them to their own judgment and condemnation.

Finally, such people, communicating unworthily, are more gravely hardened, because, while they consider themselves very secure and think they are living well, they do not take care duly to cleanse their conscience from vices. Thus Judas the apostate, the evil of betrayal which earlier, as someone says, he feared to carry out against the Savior, after the morsel, when the devil had entered into him, did not fear to fulfill boldly and without shame. O how many citizens of this lowest region are carnal monks, lifted up, proud, envious, murmuring, and entangled in various sins, whose conscience is rarely or never made clean through true contrition, yet who do not fear to approach the most holy altar almost every day.

Concerning those who, being near to this lowest region, often fall back into it through inconstancy of mind.

We must depart from Egypt, from the dark region, and hasten by the middle way of reason to the highest summit of spirit. Therefore let us now at last approach the middle region of the soul, through which one comes successively to the highest. This region of the soul is quite fittingly called rational, since in it there is continual labor in the search for the highest truth.

The citizen of this middle region always strives to raise the fervor of his mind toward higher things, but the weakness of the flesh and the motion of the concupiscent storm draw him back toward the lowest things. Hence continual labor arises; hence sighs and groans arise, because the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation presses down the sense that thinks on many things. This is the narrow way that leads to life, and few walk through it. In this region violence is imposed upon the concupiscence of the flesh until the will is subjected to the spirit at its nod. For the Lord says in the Gospel: “The kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent seize it.” In this region the war of struggle is proclaimed to those who serve God, as the wise man bears witness: “My son,” he says, “when you approach the service of God, stand in fear, and prepare your soul for temptation.”

How great and how many temptations we undergo in this middle region through the difficulty of spiritual exercise we learn daily, while, driven in various ways, we are unable to remain constantly in fervor of spirit. Now the weakness of the flesh calls us back from the zeal for progress; now the temptation of the ancient enemy vexes us; now the deceitfulness of the world hinders us.

Whoever in this middle region does not always make progress toward better things soon falls into the negligences of his former life, and there incurs the danger of failing, where he has lost the appetite for progress. The second region of the soul begins, which is called rational, in which human beings are occupied amid many labors.

The second region of the soul, then, is called rational; in it the mind is exercised with continual labor in the inquiry into supreme truth. The farther it separates itself from the lowest things, the more it draws near to the highest. And this region is proposed as being, in certain ways, likened to the ethereal heaven, in which there is both the position of the stars and continual motion.

--- Page 052 ---
For just as that motion itself is always arranged in heaven by the order caused by the first mover, so continual labor is carried on in the middle region, so that each intellectual power may make progress in its own object and may in no way depart from the order naturally implanted in it. Such powers are three: memory, intellect, and will.

From these, memory gathers the likenesses and images of things, and extends itself toward what is arduous. The intellect, discerning those images, considers them, and here investigates the true. The will, however, embraces what has been approved by the intellect and set in order; and it is occupied with the known good. When these three powers of the soul rightly preserve their order, they lead the spirit in faith toward heavenly things.

This second region is compared to the ethereal heaven, in which, just as there is continual motion and the position of the stars, so in this middle region there is continual motion and labor against the contests of the vices. And just as the motion of the stars fulfills the office assigned to it by nature in its ordered arrangement, so too in this region the equity of right order is required, so that each power of the soul, while it is subject to its own object, may make progress toward the end ordained for it, so far as this can happen to wayfarers.

Yet none of those powers can be brought simply to fullness in this region, since in this pilgrimage no one is so perfect that he could not be more perfect in his own order. For memory, in its object, which is the arduous, even if it could behold all the gathered likenesses and images of all creatures, would not be satisfied unless it could apprehend their principle, which is God. But this is wholly impossible for this region, in which we can form no images or likenesses of God. The intellect also, the will’s discerning counselor, so to speak, is extended toward the true; but however much it considers its own object, it is not satisfied until it can apprehend all truth and come, without the opinion of fantastic speculation, to knowledge of the eternal, supreme, and infallible truth. But this perfection is possible for none of the wayfarers so long as he remains in the body of mortality. The will also be unable truly to rejoice until it comes to the unchangeable and highest good.

[segment 22]
--- Page 053 ---

Nor will it be able truly to rejoice, unless it comes to that unchangeable and highest good, which no instability of change overshadows. The highest truth and unchangeable good is God, who said to Moses: “Man shall not see me and live.” And the disciple of the highest truth says: “No one has ever seen God.” But the prophet too, addressing God, says: “Truly, you are a hidden God.” Although, then, the three aforesaid powers of the soul cannot perfectly comprehend their object in this life, none of them ought to cease from its office, lest it incur the danger of failing precisely where it has abandoned the practice of striving. Therefore the citizens of this middle region mortify evil affections within themselves; they adorn themselves with continual virtues; they direct all their works by a right intention; they depart least of all from the office of reason.

The citizens of this region are scholastic, speculative, and active men, devoted to various and indeed honorable arts: men who subject the sensual or carnal powers to reason; who do not neglect to cleanse these three powers of the soul from all illicit objects; who always raise their intention to the end of honorable usefulness; who reform the will, in act or in habit, toward its proper end. Therefore the cenobite who loves this purification and desires to ascend to the third region of the spirit must, while dwelling in the middle region, observe the following principal articles.

The summary of the truth of this middle region is expressed in seven principal articles.

Whoever in this middle region desires, according to the vow of his profession, to make progress should diligently take care to observe the following articles. If he neglects to keep them, he shows that he is still a citizen not of the middle but of the lowest region. For, as we said above, no one can remain in this middle region without a continual effort to advance; in it, not to have gone forward is to fail, and not to have ascended is to descend. Therefore the monk who, having overcome the dark region, has at last deserved, with the Lord’s help, to come to this middle one,

--- Page 054 ---

must take care not to abandon his effort to advance even for a little while, but always to stretch himself toward what lies further on. And he should remember the admonition of the wise man, who says: “Unless you constantly hold yourself in the fear of the Lord, your house,” that is, your conscience, “will quickly be overthrown.” For it is impossible for anyone to make progress in holy conduct if, through fear of the Lord, he neglects to renew daily his effort to advance. The effect of this renewal is shown to be rightly ordered when the truth of the articles of the middle region is duly observed. The first article, therefore, is the diligent intention of the cenobitic end; the second is the due restraint of the sensual powers; the third is the faithful cleansing of the powers; the fourth is the careful reform of the works of the lowest region; the fifth is the ordered institution of the true cenobitic progress; the sixth is the adaptation of the powers for entrance into the third region; and the seventh is the exemplary or experiential certainty of all the aforesaid matters.

On the continual and diligent pursuit of the cenobitic end.

Article I.

Since the end is first in the intention of the wise person, and since each thing in its own kind is judged more perfect the more closely it approaches its end, the monk who cultivates this middle region should always consider why he entered religious life. If he finds that his intention is rightly ordered, he should strive to continue it; but if he finds that it is corrupt, he should not neglect to reform it according to the rectitude of its due end.

But note that, in spiritual exercises, the end through which the virtue of monastic conduct is increased in merits is described as twofold. The first is charity, which is called the principal and ultimate end, to which all monastic exercises are ordered as to their first cause. Whatever good we do must always and chiefly be ordered to this end, because no work of virtue attains merit unless charity always establishes its proper foundation.

[segment 23]
--- Page 055 ---

“God,” says John, “is charity; and he who remains in charity remains in God, and God in him.” Moreover, everyone who remains in God does not sin. The second end, the dispositive end, is directed toward the first and principal end as though through some suitable means; and this is called the secondary or less principal end. The monk must always attend to this end, and through it order his exercises toward the ultimate end. For no one can reach the principal end of charity who neglects to observe the means.

This dispositive end toward the ultimate end, as it pertains to cenobites, according to John Cassian in the Conferences of the Fathers, is this: to cast away from oneself entirely all cares of the world; to mortify one’s own wills; to commit every disposition and care of oneself to the superior; and to take no thought for tomorrow. For the cenobite who wishes to make progress in this middle region must entirely take up the renunciation of his own will, and then resign himself totally into the hands of his superior, so that within himself he may be clean from every speck of his own will, and outwardly free from every secular occupation for serving the Lord in every way. Truly, unless our cenobite has arrived at this end, he is by no means a true monk, because he is still sowing corruption in corruptible flesh, and does not at all observe the virtue of his profession.

The renunciation of monastic self-will seems principally to consist in three things, without which no one can truly be said to have denied himself. First, the mortification of self-will requires in a monk the eradication of his own judgment, because whoever relies on his own judgment or counsel disdains humbly to be subject to another. For the root from which the defense of self-will arises is called adherence to one’s own counsel or judgment. Whoever, being placed in a cenobium, desires to cling to his own judgment sins gravely against the Rule as a usurper of self-will. For it is fitting that the cenobite live by another’s command rather than by his own, if he desires to come to eternal life through the merit of obedience. Truly, to love one’s own judgment is the greatest leprosy of the soul; according to Bernard, it is the more dangerous the more hidden it has been; and the more a person abounds in it,

--- Page 056 ---

the holier he seems to himself to be.

Second, the mortification of the will requires the emptying out of one’s own affections, because no one can remain ordered and truly subject to God amid adversities and comforts if he does not strongly restrain his carnal affections.

Third, for the mortification of self-will, that poverty is required by which the monk’s affections are not captured by visible things: food, clothing, and the rest; because whoever loves these things too much shows himself, against the Rule, to be a lover of his own pleasure. For the true cenobite of this middle region, whose duty it is to devote himself fervently to the teaching of holy humility, must gladly despise all worldly things for the love of God, and love absolutely nothing apart from God. If he has anything for use with the Rule permitting it, let him beware of setting the affection of his heart on it, being ready, at the decision of the shepherd and for the usefulness of the brethren, to resign all things with a peaceful heart. Let him always choose the simple and humbler things, give thanks to God in all things, and, placing his hope and sufficiency in him alone, cheerfully say: “In you, Lord Jesus, possessing all things, for your love I gladly despise all things; I shall take no care at all for transitory things.”

Therefore let our devout cenobite always direct the eyes of his faithful consideration toward this end of monastic institution, and never withdraw from the sight of it, because whoever in action forgets the ultimate end wanders like an inexperienced and blind sailor amid the storms of the sea, soon to perish. Let the faithful monk therefore imitate a sailor skilled through devotion: just as the sailor, always having the harbor before his eyes, strives with intent care to direct his voyage there, so the mind of a good person should always be directed to God as to its shore and ultimate end. Archers set up for themselves some fixed mark, and, lest their sight be scattered from the appointed point, they close one eye. Just as the arrow of the shooter is directed toward the mark, so the intention of the good monk is always ordered toward God; in this intention the eye of lower affection is also closed, lest the sharpness of higher contemplation be scattered through empty things. For it is necessary

[segment 24]
--- Page 057 ---

For one must close off the affection of earthly curiosity and the understanding of carnal wisdom, as though by not knowing them, if we desire always to raise the gaze of the mind toward its appointed end. And just as the eagle is said to look upon the sun with eyes unstruck by its glare, so the monk, the servant of God, contemplates without interruption the end of monastic institution.

As a prudent traveler, going on with delight, considers the lodging toward which he is heading, so the good cenobite, in everything he does, always pants with fervent desire chiefly toward his own end, which is God. But a foolish traveler is one who, set upon the road, forgets the lodging toward which he is heading, and, wandering, knowingly becomes entangled in the various hindrances of others. And the monk who does not carefully consider the end of his own destination is surely wandering blind upon the road.

Therefore the good monk, and a lover of his own salvation, does not neglect the striving for his own good in spiritual exercises, but, always exercising himself in holy works with affection toward higher things, raises it up to God. Good striving is this: to omit nothing of the things God has commanded; for love of God, to obey the pastor humbly in all things; always to lift up an affection drawn away from earthly things to the memory of heavenly things; and, through continual exercise of spiritual conduct, daily to renew his holy purpose. Whatever good the devoted cenobite does, let him always offer it to almighty God with faithful attention; let him despise all earthly things with a strong spirit; let him desire to please God alone.

Let the intention of the cenobitic life always be faithful, so that it wishes to desire nothing other than the praise and honor of God. Let its attention be diligent, so that it knows how to summon all the senses of the body and the affections of the soul to the service of God, in order that it may serve its Lord faithfully and seek his glory, not its own.

On the Due Restriction of the Sensual Powers. Article II.

--- Page 058 ---

For I think there is no one among monks who is unaware how useful and necessary it is for us to restrain the powers of sensual passions. Indeed, whoever desires to attain the due end of the regular life, which we have described in this region, must subdue his inordinate natural habits and motions with prudent constancy, because no one is truly able to resign himself to another's command if he does not strongly restrain the powers of sensual passions.

For one who desires to make progress must, with constant spirit, abandon the motions and prudence of the flesh, and show himself a son of divine grace, not of flesh and blood. For Scripture says: If you live according to the rule of the flesh, you will die. Indeed, those who sow in the flesh will also reap corruption from the flesh. But flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God. But what does the Savior say to us? “A man's enemies,” he says, “are those of his own household.” And who are these household members, unless the sensual powers that oppose the spirit?

For concupiscibility and irascibility, our enemies, stir up every evil within us against the spirit, and continually disturb the house of conscience with various passions. For as long as these sensual powers carry out their natural motions and courses in a person, they do not permit him to ascend to the freedom of the spirit. Concupiscibility delights in the pleasures of the sense of the flesh; and, on the contrary, the irascible power detests the body's sufferings. Therefore, as long as this war of the flesh is waged in a person, the monk is considered restless, carnal, and a dweller in the lowest region. Whoever, then, desires to come to eternal life must strongly restrain these inordinate powers of the senses in all things.

Therefore the faithful cenobite strives to direct the concupiscible power toward sufferings that are acceptable to God, and to reform all his affections according to God's good pleasure. If he has done this, he will pray confidently to the Lord: My soul has longed to desire your justifications at all times. And that other saying:

[segment 25]
--- Page 059 ---

he will often cry out sweetly, weeping: “As the deer longs for the springs of waters, so my soul longs for you, O God.” Then, cleaving to God, let him learn prudently to set the irascible power against sensual delights, and diligently to reform it toward hatred of vices. Thus the soul, ordered to its own principle, will not fear to undergo any hard things for the love of God, and through the irascible power it will begin to resist all carnal desires strongly. And then the devout cenobite, with his spirit lifting itself up, will cry: “My soul refused to be comforted.” And that saying of the Apostle: “I have counted all things as dung.” For the soul that truly loves God despises everything except him.

From these two powers, moreover, every disordered affection is said to arise, and good affections likewise are generated from these by opposition. For joy and hope take their beginning from the concupiscible power, and the irascible power gives birth to sorrow and fear. Concerning these Saint Bernard says thus: “The whole heart of a human being is drawn by four affections; if we bring them back to God, the whole heart of the human being is converted.”

Article III. Concerning the faithful cleansing of the powers.

But we cannot sufficiently restrain the sensual powers unless we also faithfully cleanse the intellectual powers. For this cleansing great and continual diligence is needed, since the cenobite who is dissolved by the torpor of negligence is never cleansed from vicious affections. For whoever sows with corrupted powers brings back nothing other than corruption from his errors. Therefore the cenobite who inhabits this middle region must correct his interior powers from every disorder, if he wishes to remain constantly in the pursuit of making progress. These intellectual powers must be cleansed in the following way.

First, the intellect must be purged from every imagining of empty likenesses, so that,

--- Page 060 ---

rescuing itself from material things of every kind, it may rise upward to its own principle without any imagined likeness, and, prudently intent upon its own object, may turn itself wholly to the highest reason of truth.

Then let our faithful cenobite not neglect to strip his memory of every occupation with an illicit matter. Let him admit no thoughts of a shameful or less useful kind; and let him strive to repel the phantasms of irrational passions and empty meditations, and, insofar as it can be done, to think nothing foolish.

The will too must be purged from every disordered affection, so that it wills nothing except what right reason suggests ought to be willed, and rejects nothing except what reason, according to the judgment of truth, forbids it.

This restraint of the powers and cleansing of the faculties, without which, as we say, it is granted to absolutely no one, is obtained only through great, discerning, and continual diligence, strong in the struggle. We know that the kingdom of God suffers violence, and no one can subject the enemy to himself unless he has resolved to fight against him fiercely and perseveringly. From the blessed bishop Augustine as witness, we have learned that a person does not die well unless he has first died to vices and desires. But no one truly dies to these among human beings unless, sowing in tears, he resists his own wills through continual labor. Therefore the mind must be armed with saving doctrines, and the strength of patience must be assumed in all things; for just as one who is unarmed and impatient of labor cannot overthrow an armed enemy, so the monk without spiritual arms and a certain continual force of fighting cannot cleanse his intellectual powers. Although the arms of spiritual warfare are many in one’s progress, three in particular must be taken up by the monk.

First, indeed, resoluteness is necessary for the cenobite in everything he does, so that he may boldly undertake things strongly opposed to the flesh, and may in no way grow lukewarm in his holy purpose. For spiritual exercises are hard and utterly contrary to the flesh, in

[segment 26]
--- Page 061 ---

in which he cannot persevere steadfastly if he disdains to put on the strongest arms of vigor. Therefore, if you desire to subject all your affections to the spirit, if you wish to fight successfully against the vices of the flesh, take care to make for yourself a most prudent violence, and to arm yourself bravely against all things.

Next, a certain holy severity is set before you to assume, through which all contrary and adverse things of this world are borne as virtually nothing. For whoever has once truly assumed this benign severity of spirit for the love of God and the desire of heaven will from then on fear no adversities of the flesh, but will be so inwardly suffused with contemplation of divine reward that he will seem to take more delight in adversities than in prosperous things that befall him.

But severity is by no means pleasing to the weaker unless it has been somewhat tempered by the sweetness of benignity. Therefore, in consequence, that benignity also must be assumed by the soldier, so that when, tempted or even wounded in battle through the weakness of the flesh, he sometimes falters, he may soon rise again through penitence to fight more bravely. For the soldier of Christ, if he has fallen in battle, must rise again through contrition and the other remedies of penitence, as often as he is about to fight again, until at last, by God's mercy, he can overcome the adversary. If the just man falls seven times in a day and rises again, how shall the sinner, now penitent, not hope for pardon? Rise again to fight, O soldiers, as often as you have fallen when the adversary has prevailed. Always renew the campaign more bravely from the beginning, so that by perseverance you may at last overcome the enemy whom you cannot succeed in destroying by strength. Do not despair of victory, O faithful cenobite, because, often conquered by temptation, you have fallen into the snare of vice. Do not lie prostrate for long because you feel yourself weak, but rise again at once as often as you have fallen, and trust that you have a merciful Lord, by whose help you will at last be victorious against the adversary. For no one becomes supreme all at once, nor is victory over the enemy granted immediately to inexperienced soldiers, lest, to their ruin, they be cast heavily into pride.

--- Page 062 ---

A possession acquired with difficulty is always dearer, and the good that is acquired laboriously is guarded with greater diligence. For so long does the loving Father, God, permit us, conquered by the adversary's temptations, to fall at intervals into the pit of sin, until, conscious of our weakness, we learn to hope in no powers of our own, but only in his grace. But you, man, who have often broken your resolution by sinning, never delay to rise again; for even if you cannot perfectly subject the enemy to yourself, it will nevertheless be good for you if the last day can find you standing and fighting, even after often repeated ruin. Yet this counsel does not release those to whom God's mercy has granted virtue and grace for defeating the adversary. Nevertheless, let him who stands take care lest he fall.

On The Reform Of The Works Of The First Region

Article 2.

Now indeed, as we taught at the end of the first treatise, this middle region borders on the lowest region from its lowest part; if the cenobite is at all negligent and more remiss in it, he at once falls back again, without any middle ground, into the former region. Therefore whoever desires to advance and remain in this region should always, with attentive vigilance, be zealous to reform the works of the lowest region. And, lest we be prolix, whoever desires to exercise this reform in himself should assume, in this region, works contrary to the first eight articles. For, as is taught by the tradition of physicians, opposites are cured by opposites.

Therefore the faithful monks who are citizens of this middle region are first commanded to consider this: that one should strive to order himself toward God before all things. This ordering is said to be done well if God is loved with the whole heart, according to the law, above all things.

Next, the devout cenobite should understand himself as ordered toward his prelate

[segment 27]
--- Page 063 ---
he should show himself humble toward him and pay him reverence; he should strive to love him with sincere charity, and to observe according to the Rule everything he commands.

Likewise, the rector of souls should take care to walk in an ordered way toward the cenobites: he should show himself as a shepherd to the sheep, not as a hireling; he should not neglect the salvation of the soul entrusted to him; he should not set temporal things before spiritual things. He should hate vices, love the brothers, and show himself exemplary in all good things.

Next, the brothers should cleave to one another like members of one head; they should love one another in the true charity of God and not withdraw from good zeal. For all of us who, by faith in Christ, trust to come to eternal life by persevering in good works are members of Christ, the supreme head. But whoever pursues his brother with evil zeal or hatred and other evil affections first separates himself from the head, and then does injury to Christ.

Consequently, each one should show himself ordered toward himself: for the love of Christ he should humbly submit himself to all, prefer others to himself through humility, and believe himself more unworthy than all monks.

In the use of the monastery’s goods, they should always beware of exceeding the institution of the Rule, but should strive everywhere to show themselves ordered and faithful in all things. They should have nothing in private contrary to the abbot’s will, and should admit no pleasure into the use of goods.

Then faithful cenobites should take care to order themselves according to the institution of the Rule toward contempt of the world, so that they love nothing in this world, but truly despise all things for the love of God.

They should not neglect to order themselves most diligently toward penance; for humble confession of their excesses they should often have recourse, since just as no one is believed to live in this world without sin, so penance is judged necessary for all, even monks. Therefore the good cenobite should always learn to have recourse to penance through the practice of compunction, humbly accuse himself of his excesses in chapter, gladly perform the penance enjoined, and faithfully amend himself in all things.

On the ordered instruction of true monastic progress and the degrees of humility.

The fifth article.

The first virtue of a monk is humility, and the gravest crime in a cenobite is pride. For a monk who is proud with a swollen mind becomes an imitator and servant of the devil, and is stripped of all virtues, if he had any, because no virtue can remain in a mind in which pride happens to reside. Therefore the cenobite who desires to advance in this middle region should learn to order himself toward the true progress of monastic instruction through humility, because no one truly attains humility unless he dwells with zeal upon spiritual things. True progress in regular instruction, excluding every adulterous muse, arises from the degrees of humility, which, through their roots, are set down as twelve in the Rule.

The roots of this progress come from the study of sacred reading, which gradually leads a person to the fear of the Lord. From reading arises spiritual meditation, from which holy compunction is generated. The more useful manner of cenobitic meditation consists in the remembrance of the Lord’s Passion and in consideration of the benefits of God. But those who, surpassing this middle region, arrive at the tranquility of spiritual peace make their meditations more sublime through contemplation of the divine majesty and excellence. We shall speak of these in their own place.

These two roots receive their increase from the watering of continual prayer, and with the strengthening of a very firm plant they grow strong. For no meditation advances without prayer, and study without it will not save.

From these three roots of spiritual progress arises the fear of the Lord, which is the first degree of humility; from it the other degrees are brought forth, succeeding in order. But monks who neglect the saving study of the Scriptures and holy meditations together with continual prayers by no means arrive at this first degree of humility. But those who [reach] the first degree

[segment 28]
--- Page 065 ---
of humility cannot ascend, when will he be able to reach the others? For the second is never ascended if the first is neglected. Therefore whoever neglects one ascends none.

The study of sacred reading must not be undertaken for the vanity of the world, nor for the pleasure that seduces, because reading never advances toward compunction when delight in the flesh or in vanity corrupts it. A monk cannot attain devotion through the study of sacred reading unless he strives to change all curiosity into a simple spirit. Therefore whoever desires to advance from the reading of Scripture to compunction, whoever chooses to ascend to humility through the roots of fear, must not choose just any material for reading, but only that which can most greatly stir the mind to the love of God. For this purpose the reading of the conduct of the saints seems to me sufficiently suitable, because the mind of one who lies low is often raised up to the love of God when the life of his servants is recalled to memory. We also gladly admit the miracles and revelations of the torments or joys of separated souls, in conformity with the Catholic faith, by which we have found that sluggish hearts are often stirred to compunction. Finally, the passions of the holy martyrs stir up in no small measure the fervor of devotion; in them, when we bring back to mind the unconquerable love of Christ, we are pierced by the example of such constancy, and, as though touched by a certain fire of love, we sigh over our imperfection.

The material of meditation is taken from reading, and as the former is, so the latter will be. The shameful songs of poets, the curious teachings of philosophers, and the swollen writings of rhetoricians do not stir up in monks the fervor of devout compunction, but rather choke what has been conceived. Therefore, if you wish to persist in good meditations, do not occupy your mind with readings other than holy and devout ones. Indeed, grapes are not gathered from thorns, nor is compunction born from the study of vanity. Let the person who studies for knowledge of this world read those things that suit him for learning; but let the one who seeks compunction gather those things

--- Page 066 ---
that kindle the soul to the love of God through fear.

Therefore let frequent reading take its beginning from the last things that await us; then let the deeds of the holy fathers be read, so that the memory of future things may shake the mind into the fear of God, and the exemplary conduct of the saints may kindle the desire to imitate them. For the wise man says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” The conduct of the holy fathers is set before us as an example, and the destruction of the wicked is recounted to us for our instruction, so that virtue, rewarded, may move our desire, and wickedness, punished, may forbid license to sin. Therefore let the devout cenobite recall to memory the examples and virtues of the saints; let him consider not the brief pleasure of the wicked, but their eternal torment, so that by fearing unhappiness he may deserve to escape it, and by devoting himself to virtues obtain that everlasting joy.

The distinct condition of the good and the wicked is no light matter, since in each case it is described as truly enduring, and is ended by no limit. O eternity, always to be feared by all mortals: after this life has been completed, in it there is no fear for the righteous, and no hope of deliverance for the wicked. Let anyone who can think what eternity is, where there is no end, imagine a limit if he dares; and when he finds nothing in eternity except eternity, let him understand that all the time in which he lives in this world is nothing. Indeed, once the spirit has been cast out from the flesh, it is necessarily compelled to enter this abyss of eternity; and the condition which it has once obtained according to its merits it will bear without end, nor will it be changed into another. For the person who dies good in the charity of God will receive eternal life according to his merits. But the person who is evil and has once departed from this life without the reconciliation of repentance will remain with the demons in hell, to be tormented without end. From these meditations servile fear arises in the mind, and through it one gradually comes to love.

So, in order that we may be able to lead the cenobite successively through these roots to charity, it seems good to us to explain briefly, in order, the degrees of humility described in the Rule.

The first degree of humility, then, is described by Saint Benedict in the Rule as the fear of God, which is called the beginning of wisdom. Fear, however, is handed down with a threefold distinction: servile, namely, initial, and filial, which is also called chaste. From this threefold distinction three roots are said to depend, according to which the beginner in the cenobitic life ought to order his meditations. For each root has its own proper meditations; and when one is rightly disposed, another and better one is always generated.

From servile fear, then, when it is taken up not merely in a servile way but prudently and virtuously, there arise meditations on horrors and punishments. By restraining the soul from sin, these gradually introduce filial fear. For when, by consideration of punishments, the mind is turned to horror, boldness in sinning is checked, because the servant who fears blows keeps his lord’s commands for a long time. This servile fear by itself does not suffice for earning the fatherly inheritance; nevertheless, as an initial effect, it disposes one toward filial fear, which is sufficient.

Initial fear is placed midway between the two, and is easily changed from servile into filial fear. This initial fear receives meditations on the last things, and begins to fear both incurring punishments and losing the sweet homeland. And this is set forth more broadly in the Rule, where its roots also are described.

Filial fear, however, embraces its meditations in love, since it fears nothing except that the beloved father be offended. Concerning this fear the wise man says: “The fear of the Lord is glory and boasting, gladness and a crown of exultation. The fear of the Lord will delight the heart and will give gladness and joy for length of days.” And again: “It will be well at the end for the one who fears God, and on the day of his death he will be blessed.” This fear is also called chaste, because it detests evil not from fear of punishments but from love alone.

From this threefold fear we see three breadths of spiritual exercises arising around the degrees of humility, so that individual exercises correspond to the individual fears. For one kind of fear

--- Page 068 ---

namely servile fear, performs its exercises in one way; initial fear persists in virtues in another way; and filial fear proceeds in its works in a much different way. For just as affections come to the heart in various ways, so the exercises of one kind of fear are carried out in different ways. The publican, mindful of his own crimes, feared the sentence of the judge in one way; Jerome said that he heard the trumpet summoning the dead to judgment in another way. And the holy Gospel says of the former: “Standing far off, the publican would not lift his eyes to heaven, but striking his breast he said, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner.’” But blessed Jerome said: “Whether I drink or eat, I always think I hear that trumpet sounding in my ears: ‘Arise, you dead; come to judgment.’” The compunction of each arises from fear, but each is not generated from the desire of one and the same affection. For the publican, terrified by the guilt of his sins and by the sentence of the just judge, was afraid; and therefore, pierced with compunction, he said, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” Jerome, however, was shaken by fear not from consciousness of guilt, but, grounded in charity and considering himself a useless servant, he reverenced the abyss of divine justice.

The second degree is to leave behind one’s own will for the love of God, and to love one’s own desire in nothing contrary to the Rule. This degree arises from the first ascent of humility and lifts one up to the third. For one who fears God and truly fears being separated from him and undergoing punishments hates nothing more than his own will. The faithful cenobite knows that every sin arises from self-will alone, and therefore he detests it as the gate of hell. For that blessed man spoke truly: “Take away self-will, and there will no longer be hell.” Nothing burns in hell except self-will; nothing loses the grace of God except voluntary fault. Therefore whoever truly fears God and fears losing his grace begins to fear his own will as the ambusher of the soul. Consideration of the danger of self-will makes the cenobite despise his own desire and deny himself. And this denial of self-will and of one’s own desire is accomplished in three ways, as will be said in what follows.

And first, indeed, he denies himself to himself who strives to live according to the teaching of eternal wisdom, and not according to his own impulses, and to obey Christ in all things as he counsels humility. Therefore let the Savior say to the cenobite: If you wish to deny yourself and follow me, turn away your eyes so that they may not see harmful things, and your ears so that they may not hear vain things; and in place of the sweet things of this world, take up bitter things. Learn to despise the delights of your body, to love tranquility and peace of heart, to receive all tribulations for love of me patiently and gladly, and to desire to be regarded by all as vile and contemptible. Accordingly learn to break your own wills in all things, to mortify the desires of the flesh, to despise all earthly things, and, as far as it can be done, to conform your life with all your strength to the way of life of your Savior. For whoever says that he remains in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. Whoever has done these things has denied himself well.

Second, the denial of one’s own will is rightly accomplished when the advantages of others are preferred to one’s own. For the holy Apostle says: Not seeking the things that are their own, but those that belong to others. For according to Bernard’s judgment, the soul is laid down for the brothers as often as their advantages are preferred, for love of God, to one’s own utilities.

Third, the resignation of one’s own will receives the fullness of perfection when the cenobite, compunct and wholly bound over to God, desires out of love to serve him freely, without regard for reward. And this most holy resignation of one’s own will makes a person very near to God, and always rests in his love.

But just as this resignation of oneself receives its foundation from the second degree of humility, so it necessarily requires in all things a complete inward resignation to the will of God. For the Lord says in the Gospel: I have not come to do my own will, but that of the Father who sent me. Therefore perfect resignation is that by which the human will is wholly subjected to the divine will and is strengthened by complete love.

For the Lord himself taught us to pray in this way: Your will be done, as in heaven and on earth. And there is that saying of the psalmist: Into your hands I commend my spirit. Nowhere in this world is one lived more securely than where the human will is perfectly subjected to God.

Indeed, that perfect resignation of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his will to God, shows without intermediary the straight road to the highest good. Here there will be the highest tranquility of mind; the greatest security is present in the heart, because the soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will be able to fear no adversities of this world any longer, except sin alone. Whatever adversity rushes in upon it, with the Lord’s help it has learned to overcome; whatever advantage it desires, it understands that it possesses in the Lord alone. It despises all adverse things for love of him; it has placed all good things in his love. For that devout man spoke prudently: Dismiss everything, and you will find everything; abandon desire, and you will find true peace.

Finally, so that this resignation of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it implores, as I have said, the assistance of a threefold support.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance, so that the will, once denied, may never be taken back. For that holy Bernard says to those who despise this support: Why do you again involve yourselves with yourselves, when once, by mature deliberation, you handed over your will to us? For the monk who has once handed his will over to God into the hands of the abbot cannot thereafter take it back without the crime of sacrilege.

The second thing required for the perfect resignation of the will is that it never be slackened. Hence in the Apocalypse the Lord says to the lukewarm soul: I have a few things against you, because you have abandoned your first charity, that is, your fervor.

Third, resignation of the will will be perfected if it is never broken off. For the Savior says: Whoever perseveres to the end will be saved. Therefore, when you see a cenobite restless or moved by anger, know that the whole weight of the temptation proceeds from lack of resignation.

The third degree of humility is to submit oneself in all obedience to a superior for the love of God, and to show oneself an imitator of Christ in deeds, not only in words. Concerning him the holy Apostle says: “He became obedient unto death, even death on the cross.” This degree takes its beginning from the second, and raises its ascent to the third. For just as the second mortifies one’s own will by denying it, so this one lays the foundation of holy work through humble subjection. The former wholly subjects itself to the divine will; the latter, for the love of God, also obeys human beings.

The keeping of one degree without observance of the other is not sufficient in a monk; nor is the will of the cenobite subjected to the divine ordering if it refuses to obey the command of a superior. Therefore the true cenobite places all his joy in the virtue of holy obedience. He considers it the greatest gain to fulfill in all things the will of his shepherd; he believes that he incurs grave damage to his soul if he finds that he has committed even a little thing outside obedience. He always considers with faithful devotion what the Lord says in the Gospel: “He who hears you hears me; and he who despises you despises me.” Hence that blessed Bernard says to the monks: “Whatever a man commands in God’s place must be received no otherwise than if God himself were commanding it in person.” Therefore let the true monk prefer this subjection of obedience to every sacrifice, do absolutely nothing against its integrity, and constantly say within himself what is written: “Obedience is better than sacrifices.” Hence the Rule also says: “Therefore all things are to be done with the will of the abbot.”

But this degree, as we have said, arises from the second; for whoever perfectly abandons his own will out of fear of God now seeks nothing else in this world than that will in all things. But because God speaks once and does not repeat the same thing, weak man cannot know the will of the Most High; therefore the devout man commits himself

into the hands of his prelate. Let the cenobite therefore obey according to the Rule, and without doubt he fulfills the will of God through the vicar of Christ.

Concerning obedience, one must speak rather strictly according to the Gospel and the monastic Rule. For although mutual obedience among brothers is good, yet it must not be preferred to the command of a superior. Monks are indeed commanded to render obedience to one another discreetly, and, while preserving the commands of superiors, to anticipate one another in mutual services. And to bring forward an example from our own custom: obedience is owed to all according to order. If a brother calls upon you, obey willingly. If another senior commands you, leave aside the command of the first and obey this one. Again, if the prior has interposed his own accustomed command, all mutual obedience among the brothers will cease. But if the precept of the abbot intervenes, no obedience to any other person will have force to the contrary. For he is believed to act first in the monastery in the place of Christ, and therefore he is preferred to all.

There are, moreover, seven gradual circumstances of the virtue of holy obedience, which blessed Bernard described. The first degree of obedience is to obey willingly: that is, to have no will of your own at all, but to believe that your shepherd’s good pleasure is wholly your own. The second is to obey simply: that you do neither more nor otherwise than has been commanded you, nor omit anything by doing less. The third is to obey cheerfully: that you show serenity in your face and sweetness in your voice. The fourth is to obey swiftly: that the hand in the work not be slower than the command in the voice. The fifth is to obey manfully: that one not withdraw his strength from the command, nor pretend to be weak and unequal to the precepts. The sixth is to obey humbly: not proudly, not arrogantly, not contentiously; for unless obedience is humble, it will bring back not reward but punishment. The seventh is to obey perseveringly, because obedience is not true if it is interrupted before completion; and only he who perseveres to the end is rewarded.

The fruit of obedience is everlasting and is bounded by no end. Almighty God obeys our prayers only so far as we obey our superiors. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, about persistence in prayer, for Christ does not hear a man praying if that man despises Christ's vicar in the monastery. Humility raises prayer up; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the Fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, and the finder of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, closes the gates of hell, becomes the companion of angels, and is held to be the food of all the saints. But the monk who casts off subjection to obedience runs toward death.

The fourth degree of humility is patience in adversities. Our holy father Benedict speaks of it in the Rule: if a monk under obedience, in hard and contrary things, or even in any injuries inflicted on him, embraces patience with a silent conscience. This degree too takes its beginning from the third, because no one is truly to be called obedient if he has been found impatient. For, as that divine Chrysostom says, one who has been subject to the enemy cannot be acceptable to the king; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been enslaved to passionate, impatient flesh. No one will be able to conquer a fierce enemy unless he has learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, because no virtue is rightly perfected if patience abandons it. For, as blessed Gregory testifies, it is of greater merit to receive adversities patiently for God's sake than to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: Whoever believes that tribulation has come upon him for his sins more fully calls forth God's mercy toward himself for doing good. Therefore let the cenobite learn to set the virtue of patience against all things that happen to him, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. He who endures conquers; he who murmurs is overcome.

In three things the virtue of patience is especially necessary and is rewarded with the highest prizes. In insults of words

patience preserves the purpose of humility in a prudent man who loves God; in the removal of possessions and temporal advantages it receives increase; and in bodily injury, with a silent conscience, it preserves the discipline of virtue. Consider now, faithful soul, how your most loving Savior Jesus most patiently endured these three things for your redemption; and if you believe that you love him, do not be reluctant, for love of him, to tolerate similar things with patience.

From the three degrees immediately preceding we establish one summary of the cenobitic end, which, as seems reasonable to us, we call the threefold degree. For by three degrees, namely the second, third, and fourth, the end of cenobitic formation is accomplished, and the renunciation of pleasure is first brought to completion. For when patience is joined to the earlier renunciation of pleasure and to obedience, the preserver of virtues, every pleasure and concern of the body is mortified. Whoever has been worthy to ascend this threefold degree has fulfilled the Lord's command, where he says in the Gospel: Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, through the second degree of humility; and take up his cross, through the third; and follow me, through the fourth.

The fifth degree of humility is the true confession of sins. The faithful cenobite is said to ascend into it if he confesses to his abbot, with humble trust, all evil thoughts that come into his heart, or any evil things committed in secret. Indeed, if anyone sins in the preceding threefold degree through impatience at the adversary's suggestion, in this degree he is restored through contrition and humble confession. For no one is found so perfect that he does not contract some stain from earthly living, as Scripture says: In many things we all offend. For no one is clean from sin, not even an infant whose life upon the earth is one day. Necessarily, therefore, this degree follows immediately after the fourth, and in it all negligences of the preceding degrees are corrected. True confession lifts up the soldier who falls in battle, heals the man wounded by the enemy, and strengthens the weak and faint-hearted. Whoever, therefore, would stand in battle and conquer

It is certain to me, and much proved in ruin, that the cenobite who refuses to keep this degree hands himself over to the enemy to be greatly conquered by temptations.

The sixth degree of humility arises from true confession: that the monk should judge himself worthless and unworthy for all things. According to the Rule we believe him to be a true monk whom we see content, with humble patience, with every vileness and extremity in food and clothing. Vileness, however, is referred to ordinary things held in common, while extremity is placed in abject and more grievous inconveniences. For the former gladly embraces, with patience, poorer garments, foods, and whatever bodily necessities; the latter delights in all things: serving the sick, the diseased, lepers, and the abject; being confounded, degraded, and despised. Therefore the monk who is truly dead to the flesh and the world should be content with every vileness and extremity; he should show himself a faithful worker in all things; and, because of the corruption of nature by which he offends in many things, he should believe himself worthless and unworthy.

Indeed, whatever good he does in this world, however holy and religiously he lives in this age, can be sufficient for no one to merit eternal life unless God's great mercy deigns to come to his aid. We receive the greatest things for the least: we await the kingdom of God, not as owed to us from our merit, but as promised from grace. For the holy Apostle says: “The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that will be revealed in us.” But if we receive the justice of God without mercy, we are most worthy of hell. We all incur wrath by sinning, but we carry back love from grace. No one is saved except by grace; no one

is damned except through justice. Yet meanwhile, in order that we may obtain the grace of God, he requires the efforts of our good will; without that grace nothing in us works toward merit. For although no one is holy by his own powers, nevertheless no one does good well unwillingly. Thus the grace of God and the freedom of the will stand in relation to each other, and one without the other cannot suffice for saving a human being; rather, each must necessarily come together for the operation of virtue. Therefore, since without the grace of God we can do nothing good, we ought to consider ourselves worthless and unworthy for all things, so that, while we submit ourselves to God in humility, we may always obtain greater things. And therefore this sixth degree is of great merit for confession, and the single foundation of all the preceding ones. For as long as the cenobite remains in this degree of humility, he will stand firm in the earlier ones.

The seventh degree of humility is not only to believe oneself, from the inmost affection of the heart, lower than all, but also to desire with a simple mind that others believe and say this about him. This condition of holy humility sets one on high, and in this time is loved by few monks. The first condition of this profound humility seems to consist in this: that the monk ought from the heart to consider himself lower than all. The second is that he should rejoice to be considered vile and abject by all, and should desire this from the inmost affection of the heart without pretense. The third condition is placed in a higher dwelling: namely, that the monk, from the same affection, should delight in being treated most vilely and trampled down by all. Rooted in this humility, the cenobite loves every reproach and insult; he does not grow indignant when he is despised and used most vilely, because, humbly submitting his whole self in the love of God, he places himself low, and hastens to insults as to banquets with the greatest cheerfulness. Therefore, cenobite, be cautious in disparaging another; be cautious in judging another, if you desire to escape eternal death. For who are you, to judge another’s servant? He stands to be judged by his God alone.

Therefore the monk who, in this respect, reckons himself and his works as nothing gives assent to the truth, because he fears to incur the vice of falsehood if he were to believe himself to be something great. And therefore, withdrawing from himself, he fails in himself, and places his being, life, and power in God alone. Second, this affection arises from hope of acquiring greater grace, because when a person, through humility, fails in himself, he comes nearer to the grace of God. Third, the affection of this humility takes its beginning from love of God and receives continual increase in that love. Since we have said that the other five degrees of humility also pertain to the custody of the inner man, let us briefly explain them too. The eighth degree of humility, therefore, is set down in the Rule in this way: if the monk does nothing except what the common Rule of the monastery or the examples of the elders encourage. In this degree, singularity in exterior conduct contrary to the Rule is forbidden to monks; but interior singularity of greater charity and humility is forbidden to no one. Therefore let no one prefer his own inventions to the holy Rule; let no one set his own customs before the examples of the elders, because it is not enough to stand in the seventh degree if one does not keep the Rule of common life.

The ninth degree of humility follows from the Rule, by which excessive talkativeness is forbidden. It is fitting for monks to keep silence, especially when they sit in the midst of their elders. And indeed, he who speaks without being questioned is deservedly considered a fool.

The tenth degree of humility forbids ease in laughter and levity; for whoever delights in laughter is shown to be foolish and alien to the fear of the Lord.

The eleventh degree of humility enjoins modesty and seriousness upon the monk, so that when opportunity and permission to speak are granted to him, he may speak with humble gravity, without laughter, and as a monk. Let the monk’s speech be rare, brief, and reasonable, because it is not fitting for him who wishes to please the Most High to say useless things.

The twelfth degree of humility teaches the monk to order the exterior man in appearance and bearing from a humble heart, and to demonstrate by exterior conduct also that he is devout inwardly. And although this degree is last in the order of all, nevertheless in practice it begins progress, and in use it is first. Therefore Bernard, describing these twelve degrees of humility, begins from this one and ascends to the first, descending, so to speak, by order. For no one easily advances inwardly to compunction of heart and the fear of the Lord if he does not order his exterior conduct.

On the Due Adaptation of the Powers for Entry into the Third Region.
Article VI.

When, through the degrees of humility, the powers of the soul, especially of the inner person, have been fundamentally cleansed and reformed, it is now said to be very necessary in monks that they be more devoutly adapted for entry into the third region. This diligence in striving toward higher things is necessary for cenobites, as has been said, because where the effort to make progress is laid aside, acquired virtue is wholly dissipated.

No one is permitted to stand still in this second region; rather, we are all commanded to ascend to the third through continual effort. Indeed, this continual motion casts every slothful and lukewarm person back into the first region of the flesh, but it gently and without violence always impels the humble, who are fervent with desire, toward the third. Therefore whoever has deserved to obtain from the Lord, through humility, the reformation of his powers is already near the third region of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it powerfully, he must arrange the powers of his soul most diligently, adapting them to their several operations. The intellect is then said to be rightly adapted for entry into the third region when it is illuminated, according to its capacity, for knowing the highest truth. The memory is arranged for the same end when it is fully absorbed in holding and cherishing the highest truth. The will, moreover, is well adapted for the entry of the spirit if it is disposed to love the highest good, which is God.

But in order that the cenobite may be able to attain the purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, dying to the world, the mind, pacified from every inner and outer disturbance, should rest, so that it may remain buried within the bosom of divine love and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union. This rest of mind is reached by four degrees, which are rooted in a good will in four ways.

The first degree of a good will lays the beginning of virtue; it is called an upright will.

--- Page 082 ---

For the soul which, consenting to the law of God, is subjected to God through an upright will is ordered toward the passage into virtue, even if it still seems weak. With the flesh resisting, virtue is perfected in this, and the will is proved upright by the force of the spirit. Yet a will is often called upright and good even when it is found to be less strong. It cannot always accomplish the good that it loves; the evil that it hates, it grieves to have committed through weakness. Through the weakness of the flesh it is very near to the lowest region, but by uprightness of intention and by the effort to make progress it is continually kindled toward the liberty of the spirit. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things. O who could describe this battle between spirit and flesh, where the spirit itself desires to subject the flesh to itself through an upright will, and the weak flesh always resists the spirit through contrary desires?

The second degree is a will strong in uprightness, so that it both bravely flees the evil it hates against the assault of the flesh, and strongly lays hold of the good it loves by doing it, even with labor.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in every kind of riches. Indeed, the will does not long remain strong if it is not devout in the commandments of God.

The fourth degree is a full will, inasmuch as it does not divide its strength at all, but through the renunciation of all things continually stretches itself wholly toward its God. Three things are necessary for this degree, so that the good will in us may be made full.

The first is complete renunciation of oneself, since the will is shown still to be weak if it is not mortified through total self-renunciation.

The second thing required for a full good will is said to be perfect resignation. If anyone truly desires to attain it, let him fervently ascend the third degree of humility,

First, with respect to its proceeding it was unjust, because, born of a virgin, he neither contracted sin nor committed it, nor was deceit found in his mouth. Finally, he was killed contrary to human law, if a sentence of death is brought against a person neither confessed nor convicted of a crime. For our Redeemer, dead on the cross, is pierced with a lance, and, contrary to the law of nature, once deceased he is wounded by the impious.

Second, the passion of Christ is said to have been shameful with respect to its manner, for the six causes that follow. First, because of the paltry price, since blood of incomparable worth is sold by a disciple for thirty silver coins, for which no one would deign to sell his donkey. Second, because of the shameful service, because, full of wounds and half-dead, he is compelled to carry his cross, which is found to have been done to no robber. Third, because of the unworthy place, since he is crucified outside the city in the place of Calvary, where robbers and wicked murderers are punished for their deserts. Fourth, because of the shameful punishment, since he is suspended naked on the cross, a penalty owed only to robbers and criminal men. Indeed, as Scripture says, the Redeemer of the world was condemned by a most shameful death. Fifth, because of the shameful mockery, since he is suspended naked on the cross before the whole multitude and is blasphemed by everyone as though foolish and insane. Concerning this the psalmist says: “All day long my shame is before me.” Sixth, because of the shameful company, since, counted among criminals, he is suspended in the midst of robbers. What are you doing now, O Christian soul? Behold, your Savior hangs in the middle between robbers, and you have not feared to sit first in council.

On Meditation on the Benefits of God

The third root of divine fear and love is fixed in meditation on the benefits of God, through which one comes to the grace of devotion. Indeed, the beginning of devotion is placed in meditation on the benefits of God, from whose remembrance love is generated. The middle stage, as one advances in that meditation, desires nothing other than to please God above all things, since it knows that it has been forestalled by him with many benefits, without any merit of its own. The end, with burning desire, loves God above all things with the pure fervor of the mind, and longs to cling constantly to him alone without any intermediary. And although the most merciful God has bestowed many things specially on each one of us, nevertheless, because we write these our synthemata not for one person but for many, we must briefly proclaim not the particular benefits assigned to individuals, but the general benefits granted to all. Therefore we include the chief ones under the number seven, so that throughout the seven days of the week, or even at the canonical hours themselves, we may have material for daily meditation at hand. But if anyone already dwells in higher things through the elevation of the mind into God, let him not require these matters, which are set forth as for beginners. But he who, because of the weakness of his mind, cannot ascend to that supreme summit of contemplation should strive to exercise himself more fervently in these meditations on the benefits of God, so that, while he diligently continues in humbler things, purified and made stronger he may ascend to higher things. Therefore let the devout cenobite consider what he has received from the Lord God, so that he may be able to show himself grateful. Let him render to God what he has received, if he longs to be good. For God first gave us body and soul with

Second, he gave us faith with the sacraments, for just as he made us without our will, so he called us to faith without our merit. Therefore, incorporated into Christ through the faith and sacraments that he has given, we are bound to cling to him perpetually and to remain continually in his holy covenant. Therefore let us always say from the heart with the prophet: “It is good for me to cling to God, to place my hope in the Lord God.”

Third, almighty God gave us the craft of righteousness and the teachings of truth. For these we are bound to our Creator in faithful execution and perpetual service without deceit.

On the Knowledge of Oneself.

The fourth root producing divine love is called knowledge of oneself, very useful if it is referred to the preceding roots. Indeed, if by reflection we bring back to mind how many things so great a Lord has done for us, who are so little, we shall recognize that we are unworthy, vile, and altogether undeserving of all things. Four things ought to lead a person to knowledge of himself; and from consideration of these it is especially fitting for us to love God.

First, therefore, let the faithful person consider the beginning of his creation, and he will find that, when he did not exist, he was created from God’s goodness alone. Who could have asked God, when no one existed? That highest and utterly ineffable good created you, O man, in his own image when you did not exist; from goodness alone he made you a participant in his goodness. What did you merit when you did not exist? Or what advantage could your condition bring to almighty God, who, always subsisting unchangeably in himself, admits no accident? On Keeping the Memory of One’s Sins.

The sixth root.

The fifth root of the fear of God is the memory of sins, and the very foulness of sin often considered. Indeed, whoever truly understands the foulness of mortal sin always foresees the pit of this evil. But so that you may in some way recognize the gravity of sin, you must consider the seven things that follow.

First, then, consider the beauty of God’s justice, to which sin is entirely contrary and therefore to be especially avoided. For through sin the soul is wholly turned away from the highest good, and the love that is naturally in it toward its principle is changed into hatred.

--- Page 100 ---

For just as all good things are from the first good, so every love, toward whatever good it is borne, is naturally kindled by its condition. For since our love cannot rest in any goods as in its end, it has as its end, where it is to rest, that same good which it had also as its beginning. Therefore, as often as sin is committed against the beauty of God’s justice, the soul is separated from its principle. For since God is charity, he demands love in the soul; as peace, he requires peace; he loves what is like, and hates what is unlike.

Second, consider the enormity of sin, and carefully weigh how greatly its foulness displeases God. Behold, because of pride the just God expelled the most noble angel Lucifer from heaven, and with his accomplices condemned him to the burning of eternal fire. Because of disobedience he compelled our first parents, Adam and Eve, to leave paradise, and by a just judgment condemned the whole human race, in their crime, to the sentence of a double death. Finally, let us review the whole history of the Old and New Testament, and we shall find that what was clearly committed rashly against the justice of God was often gravely punished by that justice. Therefore the gravity of the crime is easily recognized if the severity of the vengeance that followed is considered.

Third, consider how grave a scandal arises from sin, especially when one sins in the presence of others. For whoever, by his evil example, provokes someone to sin shows himself an adversary of God. For God, by his own will and out of mercy, has determined to save souls redeemed by the blood of his only-begotten Son; but this man, through his turning away from God, desires to destroy them by evil example. A scandalous sin is truly called most grave with respect to guilt for this reason: because, as we have said, it is opposed to God. But if the one who gives occasion for harm is to be thought to have harmed, then the sinner subjects himself to as many sentences of death as he has caused people, by his evil example, to fall into mortal crime. For Christ says in the holy Gospel: “Woe to the man through whom scandal comes.” And again he says: “Whoever scandalizes one of these little ones...”

On the Vanity of Present Things and the Eternity of Future Things.
Chapter 6.

The sixth root of fear is raised up in the mind of one who meditates, if the vanity of visible things and the eternity of invisible things are considered with attentive care. For all things that we perceive in this world are altogether subject to vanity and corruption, and indeed will last for a very short time. All mortal affairs hang under uncertain chance, and by their own changeability promise flight. Whatever you have today may perhaps leave you tomorrow; or even now, while you are speaking, it will cease to be yours. For in this world we perceive nothing lasting and stable, but we see that all things change with time.

Now then, faithful monk, ascend to the watchtower of your mind, and consider that all things which time contains are nothing, because

--- Page 104 ---

whatever is now seen as visible will after a little while be corrupted. Take your consideration of the present from things past, and see that what has passed away does not exist, and that what now seems to exist will after a very little while not exist. Where are the kings of the nations, where the princes, where the commanders, where the rich, where those once powerful in the world? Where are all the lovers of the world; where are the people who despised the steep path of heaven? Finally, where are all those once known to you in the world, whom death meanwhile snatched away while you were in the cloister? Behold, from the origin of the world, those who lived for many times and those who lived for few have all died in their own generation, and, according to the diversity of their merits, have also received different rewards as justice dictated. The lovers of God receive everlasting bright dwellings, filled with inestimable joys, joined to the Creator of all; and the worshipers of the world, despising God’s commandments, once swollen here with honors, puffed up with knowledge, sunk in riches, and given over to pleasures and vices, have gone down to hell to be tormented without end. Tell me, what harm now comes to the saints in heaven from their once laborious contempt of the world, contrary to the flesh, through which they now enjoy the glad vision of God forever? What profit now comes to the impious, buried in hell, from the consolation of the flesh they once had, through which they now receive the torments of eternal damnation? Consider what is profitable; meditate on these things while the opportunity to take care is present.

Again, ascending higher, try with all your strength to fix the gaze of your mind in the abyss of infinite depth, and to consider how long what is eternal is. Eternity is time enclosed by no end, bounded by no span. The saints of God will exult forever in blessed glory; sinners will burn forever in hell in misery. O eternity, name terrible without end: who could think of you or speak of you without fear? Truly, whoever thinks of you never sins forever. Eternity has no end. Hear a comparison, and, when it has not been sufficient for eternity, take up fear. If we were to place a heap of sand the size of Mount Olympus, reaching all the way to heaven, from which after ten thousand years one small grain alone were removed...

The hour will come, and now it is already hastening near, in which the wretched man, when he can no longer do better, will learn what eternity is. But you, my monk, who desire to be faithful, cast away from yourself every anxiety of the world; set all your concern on the love of God. Do nothing, say nothing, think nothing except what you know can lead you to the love of your Creator, or pertains to the edification of others or to the simple necessity of the body. Separate, I beg you, precious things from the lowly, so that you may be able to delight in eternal goods. Let no anxiety of the flesh draw you away from the inner tranquillity of mind; let no pleasure corrupt you. Strive, as far as you can, always to persist in prayers with devotion, and again to lift yourself more frequently through the affection of love.

On Always Having Zeal for God.

The seventh root of the fear of God is called zeal; in this the monk is commanded to exercise himself in no small measure. For no one calmly tolerates injuries against his lord, if he truly loves him. Therefore you, monk, who desire to be a faithful servant of Jesus Christ, do not neglect his zeal within yourself. When you see an injury against God, do not dissemble; for whoever does not at once resist those who disparage his lord is shown to love him less. Let people know you as such a lover of God and enemy of vices that they will not dare to speak evil in your presence, nor presume to do any evil thing while you look on.

--- Page 106 ---

Be conspicuous for uprightness of soul, bright with the merit of your life, reverend in zeal. The upright will love you as a servant of God; the wicked will fear your face as that of a judge. Let no one presume to do anything in your sight that is alien to good morals, since all will have known that you cannot tolerate injury against God. Thus Phinehas the priest merited an everlasting covenant when, by zealously defending the law, he powerfully appeased the Lord’s wrath.

If you see order perishing in the monastery, if your brothers have begun in any way to corrupt regular observance, do not, if you are a servant of God, be silent about their vices. Rather, set yourself bravely on behalf of the house of the Lord; exercise the zeal of God as much as you can; destroy the counsels of the malicious; resist the rashness of the proud. If you are a true lover of God, by your very face alone you will scatter depraved gatherings. For no one will lay bare his plan before you if he suspects your zeal to be against it. But if the wicked do not fear your presence when they act badly, then surely they recognize no zeal of God in you.

Therefore, in order to show yourself a true servant of Christ, you ought willingly to forgive your brothers, for the love of God, injuries inflicted on yourself, and to pray to God for those who injure you. But injuries against the Lord, since it is not yours to forgive them without his will, you should rather avenge out of zeal for uprightness. Let love of blood-kinship yield in these matters; let the bond of friendship withdraw far away. Zeal for God must be preferred to all things. For friendship with a person must be observed in such a way that it is never preferred to zeal for God.

On the Mode and Degrees of Divine Love, Containing the Conclusion of This Second Region

Since no one suddenly becomes supreme, nor can anyone by his own powers suddenly ascend to that highest summit of divine love, I now think it useful to note briefly the certain modes or degrees themselves of loving God. For moving the human heart to divine love, in my judgment the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ is more effective than all other things, provided that it has been pondered in a person’s mind purely, sweetly, fervently, perseveringly, and diligently. Truly, one who often recalls to memory the life, passion, and death of our Savior, with compassion and thanksgiving, especially according to its principal articles, prepares a swift ascent of the heart to divine love. But we shall suitably treat this most holy occupation of the mind around the Lord’s passion in the second principal part of this work, where we shall set out more fully the manner of spiritual exercise. As for the account of what is said in this place, I find three modes of loving God, which I shall briefly explain in order.

The first mode commands that you love sweetly the one who loves you, and join your whole self to him gently, so that your heart loves nothing outside God and requires nothing from elsewhere. For since God is wholly all, he alone desires to possess the whole heart of the one who loves him, and he drives it away from displeasing thoughts. Therefore, if you wish to love sweetly, love totally. For the love of God makes complete either the whole person or nothing. Let meditation on the passion of Jesus Christ our Savior possess you wholly, until love grows sweetly strong in you. You must despise everything in yourself that flatters the flesh, if you desire to come to the love of God. For no one can love God sweetly in this life while he is convicted of loving anything in the world. Indeed the love of the world is utterly contrary to divine love, since where one is admitted into the inner chambers of the heart, the other is forbidden to be present. But how great a sweetness, how great a pleasantness, and how great a joy of mind the love of God brings into

[segment 50]
--- Page 109 ---

No one fully understands what sweetness, what delight, and what joy of mind the love of God produces in the heart of the one who loves, unless he has despised all the things and events of the world and has loved God alone for a long time. The heart of one who loves God loves none of those things that are in the world; it despises the riches, honors, and favors of the age; it does not fear to undergo any adversities for the love of its Lord. The love of God is never idle; it always desires to return to its beginning; it refuses to suffer no labors in this world. Therefore let the person who does not wish to become slothful love.

One’s neighbor also is to be loved sweetly for God’s sake, and is not to be offended in any way against God or justice. Let each person always show himself to his brother, in words and deeds, such as he expects others to be toward himself. For he truly loves his neighbor who exposes his possessions, body, and soul for him. Christ taught this; the Son of God did this for us. Indeed, whoever says that he truly believes in Christ ought himself to walk just as he walked. He does not truly love Christ who does not keep his commandments. For he says: “This is my commandment, that you love one another, as I have loved you.” We are members of one head, Jesus Christ; and we then believe in him when we observe his commandment concerning love toward God and toward neighbor. Therefore let us love our neighbor, because he is a member with us in Christ, so that just as one member in the body suffers with another, so a brother may be humbly helped by a brother for the love of Christ.

The second mode commands you to love wisely, and in divine love to seek nothing other than God. Stretch out your affection toward that Word which was in the beginning with God; raise your mind above everything that has been created, because unless you leave behind yourself and all things that are in the world, you will not be able to come to him whom you love. Finally, if you desire to love God wisely, do not think yourself to be anything; for where a person reckons himself to be something, he already loves unwisely.

The third mode commands that you love bravely, and that you never fear to expose body and soul for the love of God.

--- Page 110 ---

The lover of God is not brave if he is afraid to undergo death for him. For love is strong as death. One’s neighbor is loved wisely if not the person but the vice is hated. And he is loved bravely if every injury is forgiven from a spirit of doing good to that same person.

Here ends the second region.

Preface to the third treatise of the first part, which describes the supreme region of the spirit of cloistered persons.

At last, with the Lord’s help, as I am about to write of the supreme region of the spirit, I am at once restrained by fear and shame: for, being small, I fear to speak of the greatest matters, and I am deeply ashamed to teach things in which I am not sufficiently experienced. For what ability, however practiced, could recognize the state of a mind resting sweetly in God through love? Or what iron pen, even of the most eloquent orator, could explain the secret sweetness of inward contemplation? Indeed, the exercise of this third region surpasses human understanding for the most part: in it the mind of the purified person, through the fervor of divine love, is carried above itself and, while its natural essence remains, is absorbed in the abyss of divine contemplation, lulled with wondrous sweetness. It is so overwhelmed by the great sweetness of the inward majesty that it seems able neither to understand nor to express its own state. Once the operation of the senses has clearly been left behind, it also passes wholly beyond the intellect; and, as though abandoning itself still more, without image, reasoning, or understanding, it is raised by affection above itself to that supreme summit of the divine darkness. But when it has entirely failed from itself, it does not act but rests; nor, when returned to itself, can it understand what it has undergone, or reveal to anyone the manner of its elevation. For this elevation of the mind into God is so pure and so sublime that not only can the inexperienced not understand it, but even the experienced person cannot fully explain in words what it is like. But what shall I be able to say about the mountain of divine darkness, which I have not yet deserved to enter? For the well of inward contemplation is deep, and I am weak: I have brought neither a jar suited to these waters, nor have I prepared steps of ascent suitable for such heights. Polished words do not befit a fool, nor is it fitting for an unskilled and cold man to speak of the secrets of divine contemplation.

An unskilled and cold man ought not to speak about the secrets of divine contemplation, one who still remains with the weak in the valleys. For only he speaks fruitfully about profound matters who is illumined by the ray of a double spirit. Nevertheless, since I am a man placed under obedience to the annual chapter, I am compelled to take up the command of that synod and to complete, according to my powers, the work set before me. Therefore I enter the third region of cloistered souls under the guidance of the venerable father, Lord Johannes, the devout abbot of Bursfeld, so that, by reducing as far as I can to my own manner of speaking what he described in scholastic and truthful language, I may appear to have obeyed the chapter.

Here begins the third region of the soul, which is called spiritual, in which the highest tranquility of mind is possessed.

The third, or highest, region of the soul is called spiritual; in it the highest repose and tranquility of mind is found. This region is compared to the empyrean heaven, in which there is the true repose of all and the true happiness of souls. The spirit standing in this region leaves behind the subrational part and, lifted up by love into God, ascends marvelously. For whatever it does in this most holy region is judged wholly divine. Here the human spirit, rapt above itself, receives the divine inpouring; and, lulled to rest in the darkness of divine contemplation and withdrawn from all creatures, it sweetly rests. In this region almighty God directs the spirit contemplating him; and, when the intellectual powers of the soul have been simplified, recalled from every phantasm into the inner chamber, and gathered together according to their objective tendencies, he strips the mind of all multiplicity, leads it back to himself as the one principle, and also makes it one in himself, not by nature but by gracious participation. The citizens of this highest region are truly spiritual people, who, despising all things and affections of the world, love nothing in this world except God, desire nothing inordinately, but, absorbed by inward affection by

the divine Spirit, thirst vehemently for God himself and burn with an insatiable desire for his love. For they are driven in God by such great fervor of love that, lifted above themselves, they leave behind nearly all intellectual operations; and, rapt into the abyss of divine contemplation, they admit absolutely nothing except God. There, existing insensibly through the grace of love, they become one with God, while the distinctness of their natures is preserved, by the gift of participation. For the holy apostle of Jesus Christ says: “He who clings to the Lord is one spirit.” And again he says: “Those who are led by the Spirit of God are the sons of God.” The same apostle also shows himself to be a citizen of this most holy region when he cries out to the Philippians: “Our conversation is in heaven.”

For as long as we are in the body, we are pilgrims away from the Lord; but we are sweetly led, as though through sleep, to our homeland when in mind and spirit we have our conversation in heavenly things. I have said “through sleep” not properly, but by a remote comparison. For in sleep we pass through images of things intelligible to us, with the species preserved in the imagination; in contemplation, beyond all imagination, we are lifted up to God by pure mind and spirit. And therefore this highest region of the soul is called spiritual, from which all religious receive the name of spirituality. Woe to us. We are called spiritual from this most blessed region, and are named religious lovers of God, although we have never climbed the summit of this mountain through the height of contemplation, but, always weak with the weak, lie on the bed of carnal desires. Why do we bear a holy name, we who are not holy?

Let us therefore strive with all our strength, through the continual exercise of the middle region, to ascend to the height of this highest summit, if we truly desire to obtain the name of spiritual life. In this region charity holds dominion, wisdom the consulship; in it nothing is loved except God, nothing is done inordinately. This most holy region, moreover, belongs to the empyrean heaven, because those whom it spiritually receives as citizens it brings to the joys of heaven.

That this supreme region of the spirit of cloistered persons is pleasing by comparison.

Article 1.

First, then, this third region of the soul is called pleasing by comparison, because it is compared to the highest empyrean heaven. For just as in the empyrean heaven all things are tranquil, so on this summit of the supreme region the mind of the person contemplating God is disturbed by no agitations. Here is the highest rest of the contemplating soul; here no crowd of vices dwells; no bitterness of mind at all draws near.

In the empyrean heaven, indeed, the majesty of God is seen by the holy spirits through an ineffable gift; here, in darkness, through the longing of love, it is seen above understanding. There God is all in all; here nothing among all things is pleasing except God. There it is granted to the saints to rest from their labors without end; here the mind is permitted, while resting, to taste beforehand the unspeakable sweetness of inward delight.

There is no greater blessedness than in heaven, and there is no tranquility in the world so sweet as in this region. Blessed indeed are those who have deserved to ascend this mountain of divine contemplation, who love nothing at all in this world and desire nothing other than always to cling to God. O how secure is the conscience of these citizens, how joyful their way of life, where no evil is committed and nothing else is desired except that highest, unchangeable good.

The mind that has truly and perseveringly ascended this region has been purified through divine love: it desires nothing carnal, nothing disordered; it raises itself wholly in contemplation of the highest majesty through longing; it rests sweetly in God alone. To the one principle of all things, indeed, unity alone can attain, because unity flees multiplicity and seeks what is one. For the more you yield to multiplicity, the more you withdraw from unity.

Therefore the mind of a person dwelling in this supreme region of the spirit, when it seeks to raise itself in love toward the contemplation of its own principle, is accustomed to set aside every multiplicity clinging to it in imagination or understanding, so that, gathered in its own unity, it may be able to turn ardently, with a simple affection of love, toward him whom it knows to be above every position.

That this supreme region of the spirit is sweet and lovable by nearness.

Article 2.

Secondly, this supreme region of the spirit is most sweet and pleasant in likeness and by singular nearness. For the citizen of this most holy region is separated from the citizens above by only one earthen wall, so to speak, because as long as the spirit inhabits the flesh, it cries out to heaven as if through a wall. The earthen wall of the flesh, however, is the frailty of the body, from which the spirit, once freed, will go out to migrate to heaven when it is separated from the body by death.

But a spirit wounded by sins, which cannot raise itself to this region through the pure affection of love, is still said to stand farther away from the empyrean heaven. Those, however, who dwell in this most quiet region are very near the empyrean heaven, because, weighed down by no stains of sins, they despise all earthly and carnal things and always desire to live in mind and spirit among heavenly things.

In this highest region of divine contemplation the rational spirit calls its powers and capacities back into its own chamber. There, free from every phantasm, it is at leisure; it raises itself above itself into the density of the divine darkness, where, lulled by ecstatic love, it rests sweetly. O how good it is to cling to the Lord; how sweet it is to attend to God alone, so that the mind may thirst for the Lord Jesus before all things and always long to embrace him with burning desires. That this region of the soul is divine by instruction.
Article Three.

Third, this highest region of the soul is called divine by instruction, because in it the mind, fervent with divine love, is taught not by man but by the Holy Spirit. For Christ said to his lovers in the sacred Gospel: “When that Spirit of truth comes, he will teach you all truth.” And again there is that saying of the prophet: “And they shall all be taught by God.” For the Holy Spirit inhabits the heart of one who loves Jesus and illuminates it by his invisible teaching.

In this region the spirit reads true wisdom, Mary’s best part, in silent tranquility; mystical theology teaches in the secret of the divine darkness with ineffable sweetness; the mind, disposed by the fervor of most holy love, speaks with its beloved in secret and is continually raised toward him by desire. This wisdom, or mystical theology, can indeed be commended by a man, but it cannot be taught except by divine instruction. For the work of the Holy Spirit is being done when the mind is illuminated in true contemplation.

For the acquisition of mystical theology, purification of the affection from every entangling thing is required, so that the will, depraved by aversion, may be reformed toward its principle through the conversion of inward love. Indeed, unless the affection has been purged of all disordered desire, it does not ascend to the sublimity of this most sacred wisdom. That this supreme region of the spirit is founded on simplicity.

Article 4.

Fourth, this most holy region of the spirit is said to be founded on simplicity, in which the mind is united not by essence

For, as we have already said above, God is that supreme good, the One, to which our mind cannot draw near unless, having stripped off every multiplicity that belongs to attraction, imagination, and understanding, it strives to present itself entirely one within itself.

For the mind itself naturally seeks to reach the supreme good; but the more it is divided in its operation through multiplicity, the less fit it is found to be for coming to him. Multiplicity is opposed to unity, and the more the mind has turned itself toward many things, the more it withdraws from unity. One, two, three, four: they are ten. You withdraw from one when you are multiplied in number. Return to one if you desire to have the principle. For the One is the principle of all things; if you add anything to it in your mind, you at once withdraw from it. When you say “one,” you say “principle.” If you say “two,” you have already left the principle behind.

Therefore, so that you may always be able to contemplate your principle, take care at all times, stripped of all multiplicity, to make yourself one. For since all goods are from the first good, and every love is borne toward some good, being naturally kindled by that same good, our love cannot be touched by any goods and rest in the final end unless it strives finally to return, naked and pure, to its own principle. For union is made solely by the power of the One itself; and for no other reason do we say that things more similar to one another are more easily united than because we see that they are one in quality.

Therefore the mind is believed to be able to be united with the supreme principle of things, the One, only when, stripped of every multiplicity and united also to itself, it is lifted up to God by most fervent love. Just as no union occurs between things diverse in quality, so there can be no adhesion between God and a mind divided within itself. For union of one with one occurs through likeness of quality; and there will be adhesion with God for a mind simplified and stripped of all things through loving affection.

Wretched as we are, we have withdrawn by the perversity of our own will; we have squandered the portion of our inheritance with the harlots of carnal affections; we have been shamefully stripped of every beauty and paternal ornament. Why do we not return to the Father, whom we know to be ineffably merciful? Let us return at least, I beg, after our fault, since we have despised avoiding ruin. Return, return, wretched soul; return, gravely captive in affections; return, mortified by sorrows. Why do you delay? Why do you linger? Return, faithful soul, to the guide of your youth’s covenant; return to your former husband; go back to the most loving Father, who awaits you with the desire of love. For behold, he awaits you patiently, and through the prophet Isaiah he cries to you: “Return to me, for I have redeemed you. I have blotted out your iniquities like a cloud, and your sins like a mist.” What is this, O soul, that you are in the land of enemies? You have grown old in a foreign land; you have been defiled with the dead; you have been counted with those who go down into hell. Remember that almighty God created you from nothing; consider that he formed you in his image and likeness; remember whence you have fallen; see where you have gone, trampled under the feet of unclean affections among swine. Your Creator set you from the beginning in this supreme region of the spirit; he entrusted to you the natural exercise of his love; he also granted you the hope of life that will remain forever. But you have abandoned the fountain of wisdom; you have gone out into the lowest region of unlikeness; you have squandered the promised goods of the inheritance. But return, even late at last, to the exercise of penitence in the middle region, until, purified from all your iniquities, you may merit to be led back to the pleasantness of the supreme region.

For whatever happens to a just man dwelling on this mountain of love will not sadden him. For a soul always peaceful and tranquil in the love of God is lifted up by no prosperities and disturbed by no adversities.

Preface to the second principal part of the spiritual exercise of cloistered persons. It happily begins.

Since, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have recently completed the little work on the threefold region of cloistered persons, enjoined upon us by the annual chapter, it remains now that, undertaking according to our strength the second principal part of the work, we describe the mode and form of the daily spiritual exercise.

For there are many among cloistered persons who are weak and feeble; and among them some perhaps would gladly ascend to the contemplation of divine love, if they had a mode suited to beginners. For all who are established in holy religion must take the greatest care that the mind not grow sluggish in idleness, since whoever has fallen away from the continual labor of spiritual exercise gradually falls into the depth of torpor. For just as this visible fire of ours cannot continue in its operation without fuel suited to it, so the love of inward compunction cannot remain in a person’s mind without the continual exercise of devotion. For as soon as the labor of spiritual study has been interrupted through negligence, all compunction of devotion gradually vanishes. No one placed in a holy purpose can remain steadfast and firm if he does not strive each day to renew the fervor of his mind by some exercise. For when the fuel of divine love is withdrawn, devotion is changed into torpor. Scripture says: “Every idle person lives in desires.” Therefore, lest the mind gradually flow away through sloth from the holy fervor of the spirit, it must learn to renew itself daily by some spiritual exercise, so that, while the fuel of love is supplied day by day, the desire for greater progress may be more fully kindled. Thus we read that the most holy fathers once advanced in the order; thus we know that they remained in devotion, because they knew very well the instability of the human mind, and therefore they were accustomed daily to stir themselves to a new

--- Page 133 ---

fervor of spirit. One, through the study of sacred reading, drove his mind toward the compunction of devotion; another, through meditation on the Lord’s Passion, stirred himself up; another, by continual attention to his holy purpose, set before his inner eyes each day the image of death close at hand; another, by a fixed manner in his prayers, daily offered himself to the Lord with tears. None among them is believed to have been without a daily spiritual exercise of the mind; none is found to have been given to idleness, because the kingdom of God is promised to those who labor in the vineyard of the Lord, but denied to the idle.

Considering this, the devout fathers of the annual chapter unanimously resolved to adopt the present exercise and to offer it for practice to their subjects who have nothing better. And although so great a multitude of monks subject to the aforesaid chapter neither can nor ought to be restricted to the pattern of a single exercise, still it is proposed to all in common, so that anyone who does not have a better exercise may use this one. It is especially fitting that one newly converted to the order should exercise himself in the manner set down below, until, grounded in humility, he grows through compunction to better things. But whoever thinks that for a long time now he has been dwelling, by accustomed devotion, in higher things, let him at least permit these things to the little ones without envy. For I do not doubt that many live in the order devoted to better modes of spiritual exercise, who have learned by long practice to remain in the supreme region of the spirit through the ray of divine love. We in no way force our childish things upon them, for we know that they are higher than the steps of the present exercise. For we, imperfect men, write for beginners who are starting to make progress, not to teach the perfect. Rather, we are humbly ready to listen to them as teachers.

Note on the preceding prayer.

Do not think this manner of praying frivolous, nor invented by human curiosity; for if you believe that God has been accustomed to teach his ancient servants many things by revelation, you will in no way contradict this most holy form. Rather, read the wondrous works of the Lord which he has shown to those who love him, and you will find what he revealed to Mechthild and to others concerning this manner and form of prayer. Know this as thoroughly persuaded: that the prayer is most pleasing to almighty God which a devout mind has resolved to offer to God the Father in union with the Lord’s Passion. And this offering can be sent forth, in mind or by mouth, not only in the canonical hours, but also in other prayers, masses, and any good exercises or works. But if through forgetfulness it has not been sent forth beforehand, it can be inserted during prayer, or even added after its completion.

How a monk should conduct himself in the hours.

From the beginning of divine praise, the devout monk should apply great diligence, so that his mind does not admit images or thoughts about transitory things; for unless vain thoughts are driven back without delay at the beginning, they will disturb the intention of the heart throughout the whole office. Therefore the faithful worshiper should gather himself wholly into himself, and prefer nothing to the divine service against the rule.

While psalming, let him refer the very subject matter of the psalms to the corresponding focus of his intention; let him prudently attend to the sense of the prayer; let him pronounce the words distinctly and in order; let his mind tend upward. But after each psalm or reading, let him always examine himself carefully, testing whether compunction of devotion has increased.

On the Chapter of Faults.

Since nothing in human actions is altogether perfect, let the devout cenobite rejoice that he has found a remedy for his imperfection, because whatever anyone in a well-reformed monastery commits from human frailty, he corrects daily. For the chapter of faults, continued on individual days, washes away public offenses; sacramental confession, repeated daily through the grace of contrition, remits hidden negligences. We discern four chapters in the monastery, about which we shall speak in order.

The first is the passive chapter, when a brother proclaims himself or is proclaimed by another.

The first is called the passive chapter, in which our devout cenobite proclaims himself for his negligences, or is proclaimed by another. In this chapter the lover of God, who has denied his very self to himself, humbly both accuses his own negligences and permits himself to be accused by others, lest after this life he be judged harshly by the Lord. For the holy apostle of God says: If we judged ourselves, we would certainly not be judged. And the prophet Nahum says: Double affliction shall not rise up. Therefore, he who here, out of love for God, now willingly afflicts himself for his sins is worthy, as one well purified by this medicinal foretaste, to be freed from future affliction. If all cloistered persons knew how much the chapter proclamations, when willingly endured by them, would profit toward the merit of eternal life, they would endure all accusations and injuries for the love of God with inestimable joy. For we merit more when we patiently endure injuries and adversities for the love of God and fraternal charity than if we devote ourselves to good works.

Therefore, in judgment the devout monk should not fear any proclamations, but should desire to be accused by others in remembrance of the Lord’s passion, knowing that the glory of loftiness has been set before him, and is to be bestowed in heaven upon humble patience. O brother, why would you not endure the proclamations of brothers, you who expect the fellowship

of angels? Let all indignation be far from you; let murmuring be far away; let pride withdraw. For you have no cause for impatience. Either you are proclaimed justly or unjustly. If justly, why would you not endure it? It is better to be cleansed here in small matters than after this life in many. And one who sins publicly must be reproved before all. But if you are proclaimed unjustly, remember that you are a monk, and you will find that you are bound to injuries. When you are proclaimed justly, know that you are to be cleansed by humble patience; when unjustly, you show yourself to be a true monk, and you merit more. Therefore, when you are proclaimed, call to mind the fourth and sixth degrees of humility; and if you are a true monk, pray even for those who accuse you. For the true lover of God loves the one proclaiming him all the more, and always, on the same day, either commends him in prayer or performs some special service for him. If you are good and wise, the proclamation will not be able to displease you; but if you are evil and foolish, you are worthy to be proclaimed at all times. For Scripture says of the wise man: Reprove the wise man, and he will love you. But of the evil and foolish the prophet says: They hated the one who reproves in the gate, and they abominated the one who speaks perfectly.

The second chapter is the active one, in which one proclaims another.

We call the second chapter active, in which we no longer proclaim ourselves, but others. For the action of this proclamation, the highest charity is necessary; compassion also no less, and discretion, must be applied. Therefore, when you wish to proclaim your brother by reason of charity, always apply the fear of God; and, mindful of the fellowship of eternal life, say at the beginning: Our beloved brother has committed this or that; I ask that for the Lord’s sake he may wish to amend himself. Whatever must be said, let him present it in few words and with compassion; let him state the deed simply; let him not increase the fault of the one proclaimed by aggravating it; let him more quickly entrust to the president the judgment whether what he did is good or evil. For the one among many who alone aggravates a brother’s fault by passing judgment shows a bitterness of zeal and lack of discretion.

The third chapter is that of private humiliation.

The third chapter is called that of private humiliation, whenever a brother is rebuked by a senior for his negligences. For our most holy father Benedict commands in chapter 71 of the Rule that a monk, when proclaimed in any place or at any time, as soon as he perceives the spirit of his senior to be even gently moved, ought to prostrate himself at that senior’s feet, and there humbly lie until his command. Here he is in no way permitted to excuse himself or to lay the fault on another; rather the monk is commanded to be silent and humbly acknowledge himself. For he who is a true monk neither murmurs when justly corrected nor excuses himself when unjustly proclaimed. Likewise in the divine offices and in the other conventual places, as soon as a devout cloistered monk offends in anything through negligence, by humbling himself there according to custom he makes satisfaction; and he chooses rather to act with greater penance in doubtful matters than to injure his conscience with less, or to procure scandal for others. But he who still understands this humiliation, to be undergone at every time and place, as burdensome and difficult, does not walk in the spirit but still lives carnally; and unless he has made this sense tributary to himself by subjugating it, he will never be a true and devout cloistered monk.

The fourth chapter is that of deliberations and counsels.

We call the fourth chapter deliberative, when we call the brothers to counsel for some difficult matter. Saint Benedict makes clear in the Rule how this chapter ought to be celebrated, concerning the brothers to be brought into counsel. Therefore the humble and true cloistered monk, when he has been called to counsel, approaches humbly, with reverence and modesty; when asked what he thinks, he speaks few words; he is not at all indignant that his counsel is despised and another’s approved. For he says humbly what he thinks, but always preferring the counsels of others to his own, and in true subjection he commits both himself and his judgment to the presider for examination. For he annihilates himself so deeply in humility that he always believes others more than himself, and trusts that good counsel is divinely inspired in those better than himself; and in his mind he is accustomed to set his prelate before all as the vicar of his Christ. Above all, the monk who in giving counsel submits his own opinion to the abbot at all times with cheerful humility clearly makes progress toward the mortification of his own will. But when the devout cloistered monk perceives that his counsel is despised or rejected, let him give thanks to almighty God out of true humility, and recognize that the occasion for growing proud has been taken from him.

At private Masses.

Since human nature by its own strength cannot attain entrance into the kingdom of heaven, it pleased God the most merciful Father to come mercifully to our aid through the death of our Lord Jesus Christ, his only-begotten Son, so that we, who because of our weakness cannot approach the invisible majesty, might have a mediator approaching on our behalf, one who is both God and man. For the merit of the most holy passion of our mediator has opened for us the entrance to the kingdom of heaven, has given the nourishment of the spiritual life, and just as the branch draws nourishment from the vine, so our mind truly lives in Christ. Now we receive this spiritual nourishment with special sweetness in the sacrament of the body and blood of this same mediator of ours, whenever we devoutly offer him to God the Father as an odor of sweetness. And this most sacred offering of the body and blood of the Lord not only benefits us for the forgiveness of sins, but also wonderfully assists the souls of the dead who are set in purgatory. Therefore so noble a sacrifice must be offered with great and continual devotion, since all our good works, which of themselves are altogether too worthless, are made most pleasing to God the Father through this most holy offering. Therefore, so that our devout cloistered monk may be able to attend fervently to the offering of this precious sacrifice, let him be willing to note four things in order.

For since the celebration of Mass was instituted by our Redeemer himself in memory of the Lord’s passion, a person never prepares himself better and more fruitfully for it than if he burns more fervently through meditation on that most bitter passion.

The elevation of the priest’s mind to God during the celebration.

With how great reverence, devotion, and purity of mind we ought to stand before the almighty Lord at the altar, what speech could express, or what understanding could comprehend and penetrate? For there the true body and true blood of our Lord Jesus Christ are consecrated in an ineffable manner; there the Lord of majesty, truly present, is adored; there the army of invisible angels, standing before its king, rejoices together; there the whole heavenly court adores the Lord and man Jesus. Therefore the greatest purity of mind, the greatest reverence, and humility are needed when we must stand before so great a majesty, lest the Lord Jesus, who searches the reins and hearts, be able to find in us anything that offends him. Let all worldly anxieties yield and go far away from here; let all thoughts withdraw; here let nothing be thought except what pertains to the consummation of so great a mystery; let nothing be turned over in the mind except what befits the presence of so great a majesty and glory. For here the work is not one of human power, but the miracle of divine majesty is performed: the Son of God is sacrificed to the Father; the body of our Redeemer is truly received; the mystery of our redemption is celebrated. Therefore the priest standing at the altar should remember that he stands before the invisible majesty, and should take care that he think of nothing else.

On Kyrieleison.

Kyrieleison is Greek, composed of a noun and a verb; and among them these are two words. The first, Kyrie, is said in the vocative from kyrios, which means “Lord.” The second is from eleeo, which means “I have mercy”; eleeson, “have mercy,” is uttered in the imperative. From this comes kyrieleison, that is, “Lord, have mercy,” by elision from Kyrie eleison, since a vowel before a vowel can be elided for a better sound.

Three times he says Kyrieleison to the Father, so that he who, from exceeding charity, willed his Son to become incarnate of the Virgin and to die for us, may wish to come to our aid in our necessities by his mercy.

To the Son also we say Christeleison three times, in which we pray to him that he may come to our aid.

We also utter Kyrieleison three times to the Holy Spirit, in which we humbly invoke him for our help by his ineffable goodness.

On dwelling in the cell and the works to be done in it.

There are three places in one of which the devout monk and lover of God ought most often to be found: namely, in the church, in the cell, and, at the proper time, in the refectory. He does not allow wandering through the monastery, because it is written of the wicked, the reprobate, and the gyrovagues: “The wicked walk in a circuit.” But of the just man Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of God from his youth; he will sit alone and be silent, because he has lifted himself above himself.” What, then, are the chief works of the cell? The first is to attend to commands; the second is to read usefully; the third is to apply oneself to opportune meditation; the fourth is to pray frequently; the fifth is to rest fittingly. We shall speak briefly of these in order.

First, in the cell you must attend to commands.

First, when you are in the dwelling of the cell, learn to attend to commands, and not to prefer your idle devotions to obedience. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than sacrifices, and to listen is better than to offer fat.” Therefore, if something has been assigned to you to do in the cell, do not transgress the paths of obedience on any occasion, but strive to perform diligently the work imposed on you, for the love of our Lord Jesus Christ and for the merit of holy obedience. Hence Hugh, the lover of quiet, says: “Gladly remain in the cell, and always do in it something that builds you up and is enjoined by a superior; consider that it is not within your right to go out without

permission, except by the necessity of nature.” You also have concerning this the prohibition of our most holy father Benedict in the Rule, who forbids you exit and wandering without permission.

Second, in the cell you must read usefully.

It especially befits a monk, when he is in the cell, always to read something good and to occupy himself usefully in all things. For sacred reading overcomes the temptations of the devil, calms the fires of the flesh, makes one despise the world, and raises the mind to heavenly things. For Jerome, lover of divine Scripture, spoke truly: “Love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh.” Not all things are to be read by all monks, because one who is still weak in spirit must be instructed only in simple teachings. And Hugh says to the monk who loves simplicity: “Do not read in order to be considered a teacher, nor read curiosities that do not edify, because vain reading begets vain thoughts and extinguishes the devotion of the mind.” Therefore simpler things are to be read by simpler monks, not lofty things; devout things, not curious ones, so that those who, because of slowness of understanding, are not learned in higher matters may not become dissipated in vain things. But for those who are stronger in spirit, we do not forbid studies suited to them, because all things are clean to the clean, and nothing read harms those who are truly devout. Nevertheless, let no one claim for himself a strength of soul that he does not have, but let him place it in the abbot’s discretion, for him to see whether he is fit for freedom in reading.

Third, in the cell you must apply yourself to opportune meditations.

As the shoot rises from the root, so meditation is generated from reading. If the reading has been vain, the meditation too will be fruitless and useless. For the fruit of reading is meditation, and the offspring of meditation is prayer. Therefore take care to apply yourself opportunely to holy meditations, so that you may also learn sometimes to raise your mind into contemplation through the fervor of divine love. Indeed, meditation itself raises the mind

Fourth, you must pray frequently in the cell.

The holy apostle teaches us to persist in prayer without interruption, because just as breath is always necessary to us for the life of the flesh, so continuous prayer is altogether necessary for the health of the mind. For just as by readings we are instructed toward good things, so by prayers we are freed from evil. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “Pray, that you may not enter into temptation.” Prayer refreshes the sluggish mind, extinguishes the burning of carnal desire, forms conduct, and drives away demons. If you wish always to be with God, always pray. If your prayer is pure, simple, and bare, poured out to God through fervor of love, it will not and cannot be unfruitful.

Fifth, in the cell you must rest fittingly.

He rests badly who commits many evils. He rests well who scorns all the occupations of the world. Remain quiet in the cell, so that you do not wander here and there in any thought, but keep your mind pure and tranquil, so that you may merit to obtain savory knowledge. For the saint says in verse: “Wisdom fills a tranquil mind, free from cares, and will be the guest of a peaceful breast.” And again he says: “Those who are quiet and far from tumult can examine the law, if empty hearts are free. At the Vigils of the Dead.

When the vigil office after Vespers has been completed, you will be able to say the hours of the Holy Cross, and afterward to think somewhat about the immeasurable charity of God, with which he loves the souls of the faithful. Then, as much as you can, consider in what great desolation those holy souls established in purgatory are placed; how great the pain is for a spirit separated from the body and desiring to return to the center of its own beginning, not to be able to behold the face of its Creator through friendly enjoyment, but to be forced to wait in punishments and torments most grievous to it. For so great is the desolation, sadness, and sorrow of a soul separated, placed in punishments, and desiring to come to God, that no kind of torments known to us in this world could be compared with its sorrow. For in our manner of speaking there is no comparison between bodily sorrow and desolation and the state of a separated spirit, placed in longing for return to its own beginning.

On recollection after Compline. Consideration IX.

Since we cannot remain wholly unmoved in one holy state of mind, it is necessary for us often to renew our holy purpose, so that as often as during the day, wandering through the byways of sins, we depart from the one principle of our soul, the supreme God, so often through contrition and compunction we may return to that firmly resolved amendment. Hence our holy fathers established for us that we should devote ourselves to recollection after Compline and examine ourselves concerning the negligences committed during the day, so that, by the continual exercise of recollection, we may renew toward fervor of spirit the purpose of holiness which we know to be sufficiently weak and infirm. Therefore our devout cenobite, wishing to recollect himself fruitfully and with useful continuance, must examine himself chiefly in five things in order.

On Manual Labor. Consideration E.

Since our most holy father Benedict, in the Rule, commends as true monks those who live by the labor of their hands and declares them blessed, it has seemed fitting for our practice that, after the end of the day, we should say something about this most holy exercise. Indeed, we read that this work is highly commended by the holy fathers; and we know that in it they made no small progress before God and human beings. For the holy Apostle says: “Whoever does not work shall not eat.” What Augustine, more learned than the most learned, thought about this manual exercise he shows most clearly in the book On the Work of Monks. We also read that those ancient and most holy fathers who dwelt in solitude sweated no little over the work of their hands, so that they might not fall into the danger of idleness, and, living from their own labors, might burden no one, according to the example of the most blessed teacher of the nations. Would that we too, brothers, might deign to take up this holy exercise of laboring with the same intention and goodwill as they did, so that after the end of our labor we may deserve to rest with them without end in the heavenly homeland.

Four things are to be observed by a monk in labor.

When called to labor, a monk should strive to conduct himself in all things in such a way that he does not lose the merit of bodily labor through dissolution of mind. For we see many among monks who do not rightly approach labor; we see that they seek nothing but the comforts of the flesh. Some of these take on useless and less necessary labors so that, at times of fasting, they may have an occasion for regular eating.

--- Page 181 ---

They labor in order to eat; whether it profits or harms the monastery they do not care. Others, so that they may be able to run about here and there without the restraint of discipline, as though under pretext of an occasion, have chosen to arrange various labors. Others, so that they may freely indulge in empty talk and levities, procure labors useless to the cenobium. Others, so that they may be able, as if by a suitable opportunity and without suspicion, to see and speak with people, not to say secular persons, known and friendly to them according to the flesh and not according to God, love external labors. Others, because of instability of mind and because they cannot rest in the monastery, always seem to be panting after labors. But the intention of all who labor in this way is not sincere, and therefore they do not attain the best reward of labor.

But you, brother, when you are called to labor, take care to submit your intention to the regular institution, so that you love nothing except for the honor of God, the merit of obedience, and the utility of the monastery; intend nothing except the advancement of the soul. While you are at labor, do not imprudently extinguish the fervor of the spirit; do not omit the exercise of spiritual conversation, but take care diligently to observe everywhere the four things that follow. In external conduct be faithful, serviceable, and well-disposed; in the interior life, humble and devout; in speech, sparing and edifying; in manners, composed and mature. If you observe these things in labor, you will always return to your cell better.

Outwardly the monk should be faithful, well-disposed, and serviceable.

The first thing to be diligently observed by a cenobite at labor is that, in laboring, he always show himself faithful to his monastery; that he go before the brothers in serving and ministering, or also in carrying heavier things, out of charity; and that in all words and deeds he show himself well-disposed and tractable. Saint Bernard, lover of God, says: “A serious and prudent spirit prepares itself for every labor, and is not dissolved in it, but is rather gathered into itself through it; for it always has before its eyes not so much what it is doing as what it intends by doing it. For the more it intends the end of consummation, toward which it strives more truly, the more fervently and faithfully it also works with its hands, subjecting to itself the service of its whole body.”

What the monk’s conduct toward other people ought to be.

Consideration XI.

Since we have begun to speak about the maturity and composure that a monk must preserve before others, it seems useful to us now to say briefly how he ought to show himself to particular persons. For he is instructed to show himself in one way to his prelate, and is commanded to present himself in another way to his brothers and to secular people. To his rector, as holding the place of Christ, he ought to show reverence; to a brother, charity; to the hostile, gentleness; to secular people, modest piety. Therefore let the monk render to each what is his, and he will be truly composed before God and human beings. What he owes to God, and what to himself, has been sufficiently stated above; now let us pursue the matters set before us.

Above all let him strive, as far as possible, to imitate our Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who not only bore the injuries and insults of the impious with a most meek heart, but also prayed to the Father for his persecutors, that they might not perish. Imitating this example of the Savior, the holy fathers were accustomed to receive injuries patiently and meekly for the love of God, and, what is more, to love even those who wronged them. To this end let the cenobite recall to mind the degrees of meekness, which are briefly contained in these verses:

Strengthen what is fitting; bear contrary things mildly.
Preserve, restrain, and rest inwardly with yourself.
Correct prudently; be merciful; give wisely.
Pray by submitting yourself; desire martyrdom.

For if humility has possessed the mind of the cenobite, it makes him love injuries. The degrees of humility are contained in these verses.

The twelve degrees of humility.

The first of these degrees fears the Almighty.
The second places reins upon the enticements of the flesh.
The third submits itself reverently to the abbot.

The fourth, when insults are despised, patiently obeys.
The fifth reports to the abbot the secrets of the heart.
The sixth, by its own judgment, knows itself useless and unworthy.
The seventh believes itself lower than all.
The eighth does nothing that the rule of the cell does not allow.
The ninth does not speak unless another asks.
The tenth forbids laughter and condemns guffawing.
The eleventh speaks the few words of reason.
The twelfth always looks at the ground with head bowed.

On the same matters, otherwise.

Fear God; do not do your own will; let another rule you.
Endure hard things; hide nothing in your heart; seek also the lowest things.
Be refuse; do not be a wanderer alone; shut your tongue.
Let laughter be sparing, speech weighty, gestures humble.

Note: Peripsema must be read and written, not peripsima, as the unlearned read in the Epistle to the Romans; and it means the filing-off and cleansing of all things.

Let the conduct of a monk toward secular persons be modest.

Fourth, the monk’s conduct toward secular persons ought to be modest, so that neither his familiarity with them is excessive nor his withdrawal indiscreet. Hence Hugh says: With respect to people, so far as lies in you, beware of everything that can be a bad example to them, just as it befits the faithful servant of God to promote the honor of his Lord in all things and to guard against dishonor to him. Do not be familiar with any woman, even a holy one. For if they are discreet, you will please them more by this; but if they are troublesome, they are all the more to be avoided. Do not be suspicious. Be attentive to the sick in humble services. Be very truthful in your words; let your words be as though an oath, and edifying to others. Love poverty. Thus be chaste in all things and everywhere God-fearing.

In all virtues and good morals, then, as your foundation, set before yourself that most clear and most perfect example of all brightness: the life and conduct of our Lord Jesus Christ, who

--- Page 190 ---
was sent from heaven for this purpose, that he might guide us in the way of virtues and direct us by his example, just as by himself. Therefore, the more a monk takes care to conform himself to him by imitation in the way of virtues, the more without doubt he will be like and near to him in the glory of eternal happiness. Whatever you do, always look to him, and see, according to the Gospel and your own estimation, how he conducted himself in all things. For then you will act best; he conducted himself best in all things. Say therefore to yourself: If my Lord Jesus were present, what do you think he would do about this, or what would he say now? Surely he would both act and speak in the best way, and I ought to imitate him in all things with all my strength.

General conclusion of all spiritual exercises.

Wishing at last to put some end to this work, we say that the completion of all spiritual exercises consists in two things. First, in the knowledge and feeling of one’s own imperfection, so that the monk may always be conscious of his weakness and remain rooted in humility. Second, in the desire and effort to come to a higher manner of life, so that he may always desire to advance in spirit toward better things and by no means withdraw from this effort. Every cenobite ought at every moment to stretch out his mind toward this effort to advance; if he ever gives it up, he incurs the transgression of his vow and the gravest danger to his soul. Let the monk beware, however, that consideration of his own weakness not cast down the effort to advance; let him beware that the effort itself not despise the consideration of imperfection. Rather, let these two so mutually adhere to each other that neither ever withdraws from the other. But if too much consideration of one’s own imperfection should try to lead the mind into despair, let him recall to memory what the holy abbot Bernard said: Let us breathe again, brothers, for even if we are nothing in our own hearts, perhaps something of us may lie hidden in the heart of God. These two considerations, of our weakness and of divine esteem, stir up great devotion in us; so the psalmist said: I am poor and sorrowful. Behold the first. Your salvation, O God, has upheld me. Behold the second.

--- Page 191 ---

Finally, it is necessary for one who wishes to occupy himself usefully in these spiritual exercises to have a joyful and free heart, alien to every anxiety or care of the world. For the Holy Spirit is a spirit of freedom and joy; he loves a free heart, joyful in God, and, so to speak, applauds what is like himself. For just as a bird tied to the earth cannot lift itself up to heaven by flying, so a mind occupied with perishable things cannot raise itself to heavenly things. Nor should one cast down the spirit by excessive repetition; otherwise even the brain is confused, and one labors in vain through dry routine. Therefore let the cenobite, fervent in spirit, learn always to persist in these things with moderation, and by his intention continually to refer the cause of every exercise to God, who is the beginning and end of all goodness. Let him faithfully direct his effort toward fear and love of him, and fix his mind in profound humility, since one who is rooted in love is not easily abandoned by God.

Here ends the spiritual exercise of cloistered persons, by Peter Friedberg, in the noble city of Hagenau, on the eighth day before the Ides of August, in the year of salvation 1498.

Here begins the Compendium of the Spiritual Exercise. Johannes Trithemius.

The formula of the spiritual exercise for the edification of cloistered persons, published by us, could seem very brief to all the devout who are free for leisure; yet we fear that to those occupied with household affairs it may appear sufficiently long, because when thought is stretched by continual solicitude in arranging the necessities of those subject to them, the mind is easily wearied even in very brief spiritual exercises. Therefore, lest our monks, who either are given to external matters or are less devout in spiritual conversation, be able to flee loosely to an argument of excuse on the occasion of its length, we have been ordered to abbreviate the same teaching, so that the more fervent may hold it in their devotion, and the less fervent and tepid may not omit it.

There follows the practice of the spiritual exercise.

At all and each of the hours, generally, let the devout cenobite strive to direct his effort toward this end: that from his heart, at the root of God’s benefits, he may desire with all his strength to magnify God. Let there be in him so great a most fervent ardor of devotion, so great compunction, so great an overflowing flood of tears, that whoever sees him singing psalms may be moved by his example to magnify the Lord. Secondly, let him remember that he can stir others to the charity of the first commandment, since love is directed to God in vain if one’s neighbor is hated. Therefore, when all thorns of displeasure have been driven out, let the most devout cenobite, turned toward God, pray:

O almighty, eternal God, who, instructing us in the exercise of your love by the manifold creation of things, have commanded that we love you above all things with all our strength, and our neighbor as ourselves for your sake: graciously grant me the grace of your most holy love and of love of neighbor, so that, with every movement of displeasure, aversion, and anger repressed,

Who lives and reigns.

The first hour after dinner.

At the first hour after dinner, unless you are occupied with higher exercises, you shall pray to the Lord in this or a similar way, to obtain knowledge of your own frailty.

The second hour.

At the second hour after midday, let our devout cenobite recall distinctly to memory the gravity of his sins, by which, especially while living in holy religion, he has sinned against the Rule; and, turned toward the Lord, let him pray thus.

Prayer.

At the third hour after dinner, let our faithful cenobite remember his vows, which he promised to the Lord, and especially the renunciation of all ownership, namely in possessions, judgments, and will; and, moved to compunction, let him pray thus to the Lord in fervor of spirit.

Prayer.

After Vespers, let our good cenobite consider that all visible things are vain; and, with his mind lifted up to the love of invisible goods, let him cry to the Lord with as much devotion as he can.

Prayer.

Almighty, eternal God, who willed to lead us, through the visible things which you created, to the knowledge and love of invisible things: grant me so to know the vanity of present things that I may be worthy to think with a pure mind on the eternity of future things.

On the Memory of the Lord’s Passion.

Since the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ is the gate of our salvation, through which the kingdom of heaven is opened to us, we ought to exercise ourselves in it as frequently as we can with devotion, because we shall easily obtain the effect of our prayers if, through the love of compassion, we adore the Lord in the power of his wounds. Therefore, faithful cenobite, with all the diligence I can, I admonish you, exhort you, and ask you, mindful of the benefits of God, that you neglect no day at all on which you do not give thanks to your Savior for the bitterness of his

--- Page 200 ---

passion and death. And if, for any reason, you cannot do this at greater length, at least do not refuse to show yourself grateful to your Redeemer with a single word. Thus the Lord Jesus himself revealed to those of his lovers to whom he wished: that nothing is more pleasing to him, and nothing more useful to us, than to recall with thanksgiving the memory of his passion and death, and to admonish others to do the same. Receive, therefore, this shortened word, lest a more lengthy prayer make you weary.

With knees bent to the ground and hands joined together, say:

I give thanks to you, most sweet Lord Jesus, for all the wounds, pains, and insults of your most innocent passion and death. I ask you, through that same death of yours, have mercy on me now and in the hour of my death.

With a compassionate heart I give thanks, O Lord Jesus Christ, for your most bitter passion and your most innocent death. I ask you, have mercy on me.

I praise, bless, and adore you, Lord Jesus Christ, King of heaven and earth, and I give thanks to you for your holy passion, by which you freed miserable me from perpetual death. I ask you, through the power of that same passion of yours, make me a sharer forever in your most innocent death.

And so that I may not be more lengthy than is fitting, here I set an end to the compendium. For the venerable assembly of abbots of our annual chapter commanded me to avoid prolixity, lest lukewarm and worldly cloisterers claim weariness as an excuse. If the Lord Jesus gives me a life free from cares, as I have always desired, I shall try to satisfy both my own intention and another’s request. For I have resolved, for certain virgins dedicated to Christ who asked for it, to hand down a rule by which any devout cenobite may exercise himself in the Lord’s Passion.

To hand down: by means of it any devout cenobite may be able to exercise himself in the Lord’s Passion against all temptations. If he remains in it for a few months, exercising himself as he ought, he will gradually fly sweetly from Christ’s humanity to his divinity by the ray of love. For continuous meditation on the Lord’s Passion offers an easy approach to those who desire to come to the love of God; for when the mind, through compassion, is pierced by the memory of the benefits of so great a lover, then, growing warm with tears in desire, it is raised up to love. Therefore you cloistered men dedicated to God, who have no leisure to attend to many things, receive these few things. Although in them there may not be a continuous fire of love, nevertheless for those who persevere it will be able to be renewed.
