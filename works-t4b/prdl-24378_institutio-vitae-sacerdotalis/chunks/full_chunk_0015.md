For no reverence is paid to superiors if their life is confined among their subjects. It is fitting that one who presides should excel all others in a certain reverence and maturity, and wisely avoid too great a familiarity with his subjects. Indeed, the preeminence of authority is broken when it is indiscreetly mixed up with the actions of subjects. Slander no one, and do not take scandal against anyone. It is shameful for a priest to pollute his tongue with slander, since it has been set at the altar to pray for all. Nor should you lend an ear to slanderers, because the slanderer and the willing hearer are worthy of equal condemnation. The one carries the devil on his tongue, the other in his ear. Let such a person learn that he cannot readily slander when he sees that you are unwilling to listen. Do not be hasty in speech, and do not stand in the company of slanderers, because they are hateful to almighty God. Hedge your ears about with thorns, lest you hear the words of slanderers. Let no lie come forth from your mouth; let no foul speech proceed from you, but be a guardian of your tongue, lest you offend in speech. Do not be slothful or lazy, nor given to sleep and idleness, but constantly run forward in the fervor of the Holy Spirit, knowing that this is proper to a priest: to have care for the needy. Have gold not so that you may keep it, but so that you may distribute it in alms for the poor. Let the bellies of the hungry poor praise you, not the belching banquets of the rich. Let your table be with the poor and needy, for in such persons Christ is received, and the heavenly court is honored. Avoid the banquets of the proud rich, especially of the powerful, at which nothing is done concerning the salvation of souls and nothing at all is discussed concerning spiritual conduct. Let your clothing be honorable, not costly. In giving alms, do not seek glory from human beings, but expect praise and reward from God alone. Be a father of the poor and a guardian of orphans.

Let no poor person depart from you unconsoled. Do not be a striker, nor quarrelsome among the people; injure no one unjustly, but do good to everyone as much as you can, even to enemies. Never flatter sinners, but rebuke them as one who has received authority from God. Do not be a striker of the consciences of the people entrusted to you, nor pronounce the word of God indiscreetly, but with the moderation of piety. Be merciful to penitents, and do not crush the bruised reed, knowing that you have undertaken the care of the weak, not of the strong. For it is better to send a penitent with mercy to purgatory than, by the confessor’s severity, to consign one in despair to hell. Do not neglect to pray your canonical hours with reverence and devotion at the times established by the Church. Likewise, do not omit to read the Hours of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin, the vigils of the dead, and the other things owed in due order by the priestly office. Have care for your household in the fear of God; if you have a household, persuade it by word and example to preserve modesty and honorable conduct. Indeed, the reputation of a priest is harmed among the people when the people speak ill of his household.

Let your speech be strengthened by the testimony of the Scriptures, because whatever is prescribed apart from the Holy Scriptures is despised with the same ease with which it is proved. I do not want you to imitate those who, without learning, frequently cite tales and phantoms of dreams in church, and are ignorant of the mysteries of the divine Scriptures. Babbling and talkative priests, speaking without reason, do not build up the people; they labor to draw people into admiration of themselves by empty speeches and Aesop’s fables. For it is a foolish and damnable abuse, once the Scriptures and the Gospel have been set aside, to teach the Christian people the fables of Aesop and the fabricated deeds of the Romans. Nor should you wonder if the unlearned people hear these things more willingly than the Scriptures, since you read that the Apostle foretold this. “There will be a time,” he says, “when they will not endure sound doctrine, but according to their own desires they will heap up teachers for themselves, itching in their ears; and indeed they will turn their hearing away from the truth, but will be turned to fables.” Hence Jerome says: “To roll out words and, by speed of speaking, to create admiration for oneself among the unlearned crowd belongs to uneducated men.” A worn-out brow often explains what it does not know.
