Here begins the opening of the treatise of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, of the order of the holy father Benedict, On the Life of Priests, addressed to Nicolaus of Mernick, priest.

You ask me, Nicolaus, most beloved brother, and press me with frequent letters, to commend to you in writing a pattern for living praiseworthily in the priesthood. You compel me to labor beyond the powers of my intellect, and expose me to be torn by the bites of rivals, who will judge me presumptuous in this, because I wish to show you a way on which I myself have not yet learned to walk. And although the matter is so, nevertheless I prefer to undergo the judgment of rivalry rather than suffer harm to my charity toward you. For I have always cherished you with such affection that I have never been accustomed to deny you anything you asked. Therefore, desiring to satisfy your prayers, I wished to commend to you a rule of living from the writings of the saints: not because it would be altogether sufficient for your state in every respect, but so that, as some kind of beginning, it may lead you, if you wish, to honorable conduct.

First, then, I admonish you to weigh, from the definition of the name, the state of the conduct enjoined. For we are called clerics, all of us who have been specially chosen into the lot of the Lord. Let us therefore interpret the word of the name, and we shall understand the quality of the prescribed honorableness. For cleros in Greek is interpreted in Latin as “lot.” From cleros, therefore, clerics are said to be those chosen into the Lord’s lot. If, then, we recognize that we have been called into the lot of God, why do we still serve the world? Why do we seek an inheritance on the earth of the dying, we who await God, who promises himself to us? For in the Old Testament we read that the Lord commanded the priests and Levites not to receive an inheritance among the children of Israel, their brothers, because he himself was their inheritance. Now, therefore, if you consider yourself to be a cleric, you ought to walk more honorably than other men in the excellence of your conduct, and by no means love the perishable riches of this world. But if you subject your soul to the inheritance of the age, you will be placed with the foolish outside the Lord’s lot. For the Lord will not deign to receive you among his elect

if, going after gold, you have resolved to love the world. Avarice is most disgraceful in a priest of Christ, for it makes a servant of God a minister of idols. If it once takes hold of your soul, it never again lets it go in possession of itself. For no one can serve two masters who are at odds. “You cannot,” he says, “serve God and mammon.” You will not be able to be a good cleric if you are avaricious. I know, however, and by long experience in living with you I have learned, that nature made you sparing; beware lest you turn a gift of grace into fuel for avarice. For indeed those who wish to become rich in this world often fall into the temptations of the devil.

But a minister of God must be set apart from solicitude for the world, because the mind cannot go freely to God if it has been entangled in earthly cares. For this reason we wear shaved crowns on our heads, so that, having cast off superfluities, we may prepare a free ascent to God in thought. For the hairs of the head signify the superfluities of earthly action. And just as the hairs of the head arise from an excess of humor, so the anxieties of the world grow from the love of money. When, therefore, we shave the hair of the head in the form of a crown, we are admonished that, being applied to the ministry of God, we should despise all the cares of the world. And just as the head is the higher part of the body, so the mind is the highest power of reason. Therefore we wear the crown on the head so that, released from earthly cares, we may be raised more freely in mind to the contemplation of God.

Hence we cloistered men, who are called monks, adorned with very broad crowns, leave fewer cares on the head, to show that, dead to the active life, we are more fully at leisure in contemplation of heavenly things. We must attend to the definition of the name, and with fitting merits of life correspond to the lot of the heavenly Creator. Guard your heart with all vigilance, lest cupidity sink you into the depth of avarice; lest the heat of lust drag your soul, with sinners, into the filth of luxury; lest the arrogance of pride draw you with it to the lower regions to be tormented without end. Beware lest through the cares of this world you fall away from the lot of God; beware lest you lose the reputation of a good name; beware lest you proceed on the way of those who despise God. Consider the ruins of many priests around you, and always fear a similar danger for yourself.
