Thomas Ruscher of Gamundia, professor of sacred letters, unworthy preacher of the evangelical word in the church of Mainz, sends perpetual greeting to the well-reputed and catholic priest Nicolaus Mernicensis.

Lest I should seem cold in giving thanks before him from whom every best gift and every perfect gift is given, most learned Nicolaus, there comes first to mind the Scripture of Deuteronomy, chapter 30: “I have set life and death before your face.” I give thanks because today he grants this also to the one who clings to him who is true life. And I say: he who recently, in my own times, breathed into the face of someone a breath of new life; for this I give thanks to the creator of all.

And to you, O father Trithemius, worthy of praise for your understanding, I give thanks because you have chosen life not only for yourself, but establish life also for me and for many others, so that they may live, in this most flourishing little work of instruction of yours, which soon had been brought all the way to me. Giving place to nothing else, as one in need of the breath of life, I looked into it with affection of mind and desire of soul.

But, O father worthy of praise for your understanding, what you have effected in me, most devoted to you, through this so flourishing little work of your instruction, and what I felt while reading it, I would not wish to hide from my contemporaries. For when I advanced into its inner parts, it so flooded into me that my cheeks were wet.

O brother Nicolaus Mernicensis, most devoted to prayer and charity, how fortunate and how holy was your request for this flourishing little work. O man, spouse of Christ, and son Nicolaus Mernicensis, and confessor of God, how necessary are the things contained in this little work, especially in these present stormy times, when the Christian faith is torn and oppressed by harmful errors that are everywhere sown and sprout up. Necessary, I say, beyond measure.

When this flourishing instruction for the pilgrimage of the priestly life has been cast aside, all vices and disgraces rise up. For I know by the tradition of our elders that the soul of a man is nourished by understanding and by knowledge as by a certain food. I rightly judge the minds of those who have been instructed by no disciplines, by no training in morals or life, and who have drunk in nothing of the good arts, to be fasting and almost famished; yet their minds are full, but full of vices and wickedness.

MONACENSIS.

--- Page 009 ---
Nicolaus, know that I would wish myself and my contemporaries to be persuaded by this: that we should turn ourselves wholly to this flourishing little work. For through it we are able not only to avoid the penalty of crime, but also to obtain the favor of praise. For whoever does not apply to this the sense of smell of the soul will quickly be brought to ruin. For that reason you know that this very thing was fruitful for me before the first impression of its letters.

Especially since the father and lord of praiseworthy understanding, Dom Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, gathered, compiled, and handed it down with no small sweat. He is, by the manifold grace of letters, far more abundant than I am; watchful in talent, ready in eloquence, burning with zeal, and a useful householder in the house of the Lord. By providing instruction in holy life through his flourishing little work, he instructs me, weak as I am, and at the same time the whole clergy, unskilled and learned alike, for their protection.

And surely, brother, if this instruction in life is lacking to us, who, I ask, judges that we are alive? If this is lacking to us, Nicolaus, we are not worthy. For in it everything is supplied to us so fully that without these things no one is worthy of life. For if, according to the flourishing instruction of our reverend father in the Lord, Trithemius, which is at hand, we shall wholly establish ourselves while we live here, true salvation is truly promised to us; and it is never taken away from any who live a life worthy of it.

Therefore all who wish to be regarded as worthy must order their life according to the things handed down in this flourishing little work, so that they may not die, but live forevermore. Most worthy Nicolaus, so that you may be able to comprehend this, farewell always in him who created all things, and may he preserve in his mercy us who cannot stand by our own strength. Amen.

Given in the Studium at Mainz, in the year of our salvation 1494, in the month of October, on the twenty-second day of the same month.

Here begins the opening of the treatise of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, of the order of the holy father Benedict, On the Life of Priests, addressed to Nicolaus of Mernick, priest.

You ask me, Nicolaus, most beloved brother, and press me with frequent letters, to commend to you in writing a pattern for living praiseworthily in the priesthood. You compel me to labor beyond the powers of my intellect, and expose me to be torn by the bites of rivals, who will judge me presumptuous in this, because I wish to show you a way on which I myself have not yet learned to walk. And although the matter is so, nevertheless I prefer to undergo the judgment of rivalry rather than suffer harm to my charity toward you. For I have always cherished you with such affection that I have never been accustomed to deny you anything you asked. Therefore, desiring to satisfy your prayers, I wished to commend to you a rule of living from the writings of the saints: not because it would be altogether sufficient for your state in every respect, but so that, as some kind of beginning, it may lead you, if you wish, to honorable conduct.

First, then, I admonish you to weigh, from the definition of the name, the state of the conduct enjoined. For we are called clerics, all of us who have been specially chosen into the lot of the Lord. Let us therefore interpret the word of the name, and we shall understand the quality of the prescribed honorableness. For cleros in Greek is interpreted in Latin as “lot.” From cleros, therefore, clerics are said to be those chosen into the Lord’s lot. If, then, we recognize that we have been called into the lot of God, why do we still serve the world? Why do we seek an inheritance on the earth of the dying, we who await God, who promises himself to us? For in the Old Testament we read that the Lord commanded the priests and Levites not to receive an inheritance among the children of Israel, their brothers, because he himself was their inheritance. Now, therefore, if you consider yourself to be a cleric, you ought to walk more honorably than other men in the excellence of your conduct, and by no means love the perishable riches of this world. But if you subject your soul to the inheritance of the age, you will be placed with the foolish outside the Lord’s lot. For the Lord will not deign to receive you among his elect

--- Page 011 ---

if, going after gold, you have resolved to love the world. Avarice is most disgraceful in a priest of Christ, for it makes a servant of God a minister of idols. If it once takes hold of your soul, it never again lets it go in possession of itself. For no one can serve two masters who are at odds. “You cannot,” he says, “serve God and mammon.” You will not be able to be a good cleric if you are avaricious. I know, however, and by long experience in living with you I have learned, that nature made you sparing; beware lest you turn a gift of grace into fuel for avarice. For indeed those who wish to become rich in this world often fall into the temptations of the devil.

But a minister of God must be set apart from solicitude for the world, because the mind cannot go freely to God if it has been entangled in earthly cares. For this reason we wear shaved crowns on our heads, so that, having cast off superfluities, we may prepare a free ascent to God in thought. For the hairs of the head signify the superfluities of earthly action. And just as the hairs of the head arise from an excess of humor, so the anxieties of the world grow from the love of money. When, therefore, we shave the hair of the head in the form of a crown, we are admonished that, being applied to the ministry of God, we should despise all the cares of the world. And just as the head is the higher part of the body, so the mind is the highest power of reason. Therefore we wear the crown on the head so that, released from earthly cares, we may be raised more freely in mind to the contemplation of God.

Hence we cloistered men, who are called monks, adorned with very broad crowns, leave fewer cares on the head, to show that, dead to the active life, we are more fully at leisure in contemplation of heavenly things. We must attend to the definition of the name, and with fitting merits of life correspond to the lot of the heavenly Creator. Guard your heart with all vigilance, lest cupidity sink you into the depth of avarice; lest the heat of lust drag your soul, with sinners, into the filth of luxury; lest the arrogance of pride draw you with it to the lower regions to be tormented without end. Beware lest through the cares of this world you fall away from the lot of God; beware lest you lose the reputation of a good name; beware lest you proceed on the way of those who despise God. Consider the ruins of many priests around you, and always fear a similar danger for yourself.

Nowhere among mortals is life lived more securely than when danger is always feared. Hence it is written: Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil. Indeed, he who fears danger takes precautions; he who neglects it falls into it. The minister of Christ, the priest of God, must be a man, not a boy, so that he may know and understand what a burden he has undertaken, for the administration of which he will have to render account in the strict judgment of God. Let him therefore be prudent, sober, chaste, and quiet; let him be modest, kind, and gentle, so that, being appointed teacher of an unlearned people, he may suffer no contempt on account of his manner of life. It belongs to the priest, when questioned, to answer concerning the law, constantly to rebuke the vices of his subjects by words of holy preaching, and to fear no adversities for love of the truth. He who performs the priestly office among the people of God must be a man learned in divine law, so that he may know how, in due season, to bring forth new things and old, lest among the mute dogs unable to bark he lose the dignity of his office.

Now it is your duty, O Nicolaus, to rebuke the vices of others with diligent admonition and to show yourself alien to the vices of others, lest, if you are vicious and rebuke crimes, you render yourself worthy of condemnation. For no one can confidently reprove in another what he remembers, with conscience as witness, that he himself has committed. Therefore the man who sits to judge the sins of others must be irreproachable, because he who commits in himself what he rebukes in others does not correct but provokes. But today many among priests are found like hypocrites: they are angry at brothers who offend in lighter matters, and do not want anyone to be indignant at themselves. Wretched condition of human frailty: one sinner condemns another sinner, and in the same crime he pronounces an unequal sentence. I should be lying if an adulterer did not very often rebuke his neighbor for fornication; a judge vicious in many things condemns a few vices in another, and, so that he may be able to hide his own depravity, he makes manifest the offenses of all. Against such a man it is said by the Lord: Hypocrite, first cast out the beam that you carry in your own eye, and then you will see the mote in another's. Why does a sacrilegious man reprove a thief? Why does a criminal rebuke a vicious man? Every correction will be wholly fruitless when an equal foulness of life stains it. Therefore, in order that you may lack the reproach of hypocrites, beware lest, while correcting another, you offend in a similar sin.

--- Page 013 ---
Correction will never be useful among subjects while the man who presides submits his neck to public sins. Indeed, the right of correction is lost when the conduct of the corrector is defiled by the vices of his subjects. The authority of the priesthood becomes cheap if public iniquity corrupts the uprightness of one’s manner of life. Therefore every correction should take its beginning in its own author, and only then proceed securely into public. Let the man whom his own conscience bites over a similar crime suspend for a time the sentence of rebuke.

The state of believers is already for the most part corrupt, and as the people are, so too is the priest; all pursue avarice, from the greater even to the least. Woe to the sinful nation, which always slips down toward graver things while it sees its own judges guilty of an equal offense. Ecclesiastical authority has now come to such misery that we are commanded to embrace the words of priests, not their deeds. For unhappy priests say of themselves, bearing witness to their own defilement: “O faithful Christians, attend to the words of our instruction; beware of our examples.” O words most worthy of everlasting confusion, which, as much as they ought to have instructed the people of God by doctrine, so much do they scandalize them by perverse works.

The unlearned and rude now come to the priesthood without any discernment of merit; by their most evil morals they unhappily kill the sheep of Christ. No holiness of life is now required in clerics to be ordained; no learning in letters is demanded; purity of conscience is not considered. Among them it suffices if they can construe the current lot, if they can answer of what part of speech “Lord” is, if they can understand “yes” and “no.” Woe to the bishops of our tempestuous age, who, intent on secular business, do not examine the ordinations of clerics but entrust the care of examination to the unskilled; they will undergo a strict sentence, since they present themselves as the cause of every evil in the Church. Indeed, since a priest of the faithful ought to be learned and holy, by what rashness do the unskilled come to the priesthood, who can instruct neither themselves nor others? For neither does a good life without doctrine suffice in a cleric, nor does doctrine without life instruct the people healthfully. For doctrine without morals destroys the teacher; life without doctrine makes him useless. For by the barking of dogs and the ba-

with the staff of the shepherd, the madness of wolves must be driven off. Therefore the ecclesiastical shepherd should, by a good life, offer his sheep an example of justice, and by salutary doctrine feed the minds of his hearers, lest, if he does one without the other, he either neglect himself or exercise care of the flock fruitlessly. Alas, what shall I say about our priests who are unlearned and vicious, avaricious, luxurious, and utterly wicked? I am ashamed to speak, and I cannot be silent. Take thought for yourself, dearest Nicolaus, lest you become an imitator of those who, despising the books of the Scriptures, spend the whole day free for games and drinking parties; who stain themselves by mingling with women; who by their depraved conduct scandalize many in the law of God. Be in the people of God a model of honorable conduct; in all things, so far as can be done, show yourself irreproachable, so that you may remove every occasion for evil suspicion concerning you. For you ought to have testimony even from those who are outside, since the sermon of a preacher is easily despised when his life is marked among the people by evil suspicions. Therefore show yourself a cultivator of good works, so that you may be able to instruct the people of God no less by example than by speech. In order that you may be able to accomplish this salutarily, take care to avoid the companionship of the undisciplined; for he who does not flee the companionship of the wicked can scarcely live without reproach. For Scripture says: “With the holy you will be holy, and with the perverse you will be perverted.” And again: “He who touches pitch will be defiled by it.” Each person delights to have companions of his own purpose. If, therefore, you are good, love the company of the good. For if you do not keep your foot from the assembly of evildoers, you will at all times be subject to the slanders of those under you. A youthful mind is easily deceived by the companionship of the perverse. You are young, and flexible in either direction; therefore you must watch over yourself with the greatest diligence, lest you be corrupted by the company of the undisciplined. For nothing harms young men so much as the bad examples of their elders. Indeed, the younger you are, the greater the solicitude with which you must take care to guard your life in sincerity of the fear of the Lord. For it is written: “A young man according to his way, even when he has grown old, will not depart from it.” What you have learned as a young man, you will practice in old age.

On fleeing the companionship of women.
Chapter 2.

But what shall I say about familiarity with women, which is most destructive to the priests of God, since the decrees of the holy fathers forbid it to them by a sufficiently strict prohibition? If I set before you the admonitions of the most blessed father Jerome, you will immediately cry out: “I cannot govern a household without the service of a maidservant; I am not able to prepare food for myself; I do not want to clean dirty clothes. Why,” you say, “do you bring forward Jerome’s precepts against me, which bind no one except the willing? Who appointed Jerome as my lawgiver?” The sacrosanct universal Church, I say, approves his sayings; it places among the canons his precepts set forth on this matter; and do you presume to mutter against so great an authority? But you will say: “Contrary custom has abrogated this law, and after a long time it must be regarded as law.” But iniquity lies to itself, and by foolish reasoning is deceived in itself. A custom contrary to good morals can never either take away or establish law. And what is so contrary to priestly honor as to delight in the household intimacy of young women? Chastity is endangered where the society of priests passes among little women. For the first and strongest temptations of clerics arise from frequent access to women. Foolish presumption has overthrown many, who, as I might say, have handed the instruments of victory to their enemies with their own hands. Danger is avoided with the greatest difficulty if the occasion is not avoided. For while a woman stays with a cleric, and sits alone with him alone, prepares food for him as he eats, arranges his little bed as he sleeps, speaks with him, smiles at him, she supplies the kindling of lust. However holy a woman may be, she must always be feared by a cleric. For just as sparks leap out from coals, so the poison of concupiscence spreads from the constant familiarity of women. A woman familiar with a chaste man is the gate of the devil, the door of hell, the way of iniquity, the dreadful sting of the scorpion, and a harmful kind of deception. When she comes near she pricks; when she speaks she inflames; when she touches she burns fiercely

...burns fiercely. We do not condemn the sex, but we warn of the danger to chastity. We read that Saint Augustine fled women so greatly that he would not allow even his own sister to enter his episcopal house. We ourselves have known several priests who for many years governed both their household and the people entrusted to them innocently, without the service of a maidservant. Nor indeed will it be difficult for you to imitate what you see others have practiced. Beware, therefore, and do not draw near to the company of young women, since it is difficult to live chastely even among old women.

Remember with whom you speak at the altar, and do not pour out in kisses for prostitutes a mouth consecrated to divine ministries. It is most shameful, with hands consecrated by the oil of holy anointing, by which the Son of God is held and sacrificed, to touch a woman with polluted contacts. Do not give a stain to your glory and your name as a reproach to all. Everyone in public, and in the field the peasant plowmen and vine-dressers, will daily tear you fiercely apart if, as a young man, you presume to live with women, especially younger ones. You will be a proverb and a mockery to all the people if you often enter the dwellings of young women.

Against your invisible enemy, who always flies around your side laying snares, keep watch at every time, and according to Naso, resist evils at their beginnings, when the temptations are still weaker. For, as we see, after a cleric has once offered his body to the embraces of prostitutes, rarely or never does he return to perfect amendment. For all who are troubled by perverse love, the longer they have lain in the filth of lust, the more difficultly do they return to health of mind. A vice strongly grown old fixes its roots in the human heart.

O how many priests there are today in the Church of God who, submerged in the depth of lust, despise the long-suffering of God, as it is written: When the impious man has come into the depths of evils, he despises. There are, no doubt, many among clerics defiled by lust who, although at the beginning of their promotion they resolved to live chastely, deceived by negligent guarding of the senses, now without any fear of God consort with prostitutes as if licitly and by custom. Nothing is worse than the custom of sin, by which the gravest crimes come to be esteemed as if very slight.

The strongest bond of sin is the habit of sinning, which grows with time and from delay takes increase of strength. Therefore he who said in verse gave very good counsel against the fires of the flesh: Resist at the beginnings; medicine is prepared too late when evils have gained strength through long delays. But hasten, and do not defer yourself to coming hours: he who is not ready today will be less ready tomorrow. Do not delay to be converted to the Lord, says Scripture, and do not put it off from day to day, since the longer you have delayed repentance, the less fit you will be for repenting.

Now ascend the watchtower of your mind, and see the madness of foolish priests who are led captive by base concubines, so that they do not do whatever they wish, but what is commanded them by prostitutes. You would meanwhile see a solid cleric sitting beside his concubine, gazing at her with open mouth; and if she smiles at him, he laughs; if she becomes angry, he flatters her until she is reconciled to favor. The unhappy priest is led captive by a woman, and he who ought to have ruled others is overcome by the dominion of his maidservant.

O who would believe what great groans the wretch emits amid these things, whenever he thinks of casting off this harshest yoke of the arch-devil, what pains he carries in his heart? Sometimes, indeed, he is touched by such great displeasure against his beast that he plans to drive her away from himself on the next day. Meanwhile he gives insulting words and receives them; finally after words they come to blows: he strikes and is struck back; he curses and is blasphemed. O astonishing spectacle: the hands of a prostitute tear, strike, and bruise the head of a priest, which a prince is not permitted to touch.

Finally, after curses and blows, the woman turns herself to weeping; with the devil pouring them in, she sheds tears, until at last she recalls the unhappy captive to mercy and renders him appeasable to herself. You would then see the cleric suddenly changed, and so moved by the tears of his concubine that he who had suffered the injury is the first to beg forgiveness. Then a full table is set out, a feast is renewed, sighs are mixed with words, and the bargains of new nuptials are negotiated. Amid these things the solid priest sits disputing about patience and gentleness; he sighs that he has offended against his mistress, and labors with all his strength to recover her favor.

What is done after the friendship has been renewed, you may rather think than I may say. There is no fear of God before the eyes of a priest bound by the chains of a concubine; there is no dread of the punishments of hell, no thought of priestly dignity, no memory of public honor. This vice of incontinence has now prevailed so much among the clergy that what is public has almost begun to be lawful. Wretched condition of the human race: those who ought to have corrected the crimes of others commit graver ones themselves.

Shameful to say, in villages and cities priests sit openly, notorious for intercourse with their concubines. So great a number of them is found today that if some prelate wished to correct such men in his own diocese alone, overcome by sheer multitude, he would be frightened away from his purpose. If you entered the house of any one of such men, you would sometimes come upon the priest carrying in his arms his little child, the witness of his fatherly fornication, and, while reading the canonical hours, consoling the crying child with words and songs. You would also see the father, rising from bed, taking the ill-fated offspring onto his knees, offering kisses, placing him on his right knee and the breviary on his left. While praying, he smiles back at the smiling infant, soothes him when he cries, and, when he is silent, addresses him often with words and nods.

Meanwhile the coaxing mother runs up; the sweet pledge snatches the book from the father’s hand, handles the leaves, tears them, and, with saliva flowing from his mouth, stains it with his parent’s permission. Amid all this there is no devotion in the one praying, no attention given to the words; instead, all concern is spent in consoling the crying child. The words fall confused from the mouth of the one praying, interrupted by the infant’s frequent kisses, so that when the prayer is finished he scarcely remembers that he has prayed.

Meanwhile the sacristan rings the bell; the altar is prepared for sacrifice; the priest is awaited by the people in the church. The priest rushes in, his hands washed in place of the child, puts on the sacred vestments with all the haste he can, and performs the sacrifice of God with a distracted mind. O conscience, where do you remain meanwhile? Where do you lie despised? O miserable man, you who handle the Son of God with polluted hands, how long will you wound, tear, and destroy your soul? With what rashness, finally, do you approach the altar, polluted in body and soul?

--- Page 019 ---

If the great mercy of almighty God did not spare you for a time, the angels, provoked, would strike you at the altar with grave indignation. Where is the vow of perpetual chastity that you promised into the hands of the bishop when he ordained you? Where is the reverence due to the priesthood you have assumed? You have sinned: why do you not do penance? How long will you despise the long-suffering of God? Or do you not know that the patience of God leads you to penance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart, you store up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath and of the just judgment of Jesus Christ.

I think, indeed, that you plan to repent in the future; but you give no thought at all to how near death is, the cutter-off of penance. Truly, the merciful God who promised pardon to the penitent did not pledge tomorrow’s day to the one who delays. But a cleric whom a cohabiting woman has once led away captive she never, or rarely, releases as free unless death intervenes.

I pass over the fact that many, through the deceits and sorceries of their concubines, are often driven so far out of their minds that they cannot in any way shake themselves free from those women’s most shameful love. The hearing shudders at the crimes of harlots, and the purity of chastity cannot bear to hear what things are sometimes perpetrated by priests’ concubines. They prepare food for their masters from menstrual blood, and while they desire to entice them to love them, they turn them toward colic and madness. With dreadful sorceries and superstitions they entangle priests in their lusts, until, their strength gradually drained away, they render them utterly useless.

Blessed is the man whom God has guarded from an evil and lustful woman. There is no wickedness above the malice of a woman who enjoys evil loves. Whatever she devises, she attempts; whatever she can do, she dares. No one believes what efforts, ways, and inventions she uses, so that she may cast down, by an indissoluble bond to her pleasures, the man whom she plans to deceive. She joins herself to her master for the sake of her own desires, not because she loves him, but because she covets. For the crowd of concubines follows the prey, not the priest. So long as he is rich and abounds in all things, she does not leave her master’s side; but if he begins to lack bread, the concubine turns herself to another. Sometimes, however, because of the children, the priest and the concubine are glued together to such a degree that by no means can they be separated from one another, except by death.

You know what happened to your first preceptor. He, wretchedly seduced by the savage love of that prostitute whom you know, since he did not dare live with her openly for fear of her husband, despised his homeland, home, relatives, and the benefices he had, and secretly fled away with her so that he might enjoy her freely. And to this day no one knows where he has gone.

I know a certain priest who kept concubines. When at one time I rebuked him for his sin, between myself and him, I finally brought him to the point that he was willing to promise perpetual continence from then on into my hands. But when I urged him to cast out his concubine at once, he answered, “I cannot.” “Why?” I said. “If I cast her out,” he said, “who would look after my little children?” For he already had two boys by her. “I am poor,” he said; “I cannot hire people to care for them for wages. Therefore I will not cast out their mother until the little children grow up, lest I be guilty of bloodshed. Yet I will be chaste with her as with a chaste woman, and from now on I will be joined to her in no intercourse.” And I said, “What will you do if, before these children grow up, others arise in their place?” He swore that he wished to take care that this not happen. “You will not amend your fault,” I said, “unless you cast out the concubine.” It is difficult to leave habitual things behind and, while the occasion is present, to abstain from former vices. No one will warm a serpent in his bosom and remain unharmed. The man went away to his house, and to this day he has not abandoned his domestic crimes.

I have considered the works of God: whom he has despised, no one can correct. So difficult is it for a priest who has once fallen into fornication to rise again to true chastity that among all of them you can scarcely find one. For according to Scripture, “He who commits sin is the servant of sin,” and he who once submits to filthy love is scarcely ever wholly set free. For fornication is more easily fled than corrected. Therefore I warn you and entreat you to flee the company of women, lest you lose the reputation of a good name. For once a priest has been disgraced by the charge of fornication, he will never recover the honor he has lost. Avoid even conversations with women; decline frequent greetings in the marketplace, because by light words one comes to the worst deeds. For many have been deceived by a woman’s careless speech.

Be watchful at all times, and never think that you should trust yourself so long as you live in the flesh. On the remedy against lust.
Chapter 3.

Physicians have been accustomed first to show the sick the seriousness of their disease by careful discussion, and only then to apply the help of remedies. For once the seriousness of the illness is known, the bitterness of the potion causes less fear. It follows now, therefore, that we should think about the remedy. For the flesh always desires against the spirit, and unless it is restrained by salutary exercise, it casts both itself and him down into the depth of damnation.

Hence the poet aptly admonishes us, saying: “If you take away idleness, Cupid’s bows have perished; his torches lie despised and without light.” Holy Scripture also declares that every idle person is in his desires. Therefore anyone who wishes to extinguish the vice of lust must continually exercise himself in some holy work. For idleness is the enemy of the soul. Hence blessed Jerome admonishes someone, saying: “Always do some good thing, so that the devil may find you occupied.” The best remedy against evil thoughts is, indeed, occupation in some holy work. For when the mind is pleasantly intent on some good exercise, it does not think of anything else. No access will lie open to the devil if the mind is occupied in some exercise.

Take an example from holy David: when occupied with matters of war he subdued lust; when idle, remaining at home, he committed adultery. When the hand is free from work, the mind grows weak in thought. Therefore, if you wish to conquer lust, flee idleness. As for the exercises to which you ought to adapt your time, receive them in a few words, if you please. Let yourself be seen rarely in public; accustom yourself to remain at home and to devote yourself to readings. Be quiet in mind and body. Do not run about through the streets and the houses of neighbors; do not take part in the tales of rustics. For presence lessens reputation, and excessive familiarity with a priest breeds contempt among the people. A rare going-out into public, however, will display for you among the people great reverence for your maturity. Live in such a way that many may be edified by your example.

In the midst of elders, speak little, because talkativeness is often reproved where silence is praised. Indeed, among elders it befits a young man to listen, not to speak. Do not desire to seem wise in the company of priests, lest before them you be confounded as foolish. Do not be wordy and arrogant. When questioned, answer with humility and goodwill, and do not, in the manner of ranters, be clamorous in voice while you speak. Do not love forced laughter, and always and everywhere keep yourself from every dishonorable levity. Avoid useless conversations and vain words, knowing that for every idle word you have spoken you will have to render account to God. Let your speech be moderate, and do not let an oath be made in vain.

Do not enter taverns with rustics, because that is not permitted to your honor. For it is very unfitting that one who ought to rebuke sinners should, by his presence, wish to show himself a patron of sins. For entry into taverns is forbidden to priests by the holy fathers, unless they are on a journey. It is shameful for a priest of God to enter a tavern with rustics, and for the mouth that has been consecrated to God to be defiled by impure speech. The discourse of a preacher is easily despised if among rustics

if it is poured out familiarly among rustics. From the habit of familiarity they will begin to despise you and to regard you as one of their own. For no reverence is paid to superiors if their life is confined among their subjects. It is fitting that one who presides should excel all others in a certain reverence and maturity, and wisely avoid too great a familiarity with his subjects. Indeed, the preeminence of authority is broken when it is indiscreetly mixed up with the actions of subjects. Slander no one, and do not take scandal against anyone. It is shameful for a priest to pollute his tongue with slander, since it has been set at the altar to pray for all. Nor should you lend an ear to slanderers, because the slanderer and the willing hearer are worthy of equal condemnation. The one carries the devil on his tongue, the other in his ear. Let such a person learn that he cannot readily slander when he sees that you are unwilling to listen. Do not be hasty in speech, and do not stand in the company of slanderers, because they are hateful to almighty God. Hedge your ears about with thorns, lest you hear the words of slanderers. Let no lie come forth from your mouth; let no foul speech proceed from you, but be a guardian of your tongue, lest you offend in speech. Do not be slothful or lazy, nor given to sleep and idleness, but constantly run forward in the fervor of the Holy Spirit, knowing that this is proper to a priest: to have care for the needy. Have gold not so that you may keep it, but so that you may distribute it in alms for the poor. Let the bellies of the hungry poor praise you, not the belching banquets of the rich. Let your table be with the poor and needy, for in such persons Christ is received, and the heavenly court is honored. Avoid the banquets of the proud rich, especially of the powerful, at which nothing is done concerning the salvation of souls and nothing at all is discussed concerning spiritual conduct. Let your clothing be honorable, not costly. In giving alms, do not seek glory from human beings, but expect praise and reward from God alone. Be a father of the poor and a guardian of orphans.

--- Page 037 ---
Let no poor person depart from you unconsoled. Do not be a striker, nor quarrelsome among the people; injure no one unjustly, but do good to everyone as much as you can, even to enemies. Never flatter sinners, but rebuke them as one who has received authority from God. Do not be a striker of the consciences of the people entrusted to you, nor pronounce the word of God indiscreetly, but with the moderation of piety. Be merciful to penitents, and do not crush the bruised reed, knowing that you have undertaken the care of the weak, not of the strong. For it is better to send a penitent with mercy to purgatory than, by the confessor’s severity, to consign one in despair to hell. Do not neglect to pray your canonical hours with reverence and devotion at the times established by the Church. Likewise, do not omit to read the Hours of the Blessed Mary ever Virgin, the vigils of the dead, and the other things owed in due order by the priestly office. Have care for your household in the fear of God; if you have a household, persuade it by word and example to preserve modesty and honorable conduct. Indeed, the reputation of a priest is harmed among the people when the people speak ill of his household.

Let your speech be strengthened by the testimony of the Scriptures, because whatever is prescribed apart from the Holy Scriptures is despised with the same ease with which it is proved. I do not want you to imitate those who, without learning, frequently cite tales and phantoms of dreams in church, and are ignorant of the mysteries of the divine Scriptures. Babbling and talkative priests, speaking without reason, do not build up the people; they labor to draw people into admiration of themselves by empty speeches and Aesop’s fables. For it is a foolish and damnable abuse, once the Scriptures and the Gospel have been set aside, to teach the Christian people the fables of Aesop and the fabricated deeds of the Romans. Nor should you wonder if the unlearned people hear these things more willingly than the Scriptures, since you read that the Apostle foretold this. “There will be a time,” he says, “when they will not endure sound doctrine, but according to their own desires they will heap up teachers for themselves, itching in their ears; and indeed they will turn their hearing away from the truth, but will be turned to fables.” Hence Jerome says: “To roll out words and, by speed of speaking, to create admiration for oneself among the unlearned crowd belongs to uneducated men.” A worn-out brow often explains what it does not know.

Often he explains what he does not know; and when he has persuaded others, he also claims for himself a knowledge that he does not possess. Nothing is so easy as to deceive a base little crowd and an unlearned assembly by fluency of tongue; whatever it does not understand, it admires all the more. But you should speak the things that befit sound doctrine, and do not seek the applause of the ignorant if you desire to find a place among the learned. Do not imprudently desire the care of souls, unless you have first learned to govern both yourself and others justly according to ecclesiastical ordinance.

For the office of teaching the people is subject to many dangers and is troubled by daily anxieties. The ruler of souls must be without reproach, as God’s steward: not proud, not wrathful, not given to wine, not violent, not greedy for shameful gain, but hospitable, kind, sober, just, holy, continent, embracing the faithful word that accords with doctrine, so that he may be able to exhort in sound doctrine and refute those who contradict it. The one appointed as ruler of souls must teach no less by example than by speech. Even a robber can accuse avarice; but only the man who does what he teaches can correct it. He is a dainty master who disputes about fasting with a full belly. Therefore life must be sought before doctrine, because one teaches more fully by deed than by speech. For the most excellent kind of teaching is the demonstration of example. And indeed the Lord Jesus first acted, and afterward taught. He uses the greatest authority in teaching whose speech is confirmed by the witness of his work. The ruler’s speech is readily received by his subjects if it is supported by uprightness of conduct. But the man who teaches well and lives badly condemns himself by the sentence of his own mouth, and he does not turn his subjects away from bad training.

Consider at the same time how dangerous the care of souls is, especially in these times, when iniquity abounds everywhere and the charity of many has grown cold. For the days are evil, and men are slow to justice; the whole world lies in wickedness, and faith on earth is rare. Amid these things the word of God is despised by the people; the instruction of the Christian religion is neglected; priestly authority is scorned. Lay life, exposed to a thousand sins and crimes, neglects the remedies of repentance, applies itself to tricks and frauds, and despises the admonitions of priests.

And unless the ruler of souls diligently proclaims to the people committed to him their crimes and the punishments due to sinners, before God he will also be guilty of their blood. And after these things God will bring all things into judgment, where the pastor of souls is compelled to render an account for each person; and if he has been negligent in this world, he is condemned more severely. Therefore it is safer to flee humbly from the care and rule of souls than to usurp it arrogantly. For if each person can scarcely suffice to render an account of his own life in that dreadful judgment, how will he answer for so many thousands committed to him? O how well he lives who lives for himself.

It seems good and pleasant among men to live in this world as a bishop, abbot, pastor, or any prelate whatever; but who will be able to think adequately how terrible the end of the negligent man is? For a most severe judgment will be made against those who are in authority, and the powerful will suffer powerful torments. He passes through well who lives simply, to whom no earthly honor is entrusted, and to whom no care of souls is committed. For pastoral care is never without danger as long as one lives in this world. The greatest of all dangers is this: when the soul, separated from the body by death, must be presented before God, the highest shepherd, for the earthly shepherd to give an account.

Here now the crisis of final damnation threatens, when an account is demanded of stewardship badly administered, and the blood of many sheep is required from the hand of one shepherd. What then will the unskilled shepherd be able to answer to the charges, he who was negligent in the care of the sheep? He must fall silent, because he has no answer to give. Therefore, brother, if you consider yourself fit for the care of souls, if you are preparing to govern the church of Mernicen, know that you are to be brought into judgment for these things, and that according to the merit of your life you will receive either glory or torment.

But believe me: the man dies secure who, setting aside the cares of others, lives for himself alone in the fear of God. For to the one to whom much is entrusted, much will also be required from him. A priest, especially one who has the care of souls, is God’s herald, so that he may announce the will of the Almighty to the people and correct those who sin against the law of the Lord by words of rebuke. Therefore he must be learned and most constant, lest he either lapse through ignorance or, through inconstancy, fear the hatred of the perverse. It is shameful

For it is shameful for a priest, out of fear of the people, to keep silent the words of God. A priest should use a little wine, lest, if he devotes himself to drinking bouts, he become a disgrace among the people. For the old law condemns drunken priests and keeps them from access to the altar. Likewise in the Council of Agde it is read as follows: “Before all things drunkenness is to be avoided by clerics, for it is the tinder and nurse of all vices. And so, whoever is found to have been drunken, as his rank permits, shall either be removed from communion for the space of thirty days or be subjected to bodily punishment.”

For wine is a luxurious thing, and drunkenness is disgraceful; everyone who is entangled in these things will not be wise. He who burns without wine, with the fire of youth, and is inflamed by the heat of the blood, and is strong in body, should flee wine as poison, lest, suffocated by a manifold fire, he fall into the mire of lust.

A stomach heated with wine stirs up desire, dulls the sense, and weakens the head and the whole body. A fat belly produces a thin understanding. Therefore, when you are called and come to the banquets of priests, do not forget clerical honor, but show yourself modest and restrained in all things. Take care that you have neither an itching tongue nor itching ears, that is, that you neither detract from others yourself nor lend your hearing to those who do. Know that all the things you say about others are to be turned back upon yourself, and perhaps you will be found blameworthy in the very things you used to reproach in others.

But perhaps you will say: “I cannot do injury to those who report such things, because anyone who thinks about stopping everyone’s lips is attempting the impossible. What, then, is others’ detraction to me? Surely I cannot keep people from speaking?” But, as we said above, conduct yourself before a detractor in such a way that he learns not to detract from anyone, when he sees that you do not gladly listen to what he says. “Do not mingle with detractors,” says Solomon, “for their destruction will come suddenly.” And who knows the ruin of both: both, that is, of the one who detracts and of the one who lends an ear to the detractor?

Be mature in morals and conduct, sparing and prudent in words, discerning in silence and restraint. Be diligent in searching out the mysteries of the Scriptures; and if any matter of doubt occurs to you as you read, know that it should be referred to the interpretations of the elders. You have nearby the school of the most ancient city of Trier, in which there is an abundance of learned men; to them you will be able to have recourse in the ambiguity of questions.

Let nothing be dearer to you than knowledge, because the fruit of wisdom is eternal, but the use of riches is brief and momentary. Let there be no one who keeps you from salutary study: not mother, not brother, not sister. Do the work of an evangelist; learn what you are to teach, lest you prove to be a shepherd and an idol. You are one man and have no second self; for whom, then, will you store up riches? Live temperately. Do not frequent weddings and immoderate banquets, for they strip the mind of virtues and leave the purse empty of coins.

Procure for yourself, from the alms offered to you, books of divine Scripture, so that, made learned by constant reading of them, you may be able worthily to instruct the people entrusted to you, and to lift yourself up through contemplation to heavenly things. Love the knowledge of the Scriptures, and it will exalt you before God and human beings. Do not consent to the pleasures of the flesh; restrain the impulse of fury; and never forget death.

What a Priest Ought to Be Like in His Inner Conduct.
Chapter 7.

But since it is not enough for a minister of Christ to lead an honorable life before human beings, unless he also strives to appear before God clean from every crime, I exhort you to direct your heart in the fear of the Almighty and never admit any thoughts against God. For a priest of God ought to be holy in mind and body, and clean in thought. Hence the Lord says: “Be holy, because I am holy, the Lord your God, who sanctify you.” And Peter, the chief apostle, says: “You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people of acquisition, that you may proclaim the virtues of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light.”

Let charity hold the first place in your heart, for it is the fullness of the law and the completion of the prophets. For the greatest of all virtues is charity, which consists in love of God and neighbor.

[segment 18]
--- Page 042 ---

But God is to be loved with the whole heart, for his own sake, and one’s neighbor for God’s sake. No one truly loves God if he hates his neighbor. For Christ is both God and man. Therefore he does not love the whole Christ who hates a human being. God accepts no good work unless the flame of charity kindles it on the altar of the heart. For charity holds the chief place among all the virtues.

Humility follows it, which is remembered as the guardian of the virtues. So great is the excellence of this virtue that every other virtue quickly perishes unless it is strengthened by the foundation of humility. For holy Pope Gregory says: He who gathers virtues without humility carries dust into the wind. From the very place where he seems to carry something, there he is more grievously blinded. Therefore charity is the greatest ornament of a priest, and humility the most precious pearl in his garment. You also, brother, display these twin virtues in yourself, and you will be a fruitful preacher in the Church of God. Whatever you say, you will readily persuade your hearers, if you have charity and humility in your heart and diligently show them in your work.

Let accursed ambition have no place in your heart; once it has invaded the mind, it so blinds it that it brings forgetfulness of God’s salvation and of its own. Be a Christian lover of human beings and an enemy of vices. Keep the commandments of God, and do not neglect ecclesiastical sanctions. Honor all people for God’s sake, and never say that anyone should be despised. What you do not wish to be done to you, do not do to another. Let not the sun go down upon your anger. Flee the flattery of the rich, and never stroke sinners. Deny yourself your desires, so that you may become a disciple of Christ. Chastise your body, according to the apostle, bringing it into servitude. Scorn pleasures; love chaste fasting. Be devoted to the poor; feed the hungry; clothe the naked; visit the sick; free the captive; console the sorrowful; bury the dead. Do not love the world; do not seek riches; do not run after vanity. For no one serving as God’s soldier entangles himself in secular affairs. Do injury to no one, but learn patiently to bear injuries inflicted on you, following the example of your Lord, who, hanging on the cross, prayed for his persecutors, that they might not perish. Have peace with all people,

but concord and unanimity only with the devout. Do not be proud and arrogant, not sleepy and lazy, not drunken and gluttonous, not a slanderer, and not one who rashly judges anyone. Guard your actions at every hour, and let the memory of the things to come always turn in your thought. For Scripture says: Son, remember your last things, and you will not sin forever. Therefore always think that you are going to die; fear the day of judgment; dread the torments of hell. If you do these things, you will easily overcome all temptations of the flesh. Scorn the pleasures of the world, so that you may be able to enter rejoicing into the joys of heavenly happiness.

Do not deceive your soul: no one can rejoice here with the world and afterward reign with Christ. For through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God. Therefore, despising present things, let us hasten with every desire toward eternal joys. Keep your heart with all vigilance, because life proceeds from it. If you have been overtaken by some fault, do not delay to seek the remedies of penitence and to cleanse yourself in confession through true contrition.

Strive to enter by the narrow way into life, that is, not to fulfill the desires of the flesh, to hate the world, and to live by the spirit. You have been called to serve God as a soldier against the vices of the flesh. Do not rest before victory; do not cease from the battle, because no one will be crowned unless he is victorious. In this life, however, there is no secure victory, but continual battle. Whoever perseveres to the end will be saved. At his end a man will be tested. The life of a human being upon earth is military service. As a soldier of the highest king, it is not permitted to you to desert his contest. A good soldier follows his king wherever he goes. Where, then, is your king? In heaven. Why do you not follow him? What are you doing on earth, if you are a soldier of the heavenly, supreme king? Why do you not follow your Lord? He himself says: Whoever ministers to me, let him follow me; and where I am, there also my minister will be. “Let him follow,” he says, not with the steps of the feet, but with the movements of the affections. The apostle also says: No one serving as God’s soldier entangles himself in secular affairs, so that he may please the one to whom he has proved himself. Therefore, just as those who serve an earthly king obey all his commands, and wherever they are sent are ready and prompt, no one ...

You, therefore, who await the unfading crown of victory, labor like a good soldier of Jesus Christ. Let no care of earthly anxiety weigh you down; let no enticement of fleshly pleasure touch your mind. For nothing is happier than the soldier of Christ, to whom the kingdoms of heaven are promised; nothing more laborious than one who daily faces peril to his life; nothing stronger than one who conquers the devil; nothing weaker than one who is overcome by the flesh. Let no household call you away, no kinship hinder you, no flesh constrain you. I want you to be without anxiety, so that you may freely think upon the things that are God’s. Naked and unencumbered, fly to heaven, because your mother bore you naked and the earth will receive you naked. Love what you cannot lose; despise what you cannot hold; seek that in which you may rejoice happily forever. Live in such a way that you do not fear to die. A good conscience does not fear death, because it knows that through death it will come to the eternal joys of the heavenly homeland. Now, most beloved brother, you have what you asked of me. You have some small pattern of good conduct upon which you may impress yourself; you have a standard of holy exhortation to which you may diligently have recourse. I have done what I could, not as much as I wished. For the rest, live according to the Gospel and the constitutions of the holy fathers, in which the whole form of Christian perfection is seen.

Farewell, mindful of us. From Sponheim, on the Kalends of April, in the year of the Lord 1486.
