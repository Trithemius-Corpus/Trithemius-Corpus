Take care to enlarge your affection as much as you can by loving, since you can by no means comprehend God by understanding, for he stands far above every intellect. Yet access to him lies open to you by loving, if you are pure, if gentle, if tranquil, if released from all things, and rightly united to your very self.

I would not have you be deceived by taste, since whatever you see, whatever you grasp, whatever you say, is not God; and what he is, you, clothed in a frail little body, cannot know, nor understand, nor in any way express, even if you were endowed with all the tongues of the world. Strive nevertheless with prudent sobriety, only as one wholly purified from the affections of the flesh, loving no one in this world except God alone, actually stripped of passions, calm and tranquil, quiet without any disturbance. The ray of the highest good has poured its goodness into you, by which God himself is shown to you to be invisible, infinite, incomprehensible light, the truth of every truth and the cause of all things. Its splendor, or rather its shadow, is that visible and finite light, the cause of visible things, whose nature is to disclose all other things to the rest. But to God, the invisible light, all things are truly and clearly visible through himself: he enlightens every man coming into this world, through whom all things were made, and without whom nothing was made that was made.

But, O reason, do not trust the senses when you go on investigating, with mystical concern, the principle of all things, God, the unfailing light. For all the senses of the body are dulled, and perceive nothing of the highest truth. Sight did not sufficiently report to you about light; the others did not report at all. Everything spiritual is far removed from the senses.

¶ Ascend to that lofty watchtower of the mind, O reason, friend of truth, once the senses have been entirely lulled. Since it is a certain intellectual light and luminous understanding, indeed illuminated by the invisible light through a certain participation, whose object is intelligible light itself, it can lead you by the hand to the summit of the divine darkness, in which the highest Truth inhabits inaccessible light. Turn to the mind itself, wholly purified from every iniquity; it will teach you, O reason, that you are a certain rational light and a shining reason, illuminated by that invisible light itself which shines in darkness. But since, by reasoning, you seek with such eagerness the principle of light as your origin, and since that principle is above all reason and admits absolutely no positive assertion of the things that are or of the things that are not, you must, with all your powers left behind, ascend even above the mind, through an affection free and released from all things, purified from every entanglement either of carnal love or of hatred of any kind, to that supreme ray of divine inspiration. Thus, in the highest truth, which is itself also the highest certainty and an utterly incomprehensible brightness, you may find the truth of light and a brightness beyond all estimation, whose brightness and truth indeed transcend every human and angelic intellect by far beyond comparison.

Its immense abundance of goodness and truth, without detriment to itself, illuminates the minds of those truly seeking it, and powerfully and sweetly drives upward with the invisible fire of its love the very soul which it has found to be purified from all evil and carnal passions. But since the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the mind that thinks many things, one must not leap so suddenly into so sublime a contemplation of divine things; rather, the mind warns that one must ascend gradually, with the inner affection first purified, and then suitable exercises observed at intervals, lest it happen that one is darkened and utterly blinded by excessive splendor.

Our spirit, however, which I would rightly have called by a more excellent kind, is not only summoned by reason to heavenly beauty, when, that is, it argues from things known to the senses that bodily beauty, as something placed in deformity, distance, and changeability, is necessarily imperfect and therefore proceeds from incorporeal beauty.
