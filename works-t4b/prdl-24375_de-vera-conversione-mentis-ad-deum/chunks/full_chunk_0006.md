Although, however, the supreme and unchangeable good, existing far above every affirmation and negation, can be penetrated by no comparison or likeness by any mortal, since there is plainly no proportion at all between the finite and the infinite, the creature and the creator, or man and God, nevertheless in the holy Scriptures it admits many comparisons and likenesses concerning itself, because of the slowness of our understanding, although none at all of the things said of it by our presumed license belongs to it. Not only have our princes and prophets used such things in mystical comparisons and likenesses concerning the highest principle of all things, but so too have all the wiser philosophers of the gentiles, who were able to understand that there is one God, whom they held should be worshiped invisibly by nothing other than devotion of mind.

Among these was Empedocles, who, wishing by a certain imaginable likeness to persuade men that God is infinite and immeasurable, and wholly incomprehensible, fashioned a sphere whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. When he sets the structure of this before a weak mind, he makes heaven and earth narrow, and, enclosed within the greatness of that image, at once stupefied and shaken with fear, it humbly adores the divine majesty. And another man, when he attempted to demonstrate the same thing by mathematical reasoning, proposed that one imagine, from a straight line of infinite length, a circle than which no greater or smaller can be given, while the straightness of that line itself is preserved. Although the mind utterly recoils from forming this within itself as an impossible thought, nevertheless it understands by its own nature that from that line there necessarily arises a circle than which no greater or smaller can be given, while the line remains.

For this reason, in the holy Scriptures also, various comparisons and likenesses concerning God are set down; their cause is wrapped in mystical and secret wisdom, known to few, and indeed only to clean hearts, lest the hidden things of the divine mysteries be easily seized by the unclean. These things are to be revealed only to pure lovers of holiness, who, destroying every childish

phantasy in sacred contemplations entirely, are able to transcend the summit of the mind and to arrive at the utterly simple beauty of the highest goodness, without any imagination or composition.

For this reason Saint Dionysius, bishop and martyr of Christ, affirms a twofold theology. One is secret and mystical, signifying and truly perfective, because through love it sweetly carries the purified soul above mind and intellect. This theology is accessible to few and friendly to still fewer, namely only to those who, crucifying their flesh with its vices and desires, and trampling underfoot, for the love of God, all the clamor of the tumultuous age, profess that theology not in words but in deeds. The other is more familiar, apparent, and receptive, physical through letters and writings from men to men. We see that today it is held in sufficient honor among many, though it is less powerful than the first in summoning the soul to divine love. A passage from the second to the first must be prepared for us by daily labor; in the first alone is the salvation of the soul preserved.

¶ For by more diligent inquiry and by love one must pass from the created beauty of the world, which physical theology contemplates, to the ineffable beauty of the highest good, which mystical theology considers. There the mind, forgetful of all things that are in the world and released from all things, borne upward by the burning of purest love, begins sweetly, by affection, to admire the beauty of the divine light. This is your theology, cloistered men; this singular study of divine love is enjoined particularly upon you before others, and all your studies must be ordered chiefly toward it.

But alas, I am ashamed to recall the studies of cloistered men, who have turned holy theology into destructive battology: men who pursue leisure and tales, whose god is the belly, for whom in this life nothing is sweeter than eating and drinking, and being fattened with the pleasures of the flesh contrary to regular discipline. The lesser men learn these things from their superiors, when abbots and the other pastors of souls, forgetful of God and unmindful of their own salvation, under the shadow of piety most zealously pursue pride and avarice.
