We must therefore ascend, fathers, to that highest good which every soul desires, and in which alone it will obtain eternal blessedness, if, turned toward it through love, it departs from the body. Therefore let whoever can enter and advance into the inner places do so, leaving outside the sight of the eyes, and no longer looking back behind him at the customary spectacles of the senses. For one who beholds supreme beauty on the mountain of contemplation must look thereafter on absolutely nothing of bodily beauty, but, knowing bodily things to be images, traces, and shadows, must flee altogether to that of which these are works and likenesses. For created beauty ought not to hold the mind, but to lead it step by step to the uncreated; otherwise one must cry out with Ulysses, “Let us go hence, friends, fleeing to our sweet homeland.” But our homeland is there from which we came, as the holy Apostle also says: “Here we have no lasting city, but we seek the one to come.” We must not flee to it on foot, nor again prepare horses for riding, nor ships for sailing. Rather, we must let all these things go, and not even look ahead, but, with the sight of the body closed, take up and awaken in its place another sight, the inner sight of the mind, which all indeed possess, but very few use.

Yet one must not rashly burst forth toward the unfamiliar light of divine brightness, since that inward eye, suddenly awakened, can hardly look upon very bright things. Therefore the soul must be accustomed to divine spectacles and brought into them: first, indeed, by considering beautiful pursuits; then beautiful works, works, I say, not those made by the arts, but those done by upright men, by whose example, stirred to love of virtue, you may become an imitator of the goodness you have observed in them. Examine also what sort your own soul is; recall yourself into yourself and contemplate. But if you do not yet recognize yourself as beautiful and purified from every foulness of bodily familiarity, you will imitate the sculptor, who, when he desires a beautiful statue, cuts away one part.

Another part he straightens, and with polishing tools he scrapes away another; another part he smooths and wipes clean, until he expresses a beautiful face in the statue. So too you: remove what is superfluous; straighten what is crooked; by cleansing, brighten what is dark; and do not cease laboring over your statue until the radiance of divine virtue shines forth for you, and until you discern holy temperance firmly seated in pure majesty. But when you have become clean, and now dwell pure with yourself, having, that is, nothing to prevent you from thus being made one, and not again having anything else mingled within you with yourself, but being wholly free, abstracted, and released from all passions of the flesh and evil affections, then your inner sight itself, now rightly ordered and purified, can in some measure lift the mind to contemplation of the uncircumscribable light.

But if by chance the eye of the mind, which is called either intellect or affection, is still stained with filth and not yet purified, or, weak through sloth, is directed toward that most brilliant spectacle, it will at once grow dim and discern nothing. For one eye of the mind is called intellect, the other affection; unless both have been lawfully cleansed, they will render the one ascending to contemplation of divine things blind rather than a participant in the divine light, and mad rather than enlightened. For whoever, still given over to the passions of the flesh, and with them not yet truly mortified in spirit according to the monastic rule, rashly approaches the contemplation of uncircumscribable beauty, will at once be struck back by the immeasurable light and go away blind, full of great errors.

For the holy prophet David says: “If I have looked upon iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me.” And the wise man, drawing a conclusion relevant to our purpose, says: “Wisdom will not enter a malicious soul, nor dwell in a body subject to sins.” Now attend to yourselves and to your profession, cloistered men, who have been called into the portion of the contemplative life and, for that reason, freed from all cares, and who eat the alms of the faithful, lest an occasion be offered you for interrupting study if you were occupied with anxiety over the body’s necessities.
