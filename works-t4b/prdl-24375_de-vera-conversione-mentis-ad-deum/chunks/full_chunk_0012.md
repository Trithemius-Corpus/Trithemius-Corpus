Almost all of us are ignorant of the sweetness of contemplation. Very rare today is the monk, and much rarer the abbot, who devotes himself to the contemplative life after the example of the holy fathers. Indeed, if it is right to say so, few in this age know what contemplation is. Therefore, O monks, since you are not what, according to your institution, you ought to be, you are bad. For everyone who is not what he ought to be is in a bad state; but the recompense of the bad is hell. The good and beauty of the soul are rightly said to consist in this: that it be like God by a certain beauty of purity and love, withdrawn from all things that are accustomed to make it ugly, since union can take place only among like things. Whoever has reached that purity of soul by which he begins to taste the things we are saying will thereafter easily despise any violent passions and carnal or earthly loves, and will cast far away all things that previously seemed beautiful. Here the greatest and final struggle is set before us, for the sake of which we must labor altogether, lest perhaps we be left without this best contemplation. Whoever attains it in the present life passes into the future blessed by a happy vision; but, conversely, he who does not attain it is unhappy. For he is not wretched who lacks riches, money, vessels of gold and silver, and honors; nor he who does not obtain splendid possessions, fine horses, a noble wife, freeborn children, and beautiful bodies; nor he who falls from power, an abbacy, a prelacy, a principality, or a kingdom; nor he who is afflicted in this world by diseases, poverty, or disgrace, all of which will shortly pass away; but he who lacks possession of the highest good, for which certainly all the kingdoms and empires of the whole earth, sea, and heaven must be cast aside, if, after all these things have been abandoned and scorned, entrance to the contemplation of so great a good is granted to us.

End of the oration of Johannes Trithemius, abbot, delivered in the annual chapter at Erfurt at Saint Peter’s, in the year of the Lord 1500, on the penultimate day of the month of August, by the same.

From a letter of Libanius Gallus, a Platonist and most learned man.

Indeed, I cannot fail to approve your oration on salutary conversion to God. It is certainly sweet, and yet not idle; and although it flows calmly like a most pure spring, nevertheless it is filled with weighty and carefully chosen judgments, so that the attentive reader may understand that you are a man of very wide reading, singular talent, and the highest learning. Would that those whom I should rightly call [unclear] did not defile Christian theology with that manner of speaking of yours, or at least would bear with a fair mind that theology should be vindicated, by the right of postliminy, from the barbarians who made even it barbarous, and restored to its former condition and dignity by the best men learned in Greek and Latin. Against these barbarians, namely the teachers of youth, who, though unlearned, profess themselves learned, Brother Johannes Annius, a learned and upright man, not unjustly growing indignant, cries out in the questionary prologue on Philo, saying: Alas for impiety! Sacred Scripture is plain; the testimony of God is clear, giving wisdom to little ones. The divine letters do not need folds, nor wrappings, nor refoldings, because these do not give wisdom to little ones, but leave their minds in confusion and compel the minds of men to wander through their whole life, as Diodorus writes of the Greeks in the third book. Not those who fold, not those who refold, but those who unfold and elucidate me will have eternal life, as the sacred letters testify.
