Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to Johannes Engellender, jurisconsult and most eloquent orator, greetings.

I received your most delightful letter, bearing before it the ancient majesty and grace of eloquence, when our Mutianus delivered it; by it you affected me both with incredible pleasure and with a certain honorable reproach. For what greater pleasure could have befallen me than to be loved by you, a man by far most learned and most upright in every respect, whose learning, wisdom, and eloquence kings and princes admire, cultivate, and respect, so much so that all the more learned proclaim that most reverend prelate of Cologne Agrippina fortunate, because he has obtained you as the most worthy prefect of those who serve as his secretaries? Or what reproach ought to lie closer to my heart than to know myself far beneath your praises, and by no means to be the Trithemius whom, deceived by love for me, you proclaimed?

But I give immortal thanks for your charity and goodwill toward me, since you deigned to anticipate me, when I wished indeed, though I did not dare, to knock at the doors of your friendship, though I had hitherto deserved nothing at all with you. And so that I should not let these little letters go off to you empty, I have added an oration on the conversion of the mind to God, which I recently delivered at Erfurt in a gathering of the fathers, to be corrected by the file of your wisdom; and this same oration, cleansed of faults, I dedicate, give, and inscribe to you. And from now on, when I have been made more certain that these very small things have not displeased you, I, received under your patronage, promise in return that I shall attempt greater things in literary work.

Mutianus Rufus himself, through whose mediation your love for me became known to me, that most celebrated interpreter of nature and antiquity, who now, as I read before him, wrestles with Homer and the other Greek authors, of whom I have an abundance, and whose Attic monuments you will see in the near future, sends you very many greetings and commends himself to you.

Farewell. From Sponheim. In the year 1500, in the month of November, on the twentieth day.

An oration of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the true conversion of the mind to its own principle.

I would have greatly wished, reverend fathers, and would have desired with all my heart, that my mind had attained that splendor of the divine majesty by which I might make manifest, in a fitting, proper, and suitable oration, the one goal of our institution: the true, free conversion of the soul, released from the lowest things, to the principle of its essence, God, the best and greatest. For I believe that nothing can be said by me in this holy synod, at this most important time, when, shameful to say, nearly all of us, blinded by evil affections, wander disgracefully from the knowledge and love of the supreme, unchangeable principle, either more honorably or more usefully than concerning the conversion of the mind to God, since all that we, voluntarily bound to God by the most sacred laws and by the institutions of our elders, do in this mortal life ought to urge upon us nothing other than the return and passage of the soul to the center of its blessedness, which is God.

We know, moreover, that the soul, sweetly and freely turned through love toward its own principle, when it is separated from the body, turns joyfully to God himself as to the immovable center of its rest; remaining happy in him and blessed without end, it is thereafter pressed by no disturbances. Therefore, fathers, nothing sweeter can ever befall those who love God in this life than continually either to speak or to hear about the true conversion of the mind to its own principle, by which it is always stirred up, in greater measure, both to contempt of the lowest things and to love of the things above.

And I, fathers, although I am to be reckoned of no learning among you and of no merit before God, nevertheless, assigned to this province of speaking, am accustomed to strive for nothing more than to bring forward publicly, to the best of my powers, those things which may make you despisers of the world and cause your minds to burn more fully with the love of God. Therefore, as you yourselves, the judges of my testimony, will bear witness for me, I have never employed the brilliance of glittering eloquence in the orations delivered by me; I have omitted, even to the point of envy, the local introductions of the Scriptures, which some call interrupters of sentences,

lest either by a more polished treatment I should hide from simpler people the sense of necessary admonition, or disturb the memory by taking up too many passages. For I have always desired more to move the minds of my hearers to inward compunction and divine love than either to admiration of myself or to love of secular knowledge.

Yet this is accustomed to draw me not a little away from my purpose: that I must speak in a public assembly of elders, whom both their most holy manners ought to make worthy of reverence, and their learning in the Scriptures, abundantly rich in every way, ought deservedly to make as terrible to me as an army arrayed in battle; I must speak, a sinful and unlearned man, to whom it is not unjustly often imputed as a fault by many that I have not joined together the rhapsodies, gleaming with varied beauty, by which powers are assigned to equals of most mighty Hercules, to shake the club from him, and to train recruits able to wield spears swifter than the wind. I must speak especially about those matters which, withdrawn far from the senses, are accessible only, such as they are, to those whom that divine ray, stripped of the evil passions of the flesh, has led by the ascent of inward love above the understanding and into the mystical spectacles of divine things.

Therefore, for the moment I commit the account of my name to you, who have imposed upon me, though insufficient, this burden of speaking, for your authority has always been to me in place of Apollo’s oracles. I desire in my whole life to be devoted to you as much as I can, as I remember that I have already shown myself before now, and as I shall promise for the future, wholly myself, and, if after the renunciation of all things it is still permitted to call anything my own, my will, to your commands and to those of the supreme creator of all.

But now, fathers, as we are about to say a few things on the conversion of the mind, let us humbly invoke the most excellent goodness of the supreme and unchangeable good, which alone by its mercy and compassion can convert us to itself, since it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God, whose grace is ready for those who love him. And let us say: Come, Holy Spirit.
