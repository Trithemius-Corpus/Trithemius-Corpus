*Homily XVIII.*

*Folio XLII.*

...a certain monk passing along the public road, and, as though he had offered himself as a traveler on account of the rain, threw him into the mud. By treating the man with injury, he made him a pitiable spectacle of his own shamelessness. No one was present to restrain the insolent men. Yet the monk’s patience overcame the raging men, and, full of the love of Christ, while he was being badly treated, he answered nothing but, “Thanks be to God.” Thus the more he showed himself tranquil in mind and spirit, the more the scoundrels dragged the innocent man about, thinking him heated with wine, though the love of Christ had made him joyful and glad.

He left all monks a great lesson in patience: when he could have avenged himself, though wronged, he did not wish to do so. Would that, my brothers, for love of God and for our sins, we were as patient in the smallest things as that good man was in the greatest. There are among you certain men, I say it unwillingly, who are far too insolent and impatient: they are moved to anger by a word, even one not insulting, and when they are justly corrected for their negligences, they murmur unjustly, adding sin to sin through impatience.

But to what end do I set before them the patience of the man just mentioned, when they do not heed Jesus Christ, the most patient of all men? Indeed, whoever does not embrace patience and humility after the example of the King of heaven will be recalled to gentleness by the imitation of no man. For the Son of God is the highest and supreme example of patience and humility; whoever is not bent to endurance by the recollection of his passion is rightly judged hardened and a companion of demons.

No good Christian doubts that the words of our Lord Jesus Christ are true. Christ says in the holy Gospel: Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the earth. If the meek are to be called blessed, what prevents the resentful, wrathful, and unmeek from being named accursed? Certainly nothing. And if, as the Lord himself and our Savior Jesus Christ bears witness, we possess our souls in patience, there is no doubt that through impatience we lose them.

It is therefore especially fitting that monks be meek, gentle, and patient, since they have solemnly devoted themselves to the imitation of Christ and the apostles. But Christ, the meekest of all, commands his disciples and imitators in the Gospel, saying: Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls. Hence the holy bishop Augustine says: If you wish to reign with Christ in heaven, first learn from him to be meek, patient, humble, and gentle, for unless you have lived in this world in imitation of his life, you will by no means reign with him forever.

The divine Ambrose also says: When I have laid aside every sin, stripped off every malice, and, content with simplicity, have become poor in evils, it remains for me to temper my conduct. For what does it profit me to be free of crimes, unless I am meek and gentle? He is meek who yields to wickedness and does not resist in evil, but overcomes evil with good. And, as Chrysostom bears witness, he is gentle who, when provoked by injuries, neither thinks of doing evil nor does it.

Cassiodorus says: We call the gentle “accustomed to the hand,” that is, patient and meek, those who tolerate the iniquities of others and do not presume to burden anyone. Of such persons it is said in the psalm: My soul shall be praised in the Lord; let the gentle hear and rejoice. He did not say, let those learned in the law rejoice, nor those who fast, nor those who sing psalms, but the gentle, who, together with charity, have above all been accustomed to possess moderation in all things.

Those, however, are truly meek, gentle, and patient who harm no one, injure no one, offend absolutely no one, but, standing firm in their simple humility, always bear all injuries, insults, and mockeries with equanimity for the love of Christ, and are moved by no pain of spirit against those who harm them, just as we have already said that monk was whom the raging boys at Bingen treated with exceedingly insulting behavior.

But it is easy to be meek, patient, and gentle so long as nothing is done except according to the will and judgment of the patient person. Yet how far he is from true gentleness will become clear if even a slight occasion arises. It is a great virtue if you do not harm the one by whom you have been harmed; great strength of soul if, for the love of God, you forgive injury to the one who inflicts it; great glory if you observe the commandment of Christ and pour out prayers for those who injure you. No one has known the strength of his own soul whom a notable insult has not yet burned; let no one boast that he is meek or patient who has not been sated with reproaches.
