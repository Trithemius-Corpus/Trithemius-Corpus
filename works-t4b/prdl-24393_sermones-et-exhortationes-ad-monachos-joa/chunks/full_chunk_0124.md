If you wish to be chaste, be abstinent. It is most shameful for a monk to serve his belly and gluttony, when for the love of God he has renounced all the pleasures of the world at once.

The twelfth thing that makes a monk useful and vigorous in regular observance is that he be most diligent in the service of God. For truly, whoever is a true servant of God, as all monks ought to be, is always fervent and diligent in the work of his Creator; and because he truly knows that God is present everywhere, he prepares himself with complete devotion for dutiful service to him. For it is written: Cursed is the man who does the work of God negligently. Therefore, dearest brothers, when you approach the service of the highest and unchangeable God, sing psalms sweetly, wisely, fervently, and cheerfully; and with as much devotion and purity of mind as you can, diligently praise your Creator and Savior. Let all sluggishness be far from the hearts of those who sing psalms; let no drowsiness be admitted while praising, because, since God is present in the midst of those who sing, it is altogether fitting that fervor of devotion be present in their souls. At every time of divine service let all things be clean, all things pure, all things filled most fully with the fervor of charity, so that improper thoughts may have no place, and all things may be done as they ought.

When the day has been completed and compline has been sung according to custom, as soon as a monk has reached the secrecy of his cell, let him examine his conscience as to what business he has occupied himself with. This is the excellent and praiseworthy custom of the brothers of the Bursfeld reform: that no one should presume to lay himself down on his bed after compline unless he has first taken the greatest care to examine his conscience concerning the faults or negligences committed during the day. But if, after making this examination, you find that you have offended in anything during the day, you know from long practice what must be done. Hidden faults must soon, on the next day, be wiped away by contrition and humble confession; public faults, however, must be referred to the chapter of faults, which is held daily. This is the custom of the reformed order; this is the institution of holy reform: that we never permit the faults of delinquent brothers to grow, but, as soon as they begin to arise, we take care to cut them away. The soul must be cleansed daily; the resolve of holiness must be renewed daily; for just as we cannot remain in the same state without change, so it is necessary for us, by continual exercise, to rouse the mind more frequently to the former fervor of charity.

What are the marks of true humility.

Sermon III.

Let none of you, most beloved brothers, deceive himself by his own estimate of himself; let no one mislead his own heart by foolish and empty presumption. Not all who wish to appear humble are humble before God, nor are those who keep silence under injuries always truly patient. For the roots of true virtues are hidden in the secrecy of the heart, and not in the mere display of outward showing. For we discern some among you who desire rather to be considered humble than truly to be so, and who show many signs of pride, but either no signs of humility or certainly very few. We feel intense compassion, as we rightly should, for those who err so greatly: they desire to have the name of virtue, but they despise virtue itself. Whom, I ask, do you miserable men deceive? Are you not shamefully destroying yourselves by leading yourselves astray, you who, despising truth, love a lie? But so that you may know how proudly you live, we shall set out in order the marks and signs of true humility. Some of these remain hidden in the heart alone; others, breaking forth outwardly as well, ought to be manifest to all who behold them. Let this be for a monk the true mirror of perfect humility, in which, looking each day, he may examine himself.

The first mark of true humility, then, each person can recognize in himself when he can regard himself as lower and viler than all people, not with feigned speech, but by the humble judgment of the heart. This sign of deep humility is the greatest: that a monk, always advancing toward a better way of life, believe himself to be a useless and unworthy servant of God. True humility belongs to the heart, not to speech; it is a striving of the mind, not merely an outward bodily display.
