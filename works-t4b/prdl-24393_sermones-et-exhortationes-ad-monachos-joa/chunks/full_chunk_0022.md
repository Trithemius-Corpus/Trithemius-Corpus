*Homily V.*

*Fol. XII.*

...for purity of mind, Wisdom admonishes us in Ecclesiasticus by frequent meditation on death, saying: “In all your works remember your last things, and you will never sin.” The thought of future death brings on fear; fear necessarily shakes the mind and, like a nail driven by a hammer, fastens all the motions of the flesh to the wood of the cross. In view of this consideration, many of the pagan philosophers used to say that the whole life of a wise man is a kind of continual meditation on death. Hence Saint Jerome also says: “Nothing profits you so much for temperance in all things as frequent meditation on this brief and uncertain life,” which, like a leaf snatched away by the wind, falls into death when it is not feared.

Therefore remember your death, which you cannot escape by any counsel, and you will easily be able to overcome all the motions of the flesh. For whoever thinks every day that he is going to die despises present things and wholly prepares himself in mind for future things. For I know, and in part confess that I have learned by experience, that the human mind and will cannot long remain in one state within the holy fervor of the spiritual life; and therefore I have resolved often to renew the memory of death before your eyes, so that, while the mind is struck by fear of future things, it may daily be renewed in the holy purpose.

For the human soul, bound to the body by its own exercises, is exceedingly fluid and unstable: now it is carried off to this thing, now it is moved to another. Therefore the Wisdom of God says through Solomon in Proverbs: “My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes keep my ways.” It is as though he were saying: If you have handed over the whole love of your heart to divine wisdom, you will never be unstable or weakened in keeping the commandments of the Lord. For just as the heart is the first principle of motion in man, so the guarding of it is most important for the preservation of life; for if it is struck by an attack of diseases, the whole life of the body is already endangered. Hence it is written: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.”

By the name of the heart, however, we understand the affection by which anyone inclines toward love of some thing, clinging to it so much that it entangles the whole will. Hence the Psalmist says: “If riches abound, do not set your heart upon them,” that is, do not love them by the desire of the will. Nothing is more changeable than the human heart: it is altered by almost every single thought, and it can scarcely remain in the same purpose unless it restrains itself by a certain forceful intention. Saint David, not ignorant of this, said to the Lord: “But I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty. You turned your face away from me, and I was troubled.”

Therefore, my dearest brothers, whoever among you desires to serve our Lord and Savior in true purity of heart, whoever wishes to be a monk both in deed and in name, let him learn first to take thought not for himself but for God; and let him take care always, by a fresh beginning, to renew the good purpose of holy conduct, not only daily, but as far as he can even at every hour and every moment. For the fervor of spiritual conduct, to the practical exercise of which the whole institution of the cloistered life is known to be ordered, cannot long remain at the same point; for if it does not always advance toward what lies ahead, it necessarily falls back to what is lower and, what is worse, weaker.

In the arena of spiritual exercise there is no fixed standing still without motion, because it is the property of the human heart, by its natural movement, always either to advance successfully toward better things or to fall back toward what is evil. Therefore, if you do not make progress in spiritual conduct, you necessarily fail; if you do not advance toward holier things, you certainly go backward. Since experience has made this clear to us, I do not cease to admonish your charity that in good conduct your desire should always be stretched out toward what is further, lest you fall into the destructive danger of failing precisely where you have not renewed your longing to advance.

The fervor of the spiritual purpose is directed toward love, and it is preserved only in love. For the love of God is not absurdly compared to fire: just as fire, when the fuel of wood is taken away, is necessarily extinguished, so the love of God is endangered if it lacks the continual nourishment of good works and the memory of eternal things. Hence that saying of the Savior in the Gospel: “I have come to send fire upon the earth, and what do I wish except that it burn?” O inexpressibly happy is that conscience in whose breast nothing burns except the love of God, which loves God above all things and despises all pursuits of vanity. And consider, my brothers, most diligently, your consciences, and [unclear] whether we love God above all things.
