Homily XIII. Folio XXXIII.

Nor does delaying repentance bring security. He who does not repent while he is healthy is foolish; and he who does not amend his faults when he can, by the just judgment of God loses the power he has deferred. Repentance is fruitful when it has been voluntary by freedom of choice; but when the danger of death is already pressing, it seems rather to be necessary than to have been taken up by repentance's own choice.

Let no one say in his heart, my brothers, let no one think that the labor of repenting is hard, and for that reason must be deferred until the last little portion of this life. That thought is exceedingly dangerous, since the fixed term of life, which divine providence has appointed for each person, just as he has willed it to be hidden from all, so he will not prolong it for anyone who deliberately defers repentance.

If we wish to be safe and secure in conscience, let us do penance while we are healthy, while we are able to sin, while no necessity of approaching death yet presses upon us. For it is a very doubtful thing to do penance when necessity already compels, and the nearness of death makes you no longer able to sin: when, that is, you have not dismissed your sins, but they, finding you powerless, have dismissed you.

O how many, deceived by the hope of a longer life, descend into the abyss of eternal damnation, who when healthy scorned to do penance. For the longer you defer repentance, the less disposed to repent you will always be found. Wickedness gains strength with the passage of time, and there is no sin so small that it does not grow by neglect. Therefore embrace repentance while you are healthy, and do not put it off from day to day, since you know what you can do today, but you do not know what tomorrow will be. For each day brings its own new troubles, and he who today is not disposed toward repentance will perhaps be less fit tomorrow.

But let none of you say, “What sin have I committed that repentance should seem necessary to me, when my whole life is nothing other than a certain hard and long necessity of repenting?” You have heard Scripture testify that there is no man on earth who does good and does not sin, since no one is clean before God. For even if he does not commit the great crimes of human wickedness, such as homicide, theft, adultery, fornications, and things like these, nevertheless, because you have been set in that calling in which you ought with all your strength to raise every effort toward the state of perfection, sins that are small for mortals living in the world are certainly imputed to you as more serious.

For all sins, so far as possible, must be avoided by cloistered men, not only those that impede salvation, but also those that stain the perfection enjoined upon them. And which of you lives with as much purity as monastic perfection requires? It is a great sin for a monk if he has set aside his effort toward perfection, if he has lived negligently in the monastery, if he has transgressed even one commandment of the canon and the Gospel. And who among you, brothers, loves the Lord God with such sincerity, such charity, and such fervor of love as a man is bound to love his maker and redeemer? Or whom will you give me from among all of you who does not at some time exceed, either in taking food or in drink, more than befits the integrity of monks? Which of you does not offend in word, or who is not sometimes disturbed by evil thoughts? Whom among you has acedia not struck, and whom has sad sloth not troubled? Who is there in this small congregation of yours whom elation does not touch, and whom impatience has not quite often made murmuring?

Let each one of you consider the paths of his way of life, and he will find that he fails in many things, even at any moment. For what if a monk has committed no evil? What if he has kept himself clean from this wicked age? He will not be able to be perfect unless he has both turned away from evil and done the good that his perfection requires. For one without the other does not suffice, but from both perfection rises up in a man.

Therefore, brothers, we must always keep watch with the highest zeal, so that we may satisfy the institution of the holy fathers, according to which by public profession we have solemnly bound ourselves to live, in order that we may direct our effort without interruption toward the perfection set before us. Let not the consideration of our own weakness draw us back from holy effort, because we are helped no less by the grace of the creator than we run by our own desire. Just as every day, by living rather negligently, we fail on many occasions in many things, so also at every moment, rising from the torpor of mind, let us renew holy effort in a good purpose. Let us join tears to holy prayers, and with devout supplications always call upon God's help.

Let us keep charity inviolable in all things, since it is charity that covers the multitude of sins. Each day the good soldier of Christ, who desires to be faithful to his Lord, should examine himself by discussion at least once, and should consider more diligently how much he has advanced or fallen back in holy desire on that day. Whenever he finds that he has failed in anything, let him immediately do penance, and with tears ask mercy from the Lord. Let him not wait doubtfully for tomorrow; salvation is today, and he who can quickly find pardon should in no way insert a delay. Let him at once grieve in heart that he has sinned against the Lord; let him also renew his holy purpose with new fervor, and let him judge that he has utterly lost that day on which he did not become better by advancing.

Prayer should always be on the monk's lips; constant compunction should be in his heart; tears of devotion should continually flow from his eyes; and the exercises of inward love should never recede from his memory. The most bitter remembrance of the Lord's passion should often be in the mind of the cloistered man; and all the prayers he makes to the Father should be offered to God in the power of the blood and wounds of our Lord, whether prayer is being extended to God or meditation on his words is being turned over in the mind. Let the devout monk turn away from evil and do the good he can, with cheerfulness and discretion of the Spirit of God, since, as has been said, for doing penance it is not enough to change one's morals for the better and withdraw from evil deeds, unless one also strives to adorn one's conscience with good works.

For true repentance, therefore, as you know, my brothers, four things are required. The first is true contrition for sins, which is a voluntary sorrow of the heart, undertaken in detestation of the evils one has committed. Of this Scripture says: “A contrite and humbled heart, O God, you will not despise.” And again: “Those who sow in tears” [unclear]. Sorrow of contrition is greatly useful; the greater it is, the more fruitful repentance will be. For true contrition is that which both sorrow of mind commends and the continual cleansing of tears attests. Great is the usefulness of tears, because there is no sin so grave that contrition, accompanied by tears, does not destroy it.

Hence the holy teacher Augustine says: “O humble tear, how powerful and virtuous you are, for yours is the kingdom, yours is the power. You do not fear the gaze of the judge; you impose silence on accusing enemies; you alone enter to the king, but you do not return alone; you conquer the unconquerable and bind the omnipotent.” The same man also says: “The holier anyone is and the fuller of holy desire, the more abundant is his weeping in prayer.” True compunction has sorrow; sorrow, moreover, is shown by tears, and by these repentance is adorned as if with certain ornaments. True compunction makes the soul shudder at sin, and when it has begun from fear, increased by its own growth, it gradually rises up into love. Its virtue is great and its power truly wonderful, before which every enormity of sins soon gives way. For whomever true compunction of heart has made suppliant, the merciful Lord soon hears him.

O happy tears, O saving affliction and truly sweet bitterness, by which [unclear], through which the compunct soul rises up to the mercy of God. For, as the holy [unclear] says, [unclear] is renewed. The second thing that pertains to the truth of repentance is a firm purpose of amendment, since whoever has the word of confession on his lips and does not have the purpose of amendment in his heart is either deceitful or empty. But whoever has it in his heart and not on his lips is either proud or timid, and by no means fit for true repentance. For Scripture says: “With the heart one believes unto righteousness, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.” This sentence, lovers of Christ, is especially true in the time when the statute of the Church has decreed confession necessary for salvation. The third, therefore, is confession of the mouth. For the truth of repentance, contrition of heart alone is not sufficient, especially from this point, nor is the purpose of future amendment alone sufficient. But what this saving confession ought to be, I know that none of you is unaware.
