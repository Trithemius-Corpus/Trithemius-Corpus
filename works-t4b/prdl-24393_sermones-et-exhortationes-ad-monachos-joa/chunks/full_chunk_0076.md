Homily XVI. Fol. XXXIX.

For food will by no means be fruitfully withheld from the body unless the mind is called back from all iniquity. For Saint Jerome says: What does it profit for the body to be made thin by abstinence, if the soul swells with pride? What virtue is there in not drinking wine, yet being drunk with anger, hatred, and the other poisonous cups of the soul?

Therefore let none of you promise himself holiness from abstinence from food alone, since whatever is required for true penitence is also judged necessary for fasting. For fasts profit unto salvation for those who are contrite for their sins with a true purpose of amendment, and who fervently apply themselves to good and holy works. Hence all who fast from food and drink must also abstain from vice, so that the mind, adorned with the beauty of the virtues, may burn sweetly in the love of Christ and in no way grow sluggish from progress in inward conduct. For the virtue of abstinence is nothing unless it has been adorned with works of charity.

Therefore, monk, hear my counsel: if you wish to make your fast fruitful before God, first cleanse your conscience from every stain of sin, so that no desire for evil may remain in you, and so that the mind may grow fervent in the exercise of divine love and be laudably adorned with the holy virtues. By the remedy of contrition and confession wipe away past sins; and by persisting in prayers with tears, strengthen your mind against all temptations with God’s help. Toward God, as far as you can, be fervent in the exercise of love; toward yourself, maintain good tranquillity in a pure conscience; toward your brother, be rightly ordered in true charity. Always be foremost in humility, firm in constancy and obedience, pure in chastity of mind and body, pious in meekness, a monk in conduct, continually kindled with desire for perfection. If you commend your abstinence by this kind of cleanness and purity, you will fast rightly.

Diligently exercise your mind in meditation on the passion of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, so that no day passes in which you do not at least once ponder his life and death with thanksgiving and tears. For crude and exceedingly hard, not to say ungrateful, is the Christian who is so submerged in temporal affairs or occupied with the pleasures of the flesh that he does not call to mind at least once each day, with thanksgiving, the most bitter passion of his Redeemer.

But the monk who forgets the benefits of our Lord Jesus Christ, who does not recall his passion even once each day with thanksgiving, who does not meditate every day on the Lord’s evangelical law, is rightly the most ungrateful of all men. Nothing is more powerful than that prayer which is offered to God the Father in union with, and in the power of, the Lord’s passion. Justly indeed is that spirit to be condemned which, though redeemed by the most precious blood of Christ, is enticed away by the pleasures of its flesh, so that it does not remember such great benefits of God toward itself.

For there was no reason that the Son of God should become man except that he wished to justify us sinners: born in the world for us, suffering in the world for us, cruelly dead upon the wood for our crimes. The just one came into the world to justify us who were sinners; the pious one came to the impious, so that we might learn piety, humility, and patience from him. The physician came to the sick, life to the dead, consolation to the despairing; it was not our merits that drew him from heaven into the world, but our sins.

It is fitting, therefore, my brothers, that with all devotion we celebrate this Lenten fast dedicated to his most holy passion, since he first deigned to do such great things for our salvation, and, in order to snatch us from the destruction of death, did not hesitate to give himself for us unto death. Let us do for love of Christ whatever good we can; in view of his passion and death, let us endure every adversity that happens to us in this world, since the Lord Jesus, who in charity willed to suffer for us, has truly shown himself most worthy that we love him with all our heart.

For if, brother, you recognize yourself as a sinner, Christ has saved you by his death. Contemplate him as it is fitting, hanging on the cross, and you will be healed from every bite of sin. Nothing is found in this world more effective for curing the wounds of conscience, nothing more powerful for purging the terrified eye of the mind, than constant meditation on the wounds of Christ. For the most precious blood of the Lord Jesus, poured out, has been made a sufficient medicine for sin. Hence the holy bishop Augustine says: Brothers, that we who are wounded by sin may be healed, let us look upon Christ crucified. For just as those who looked upon the bronze serpent in the desert did not perish from the bites of serpents, so those who with faith and devotion look upon the death of Jesus Christ are healed from the diseases of sins.

On the Lenten fast of monks.

But if you trust that you have been justified from sin, so much the more, and more fervently, must you look upon Christ hanging on the cross, without the merit of whose passion no one is found just on earth. For outside faith in Christ there is no righteousness. However religiously a person may live, if he does not believe in our Lord Jesus Christ, he will by no means obtain eternal salvation; rather, the wrath of God remains upon him, and he will necessarily perish forever. The blood of Christ has opened the gate of salvation, through which no one can enter except one who faithfully believes in Christ. For this reason Christ humbly subjected himself to the condemnation of death, so that he might free believers from the yoke of perpetual condemnation.

O infinite charity, O piety without measure! Who, believing in you, eternal Majesty, would not marvel at such astonishing benefits granted to us? For your passion, Lord Jesus, is the price of our salvation, the refuge of sinners, the singular remedy for infirm minds, the fountain, origin, and end of all good things. Therefore now, dearest brothers, let us recollect and diligently call back to memory the bitterness of the Lord’s passion, so that, since we necessarily recognize that we are sinners, we may at last be justified through the merit of our Savior himself.

For meditation on the Lord’s passion surpasses all exercises of this kind, if it is continued for a long time with the fervor of inward devotion. That mind of a monk which, kindled by zeal for divine love, has been accustomed each day to keep the memory of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ with tears for at least a modest hour, does not easily lie open to the suggestions of demons. For the heart which the love of Jesus possesses always remains inaccessible and closed to all evil thoughts.

Happy indeed is that monk, and never to be named except with honor, who meditates day and night with loving zeal on the teaching and passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; who never forgets his Savior; who, whether he eats or drinks or does anything else, always beholds the Lord Jesus hanging on the cross with the eyes of the mind.
