For there are some who write that from this monastic religion of ours fifteen thousand seven hundred men and nuns have been entered into the catalogue of the saints. This multitude of saints in the order, brothers, is set before us for imitation, not for vain glory. What can their multitude contribute in the end to our salvation, if we do not imitate the way of life in which they went before us? Whenever we desire profitably to praise the most holy men of our order, let us hasten by holy conduct to come into their fellowship. For then we shall glory fruitfully in the multitude of the saints of our order, if, by living rightly, we strive to increase their number. Therefore we set before you so great a multitude of saints, so that, when you see that many before you in this very order conquered the world and the flesh, you may at last rise up from your lukewarm manner of life. The virtue that God’s mercy granted to them he will not deny to you, if you ask in true charity.

They conquered the world and loved Christ with total devotion, despising all things for love of him. They despised all passing things, and the Lord Jesus provided whatever seemed necessary to them for the support of life. This fervor of devotion among monks endured after the first institution of the order for many years in great constancy, and converted the souls of many thousands of people to the Lord. But nothing among human things is stable; nothing among perishable things is everlasting. Sacred religion brought riches upon the monks, until in time the daughter suffocated the mother. For, as Scripture says, all who wish to become rich fall into the temptations of the devil. The beloved grew fat and kicked; fattened and enlarged by earthly possessions, he abandoned the holy pursuits of the first institution, went after vanities and false follies, and an abundance of temporal things made him needy and wretched in spiritual riches.

This pitiable deformity of our order began almost two hundred years after the passing of the divine father Benedict, and with time it grew more and more, until at last the harmful poison spread through the whole order. Yet this deformity occupied the order not suddenly, but through the succession of many years, until it had almost wholly taken possession of it; although almighty God never left it wholly without good men, but always reserved for himself some who by no means bent their knees to Baal. For men, and women too, arose whose righteousness is never handed over to oblivion; pitying the ruin of the order, they put their shoulder under the labor, and, the Lord generously granting aid, restored it in many places.

Indeed, many varied particular reformations often flourished in the order at different times, but there was never any general one that brought the whole order back to the integrity of regular observance. Finally, we now see that the particular restorations, which we have said were many, have almost all collapsed again. Most recently, however, namely in the Christian year 1420, when the general council was being celebrated at Constance, a certain new and, up to now, last particular reformation took its beginning in Germany. It took the word used as its name from a certain monastery in the regions of Saxony, then indeed the poorest of all, which is called Bursfelde.

The first author and leader of this most holy reformation was Johannes Rode, abbot of the distinguished monastery of Saint Matthias at Trier. Having been appointed general visitor of the order by the aforesaid Council of Constance, lest, while preaching to others, he himself should be judged reprobate, he first displayed reformation in himself. For first of all he reformed both himself and the monks of the said monastery; he issued constitutions conforming to the Rule; and he obtained confirmation of the holy work from the apostolic see. But among all the monasteries of the order, Bursfelde, a humble and rather poor monastery, first received this reformation, which it afterwards extended to Reinhausen and to a few monasteries of Saxony neighboring it.

When at last Johannes Rode, abbot of Saint Matthias and leader of our reformation, had died, since in that place no suitable man was found for extending the undertaking begun, Johannes de Hagen, abbot of Bursfelde, who had already reformed certain monasteries, a holy man and very zealous for the order, was elected at the urging of Jacob, archbishop of Trier. Content with his poverty, he did not fully consent to leave Bursfelde, lest he seem to have preferred riches to poverty. He therefore remained in his own monastery, and another was appointed in the place of the first reformer. Since he seemed comparable to his predecessor neither in learning nor in zealous affection, he was able to accomplish little for the continuation of the undertaking begun, nor to enlarge the reformation among many. Meanwhile it happened that Nicholas of Cusa, presbyter cardinal of the holy Roman Church and legate of the apostolic see, came into Germany. Since he burned with great zeal for our order, he established and granted many things for its reformation. Hearing, moreover, the good fame of the aforesaid abbot in Bursfelde and his most fervent purpose for regular discipline, he called him to himself and appointed him general reformer of the order; and through his industry he nobly reformed many things.

On this occasion at last he instituted an annual chapter for the preservation of the reformation; he transferred the presidency from Saint Matthias to Bursfelde; and he commanded that all the things he had instituted for the good of the reformation be confirmed by the apostolic see. The first abbot of Bursfelde in the reformation was Johannes, a monk appointed from Reinhausen; the second was the aforesaid Johannes de Hagen; the third was Theodericus, whom we have seen; and the fourth is Johannes, who has recently been appointed and is now in office. Brothers most beloved, we briefly touch upon the origins of our reformation so that you may recognize with how much labor it began, and so that you may persevere constant unto death in the holy observance of the Rule.

Among all the reformations of our order that preceded it, none was more conformable and agreeable to the one Rule of the divine father Benedict, and none more alien to foreign aids. We have read the statutes of almost all other reformations, but we judge none more suited than ours to the Rule and to the purity of Benedict’s intention. This Bursfelde reformation is most holy in the order; it teaches true obedience and guards subjection in humility. In this holy religion monks live poor and without personal property; they live humbly; they live obediently; they live chastely. O venerable union of monks, in which twin charity rules, obedience serves, humility obeys, prayer keeps watch, perseverance fights, and joyful chastity triumphs. This is the school of regular discipline, the instruction of Christian humility, which directs conduct toward uprightness and restrains the vices of the flesh. In this arena of virtues no one is permitted to follow his own will against the Rule; no one is forbidden to do good. In this holy union men live according to the purity of the Rule, and if anyone begins to wander from the path of uprightness, the care of the elders soon calls him back to the observance of justice. The Rule of the most holy father Benedict is read publicly three times in the year, so that no one may try to excuse himself from observance under the shadow of ignorance.

There are two things that promise me great hope for the perseverance of this holy reformation: the annual chapter and the diligent biennial visitation of the monasteries. But if either of these is neglected, the reformation soon falls into danger. Therefore think now, most beloved brothers, and take the most diligent care you can, lest you occupy this monastery of the divine bishop Martin uselessly under the title of so venerable a reformation, since unless you live purely, holily, and blamelessly according to the Rule and the statutes of the order, you trust in the name in vain.

We shall speak to you a few true admonitions for salvation, through which the zealous observance of our Bursfelde reformation will possess both name and dignity by merit. First, before all else, direct all your efforts and all your desires toward monastic perfection; and whenever you sense yourselves growing lukewarm, renew them by attentive remembrance of the Lord’s passion and most innocent death. Although we are not bound to be perfect by necessity of salvation, nevertheless we are bound to strive for perfection with all our desires. For when a monk casts away the striving for perfection, he falls into the pit of defection. The second thing that the sacred custom of our reformation has instituted for you to keep strongly preserves the striving for perfection in the desire already conceived. Therefore, when you rise at the signal for Prime, do these things in secret. Examine and discuss the course of the coming day, considering prudently what must usefully be done. Set in your hearts how cautiously you wish to live for the whole day, and arm your souls with patience against all adversities that may occur. Whatever you do, complete it in the fervor of charity, and always order all words and deeds to the praise of God.

The third thing we judge useful and necessary for you is that with most devout prayers you always strive in all things to implore the help of God. And it seems very fitting to us, as we have often said and always admonish, that each of you should at least once a day keep remembrance of the Lord’s passion, and once give thanks to God for the many benefits received from him. So that this may be done uniformly by each one of you, listen to this brief form that we have ordained; if you wish, write it down for yourselves: I adore, praise, bless, and glorify you, Lord Jesus Christ, Son of the living God, savior and giver of life to the souls of all the faithful; and I give you thanks from the depth of my heart for your most holy incarnation and birth from the most pure virgin Mary; for your circumcision; for your flight into Egypt; for your seven years of exile there; and for [unclear].
