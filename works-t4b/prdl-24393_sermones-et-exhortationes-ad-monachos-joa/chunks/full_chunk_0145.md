Although we cannot wholly avoid venial sins, nevertheless we ought to strive with all our strength that they not multiply in us. But the one who, by the Lord’s mercy, advances daily in this degree toward better things falls less often into danger; and whenever such persons have fallen, they rise again through penitence. Yet their condition is close to danger: just as a garment, often torn and mended, is at last made useless, so a person who falls more often by sin is frequently stifled by evil habit. If any depart this life from this degree through the Lord’s mercies, they will be saved, but not without the very grievous punishments of purgatory. Therefore one must hasten toward higher things, so that they may guard, as far as they can, not only against mortal sins but also against venial ones.

They are believed to ascend the second degree who strive, as far as they can, to avoid venial sins as well as mortal crimes. Yet because of the commerce of the weak body, they do not persevere in their purpose, but often fall into various negligences of this life, although they do not commit mortal sins. We lukewarm people think that we have somehow ascended to this degree, because, having been placed under the observance of regular discipline, we keep our minds, with the Lord’s help, from the graver sins. For we are all too lukewarm; many of us live negligently, and we are slow to perform the holy exercises of virtue.

Yet there are many set in the climate of this second degree whom servile fear possesses; and if that alone is present, it by no means brings one near to the kingdom of God. For to attain the felicity of heaven above, such servile fear without charity does not suffice, since he is a servant who does not sin through fear of punishment, and if he does not do evil, he does not do so from love of God. It is good, however, to begin from servile fear, provided that little by little one can come from good fear to filial fear. Yet to withdraw from evil out of fear of penalties is by no means sufficient for attaining the kingdom of God, though it prepares those who labor for filial fear. Thus the first degree of those making progress is not to commit wrong from fear of punishment, and not to sin lest they be condemned. For charity alone, discerning all merits, admits into the kingdom of God what is fit to enter, and judges whatever has been done outside it to be unworthy of the kingdom of God. Therefore, whoever has deserved to ascend this second degree, whoever has learned not to sin from fear of punishments, if you wish to press on to the joy of eternal felicity, take care in every way to hasten to higher things. Since, as Scripture bears witness, no one is suddenly made highest, begin from the lower things; yet we judge it dangerous to stand idle too long in the valley after you have set out. Therefore ascend daily as much as you can, and do not persist in the filth of beastly action; for if you do not advance, you fall back; if you do not ascend, you descend.

The third degree includes those who, persevering in a good purpose, keep themselves from every danger of mortal sin and withdraw, as far as they can, even from venial sins. They love purity of conscience and knowingly commit nothing base that could stain it. Yet in this one respect they are lower than those who follow, because they do not strive to withdraw from evil simply for love of God, but because they trust that they will receive the kingdom of God for their merits. Whoever serves God for reward is not in the state of perfection; with the weak he still remains far from the summit of the blessed, in lower things. Whoever truly loves God with a pure mind serves and obeys him neither from fear of penalties nor with an eye to reward, but only for love of him. And therefore all the degrees written above are set before beginners, through which, ascending little by little, they may at last reach perfection. It is good to withdraw from evil out of fear of penalties; it is good to do good out of love of reward; and although this is by no means sufficient for perfection, nevertheless it provides a beginning and a way for those who desire to make progress. For fear customarily leads to filial and chaste love, from whose root perfect charity flows. But afterward, once minds have reached it, they cast out all servile fear and fear of reward.

The fourth degree displays the way of perfection to those ascending, and it is rightly judged higher than the degrees described above. For whatever good is seen in them, those at this degree possess more perfectly. Those ascending this fourth degree, with all zeal, keep themselves not only from the plague of mortal sins, but also from venial sins as far as is possible for them; they eagerly engage in good and holy works, and above all desire to please God. In one respect they surpass those who precede them: they neither withdraw from evil through fear of punishment nor do good in hope of reward, but do all things for love of God alone, and seek nothing other than God’s honor. This is the way set before those striving for perfection; this is the life of holy justice in regular discipline: that we love God with a pure mind above all things, and do all our works for his honor. For the true lover of God neither fears punishment nor thinks of reward, but, walking in simplicity of heart, entrusts his will to the divine ordinance. For because God is the highest good, he must be loved not for the reward of the heavenly kingdom, nor for any other thing, but for his own sake; and he himself, as the chief good, must principally be held in one’s intention.
