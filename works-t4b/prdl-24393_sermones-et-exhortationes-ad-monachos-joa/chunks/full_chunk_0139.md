Sermon VI. Folio LXX.

...from our sins in the blood of his only-begotten Son. The Creator most greatly loves his creature, which he brought forth from nothing; it is fitting that the creature itself should most greatly love its Creator, by whose goodness it was brought into being. God loves us with the greatest charity, than which none greater can be spoken or conceived; and he loves us so infinitely that he neither ought nor could love more, since in God all things are perfect, and in him there is no capacity for change or shadow of alteration. Consider now, my brothers, with how great a fervor of mind and with how great a desire of heart the devout person ought to love him, by whom he is loved even without measure in the highest degree of love. And let us give back our soul in love for him above all things, as much as we can: for he is worthy that we love him with all our strength. Yet however much we love him, we shall by no means come to that measure by which he himself loves us. For he loves us as worthily as possible; nor can his love toward us grow greater, since he already perfectly loves his creature. But however much we love him, we can always make further progress in loving, and love him more ardently even to the point of death.

O we weak and sluggish people, we who are committed to a prison of clay: what shall we repay to so great a lover, who has gone before us by so great a degree of love, when there can be no comparison of the creature with the Creator? The sixth thing that deservedly ought to stir us to the love of God is the consideration of his supreme kindness toward us: that, after so many thousands of men had been passed over and left in error, he mercifully called us in Europe, along with a few others, to the knowledge of the Christian faith. Of all benefits after the death of Christ, I judge this to be the chief: that he caused us to be Christians in the faith. For outside the faith of Christ no one will be saved. Christ is proclaimed here, but in many places he is utterly unknown. How many kingdoms there are today in Asia and Africa, how many peoples, how many islands of the sea, where there is no knowledge of Christ at all and no faith; where every religion is in error and without the merit of salvation, even if some outside Christianity seem to adore and worship one God. For Christ is the Savior of faithful souls; whoever has not believed in him has already been judged, and without doubt will be condemned. How shall we ever be able to offer God worthy thanks, because he established us, so to speak, to be born in the Catholic faith, and by the blood of his only-begotten Son freed us from the destruction of death? Therefore let us be mindful of so great a benefit, and let us take care to use it in such a way that we are not judged for ingratitude. Let us bless God the Father at every time, because he has poured out such great spiritual goods upon us, and has not permitted us to be born removed from the unity of the Catholic Church.

The seventh thing that is believed able to produce the love of God in our hearts is considered in the promise of eternal happiness. For if we consider how great are the goods in heaven which he has promised to give us, then love for him will deservedly burn sweetly in our hearts. He has promised us his whole self, he who alone is the highest and wholly unchangeable good. Since he is indivisible, he will grant himself to be enjoyed by all in such a way that nevertheless he will be possessed whole by each. He loves all by whom he is loved, and he loves each by whom he is held. The more we now love God with a pure mind, the more there we shall receive what was promised.

On the Knowledge of Spiritual Grace. Sermon Six.

The holy apostle of Christ Paul teaches us that we cannot always believe every spirit, but that promptings must be diligently examined, whether they are from God, who is light, or whether something hostile rises up against us from the side of darkness. For since our adversary is very cunning, he never leaves anything untried, and devises all manner of ways by which he may catch us off guard. He attacks those who are already making progress in one way, and deals in another way with those who still live in the torpor of negligence. The more we have advanced in the commandments of God, the more necessary it is that we keep watch against the devil plotting against us. Indeed, he pursues those who flee temptations, and rages with envious malice against those who desire to please God. He is accustomed to assail secretly, under the appearance of good, those whom he knows he cannot overcome by open assault. He is accustomed to suggest many things to those making progress and still insufficiently grounded in the fear of God, by which he very often tries to trip up unguarded minds under the appearance of good. Against his snares the best remedy is this: that the monk submit all the thoughts of his heart to the examination of the elders. For the demons hate public light, and are ashamed that the thoughts which they suggest should come to the knowledge of spiritual elders.
