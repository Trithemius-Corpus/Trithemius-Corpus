Nor does he pray only for his own sins, but also for those of the whole world. The chief remedy against the mind’s exaltation is the remembrance of one’s own offenses.

The third mark of humility is that he desires to keep hidden in secret all the good works he does, and conceals his own progress out of zeal for humility. He is not vainly proud, but humble; nor does he seek praise from men, because he hopes for the glory of eternal happiness. Whoever among you considers himself humble and a true monk should not, for God’s sake, disdain this sign of profound submission and abasement.

The fourth indication of true humility is that, voluntarily descending into the abyss of self-contempt, he considers all his brethren, even those openly sinning, better than himself before God; forgetting his own good deeds, he wisely considers his evils, while passing over the evils of others in silence and, when fitting, making known whatever good they have done. This is the condition of true humility: always to admire and proclaim the good works of others, and to make little of and hide one’s own, prudently and secretly, for the avoidance of pride.

The fifth sign of humility, once attained, is that he is quiet in his simplicity and does not speak about the defects of others unless compelled either by the reason of his office or by the command of charity. Yet prudent humility preserves moderation in correction, so that the one whom it cannot make better by correcting him more severely, it endeavors to admonish gently and meekly. It also sometimes wisely passes over rebukes, and waits for and observes a more opportune time.

The sixth sign of humility appears when a monk, out of love of humility, is found slow and not ready to accuse and rebuke the sins of others. For he who is truly humble always considers his own weakness, and silently weighs in secret how many evils he himself has committed against God’s justice. Hence he greatly fears to rebuke the deeds of others, because he recognizes himself as a sinner and therefore unworthy to judge others. From this norm of humility we except the prelates and rulers of monks, insofar as we know them to be bound to the correction of their subjects.

The seventh sign of true humility is that he gladly receives corrections and amendments for his faults and negligences, and is embittered by no grief against the one who rebukes him. Whenever the humble man is rebuked by someone, he rejoices with the prophet in spirit, saying: Let the just man correct me in mercy and rebuke me; but let not the oil of the sinner fatten my head. Of the proud, however, Scripture says: They hated the one who rebuked in the gate. He who is truly humble loves the one who rebukes him, but flees the flatterer.

The eighth indication of true humility is clearly shown when, having been rebuked, he does not defend, deny, cloak, or excuse his fault, but confesses it with simple shamefacedness. The truly humble monk, when he is rebuked, does not murmur; he does not protest that the measure of fairness has been exceeded, but at once, falling to the ground before the judge, cries out most meekly: I have sinned; I confess the fault; I ask pardon; I promise amendment.

The ninth indication of monastic humility makes one mild, sweet, and modest in speech, and not loud in voice. The monk whom you see to be loud and hasty in speaking you will judge not humble, but proud and rash. The humble man speaks modestly and almost under silence, and is always found readier to listen than to speak before others. It is shameful for a monk to raise his voice too much when he speaks, since, crucified to the world, he is commanded to love silence.

He possesses the tenth indication of perfect humility who is accustomed to interpret everything he has heard about his neighbor in the better sense. For our Savior says in the holy Gospel: Do not judge, and you shall not be judged. And again: With what measure you have measured, it shall be measured back to you. The Apostle also says: Do not judge before the time, until the Lord comes, who will both illuminate the hidden things of darkness and make manifest the counsels of hearts. Who are you, who judge another’s servant? To his own lord he stands or falls, and not to you.

The eleventh indication of holy humility is that he despises no man in his heart on account of any excesses whatever, but places all before himself in the merit of sanctity. He also considers all who offend publicly to be better than himself, and for that reason condemns no one. He always thinks within himself, in his heart, what evil he himself has done, but humbly weighs the good deeds of others as surpassing his own. Hence he spurns no one, makes little of no one, despises no one, and desires all to be saved in the Lord.

The twelfth sign of perfect true humility is that he does not desire to be praised by men, but whenever he is commended by someone, he blushes and groans inwardly with sorrow of mind. But if he hears the praises of others recited in the convent, rejoicing with their praise and exulting, he becomes cheerful in spirit. He who is perfectly humble cannot hear his own praises without bitterness of heart, because he has placed his hope wholly in heaven and despises all the glory of this life.

The thirteenth indication of true humility is not only to despise praises and favors in the truth of the mind, but also prudently to avoid those by whom he sees himself being praised. Those who are truly humble flee their praisers, and whenever they are praised, they fall silent and answer nothing. Esteem for a great reputation is burdensome to the humble man, and he is confounded within himself all the more, the more he is proclaimed by men. For true humility, when blamed, makes progress; when praised, it incurs danger.

The fourteenth perfect sign of monastic humility is that, in corrections and detractions, if he suffers any, he conceives spiritual joy and a great increase of divine love. When he is wronged, he is pierced with compunction and is stirred all the more toward contempt of the world. Whenever he is reproved injuriously by someone, if he is humble, he judges himself justly corrected. For he does not consider his own innocence in the accusation, but humbly and contritely weighs how gravely he has offended God in other matters.

The fifteenth indication of perfect humility is that he never resists the one correcting him, but preserves silence in all tranquility of spirit. He does not throw back into his reprover’s face even the faults very well known to him, nor does he seek any grounds for argument. He does not complain that an insult is being done to him; he offers no rash excuse in opposition, nor is he accustomed to rebuke words with words, but, as though mute, to keep silence in all things.

The sixteenth sign of profound humility is that he despises all the consolations of the world and seeks no friendships of mortals. For true humility, which is undertaken for the love of Christ, delights nowhere except in Christ alone. But one cannot remain in the love of Christ unless he has learned to deny both his own will and the world with all its disordered pleasures. Thus the Lord says in the holy Gospel: If anyone comes to me and does not hate his father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, and even his own soul, he cannot be my disciple.

The seventeenth sign of humility is that he does not divide himself among many things; he does not greatly seek the assistance and benefits of men, but humbly remains in his own moderation, or even in extremity. For benefits received from men he is not accustomed to applaud much, but shows himself grateful in few and simple words. Little gifts weaken the spirits of the infirm and often render the unwary useless and excessive in speech. Indeed, he who is truly humble does not delight in the conversations of givers, because they often impede the purpose of purity. The love of Christ does not love publicity.

The eighteenth sign of humility is that he sympathizes from the heart with all who suffer for the love of Christ, patiently bears the defects and weaknesses of others, and prays for the amendment of those who offend. For true humility shows the bowels of pity and compassion toward weak sinners; but false and simulated humility, because it is truly pride, swells against the fragile with harsh and indiscreet severity, and often becomes a cause of danger when in correcting it exceeds the rule of charity. But he who is truly humble always fears to exceed the due measure of correction, and therefore inclines more to mercy than to excessive severity.

The nineteenth indication of true humility perseveres with a mind free from all envy, and favors and prays for all men. He envies no one the gifts of divine kindness; he favors the grace of God in all and rejoices with the progress of others. He judges no one unworthy of God’s benefits; he thinks all better than himself. He considers the advantage of his neighbors his own, because he loves all as himself.

The twentieth sign of perfect humility recalls to memory, with bitterness of heart, the multitude of his sins, and therefore considers himself unworthy of every benefit and grace of God. Hence, seeking the depth of humility, he imitates the publican of the Gospel, who, having the memory of his offenses, did not wish to lift his eyes to heaven. For the monk who is truly humble for the love of Christ always bears in his heart the holy sorrow of compunction and penitence for the evils he has done.

The twenty-first indication of cenobitic humility is that he trusts the judgment of others more than his own, and totally subjects his own will, for Christ’s sake, to another’s command. Let those among you who follow the judgment of their own head consider whether they can or wish to apprehend this sign of monastic humility. For today we discern few observers of true monastic humility, though we see many monks professing it. O times, O morals, in which we all promise humility, and very few of us truly take care to preserve inviolate what has been promised.

The twenty-second sign of cenobitic humility is that he abominates and despises beautiful, new, and costly garments, and is content to have all clothing cast off by others.
