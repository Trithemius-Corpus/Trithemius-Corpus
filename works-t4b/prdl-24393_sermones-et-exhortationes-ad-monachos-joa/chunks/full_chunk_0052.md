On monks sent on the road.

...will return to the monastery of necessity, and impure. When a stream flows out, by its muddiness it soils the beauty of the open meadows around it; and when a monk attends to the tales of secular people, he stains the purity of his conscience. Therefore, before you set your foot outside the monastery, arm yourself with holy prayers; and before you go out, do not fail to commend your road to the Lord with prayers. Then carry out what the lawgiver commanded, and entrust yourself for protection to the prayers both of the abbot and of all the brothers.

Prescribe for yourself the canon of strict regular observance, so that, holding it firmly in memory, you may guard your soul from every drift toward the vanities that meet you. Go nowhere out of the monastery without the abbot’s blessing, because, since you ought to be dead and buried to this world, nothing further will be lawful for you without the command of your superior. Recognize yourself as a soldier of King Jesus Christ: let your whole life be governed by command. For a monk who goes out of the monastery anywhere without the abbot’s permission is like a little sheep in the darkness, burned up in the fire of a house.

But a true monk of Christ, even when he is outside the monastery in body, remains in the cloister of the mind; and he is no less alone when with many than he is accustomed to be solitary in his cell. The lover of Christ thinks of Jesus even in the midst of a multitude; and he who is a true worker of virtues keeps his heart firm in the fear of God in every place. For even if he is not in the monastery in body, nevertheless by the strength of his spirit he always stands as firmly as if alone in the cloister. The spirit is not defiled by the body’s movement, when the solitude of the mind is guarded.

O monk, you must live with the same holiness on the road with which you are commanded to conduct yourself in the monastery, and you must walk with a certain greater caution, lest you scandalize anyone by a more lax example, where there seem to be as many judges of monks as there are onlookers present. The eyes of all secular people look toward monks, and in their works they seek the holiness they hear them profess. A monk gives more offense in one rash word than a man given to the world does in ten thousand. For however frivolous and scurrilous a worldly man may seem, he has nevertheless not learned to tolerate calmly the insolence of monks, because in rustic simplicity everyone believes cloistered men to be not as they are, but as they ought to be. Hence a bad word or deed among them is charged to a monk’s damnation, while it is imputed to pardon in a worldly man.

And not without reason. For a monk who has professed a holy and approved rule is bound to direct his whole effort toward perfection, and in every purity of mind and body to surpass by far the conduct of secular people in word and deed. For a light or idle word, which is granted to secular people for pardon, is reckoned to cloistered men for destruction. Gold is more precious than all metals and is more greatly desired by mortals; and in none is a stain or corruption of falsity more quickly charged. And the defect that is discerned in gold with judicial severity is overlooked in other metals because of the cheapness of their nature. Gold gladdens the eyes by its beauty, and by its virtue strengthens the heart of life. Gold, when fused in fire, is not diminished; and the true monk of God is not changed from the purpose of holiness in the tribulation of persecution.

A small stain on a golden vessel greatly displeases in the sight of a powerful man; if a clay vessel is crooked, no one detests it. When a monk offends in small matters, a great outcry of many arises against him, because his conduct is compared to gold. The canonical life of monks is a golden mirror. Gold in the midst of mud receives no stain upon its nature, but preserves all its beauty inviolate. So too the true monk and lover of Christ remains, when sent on the road among people, the same as he has learned to conduct himself in the privacy of his little cell, because he walks before the Lord pure in mind and body alike. For if he is a true monk, he is not stained by the dust of another’s conduct, nor is he changed from the rectitude of his holy purpose by anyone’s presence or conversation. Among the tumultuous conversations of worldly people he thinks of something else, and the lax conduct of others does not delight his mind. He who is firm in heart is not divided even in the midst of disturbances.

Therefore, monk, you who desire to be good, gather your mind back to yourself; and before you leave your cell for the journey, set your heart in order. Let the fear of the Lord always be the companion of your journey.
