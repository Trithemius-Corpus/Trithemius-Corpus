*That one must pray without ceasing.*

There is no usefulness at all. Hence Saint Jerome says: God is to be prayed to not in words alone, but with the heart. Therefore the recitation of five psalms with purity of heart, serenity, and spiritual cheerfulness is better than the chanting of the whole Psalter with anxiety of heart and sadness.

And blessed Pope Gregory says: True petition is not in the sounds of the mouth, but in the voices of the heart, that is, in holy thoughts. For it is not words that make the stronger voices in the ears of God, but desires.

Therefore, except for those prayers which we are bound to say either by decree of the Church or by the institution of the monastic order, in the rest, prolixity and the multiplication of words contribute nothing, or very little, to true and inward devotion of mind. For where there is not fervent desire in the love of God, a suspicious lengthening of speech is introduced. For Saint Augustine says: Let much speaking be absent from prayer, but let fervent attention persevere; for to speak much in praying is to conduct a necessary matter with superfluous words.

Prayer, indeed, comprehends manifold matters in few words, so that it may quickly satisfy the simplicity of faith and what is sufficient for salvation, and by the depth of its mysteries may suffice for prudent and discerning minds. But for those who, because of the hardness of the inner heart, lack fervent compunction when they have given themselves to prayer, words are needed for gain, and cries with tears, so that by daily exercise of the mind they may at last kindle within themselves the burning fire of divine love, until in the affection of holy prayer their spirit rises together to God. But afterwards, when the fervor of divine love and compunction penetrates the mind, there is then no further need for the exercise of words; rather, the continuation of spiritual fuel is required, lest the fervor of the spiritual fire be extinguished.

For this purpose our most holy Father wished our prayers to be shortened in content, if anyone should wish to pray in the spirit. But there are some who are so hard of heart that they find little or no compunction while praying, and for them liberty often breeds ruin, and solitude often breeds despair.

Attend carefully, beloved brothers, to these things that I say to you, and consider how greatly you are bound to pray without ceasing. It is not enough for a monk, striving toward perfection in the school of Christ the eternal king, to gloss over the canonical prayers; nor is it sufficient for a man solemnly dedicated to God by vow to fulfill the common prayers of the order in community. For he who is commanded always to pray without ceasing is bound, beyond verbal prayers, also to spiritual prayers. For the prayers instituted by the Church or the order are under the merit of obedience and under the law of necessity by reason of precept; but that manner of praying which is called spiritual, useful and necessary at all times for a monk’s perfection, is strengthened in freedom of spirit.

Hence it must be noted that for prayers which are by precept of the Church or of the order, the verbal utterance of petitions is required by necessity, with the intention of satisfying the statute by which one is bound to pray. Let those see how they satisfy the Church who in certain churches do not express the seven canonical prayers by the utterance of words, but, with closed mouth, perform the solemn precept by thought alone, their eyes serving as ministers. But in those prayers which we undertake to say not by command, but by our own will and by devotion alone, the fervor of the mind in devotion is sufficient without the petition of words, because God seeks such worshipers, as we have said, who adore in spirit and truth. Yet the canonical prayers which we undertake to say at the hours we are bound to speak, and not merely to think. In both cases devotion of mind toward God is useful; without it no prayers are believed to be effective.

And so, my brothers, when we stand for prayer, we ought to watch with the utmost care and attend to the prayers that we say, lest we pray without the fruit of inward compunction. Therefore let every thought of the flesh withdraw far away; let no occupation with household affairs hinder the mind; nor let the soul think anything else within itself except this alone, what it prays. For this reason the priest too, in the prayer before the sacrifice, prepares the minds of the brothers by saying, Lift up your hearts, so that when the people answer, We have them lifted to the Lord, he admonishes them that they ought to think of nothing else than the very God whom they invoke.

Therefore let the breast be closed against the adversary and open to the Lord alone; nor in the time of holy prayer let it admit any worldly occupation at all. Great is the usefulness of pure prayer, which, like a faithful messenger, carries out the command of a fervent mind and, flying upward, ascends into heaven, where the flesh does not reach. For by what wings is prayer sustained, so that it may fly up into heaven? One of them is called fasting, and the other is named almsgiving. Hence Saint John Chrysostom says: Nothing is more powerful than a person who prays; he who prays with [unclear].
