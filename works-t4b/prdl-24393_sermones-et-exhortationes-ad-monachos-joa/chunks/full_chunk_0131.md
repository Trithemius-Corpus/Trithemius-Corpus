Whatever holy mother Church of the faithful has established as to be believed or observed, let him faithfully and firmly believe and keep, and let him in no way depart from her determination. All who remained outside the ark were once destroyed by the divine flood; Noah alone, with those who were in the ark, escaped safely. The ark prefigured the Church, and the event itself signified a mystery. Therefore there is no salvation for human beings outside the Church, which our Lord Jesus Christ built upon the most firm rock. Every unbeliever is not in the Church; therefore he will necessarily perish, just as those once did who were not in the ark. For all who do not believe in the Lord and Savior of the world, Jesus Christ, Jews, pagans, gentiles, Saracens, together with heretics and schismatics who have withdrawn themselves from the unity of the Catholic Church, have placed themselves outside the ark of salvation; if they persevere in error, without doubt they are to be damned forever with the demons.

Since, therefore, faith is the foundation of salvation, without which it is impossible to please God, the monk must know what he ought to believe, lest it happen that he err. Let him learn the Gospel and the constitutions of the Church; for it says, “He who does not know will not be known.” It is most shameful for a monk not to know the rules of the faith, and to be wholly ignorant of what he is bound to believe for salvation. We do not say that you ought to examine by curious argument the things proposed to you for belief, since, as Saint Pope Gregory says, faith has no merit where human reason supplies proof. But this we admonish: that the monk ought to be studious in the divine Scriptures, so that he may know what holy mother Church commands him to believe and may beware of positions and errors contrary to the faith. Therefore let every monk, as a soldier of Jesus Christ, be firm and stable in the Catholic faith, sober and studious in the Scriptures, and constant in good works.

The sixth thing that we believe necessary for a monk walking in the way of eternal salvation is that he should strive to know himself. For knowledge of oneself is the best of all branches of knowledge, and therefore it is rightly to be preferred to all the studies of mortals. Yet the beginning of this saving knowledge is exceedingly difficult, because very few are those who do not love the world. For no one who follows the world and loves the vanities that pass away can be a knower of himself. Nothing hinders a person more from knowledge of himself than the person himself. He who is fed by the pleasures of his flesh, who is lifted up into pride by the vain knowledge of this world, whether by riches or by honors, is not permitted to come to knowledge of himself. Indeed, to know many things and neglect oneself is not to be wise, but rather to be mad. It contributes to the accumulation of a graver damnation to learn many things and shamefully to be ignorant of oneself.

Let each of you, therefore, consider what he is, whence he is, and what he is going to be. A human being, in himself, is certainly nothing; whatever he is, he is from God and for God. What is a human being but a wayfarer and pilgrim, as it were for one day upon the earth, who today is alive in the world, but tomorrow, snatched away by death, is seen no more? Our whole life, every action, every day, every hour, shows how much we are nothing: by living we die, by acting we are corrupted; as time runs on, we fail and are changed. I compare our unstable, wretched, and weak race of human beings, how windy and vain it is in itself, with those who have gone before us to death. Truly he speaks rightly who compares us to the leaf of a tree. The wind seizes a leaf from the tree and casts it to the ground; in its place another new one afterward grows, subject to a similar fall and succeeding to the same fate. But what has fallen to the ground is dissolved into the ground and never afterward returns to the tree. Hence a certain wise man of the Greeks says: “As is the race of leaves, so too is the race of men.” The race of leaves is very like the race of human beings, for it flourishes for a time and is dissolved into the earth; neither the leaf returns to the tree nor the human being to life.

Now follows the third thing, which I think very necessary for a monk walking in the way of God. The person who understands himself to be a wayfarer in this life of very brief duration truly shows himself humble in all things. For why should earth and ashes be proud? He who truly knows himself cannot be exalted. My brothers, we have no cause at all for self-exaltation and pride, but much and continual cause for humility, since indeed we are earth and a certain compacting of dust, and after a few days are to return again into dust. But because true humility belongs to few, the way of salvation is therefore called narrow and is found by few. By humility one comes to the kingdom, as the Lord says in the holy Gospel: everyone who humbles himself for God’s sake will be exalted, and he who exalts himself will be humbled.
