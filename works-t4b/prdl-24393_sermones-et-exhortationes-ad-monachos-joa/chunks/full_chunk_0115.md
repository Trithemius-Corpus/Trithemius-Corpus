John Trithemius, abbot of the monastery of Sponheim, though unworthy, to the reverend father Lord Johannes de Colenhausen, abbot of Seligenstadt and formerly of Sponheim, my dearest predecessor: with prayers, a mind ready and prepared to please.

Recently, venerable teacher, I sent you the first book of my Exhortations to Monks, containing twenty-five homilies. After I learned that it had been approved by your examination, I judged that this second book also, which I now send, should confidently be inscribed to your name. In the former book I collected, for the instruction of the brothers, twenty-five homilies which I had delivered before them; in this one I have arranged seven sermons according to the order in which they were spoken in their presence.

I do not fear to undergo the examination of your paternity, since I have always had you as a gentle teacher, and long ago found you to be a sincere lover of truth. For you are vigorous in intellect, both gentle and profound, able easily to distinguish true from false and, where necessity requires, to amend everything sufficiently. Therefore I pray that, according to the gentleness innate in you, you may patiently bear the rudeness of my talent, since I am both a little child in knowledge of the Scriptures and a novice in the order of prelates. Exercise in the Scriptures, I trust, will give me practice, and continued concern for writing will give me progress.

The occupation of household business and the poverty of the place very often draw me back from salutary study and carry my mind elsewhere, against the purpose of my will. Leisure for writing is granted me only on feast days and Sundays; on these, out of reverence for God, I am accustomed to admit no cases of the country people. Sometimes also, after the customary chanting of matins, I am accustomed to spend what remains of the night on the study of letters, and, as far as I can, to work by lamplight on something for the instruction of my brothers.

For I desire in all things to follow your footsteps and, if I can, perpetually to observe the praiseworthy custom of teaching those subject to me which you introduced. I know that I am bound to this by my office; and although I am rude and unlearned, nevertheless I willingly wish, according to my powers, to be diligent both in learning and in teaching. I submit these sermons to your examination, and with humble supplication I ask that the file of your mastery may correct them. I have gathered a few out of many into order, since not all were to be entrusted to writing, nor did an excessive extension of the book seem to me either necessary or useful.

For on feast days, when we have respite from the tumult of worldly business, we devote ourselves to reading according to the rule of the Scriptures. And lest I should seem to occupy the place of shepherd in vain, what I am accustomed to work out by night I am accustomed, after none, in place of the regular study, to speak and to recite from a small sheet before the brothers in chapter. For I find that this concern of mine not only profits the brothers for edification, but also helps me toward practice in composition. Farewell, venerable teacher, and pray for us. From Sponheim, on the twelfth day of the month of September, in the year of the Christians 1486.
