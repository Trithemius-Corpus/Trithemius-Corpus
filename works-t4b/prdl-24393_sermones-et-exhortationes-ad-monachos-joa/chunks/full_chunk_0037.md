That One Must Pray Without Interruption.

...that they may be forgiven you. Before prayer, prepare your soul, and do not be like a man who tempts God. Let your mind be pure and burn in the love of the Lord; let your conscience be clean and strengthened in the purpose of holy conduct, and your prayer will be pleasing to divine ears. If you pray otherwise, you will obtain nothing. He who lives well in his works prays well, and he who prays with holy works obtains easily. Finally, prayer is made very effective and pleasing in the sight of God if it is at all times in harmony with the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. For he is God and man, the mediator between God and us, through whom we have a safe access to the Father, as he himself bears witness in the holy Gospel: “No one,” he says, “comes to the Father except through me.”

Therefore let no day pass in which you do not, at least once, give thanks in prayer to Christ, who by the pain of his wounds willed the sicknesses of your soul to be healed, who poured out his blood for you, and by his most innocent death redeemed you, wretched as you were, from the destruction of eternal death. A man is certainly most ungrateful and unworthy of the Christian name if he has been so forgetful of Christ’s many benefits that he does not every day, at least once, return acts of thanksgiving to him. But the monk, who has died to the world and is commanded to live for God alone, should continually carry in his breast the memory of the Lord’s passion: always giving thanks to his Redeemer, always blessing him, always adoring him, and daily, indeed as far as possible at every moment, turning over in his mind with tears and compassion the bitterness of Christ’s wounds.

Every day, after each of the regular prayers which the Greeks call canonical, with knees bent and mind lifted up to heaven, let him say:

I give thanks to you, and blessing you I adore you, O mediator of God and men, most sweet Christ Jesus, God and man: for your most holy incarnation; for your birth from the Virgin; for your circumcision; for your flight into Egypt and the seven-year exile which you endured for us. I adore you, Lord Savior Jesus Christ, for your labors, for hunger and thirst, cold and nakedness; for your journeys; for your teaching and preaching; for your miracles; for the calling of the nations; and for the justification of sinners. I give thanks to you, Lord, Son of the living God, for the pain of your wounds. I bless you for the shedding of your most holy blood. I adore you for the death which you most innocently endured, and I ask you, through all your passion, pity, and mercy: have mercy on me now and in the hour of my death.

With this very brief prayer the devout monk should pray at least seven times in the day, and whenever he passes before an image of the Crucified, he should in the same way adore Christ with fervor of mind. Whoever is a true lover of Christ always thinks of Christ, speaks of Christ, and venerates Christ; he does not allow Christ to be absent from himself, but senses him present everywhere. The passion of Christ is never absent from his memory, and from the greatness of love he forgets himself, wholly occupied in Christ in mind and desire. Whoever burns in spirit with the love of Christ contemns earthly things, conquers the flesh, and desires only heavenly things. Such a man, waking, always thinks of God with desire; sleeping, he dreams of Christ; and, continually sighing, he cries out with blessed Ignatius: “My love has been crucified.” With continual sighs he sighs, and Jesus, whom he loves with his whole heart, he sees not with the eyes but with the spirit present to him.

When the desire of love presses upon him, as often as he gazes on the Crucified, he is at once dissolved in tears and, burning with the fire of charity, cries out: O love, stronger than all strengths, how sweetly he burns who is kindled with desire for Christ. Rightly did he speak who says: “Love is strong as death.” For what can be stronger than the love of Christ, in whose ardor so many thousands of martyrs overcame, despising every kind of death? O love of the crucified Jesus, have mercy on me. Grant me to feel that sweetness of your love of which I speak, so that I may always carry in my heart your most bitter passion. I know, my brothers, I know that there is no more effective manner of praying, nor any more useful for us who are still weak, than in the memory and power of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore, since it is clear that without meditation there is no ascent of the mind to God, every good work must be begun in the name of Christ, because then we pour out prayers to God profitably when, with purified conscience, we pray through his only-begotten Son.

On the Guarding and Origin of Thoughts. Homily Nine.

Solomon, that once most wise king, in the book which he entitled Proverbs, exhorting his son to true wisdom, said among other things: “My son, attend to my wisdom, and incline your ear to my prudence, that you may guard your thoughts, and that your lips may preserve discipline.” For it is no small part of wisdom, dearly beloved brothers, if a monk knows how to discern his thoughts prudently and to guard their movements carefully at all times. It is a great thing to think nothing evil; but it belongs to no less prudence to be able to repel evil thoughts from their beginning. For the deep inward state of the human heart is always mobile, changeable, manifold, and unstable; to observe its beginnings and restrain its movements is the highest prudence and a great sign of wisdom.

If I have called the heart of man a mill driven by perpetual motion, I have not strayed from the truth, especially if someone wishes to examine its restlessness very closely. For what else is the human heart than a kind of mill, always grinding something in motion? The human heart is never idle; it never rests without motion, but is always doing something. And just as an empty mill consumes itself by its own motion, so the heart of man shamefully runs down from within itself when it is not held fast by thought of the good. It is always moved toward some object with the desire of its love; it is always grinding, always acting, always occupied about something delightful, whether it is good or evil. As the body is not moved without the soul, so the mind of man does not remain fixed in the good unless it is continually restrained by holy exercises.

For this reason the wise man said in Ecclesiasticus: “Gladness of heart is the life of man, and a treasure without defect is holiness, and the rejoicing of a man is length of days. Have mercy on your soul, pleasing God, and contain yourself; gather your heart in his holiness, and drive sadness far away from you.” What is the gathering of the heart, if not the diligent guarding of thought? For he gathers his heart in the holiness of the Lord who continually observes the movements of his thoughts in the fear of God. And therefore Scripture says: “With all custody guard your heart, for from it life proceeds.”

Thoughts take the beginnings of their movements from those things which are presented by the senses, and they wander the more dangerously the less they are considered from the beginning by the judgment of reason. Therefore whoever desires to be watchful over his thoughts, whoever longs to gather his heart in the holiness of the Lord, should first guard his exterior senses most diligently and place the bridle of prudence on each of them. Hence, concerning hearing and sight, Scripture commands thus: “Hedge your ears with thorns, and do not listen to a wicked tongue; make doors for your mouth, and bars for your ears.” “Blessed is the man who stops his ears lest he hear bloodshed, and closes his eyes lest he see evil.” Likewise in holy Job we read: “I made a covenant with my eyes, that I would not even think about a virgin.”

But against those who have fallen through the service of their senses into the pit of sins, the Lord bears witness through the prophet Jeremiah: “Death has climbed in through your windows; it has entered your houses to destroy the little children outside and the young men from the streets.” Death enters our houses through the windows whenever an iniquitous thought is admitted into the mind through the service of sight or hearing. For the house is the conscience, of which Scripture says: “Unless you hold yourself constantly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overturned.” Therefore the house of conscience must be fortified by the fear of the Lord, and the senses also must be restrained most diligently, because he does not sufficiently guard his thoughts who does not turn his ears and eyes away from the occasion of evil.

And so, if you wish to be a prudent guardian of your thoughts and a true gatherer of the heart in the holiness of the Lord, hedge your ears with the thorns of compunction and the fear of God, lest they hear evil, and turn your eyes away, lest through sight they introduce sin. What you are not permitted to desire, do not look upon with desire; and what it is not expedient to have, do not think upon. Many have perished through the service of the senses, and because they go on carelessly looking at outward things, they have very often shamefully lost their inward goods. He who does not fear danger is certainly already near destruction, and he who presumes on his own strength runs hastily toward the ruin of sin. He who darts his eye into the flash of lightning will be blinded for a time, and he who gazes at the beauty of women will not be free from danger.
