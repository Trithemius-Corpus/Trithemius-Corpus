That one must pray without interruption.

...has no usefulness at all. Hence Saint Jerome says: God is to be prayed to not only in words, but with the heart. Therefore the chanting of five psalms with purity of heart, serenity, and spiritual gladness is better than the chanting of the whole Psalter with anxiety and sadness of heart. And the divine Pope Gregory says: True petition is not in the sounds of the mouth, but in the voices of the heart, that is, in holy thoughts. For words do not make stronger sounds in the ears of God, but desires do.

Therefore, apart from those prayers which we are bound to say either by decree of the Church or by the institution of the monastic order, complexity in the rest and multiplication of words contribute nothing, or little, to true inward devotion of mind; for where there is no fervent desire in the love of God, a superfluous extension of words is introduced. For holy Augustine says: Let much talking be absent from prayer, but let fervent intention persevere; for to speak much in prayer is to carry on a necessary matter with superfluous words. For prayer comprehends many things in few words, so that the simplicity of faith may quickly learn the sufficiency of its salvation, and the prudence of the more ingenious may be amazed at the depth of the mysteries.

But those who, because of hardness of heart, lack the compunction of inward fervor, when they prostrate themselves for prayer need longer speech, crying, and tears, so that by prolonged exercise of the mind they may at last strike out within themselves the burning fire of divine love, until they rise into the affection of holy prayer in the Spirit of God. But after the fervor of divine love and compunction has penetrated the mind, there is no longer need of the exercise of words; rather, the continuation of spiritual nourishment is required, lest the fervor of the spiritual fire be extinguished. To this end our most holy father wished prayer in community to be shortened, so that, if anyone wished to pray in the spirit, he could do so apart.

There are, however, some who are so hard of heart that in praying they find little or nothing of spiritual compunction; for them liberty brings ruin, and solitude often begets despair. Attend, I beg you, dearest brothers, to what I say to you, and consider how much you are bound to pray without interruption. It is not enough for a monk, striving toward perfection in the school of Christ the eternal king, to discharge the canonical prayers; nor is it sufficient for a man solemnly dedicated to God by vow to fulfill the common prayers of the order in community. For he who is commanded always to pray without interruption is bound beyond verbal prayers also to spiritual prayers. For prayers instituted by the Church or the order fall under the merit of obedience and under the law of necessity by reason of precept; but that manner of praying which is called spiritual is strengthened at all times for the monk as useful and necessary for perfection in the liberty of the spirit.

Hence it must be noted that for prayers which are of the precept of the Church or the order, the utterance of words is necessarily required, with the intention of satisfying the statute by which anyone is bound to pray. Let those who do not express the seven canonical prayers by utterance of words, but with closed mouth run through the sequence of prayers by thought alone, with the eyes assisting, see how they satisfy the command of the Church. But in those prayers which we undertake to say not by command but by our own will and from devotion alone, the fervor of the mind toward God suffices without the utterance of words; for God, as we have said, requires such worshipers as worship in spirit and truth. But we are bound to say the canonical prayers which they call the Hours, and not only to think them.

In both cases devotion of mind toward God is useful; without it no prayers are believed to be effective. And so, my brothers, when we stand for prayer, we ought with the greatest zeal to keep watch and attend to the prayers which we pour out, lest we pray without the fruit of inward compunction. Therefore let every thought of the flesh withdraw far away; let no occupation with household affairs impede the mind; and let the soul at that time think of nothing else whatever except this alone, which is being prayed. For this reason also the priest, after the customary preface has been given before the sacrifice, prepares the minds of the brothers, saying, “Lift up your hearts,” so that when the people answer, “We have lifted them to the Lord,” they may be warned that they ought to think of nothing other than the very God whom they invoke.

Let the breast, then, be closed against the adversary and lie open to the Lord alone; and in the time of holy prayer let it admit absolutely no concern of worldly occupation. Great indeed is the usefulness of pure prayer, which, like a faithful messenger, carries out the command of a fervent mind and, flying, ascends into heaven where the flesh does not reach. For prayer is sustained by two wings, that it may fly up into heaven: one of them is called fasting, and the other is named almsgiving. Hence Saint John Chrysostom says: Nothing is more powerful than a person praying, who prays with fasting...
