Sermon I. Folio LXI.

The monk, therefore, who is strong with true virtue neither ventures rashly nor fears without counsel. For fortitude together with constancy is the royal road; from it the rash and obstinate man turns aside to the right, while the fearful, faint-hearted, and timid man turns aside to the left. The monk who is constant and strong in spirit never withdraws from the purpose of holiness, but is neither cast down by adversities nor lifted up by prosperity. Hence the most holy bishop Ambrose also says: “The fool is changed like the moon. But the wise man is not broken by fear, is not altered by power, is not puffed up by prosperity, and is not submerged by sorrows.” For where there are wisdom and virtue, there are constancy and fortitude of spirit. The wise man, therefore, is always the same in spirit; he is neither diminished nor increased by changes of affairs, nor does he waver like a little child, so as to be carried about by every wind of doctrine, but he remains perfect in Christ, founded in charity and rooted in the perfection of faith.

Lastly, my dearest brothers, perseverance is necessary for you in every good and virtuous work; without it no one ever comes to the crown of glory. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “He who has persevered to the end will be saved.” Take away perseverance, and virtue will have no prize, service no reward, nor will a benefit merit grace. For perseverance alone deserves to receive the crown of praise for good works; without it, it profits nothing to have begun the good. No virtue has merit to which perseverance has not given increase. The kingdom of eternal blessedness is not promised to those who begin the good, but to those who persevere in the good even unto death, because not in the morning, not at midday, but when evening had come, the denarius was paid to those laboring in the vineyard.

For it is not the man who began well who is to be praised, but the one who continued well to the end is to be honored. Therefore, my brothers, I am accustomed earnestly to admonish you, and I must never cease, that you diligently attend to your calling and walk cautiously in all religion and holiness, as befits sworn soldiers of Christ. For now is the time for doing good, because when the hour of death has arrived it will utterly cut off every capacity for working. And therefore, as long as you live sound and unharmed, do good; always consider the future recompense, and grow daily in spiritual progress. Seek the Lord while he can be found. Let the life of Christ the Savior be your imitation; you are bound, by the foundations of your institution, to follow him not by bodily steps, but by your powers, and by likeness of affections. Walk cautiously in all things; always place the fear of God before the gaze of your mind in true humility. For great purity is required of you who have consented, in mind and body, to the holy warfare of Christ. It now belongs to you, brothers, to despise the world and all things that are in the world for God’s sake with constant fortitude; to overcome all movements of fleshly pleasures; to flee sin; and to occupy yourselves continually in good and holy works. Your life also, so far as human weakness permits, ought to imitate angelic purity, and to flee and detest all depravity. The holy angels are never idle, but are always attentive, without weariness or tedium, to the praises of God. The holy angels love God above all things, and do not withdraw from his charity even for a moment. In imitation of them, the monk ought to be assiduous in the service of Christ, and always occupied in good works according to the Gospel and the Rule. The monk ought to have most fervent charity for God, and true, orderly, and sincere love of neighbor. For burning love toward God perfects the monk and does not allow him to be idle. For the four-times most holy pope Gregory says: “The love of God is never idle. For it works great things if it exists; but if it refuses to work, it was never true love of God.” For if the mad poet spoke truth concerning carnal love, saying, “let him love who does not wish to become idle,” how much more strongly does the most chaste love of God impel the mind it possesses toward arduous things. Love God, brothers, with the whole heart, as far as the strength of desire can extend itself, and you will most easily be able to observe all the things commanded you. For nothing is difficult to one who loves; rather, burning love makes all hard things, all grave and arduous things, easy.

What the ordering of a good monastic life is: Sermon Two.

We have said that the institution of our most holy father Benedict, which by the customary term we call religion or order, shone forth as most holy at the beginning of its foundation, and was very greatly and manifoldly spread throughout the whole church of the Latins. Hence for very many years in this same order many wise, learned, and most holy men flourished,

who magnificently illuminated the whole Church. For there are those who write that from this monastic religion of ours fifteen thousand six hundred persons, men as well as nuns, have been enrolled in the catalogue of the saints. This multitude of saints in our order, brothers, is set before us for imitation, not for vain glory. How can their multitude conduce to our salvation, if we do not imitate the way of life in which they went before us? Whenever we wish usefully to praise the most holy streams of our order, let us hasten by holy conduct to come into their fellowship. Then indeed we shall glory fruitfully in the multitude of saints of our order, if, by living rightly, we earnestly strive to increase their number. For this reason we set before you so great a multitude of saints: so that, when you see before you in this very order many who have overcome the world and the flesh, you may at last rise up from your lukewarm way of life. The virtue that God’s mercy bestowed on them he will not deny to you, if you ask in true charity. They overcame the world because they loved Christ with complete devotion, despising all things for love of him. They despised all things, and the Lord Jesus provided for them whatever seemed necessary for the sustenance of life.

This fervor of devotion among monks endured for many years after the first institution of the order, with great constancy, and converted the souls of many thousands of people to the Lord. But nothing in human affairs is stable; nothing among perishable things is perpetual. Holy religion brought riches to monks, until in time the daughter suffocated the mother. For, as Scripture says, all who wish to become rich fall into the temptations of the devil. The beloved grew fat and kicked back; made gross and enlarged by earthly possessions, he abandoned the holy pursuits of his first institution and went after vanities and false madnesses. Abundance of temporal things made him poor and miserable in spiritual riches.

This pitiable deformity of our order began almost two hundred years after the passing of the divine father Benedict, and grew more and more with time, until at last the harmful poison spread through the whole order. Yet this deformity did not seize the order suddenly, but through the succession of many years it occupied almost the whole of it, although almighty God never left it wholly destitute of good men, but always kept for himself some who in no way bent their knees to Baal. For men of mercy arose, whose just deeds will never be handed over to oblivion; pitying the ruin of the order, they put their shoulder beneath the labor and, with the Lord bestowing his aid, restored it in many places. Many different partial reforms in the order indeed flourished very often at various times, but there was never any general reform that brought the whole order back to the integrity of regular observance. Finally, we now see almost all the partial restorations, which we have said were many, collapsed again.

Most recently, however, namely in the year 1420 of the Christians, when the general council was being celebrated at Constance, a certain new and up to that time latest partial reform took its beginning in Germany. From a certain monastery in the region of Saxony, then indeed the poorest of all, called Bursfelde, it receives the word of its name. The first author and prince of this most holy reform was Johannes Rode, abbot of the distinguished monastery of Saint Matthias at Trier. Having been appointed general visitor of the order by the aforesaid Council of Constance, lest he himself should be judged reprobate while preaching to others, he first displayed reform in himself. For he reformed both himself first of all and the monks of the said monastery; he issued constitutions conforming to the Rule, and obtained confirmation of the holy work from the Apostolic See. Among all the monasteries of the order, however, Bursfelde, a humble and rather poor monastery, first received this reform, which it afterwards spread to Reinhausen and to a few neighboring monasteries of Saxony.

At length, when Johannes Rode, abbot of Saint Matthias and prince of our reform, had died, and when no suitable successor for extending the business that had been begun was found in that same place, Johannes de Hagen, abbot of Bursfelde, who had already reformed certain monasteries, a holy man and very zealous for the order, was chosen at the insistence of Jakob, archbishop of Trier. Content with his poverty, he did not consent to leave Bursfelde, lest he seem to have preferred riches to poverty. He therefore remained in his own monastery, and another was appointed in the place of the first reformer. Since this man seemed comparable to his predecessor neither in learning nor in zeal, he was able to accomplish little for the continuation of the business that had been begun, nor to extend the reform among many.
