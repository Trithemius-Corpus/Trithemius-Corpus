*Homily XII.*

The servant of Christ is believed to act in Christ’s place in the monastery; therefore he is rightly honored by all. Live, then, as though you always had him present before your eyes, and whatever you would not presume to do or say while he was looking on, do not attempt in any way when he is absent. For it is foolish consolation, when conscience is gnawed by guilt, for sin to lie hidden from men, and for one who knows himself sick unto death to flee the judgment of his shepherd. A monk who does evil and wants it hidden from his prelate has violated the integrity of the monastic rule and has gravely sinned against the fifth degree of humility. For it commands thus: The fifth degree of humility is this: if, by humble confession, he has not concealed from his abbot all evil thoughts coming into his heart and evil deeds done by him in secret.

These are the admonitions of father Benedict; these are the holy teachings of monastic instruction. If you, monk, despise or neglect them, you are proved forgetful of your own salvation. It is a sure sign of humility if a monk has not refused to open his heart to his senior, and wants none of the evils he has committed to be hidden from his shepherd. He who confesses his offense with humility will be strengthened against the adversary of his salvation; and he who hides his thoughts will advance into evil. For there is no evil thought so small that it will not gradually grow if neglected, and no vice so slight that it will not gain strength if it is dissembled.

Therefore evil thoughts must be driven away at once, from the beginning, because when a depraved thought is not immediately extinguished, after delight it begets delight; delight, in turn, begets consent; consent plunges one into action. Then the action of evil, more often repeated, produces habit; habit, necessity; necessity brings on despair; despair, contempt; contempt, obstinacy; and that soon begets the eternal death of the soul. For Scripture says: When the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises. Is not your abbot, O monk, the shepherd and physician of your soul? Am I not, my dearest brothers, after God, the one who feeds your mortal bodies with necessary nourishment? Am I not compelled to minister to you the word of salvation? If you, the sheep, flee the shepherd, what pastures of the spirit do you expect from us? The sick man who flees the physician despises his own salvation and willingly kills his own soul.

Fo. XXIX.

A monk, therefore, walking in the way of malice and turning aside from the paths of justice, is near to death; and he falls so much the more into the depth of vices as he is less brought back by the judgment of the one correcting him. But he is truly a monk who guards his soul from every iniquity and does nothing at all that he would wish his shepherd not to know. He who conceals his way is not pure, and he who flees the presence of judges increases suspicion. Like a sheep you walk in the desert, to be seized by the wolf, negligent and impure monk, who withdraws yourself from the knowledge of your shepherd and are devoured by demons. A diseased sheep running to destruction, and a monk hiding every sin: there is no salvation for them unless they return to the physician.

Monks returning from a journey should immediately, on that very day, according to the monastic rule, prostrate themselves before the brothers and in the sight of all, so that whatever has been committed on the way through negligence may be expiated by the common prayers. For the common prayer of many avails greatly for washing away sins, if it has been poured out to the Lord with the fervor of devotion.

Here ends Homily XII, On monks sent on a journey.

*On the daily expiation of sins.*

*Homily XIII.*

Since we have been placed in this corruptible life, dearest brothers, we offend daily in many things; and however holily we may seem to conduct ourselves, we are not free from sin. For the body, which is corrupted, weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the mind that thinks many thoughts. For Scripture says: There is no just man on earth who does good and does not sin. Likewise the evangelist John says: If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. Indeed, the soul sins through commerce with the flesh, when it is too much delighted, and the strength of the spirit is lessened by despair. For the mind is deceived by the presentation of sensible things, and readiness to offend is strengthened by long habit. The weakness of the flesh weakens the fervor of the spirit, and the frailty of the will resists the holy purpose of the mind.

Hence Solomon, the wisest king, says in the book of Proverbs: Seven times in a day the just man will fall and rise again, but the wicked will fall into evil. By the witness of this sentence we are admonished not to grow lukewarm in the purpose of holiness, even if it should happen that we have fallen seven times in a day. As often, O monk, as you fall from the purpose of holiness through sin, so often rise again to penance, intending thereafter never to return. For however often men have fallen, they can always rise again to repentance, and by tears and prayers obtain the grace of God which they had lost by sinning. For the heart of man is flexible and can be moved in either direction, because no man in the flesh can become so evil, if he makes use of the grace of the Lord.

Since these things are so, my brothers, you must be admonished by today’s exhortation to walk carefully, so that anyone who, with conscience as witness, already walks in the paths of justice may persevere daily by making progress in that path; and anyone who understands himself to be guilty before the Lord may amend his life as quickly as possible. If human frailty daily drives you, or, to speak more properly, casts you down and makes you offend, let the love of God and of your own salvation also stir you daily to expiate your fault humbly through true contrition. For thus the just man falls seven times in a day and rises again, since even if he slips through human frailty, nevertheless, always persevering in the purpose of holiness, he is strengthened more and more in soul in the love of God.

For while he keeps his conscience immune from grave sins, he is unwillingly defiled by daily negligences; and when he sees that he cannot live without offenses, he groans the more deeply, humbly acknowledging his own weakness. But if he has also been overtaken by a graver offense, he soon rises again to amendment through penance. Therefore, considering the condition of human frailty, the ancient governors of monastic discipline established various kinds of expiation, by which monks of good will might daily make progress toward the better, and the wicked might be compelled to the observance of regular life.

For in the Rule the holy father Benedict commands thus, saying: With all solicitude the abbot should take care concerning delinquent brothers, because the physician is needed not by the healthy but by those who are ill. For this reason the first leaders in the observance of the rule established, with provident consideration, that every day after Prime an assembly of faults should be held in the appointed place; in it, with the abbot or prior presiding, the deeds of all the brothers should be brought into the open with equal examination.

This place, my brothers, bears the figure of the purgatory of souls, because here not only are past sins expiated, but we are also forearmed against future ones. For in this place, which we call the chapter house, whatever you have done wrong openly is corrected and expiated through salutary penance. Here every day, with the reader reciting it, you come to know your monastic law, according to whose integrity you have promised to live. Here on each day you hear the exhortatory word of salvation. Here you listen to brief recitations of the victories of Christ’s martyrs and their manifold sufferings for the orthodox faith. This is the school of monks, necessary for the consideration of regular discipline, in which everything commanded to cloistered men for observance is learned.
