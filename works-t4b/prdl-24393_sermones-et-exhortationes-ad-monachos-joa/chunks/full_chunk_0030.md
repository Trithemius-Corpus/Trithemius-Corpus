I blush to say it, and yet I am compelled to declare it: your labors outside the monastery and the cell bring more destruction to the purity of the soul than salvation. For what is your labor, and what are your pursuits in your labors? To wander about, run here and there, indulge in feasts and cups, and occupy the remaining time idly with vain and useless things.

You do not have in yourselves that fervor of the Holy Spirit with which we read that the monks of our former institution were once inflamed: that, from desire for the necessity of the community, with such zeal and such effort, all and each labored with their own hands, so that almost the whole business of the monastery was placed in the prudence of each one. For in this way each and all ought to have labored with their own hands for the support of the congregation. Amid these things, too, they performed the service of divine worship with such eagerness day and night that they seemed in no way at all to have been occupied in the labors of their hands.

Who among you, brothers, is an imitator of such men, so that he regards the needs of our whole community as his own, or wishes and strives by his own labor, according to his strength, to relieve them? They, while laboring with their hands, continually implored God with their hearts; but you, intent on subtleties, neither satisfy the office of the hands nor remember the monk. In the time of regular fasting, they either diligently kept the measure of abstinence appointed for them, or, if they could not because of the greatness of their labors, they ate for this reason: so that they might be rendered fit and suitable for labor. But you do not eat so that you may labor; rather you labor so that you may eat, as though according to the Rule one had to fast. You take occasion from labor to relax the fast.

Indeed, how foully and uselessly some of you handle your garments: what zealous lover of regular discipline could pass it over? For what is there, my brothers, that we consume wastefully? Everything that first comes to the monasteries as God’s servant is Christ’s patrimony, and the whole of it is dedicated to God; nor is it lawful for anyone to misuse the things of Christ or to turn them to another use. Do you not know what our legislator Benedict says plainly enough to you in the Rule? “If anyone treats the things of the monastery filthily or negligently, let him be corrected. If he does not amend, let him be subjected to regular discipline.” And in the chapter on the cellarer of the monastery he says: “Let him regard all the vessels of the monastery and all its substance as if they were the sacred vessels of the altar.”

Finally, I cannot and certainly ought not to keep silent about how filthily and negligently many of you handle everything, as if they were either not seen by God, or no one were causing loss to the goods of the monastery by equal sloth. Know, brothers, that I announce to you in warning that for these things also, just as for your other sins, you will have to render account to the Lord God. For this reason we have reduced your labors outside the monastery, lest by working badly you sin more gravely; and we have enjoined upon you manual work: to write books, bind them, and things similar to these, which you can carry out within the enclosures of the monastery with tranquility of mind and body. But in this too your hands are slow, though you would have enough leisure in the whole week to do it diligently for the love of God.

There is no manual work that, in my judgment, befits a monk more than writing books of sacred reading and preparing what is necessary for others who write. For this holy labor, although prayer often interrupts it, requires vigilance for the eye of the mind no less than for that of the body. Necessity also urges us to apply ourselves diligently to writing books, if we desire to have at hand the things from which we may be usefully occupied with one another in spiritual study. For although the library of this monastery, and all that belonged to it, was once distinguished and great, it was so scattered, sold, and alienated by the deformed monks before us that no more than fourteen volumes were found here by us.

Indeed, by the industry of the art of printing, recently invented in our days at Mainz, many volumes are brought to light every day; but it is by no means possible for us to buy them all, since we are still oppressed by the greatest poverty. Therefore I admonish and teach you that, since you do not come readily enough to external labors of the hands, you should labor as diligently as possible in writing volumes for the honor of God, because just as idleness is the enemy of the soul, so moderate labor is the preserver of the spiritual life. For the monk, because he is a soldier of the eternal king, must never be idle. Idleness begets acedia, and that brings upon the soul the ruinous poverty of death.
