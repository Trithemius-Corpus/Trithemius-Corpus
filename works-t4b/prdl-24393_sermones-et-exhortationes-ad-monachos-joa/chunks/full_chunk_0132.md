*Sermon III. Fol. LXVII*

Fourth, for the monk travelling toward heaven it is necessary that he be free from every defilement, since no one is admitted into the kingdom of God unless he is commended by the utmost purity of conscience. In holy virtues and good works conscience must be cleansed, for by them the gate of heaven is opened, and for the peace of God the love of the world is despised. Hence Peter, the most Christian chief apostle, says in his second epistle: “For this very reason, add virtue to your faith; and to virtue, knowledge; and to knowledge, abstinence; and to abstinence, patience; and to patience, piety; and to piety, love of the brethren; and to love of the brethren, charity. For if these things are present in you and abound, they will not leave you empty or without fruit in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Faith, adorned by works of true charity, is one strengthening force for virtue as it stands firm, and the way of the good, which leads souls withdrawn from the flesh not into the fabulous Elysian fields of the poets, but into the angelic choirs.

Fifth, the traveller on the arduous road of the heavenly kingdom must follow the will of God in all things by keeping his commandments. Whatever is said or done must be examined piously and carefully, to see whether it agrees with God and with the ordinances of the Church. But if it is found to be contrary either to God or to the Church, let him flee it at once, lest he fall into sin. It belongs to a wise man to commit nothing unexamined, and before he acts to consider everything carefully by prudent counsel. Whoever on a difficult road wishes to be safe and secure must, according to his strength, imitate the life and conduct of our Lord Jesus Christ. This is the light for all toward eternal life.

Sixth, in our judgment it is necessary for the traveller to love salutary study of the scriptures, to be diligent in reading, and not to be given to vain superstitions, but simple, pure, and upright in mind. It is not fitting for monks to wander without discrimination among the writings of the Gentiles, nor to accommodate divine things shamefully to the fables of the poets. Whoever follows vanity will never be wise, and whoever seeks curiosities will quickly fall away. Let the monk flee every reading of vain superstition, because whatever does not lead to knowledge of the divine scriptures must at all times be avoided by our traveller.

Seventh, in our judgment it is very necessary for the traveller that he prudently direct his desires and thoughts and guard them from every disordered concupiscence. Our unruly little ass must be restrained by the bridle of discipline, lest the flesh draw the spirit headlong into a snare. Whoever thinks that he is walking in the sight of him who always sees all things preserves his conscience pure and watches over his conduct with the greatest diligence. He is always vigilant in the fear of God; he always chastises his little body with fasting and abstinence, for love of the heavenly homeland. The monk who recognizes himself as a pilgrim and stranger in this world never forgets the heavenly homeland, for he sees nothing on the road of the present life in which his soul may take delight. A prudent traveller considers none of the sights along the roads; nothing among passing things delights him in his pilgrimage, but at every time he sighs for the end of the journey. He always thinks sweetly of his homeland; he bears it grievously that his pilgrimage is prolonged or delayed; and therefore he desires to have nothing in this world except the Lord Jesus, in whose sight he earnestly hastens to appear.

Eighth, in our judgment, the perfection of divine charity is necessary for the traveller. For no one will be able to come to the heavenly homeland who departs from the path of charity. The charity of God is the way to God; if anyone neglects to keep it burning within himself, he has wandered miserably from the true road of the heavenly homeland. Scripture says: God is charity; and he who remains in charity remains in God, and God in him. But he who is not in charity has already withdrawn far from the way of salvation; and walking in the darkness of sins, he will never come to the kingdom.

*How Sin Is to Be Fled*

*Sermon V.*

You have heard, my very dear brothers, what Saint John the Apostle says about sinners: “Whoever commits sin is from the devil, for the devil sins from the beginning.” A truly fearful and greatly dreaded sentence. If all who commit sin are from the devil, and we all offend in many things, how many shall we find immune from sin? Yet he says afterward: “Everyone who is born of God does not commit sin, because his seed remains in him; and he cannot sin, because he is born of God.” But he had said before: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” What does this apparent contradiction mean, that no one of the sons of God is without sin, and yet he who is born of God does not commit sin? There is no contradiction here, because both are true: no one is without sin, since no one is without the law of sin; and he who is born of God does not commit sin, because through the law of the mind, that is, through charity, which is the seed of God, he does not commit sin. For charity covers the multitude of sins; without it there is no good work, and it alone has power to destroy every sin. Hence the holy bishop Augustine says: No saint and no just man lacks sin; yet because of this he does not cease to be just or holy, if he holds holiness with affection. But those sins of the just are not to be thought mortal or enormous, but small and venial, without which life is scarcely lived; for every mortal sin must be far from the just man, if he is to retain the name of justice. Mortal sin separates a person from God, kills the soul, and damns it eternally with the demons. By God’s grace we can avoid this pernicious kind of mortal sin, and by uprightness of will we ought diligently to do so. For this reason, we monks have been made mindful of our deed, so that, living most cautiously under the command of saving obedience, we may not fall into the pit of mortal sin. Therefore the monk must always keep watch in good works, so that he may live free from mortal crime. But whoever on the way of God feels the flesh troublesome and contrary to him must make it subject and, as it were, obedient to the spirit by the remembrance of punishments and by fear.

First, therefore, the monk who loves his own salvation must flee the deceitful world and never permit himself to be caught by the flatteries of the flesh. For what is the world but a great, deep, and restless sea, in which there are as many crawling evils as there are evil desires of men not ordered toward the good? O world, deceitful, deceptive, and unclean, you shamefully deceive those who cling to you; you submerge all who follow you in the depth of hell. How happy are those who despise you for the love of Christ and, making a useful separation from your vanities, depart from you in time. Whatever we perceive in the world is vain, and every lover of the world is made vain and will shortly perish. Think that you will die soon, for nothing so draws people back from sin as frequent and intense meditation on death. For Scripture says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” Thinking on the necessity of dying strikes the mind with fear and restrains it from vice.

Second, what persuades one to flee sin is fear of the future judgment, at which all people must be presented. No one can escape this dreadful judgment, but we must all appear there. Two judgments hang over us to be feared, and we shall be able to evade neither of them; necessity compels us to appear at both. One is called particular, in which immediately after death the soul is judged according to its merits, either to glory or to punishment. The other is called general, which will be at the end of the world, where we shall all be judged publicly by the only-begotten Son of God. Each judgment is exceedingly terrible and greatly to be feared by all mortals. Hence the prophet Joel says: “The day of the Lord is great and very terrible; and who will endure it?” Likewise in Ecclesiastes King Solomon says: “All things that are done under the sun God will bring into judgment, for every error, whether it be good or evil.” Likewise in Isaiah it is read thus: “The Lord will come to judgment with the elders of his people and its princes.” And in the book of the prophet Amos it is written thus: “Woe to those who desire the day of the Lord. What is it to you? For that day of the Lord is darkness and not light.” Likewise Zephaniah, prophet of the Lord, foretelling the bitterness of the future judgment, said: “The voice of the day of the Lord is bitter; there the strong man will suffer tribulation. That day is a day of wrath, a day of tribulation and distress, a day of calamity and misery, a day of darkness and gloom, a day of cloud and whirlwind. In the fire of the Lord’s zeal the whole earth will be devoured, because he will make a consummation with haste for all who dwell on the earth.” Keep always in memory, most dear brothers, this exceedingly terrible day, to be dreaded by all mortals, which you believe will certainly come. For the most holy bishop Ambrose says: Nothing is more profitable for an honorable life than to believe that he will be the future judge whom neither the secrets of minds deceive nor dishonorable things delight. For, as Saint Christopher says, we must all be made manifest before the tribunal of Christ, so that each one may receive the things proper to the body, according as he has done, whether good or evil. How greatly that day of the future and most terrible judgment is to be feared, the holy priest Jerome shows when he says: Whether I eat or drink, or do anything else, that voice always seems to sound in my ears: “Arise, you dead, and come to judgment.” Whenever I think of the day of judgment, I tremble in my whole heart and body. Therefore, if there is any joy in the present life, it must be conducted in such a way that the bitterness of the future judgment never withdraws from memory.
