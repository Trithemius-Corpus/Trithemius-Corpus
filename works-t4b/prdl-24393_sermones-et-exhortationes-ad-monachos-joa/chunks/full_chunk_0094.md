Homily XX. Fol. XLVII.

...the obedience of subjection. For a monk without obedience is a monster of the devil; and the man who resists the commands of his superiors will inherit the punishments of hell. The disobedience of the first man lost, in the beginning, the happiness of eternal life; the obedience of Jesus Christ restored it. For he was made obedient to the Father for us, even unto death; and by his most innocent passion he restored to us the happiness that had been lost.

Therefore humble obedience is said to belong to salvation, as our holy lawgiver Benedict cries out in the Rule: “Listen, O son, to the precepts of the master, and incline the ear of your heart; gladly receive the admonition of a loving father, and effectively embrace it, so that by the labor of obedience you may return to him from whom you had withdrawn by the sloth of disobedience.”

Obedience is the tree of eternal salvation, which grows in the garden of monastic humility. Obedience is the dwelling-place of the Holy Spirit, and the tomb of every self-will. Obedience is the summit of simplicity and merit, and the one proof of monastic discipline. Obedience is the destruction and everlasting ruin of all pride, and the correction of every vicious sinner. Obedience conquers every malice and extinguishes every disordered pleasure of the flesh. Obedience makes conscience pure and clean, and preserves true humility in the mind. Obedience is the remedy for the wounded soul and the foundation of the whole of monastic observance.

Obedience is the faithful guardian of all good works and the discipline of a sincere mind. Obedience is the beginning of monastic life and the glorious ornament of the monk. Obedience is the conservatory of peace and concord; without it the strength of monastic discipline perishes. Obedience always directs the mind’s gaze toward God; and the man who is truly monastic in spirit despises all the consolations of this world. Obedience makes the monastic man near to God and far removed from every filth of the soul. Obedience is the adornment of the soul and the garment of beauty; without it no one comes to the eternal brightness of the kingdom.

Obedience is the mother of victory; and the man who has learned to be humbly subject for the honor of God will obtain the crown of martyrs in heaven. Obedience separates the monk from the world; and whoever follows it with his whole heart will be great before the Most High. Obedience is the consummation of Christian perfection; in its humble workshop, the more a monk advances, the holier he becomes. Obedience is certain security at the hour of death; for the more prepared a monk has been for obedience to the commandments, the more safely he will fly forth when he is about to depart from here.

Obedience is the reward of the humble and the special mark of merits. Obedience is the form of Christian gentleness and the strongest nourishment of inward charity. Obedience is the shadow of chastity and the ornament of innocent purity. Obedience is the wisdom of monks; and acquiescence in the commands of prelates is the highest philosophy of cloistered men. Let obedience and humility be the monk’s resources and riches; and to possess continually in the mind the memory of the Lord’s passion is the greatest sweetness, above all the feasts of the world.

Obedience and humility make the monk; but the man who is rebellious and proud bears the monastic habit without cause. Obedience is, in the mind, the living voluntary mortification of the members; its merit is of such great dignity that a human being has nothing greater which may be offered for the honor of God. Let each person offer for the love of God what he can; without doubt, the man who, through obedience, sacrifices his own will for God offers more than all.

It is indeed a great good to distribute one’s possessions openly for the love of Christ for the use of the poor, and to relieve the poverty of the needy with alms. Yet as much as the free choice of the will is nobler, in comparison with all necessity, than heaven is distant from earth, so much more does that man offer who, for the love of God, has of his own free will denied himself forever.

Whatever of external substance you offer to God is yours, yet it is not yours but another’s, and it is of lesser value both in estimation and in reward than your own self. You have one thing of your own which, offered to God, pleases him above all things. What is this, if not the free will which the almighty Creator placed in your power? But a good will is greater than all riches; whoever offers this to God is prepared to offer something unspeakably acceptable.

This most precious thing, my brothers, you once offered to God when, wishing to deny all worldly things for his honor, you put on the monastic habit. Remember the words of your holy profession, and let that solemn promise never depart from memory, which each one of you made in the sight of God before us, saying: “And I, brother Johannes, promise stability, conversion of my way of life, and obedience according to the Rule of Saint Benedict, before God and his saints, in this monastery which is built in honor of blessed Martin, in the presence of lord Johannes, abbot.” Behold, you see that we are bound to voluntary obedience; behold...
