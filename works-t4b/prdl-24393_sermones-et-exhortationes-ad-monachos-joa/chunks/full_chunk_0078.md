On the sleep and wakefulness of monks. Homily XVII.

The monk who always thinks on the death of morals permits no time to pass in idleness. For Scripture says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” Therefore concerning the forgetful and foolish it is written: “They are a nation without counsel and without prudence. Would that they were wise and understood and provided for their last things.” The memory of death brings watchfulness into the soul, and also every orderly thought of the mind. He who has rightly deserved death never grows sluggish in good work. For the wise man says: “Whatever your hand is able to do, work earnestly; for neither work, nor reason, nor wisdom, nor knowledge will be in the underworld, to which you are hastening.”

For the monk who is a true worshiper of God spends no moment of time uselessly, but night and day, always watchful in mind in the practice of good work, performs bodily duties with the greatest devotion and constantly says to God in his soul: “I sleep, but my heart keeps watch for the Lord.” Sleep is the image of death and a binding of the bodily senses. But he who is a true lover of Christ, even if he sleeps in body, keeps watch in mind, and praying without ceasing cries out to the Lord with an affection of love: “Before you is every desire of my mind and every thought, O Jesus crucified, my love.”

I blush, my brothers, and am deeply confounded to put forward a sermon on the watchfulness of monks, because when I recall to memory the customs of the preceding holy fathers in keeping vigil, I recognize how far our weakness differs from their perfection. They, for a long time, in successive love and in the praises of God, often spent whole nights without sleep; but to us scarcely the space of eight hours for sleeping seems at times sufficient, of which we do not cease to complain of five before matins and three afterward. For the true abstinence of monks consists not only in sparing use of food and drink, but also in the continuation of vigils for the praise of God, because the maceration of the flesh, by which the old man is restrained by them, is in vain, if the whole body is fattened through drowsiness. Indeed, not only is the body refreshed for its subsistence by food and drink, but it is also restored by the rest of sleep. What sort of monastic fast is that: to spare oneself by abstaining from foods and drinks, and to indulge oneself in sleep to satiety? It makes little difference whether you refresh the body by food or by sleep, since there is gluttony in both.

For this reason the old and first leaders of monks, guarding temperance no less in sleeping than in eating and drinking, endured as men most continent in both. Some of them, standing on their feet, others lying on the bare ground, granted to nature only the very smallest amount of sleep. We know that one of them, not least in the merit of sanctity, said: “One hour is enough for a monk to sleep, provided he has been a warrior and adversary of vices.” For sleep nourishes luxury no less than food and drink. Another of the saints thought that two or three hours for sleeping were enough for a monk, so that he might commend sparingness in food also by temperance in rest. But the recent reformers of our order, who under the name of the Bursfeld Union handed down to us throughout Germany a manner of life according to the purity of the Rule, considering the weakness of the human condition, ordered the arrangement of vigils very moderately.

For indeed they mercifully allowed monks in community a space of five hours before the night praises, and likewise added two, or two and a half, after matins for sleep. He for whom this time, namely seven hours, is not sufficient for rest shows himself a very sluggish and slothful monk. Hence we are admonished to keep watch not only in mind but also in body, since he who disdains to keep watch bodily in the service of God is scarcely able to preserve his heart in true purity.

Let us keep watch, therefore, most beloved brothers, especially in these holy days of Lent, in which we have declared war on vices and are firmly prepared to conquer and overcome the ancient enemy, the devil. For you have heard the holy prince of the apostles admonishing in these words: “Brothers, be sober and keep watch.” Why did he give this warning? Because, he says, “your adversary the devil goes about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour; resist him, strong in faith.” And so, my brothers, let us keep watch as long as we are set in battle, lest at any time, weighed down by drowsiness or idleness, we be overcome by the adversary of our salvation. For as long as we live in the flesh, it is necessary that by watchfulness and chastisement of our body we fight bravely against the enemy. Let the labor of the contest not frighten you, because although our adversary is troublesome, nevertheless he is so weak and feeble that he can overcome no one except one who is willing, and one who has utterly cast off all guard over himself.

But the Lord Jesus Christ is our strong helper and protector, who grants strength to those who fight eagerly and will bestow on the victors an unfading crown in heaven with all his saints. As dust is scattered by the wind, so our adversary the devil, confounded and terrified by the voice of those singing psalms with humble devotion, is driven away. Therefore let none of you, most beloved brothers, act negligently; let no one in the time of prayer grow dull through sluggishness of mind and drowsiness; let no one fear the contest with the devil with disordered fear. Stand manfully for the perpetual salvation of your souls, fighting against a weak adversary, and hold your own flesh no less suspect than demons. For you know, as not only Scripture testifies but also long experience teaches, that the flesh desires against the spirit; and therefore the monk who is not harmed by the wantonness of his own flesh is rarely overcome by demons in the contest. Walk, therefore, in the Spirit, and you will not fulfill the desires of the flesh.

Let him who is watchful and a strong fighter rouse the weaker brother, who is sluggish in devotion, and restore him as far as he can, both by words and by examples, to the holy fervor of the spirit. Let him who is spiritual among you not despise his disorderly brother, but rather bring him back through humble admonition to the pursuit of piety. For Scripture says: “Bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ.” For indeed, if, consenting together in brotherly love toward one another through faith in Jesus Christ, you have always been of one mind, without doubt you will easily conquer your adversary the devil. For where the true charity of Christ makes monks living in a monastery of one mind, there God is worshiped in all holiness; there the invisible presence of holy angels is believed to be present, through whom the rashness of malignant spirits is restrained. In such a congregation of saints God dwells, whom the army of the heavenly kingdom praises together with the voices of the monks singing psalms. A multitude of monks fervent in the love of God is the paradise of the delights of heavenly graces. A city adorned with a multitude of towers is a congregation of monks thinking the same thing in the Lord.

Keep watch, my brothers, I beseech you, keep watch in holy prayers, and especially at that time shake off drowsiness and dullness of mind when you stand in church before the Lord to sing psalms. For if, standing before God in psalmody, you are fervent in prayer, you will without doubt feel his grace present with you.
