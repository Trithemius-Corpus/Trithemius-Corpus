Hence the holy pope Gregory says: “It is a great labor of discretion for the exactor of foods both to supply something to the body and to deny it something; by not giving, to restrain gluttony, and by giving, to nourish nature.” For through abstinence vices are to be extinguished, not the flesh; and through food the body is to be nourished, not sins.

Thus it is necessary for a monk to preserve the art of continence in his diet, so that he kills not the flesh but the vices. For often, while we pursue the enemy in the flesh, by immoderate abstinence we severely slay the spirit of devotion which we love. Very often also, while we spare the flesh, pitying it like a fellow citizen, we nourish an enemy for battle against ourselves. But when the vice of gluttony rules, men lose everything they have bravely accomplished, because when the belly is not restrained, all virtues are overthrown together. For one does not rise to the conflict of spiritual combat unless the enemy placed within us, namely the appetite of gluttony, is first subdued by us with God’s help; for unless we overcome the things that are nearer to us, we hasten in vain to fight against those set farther off. Indeed, war is waged in vain in the field against foreign enemies if a treacherous citizen is held within the city walls. We never overcome the assaults of demons unless we first overcome gluttony in ourselves.

Brothers, let no one seduce himself with empty persuasion, so as to believe that there is no sin among beans and vegetables, if the mind relaxes the appetite for receiving them with excessive pleasure. For in consumption appetite can be excessive anywhere, when it is extended beyond the norm of nature. See, we never eat meat, whether in the monastery or outside it; here in this country district we very rarely see fish; no one among us has ever seen more sumptuous foods; we neither can nor indeed ought to have delicate dishes. Our foods are barley, oats, beans, peas and vegetables, milk, cheese, and eggs. From these we receive the daily sustenance of our life, and hunger stirs appetite so that disgust at them may not arise. Outside the season of the Lenten fast, fish are so rare for us that scarcely once in a month a few, and very tiny ones, are brought from the river Nab. In the whole Bursfelde reform there is no monastery less provided for in the necessity of fish.

But surely for that reason we can still fall into the vice of gluttony, since we cannot find more luxurious foods? Indeed, since the fault is not in food, as has been said, but in appetite, the eater of peas or beans can sin no less in eating than one who is accustomed to eat the most sumptuous and exquisite foods. Poverty does not make us utterly free from fault, but marks out for us a simple and rustic diet, by which nature may somehow be sustained. This, my brothers, I beg us to take care over: that in the very thing which produces disgust at delicacies, our conscience may not be polluted by excess. Let us receive with patience and thanksgiving from what the Lord has given as much as necessity itself requires, and let us not think that the snares of the devil cannot harm us, for he overthrew the first men in paradise not with more luxurious food, but with a small and cheap apple. For the vice is not in the food but in the appetite, when we either desire things forbidden to us or, eagerly indulging in things permitted, use them otherwise than we ought.

Nor does abstinence befit anyone more than a monk, who, since he has been specially called to Christ’s military service, ought not by his profession to have leisure for any delicacies. Our poverty imposes on us a certain necessity of abstinence, because we can restrain ourselves to temperance so much the more easily when, the less we abound in foods, the more purely we fast in holiness of soul. For temperance is more easily preserved in poverty, while among riches it is certainly more quickly endangered.

What the food of monks ought to be, blessed Jerome makes clear, saying in a certain letter to Paulinus: “Let your food be cheap and taken in the evening: vegetables, namely, and legumes; and count it among the highest delights sometimes to eat a few little fish. One who longs for Christ and is fed by that heavenly bread does not greatly ask what dung he may make from costly foods. The lover of Christ loves abstinence; he cultivates fasting as the one remedy of soul and body; with the utmost zeal he takes care that his heart not be weighed down by drunkenness, and he takes no more food than nature, content with little, demands.”
