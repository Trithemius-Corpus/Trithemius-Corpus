On abstinence and the fasting of monks.

Woe to our times, in which many clerics and monks have already shamefully fallen away from the virtue of abstinence, and those who ought to have shown the people of God the way of salvation no less by example than by word, given over to gluttonies and immersed in continual drinking and pleasures, contrary to every propriety of clerics, hasten to go before them into the perdition of eternal death.

Scripture says: The beginning of human life is bread and water. And now our monks and cloistered men buy for themselves, at the greatest expense, whatever is sold most dearly, whatever is found most luxurious; by gorging their bellies they bury it, and through their intemperance corrupt soul and body alike. Amid banquets they argue about fasting, and when they have become drunk they praise temperance. There each man boasts that he is the conqueror of vices, although he has already been overcome by gluttony and drunkenness.

Our Lord Jesus Christ says in the holy Gospel according to Luke: See that your hearts are not weighed down with excess. It does not befit a man dedicated to God to eat or drink to satiety, nor is it lawful for a monk to eat more than nature requires. Let eating satisfy hunger; let it not bring on repletion. Let belching be far away, which satiety is accustomed to produce at feasts; let vomiting and indigestion never be heard of among monks. It is necessary that all of us cloistered men use a great moderation of discretion, so that one may nourish the body with foods and drinks in such a way that, serving the spirit, it may be able to live, and not, grown fat, begin to grow wanton against the command of the mind.

Let satiety always be absent from drinking, for nothing so inflames the body and excites the genital members as undigested food and a belly heated by wine or beer. Hence Jerome, admonishing Eustochium concerning temperance, the preserver of all virtues, says in a certain letter: Let the bride of Christ flee wine as poison; for wine and youth itself are weapons against adolescence, already inclined to gluttony. Avarice does not shake adolescence so much, nor does pride puff it up, nor ambition delight it, as those vices of the flesh which seem to us to be bound up with pleasure. We shall easily lack the other vices if we keep watch, even moderately, in the fear of the Lord. But this enemy of ours is always shut up within us; wherever we go, we carry this adversary with us. Wine and youth: a double blaze of pleasure. Why do we add oil to the flame, or why do we administer fiery fuel to a little body already burning?

Excessive eating has laid low many who were walking chastely in the middle of the road, and those whom the devil could not conquer in their poverty he casts into the depths of luxury by excessive drinking of wine. For this reason the most holy founder of our way of life, Benedict, when enumerating the instruments of good works, placed among the chief ones: to chastise the body, to embrace hardships, to love fasting. For no one truly follows Christ who, nourishing his flesh delicately, lives in the monastery without daily abstinence.

Moderate abstinence, pleasing to God, works many good things in us if it is continued with religious intention to the end of life. For first of all it increases merit, and nourishes in us every kind of virtue. For what was the reason that almighty God forbade our first parents to eat the fruit under threat of death, except that he might offer them a cause of merit through the good of obedience? For, as Scripture bears witness, he created two intellects from the beginning, namely the angelic and the human, to which he would communicate himself by grace for the enjoyment of eternal happiness. Yet although he created each by the gift of his grace, he most rightly ordained that they should also be perpetually happy by their own merit, so that in them both mercy might have a place and conversion might obtain the merit necessary for salvation.

But the angelic intellect was at once divided in two: when some, falling of their own accord through pride, and others, humble and subject through love, clung to their beginning. Those, indeed, apostates from their beginning, withdrew in misery; these, however, were granted everlasting blessedness for the merit of their adhesion. The human intellect, however, was sent down into flesh; and, when the devil persuaded him, he transgressed the commandment of God and feasted on the food forbidden to him. From this there followed the destruction of the whole human race, which no one could remove except the Son of God, made man from a virgin for us.
