Therefore great discretion is needed, so that only the food necessary for the support of life is supplied to the body, and no excess of indulgence occurs. Hence the holy Pope Gregory says: “It is a great labor of discretion to give something to the body asking for food, and to deny it something: by not giving, to restrain gluttony, and by giving, to nourish nature.” For through abstinence vices are to be extinguished, not the flesh; and through food the body is to be nourished, not sins.

Thus, then, it is necessary that the monk maintain continence in his diet, so that he kills not the flesh, but vices. For often, while we pursue the enemy in the flesh, we cruelly slaughter, through immoderate abstinence, the spirit of devotion which we love. More often also, while we are too sympathetic to the flesh and spare it as though it were a fellow-citizen, we nourish a very evil enemy against ourselves. But when the vice of gluttony has mastery, men lose all the brave deeds they have done; for when the belly is not restrained, all virtues are overthrown together.

For a person does not rise up to the conflict of spiritual combat if the enemy placed very near within us, namely the appetite of gluttony, is not first subdued by us, with God’s help; for unless we overcome the things that are nearer to us, we hasten in vain to fight against those placed farther away. For war is waged in vain in the field against foreign enemies if within the city walls there is a citizen lying in ambush. We never overcome the assaults of demons unless we first overcome gluttony in ourselves.

Let no one, brothers, deceive himself by empty persuasion, so as to believe that there is no sin among beans and vegetables, if the mind seeks to take them with too much eager pleasure. For in taking food there can be excess everywhere, when appetite is extended beyond the rule of nature. See, we eat meat neither in the monastery nor outside it; here in this open countryside we see fish very rarely; no one among us has ever seen richer foods; delicacies we neither can nor ought to have. Our foods are barley, oats, beans, peas, and vegetables, also milk, cheese, and eggs. From these we receive the daily supports of our life, and hunger stirs appetite so that disgust for them may not arise. Outside the time of the Lenten fast, fish are so rare for us that scarcely once a month a few are brought from the river Nahe, and those are very small. In the whole Bursfelde reform there is no monastery less provided with the necessity of fish.

But can we therefore not fall into the vice of gluttony, because we cannot find rich foods? Indeed, since the vice is not in the food, as has already been said, but in the appetite, the eater of peas or beans can sin no less in eating than he who is accustomed to chew the richest and choicest foods. Poverty does not make us wholly safe from gluttony, although it diminishes the occasions for sinning for those who sin. We have, indeed, simple and rustic foods by which nature may somehow be sustained. This, my brothers, I pray that we take care of: that in that which disgust creates instead of delicacy, our conscience may not be polluted by excess. Let us take with patience and thanksgiving from what the Lord has given as much as necessity itself requires; and let us not think that the snares of the devil cannot harm us, since he overthrew the first human beings in paradise not by rich food, but by a small and common apple. For the vice is not in the food, but in the appetite, when either we desire things forbidden to us, or, eagerly gaping after permitted things, we use them otherwise than we ought.

Nor does abstinence befit anyone more than a monk, who, since he has been specially called to the warfare of Christ, ought by the content of his profession not to have leisure for any delicacies. Our poverty imposes upon us a certain necessity of abstinence; for we can restrain ourselves to temperance all the more easily, the less we find that by which we might overflow with delights. Let us therefore turn this most welcome necessity, I pray, into weapons of salvation, so that the less we are refreshed by rich foods, the more purely we may fast in holiness of mind. For temperance is more easily preserved in poverty, though in riches it is indeed quickly endangered.

What the food of monks ought to be, blessed Jerome makes clear, saying in a certain letter to Paulinus: “Let your food be cheap and taken in the evening: namely vegetables and legumes; and sometimes consider it the height of delicacy to eat a few little fish. He who desires Christ and feeds on that heavenly bread does not greatly seek what dung he may make from costly foods. The lover of Christ loves abstinence; he cultivates fasting as the one remedy of soul and body; he takes the greatest care that his heart not be weighed down by surfeiting, nor does he take more food than nature, content with little, demands.”

But the monk who rejects fasting, who hates abstinence, the nurse of virtues; who stuffs foods down with eagerness; who drinks wine or strong drink and whatever can intoxicate, to satiety: he is unworthy of the very name of monk, and cannot be a disciple of Christ.

Woe to our times, in which already very many clerics and monks have shamefully cast aside the virtue of abstinence. They ought to have shown the people of God the way of salvation no less by example than by word; but, given over to feasting and continual drinking and pleasures, and immersed contrary to all clerical honor, they hasten to precede them into the perdition of eternal death.

Scripture says: “The beginning of human life is bread and water.” But now our monks and cloistered men buy for themselves whatever is sold more dearly, whatever they find more lavish, at a great price; by swallowing it down they bury it in their bellies, and through intemperance they corrupt soul and body together. Amid banquets they debate fasting, and when they are drunk they praise temperance. There each one boasts that he is victor over vice, when he has already been overcome by gluttony and drunkenness.

Our Lord Jesus Christ says in the holy Gospel according to Luke: “Take heed that your hearts not be weighed down in surfeiting.” It does not befit a person dedicated to God to eat or drink to satiety, nor is it lawful for a monk to eat more than nature requires. Eating should settle hunger, not induce fullness. Let belches, which satiety is accustomed to produce in feasters, be far away; let vomiting and indigestion never be heard of among monks. All cloistered men must use great moderation of discretion, so that they nourish the body with foods and drinks in such a way that, serving the spirit, it can live; and not so that, fattened, it begins to grow wanton against the command of the mind. Let satiety always be far away, because nothing so inflames the body and excites the genital members as undigested food and a belly hot with wine or beer.

Hence the holy Jerome, admonishing Eustochium about temperance, the preserver of all virtues, says in a certain letter: “Let the bride of Christ flee wine as poison; for wine and youth itself are weapons against adolescence, which is more prone to gluttony. Avarice does not so shake adolescence, nor does pride puff it up, nor ambition delight it, as do those enticements of pleasure which seem innate in us.”
