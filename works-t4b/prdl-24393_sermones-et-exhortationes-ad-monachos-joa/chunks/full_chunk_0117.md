Consider yourselves diligently now, my brothers, and question your consciences, whether you have the true charity of God, without which all other things avail nothing for salvation. For you have heard the Apostle’s clear judgment, by which he says that charity is necessary for salvation. Let no one trust in the wisdom of this world; let no one vainly exalt himself over the natural gifts he has received; let no one presume upon bodily continence, since, if charity is lacking, the observance of the other things contributes nothing to salvation.

But lest anyone presume upon a false estimate of himself, the holy Apostle set forth certain signs and operations of charity, saying: Charity is patient, is kind. Charity does not envy, does not act perversely, is not puffed up, is not ambitious, and does not seek what is its own; it is not provoked, does not think evil, does not rejoice over iniquity, but rejoices together with the truth. It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Charity never fails. Have the charity of God within you, my brothers; unless you keep it, you labor in vain by contending.

Humility, I have told you more often, is necessary for you above all: it is not only a great virtue, but also the nurse of the remaining virtues and the chief guardian of them all. For the great Pope Gregory says: Whoever gathers virtues without humility is like one carrying dust into the wind. For humility is the greatest virtue of monks, since they are bound to be humbler than others living in the world, the more they are obliged by profession to strive and tend toward greater perfection. Humility is the stable foundation of the virtues; whatever is built upon it will be overturned by no whirlwind of storm. Through pride the devil fell from heaven; through humility man ascends. We are called monks in vain if we have not been humble; for just as fire, if it lacks the fuel of wood, is extinguished, so a monk without humility is judged very unworthy of his name. By Christian humility the devil is conquered, and, as if struck by a nail, is overcome as he flees. No more splendid jewel is seen in the monastic adornment than humility; if a monk despises it, he has denied both his profession and the humble Christ.

Prudence succeeds humility. It always directs all things according to the rule of reason and does nothing except what is right, honorable, and praiseworthy. Prudence knows what it ought virtuously to seek, and it well knows what it ought to avoid according to the right judgment of reason. True and perfect prudence is that by which we seek that highest, unchangeable good, and order all things to their due and proper end. Prudence preserves the observance of regular discipline stable in its strength, and leads wandering monks back to the complete restoration of their lost purpose. Prudence directs a monk on the right way of salvation and does not allow him to decline to either side. Prudence preserves peace and concord among the brothers, and orders right action in each matter.

Prudence is very necessary for you, my brothers, so that you may know in every action what you should take up and what again should be avoided. True prudence, not of the flesh but of the spirit, teaches you to despise the world and to desire only those things that are future and eternal. Prudence teaches a man to stand firm within himself, and not to decline from the purpose of holiness either in prosperity or in adversity. Prudence teaches how to order present things well, recalls past things to memory for instruction, and foresees future things so that nothing may happen unexpectedly. Prudence usefully and fruitfully corrects those who offend, and composes everything that it either does, says, or thinks, according to the judgment of reason, toward its due end.

Therefore be prudent as serpents and simple as doves, because prudence without goodness is malice, and simplicity without rational prudence is called folly. For the most holy Paul, Apostle of Christ, says: The prudence of the flesh is death. Be prudent, lest you be deceived by the fraud of others, but simple, lest you deceive anyone. For this is the office of the prudent person: neither to be capable of being deceived, nor to wish to deceive anyone. Prudence acts by discerning virtues; for one is called prudent from providing beforehand.

Justice accompanies prudence, and it is the most splendid of all the virtues. For without justice all the remaining virtues are nothing. Justice is the preserver of religion, the mother of grace, the nurse of observance, the nourisher of truth, the begetter of peace, and the origin of piety.
