On the True Humility of Monks.

We were impelled to undertake it so that, circumcised in mind, we might be able to enter into the promised inheritance of the saints. O great power of humility, which made the regent of heaven and earth withdraw from before the face of one most lowly little man, and dwell in exile for seven years in the land of Egypt. You caused Christ Jesus, the only-begotten Son of God, who deigned to take on a human body joined to himself for our salvation, also to undergo the most bitter passion and death by his own willing choice. Great is your dominion, O venerable sanctity of humility, since the kingdom of heaven is promised to you; to your devotees the gift of eternal blessedness is granted; and whoever keeps to your footsteps is raised up to the fellowship of the angels of heaven, as Christ says in the holy Gospel: “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted.” He taught what he himself had imitated from heaven, when he says: “Learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart, and you will find rest for your souls.”

The most blessed mother too, the intact Virgin Mary, in her sweetest canticle of praise says to God: “He has put down the mighty from their seats and exalted the humble.” And the prophet says in the psalm: “The Lord has regarded the prayers of the humble and has not despised their petitions.” The prayer of one who humbles himself penetrates the clouds; until it draws near it will not be comforted, and it will not depart until the Most High looks upon it. A monk who is pure in his conscience, made humble to God, is most pleasing; and the more he has cast himself down by humbling himself for God’s honor, the more glorious he will appear in the kingdom. The monk who has truly humbled himself is made a participant in the grace of the Most High and will rejoice forever with the king of heaven, Jesus Christ.

He who truly desires to imitate Christ should also assume true humility; clothed and fortified with it as with a breastplate of strength, he may do great things, yet think nothing of himself except what is humble, and not incur the stain of vain glory. For this is the true perfection of monks: always humbly to recognize their own imperfection, and to ponder carefully what good is lacking to them rather than what they possess. For true humility is always stretched forward toward what lies ahead through the desire of charity; and, cautiously mindful of the things of the past, lest it lose through fear what it has already gained, and desiring to acquire through the zeal of devotion what it has not yet gained, it does not cease in the work of holiness. It considers most diligently in what things it is still deficient, and does not weigh in what things it has advanced.

Indeed, whoever thinks that he is already something still stands far away from true humility, since true humility does great things and thinks of itself as very little; and the more it has advanced, the more deeply it recognizes its own weakness. For Scripture commands, saying: “The greater you are, humble yourself in all things, and you will find grace before God.” And again it says: “Humble your spirit greatly, for the vengeance on the flesh of the wicked is fire and worm.”

Therefore, my brothers, assume true humility, not that which is sometimes displayed before men by pretenders in outward gesture and voice, but Christian humility, which they possess by the pure affection of the heart. For it is one thing to have the virtue of humility, and another to have the likeness of its image. True humility is always patient with a tranquil heart toward all injuries and insults; it is obedient without dispute to the commands not only of superiors but even of equals; in adversities it preserves constancy, in bodily necessity moderation, in conduct continence, in conversation honorableness. It is cheerful, not lax; serious, not melancholic; generous in acts of mercy, not wasteful; compassionate toward the weak, not remiss.

Christian humility is ceaselessly pierced with compunction in the love of God; fervent in the spirit of devotion, it is continually moistened with tears; it always desires to see its prince, Jesus Christ, and, following his admonitions in spirit, sighs from sorrow. For it grieves over its sin, and whoever embraces true humility groans that he cannot advance as much as he desires, whoever imitates the Son of God. Nothing is more excellent than Christian humility, nothing more splendid than self-knowledge; it is never lifted up in the height of pastoral office, and, being subject to all, it rules over all. For he who is small in his own eyes is made great before God; and he who considers himself to be something great in this world is judged of no account before God. Each person will be precious before the inner judge to the degree that, doing good and never ceasing, he has been more contemptible in his own estimation.

Humility is the stable foundation of all virtues, because every spiritual building that is raised up to the height of Christian perfection is sustained by the rock of humility alone. Indeed, if true humility is lacking, the whole gathering of the other virtues will be nothing other than a kind of ruin of a house built without a foundation on sand. For in the spiritual building humility lays a stable foundation; charity orders the breadth of that house; the wall is raised on high by the continuous joining of good works in the fervor of the spirit; in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ and in the firm hope of the promise of eternal protection and blessedness, it is set straight at the summit; and its length is arranged in the most beautiful order in the long-suffering of patience.

Therefore, my brothers, again and again I admonish, adjure, and beseech you to take care to found the building of spiritual exercise in the depth of humility, because no good work is pleasing to God that has been offered outside true humility. Now there are many things that are accustomed to stir the servants of God to true humility. For the consideration of their own worthlessness humbles some within themselves before God, because, when they consider that they have already often experienced themselves to be wretched and weak, they place all their hope and confidence in God, humbled. Others, weighing the multitude of God’s benefits toward them, fear the judgment of ingratitude; and therefore in the sight of God, with great fear and reverence of gratitude, they always walk humbly.

And who among us, brothers, is there to whom the compassion of almighty God has not granted many benefits, even singular ones? If we wish to be grateful for them, above all we must be humble. Others, converted by divine mercy after a multitude of sins to penitence, diligently recall to memory how many evils they have committed against the law of the almighty; and for these, humbled in bitterness of spirit, they detest their own impieties with horror. But we offend in many things; hence it is necessary that we humble ourselves always in mind and heart, and even in body, so that grace may abound where sin previously abounded.

Often, moreover, the providence of divine mercy permits certain persons to sin enormously for a time, so that afterward, converted to penitence, they may learn to remain in true humility. A humble penitent is higher than one who has not sinned but is proud, because humility justifies penitents, while pride condemns the arrogant eternally, even if they have done nothing else evil than this alone, that they are proud.

Others, carrying in their heart by daily meditation the bitter passion of Christ Jesus, humble themselves so much the more, the more they know that they are very insufficient debtors for such great and wondrous benefits of their Redeemer. The Son of God humbled himself, being made man for us, and, filled with many reproaches, voluntarily underwent the most shameful death of the cross, to free us from the destruction of perpetual death. What Christian man, and especially what monk, will not greatly humble his soul before God when he has recalled to memory such great humility of his Creator and Redeemer? Indeed, the man whom Christ’s humility does not stir to meekness: no admonitions of men will profit in him. In vain are we called monks, unless according to our strength we have humbly followed Christ. For this reason he said in the holy Gospel that he is the way, so that we may imitate his humility, and the way of life of so great a master may become the spiritual form of the disciple.

And so, brothers, as much as we can, let us labor with all our strength, since for us he humbled himself into such great humility. Therefore the highest medicine for inducing true humility in the servant of God is this: if daily and very often he recalls to memory the most bitter passion of Christ Jesus. Tribulations, adversities, and illnesses, together with poverty or want, have also made many humble who perhaps in prosperous and favorable circumstances would have perished, puffed up. And we trust that the poverty which we often say conduces to our salvation does so, since we have a great cause of humility, but find no matter at all for elation and pride. Others, by continual exercise of virtues and by the memory of the death which they must necessarily suffer shortly, have come to great humility. Whom, even the proudest, would not the intent meditation of death humble? Or who is so fierce in spirit that he would not little by little lay aside his swelling pride, if he considered more diligently that he will die shortly?
