That monks must live cautiously.

Justice is the virtue which distributes and preserves for each person what is his own. It neglects private advantage in order to preserve common advantage and equity; without it no commonwealth can long subsist in peace. For the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “A kingdom is transferred from nation to nation because of injustices, injuries, insults, and various deceits.” The order of justice looks first to God, then to the fatherland, thereafter to parents, and afterwards to all people in common, according to the distinctions of ranks and the merits of dignity. To God, in the first place, justice dictates that we owe religion, faith, worship, devotion, acts of thanksgiving, and true love from the whole heart, under obedient submission. Then to ourselves we owe purity of mind and body, and continence with zeal for perfection, so that through obedience we may be permitted to return to him by whom we were created. In the third place, to the fatherland we owe advantage, honor, and defense, and, as far as we can, the procuring of its interests together with justice itself. Fourth, we are bound to our parents by honor and obedience, and in their need by care and every kind of support. Fifth, to our prelates and superiors we owe obedience and humble subjection for the love of Christ. Sixth, prelates owe teaching and nourishment to their subjects, and correction to those who offend. In the seventh place, however, we are bound to give honor to elders and princes, and blameless subjection for the love of God. Eighth, we owe reverence to venerable persons, and honor to priests. Consequently, we are bound to equals by friendship, to inferiors by condescension, to benefactors by reciprocation, to friends by fellowship, to enemies by patience, to the poor by mercy, to the wretched by consolation, and to all by charity. This, then, is true justice: by it we love God with the whole heart for his own sake, and our neighbor in God as ourselves, and enemies for God’s sake, distributing to each person what is owed to him by right and providing for the advantage of all.

But since excessive justice easily falls into sin, temperance is necessary for you in all things; it alone makes people perfect. Temperance is the virtue which, by reason, discerns and conquers the impulses of the soul and every lust, lest impulse in action exceed the equity of the law. Temperance is a most excellent virtue: it governs pleasures and preserves all things in due order. Apply temperance, and the discretion necessary to the virtues, to all your actions, words, and also thoughts. Whatever good you do with discretion, accept it under the name and merit of virtue; but what is done without discretion must be called vice. Discretion is the moderator of all virtues and the greatest ornament of prudence and justice. Discretion is necessary not only for prelates but also for subjects, since it gives order to virtues, grants moderation to affections, and bestows most beautiful grace and honorableness on all human counsels. Hence a certain wise man says: “There is a measure in things; there are, finally, fixed limits, beyond which and short of which the right cannot stand.”

For this, brothers, strength of soul is necessary for you, through which you may become not only steadfast in adversities but also prompt and cheerful in difficult things. For who, without the greatest strength of soul, could despise this world with its pleasures, and persevere in holy monastic religion under the laws of obedience even unto death? Strength of soul receives its powers from the charity of Christ, and stands constant in desire for virtue no less in adversities than in prosperity. Strength walks the way of uprightness without fear, and to enemies it seems terrible and fearsome with a certain holy pride. Strength of soul despises small injuries and easily overcomes all the pleasures of the flesh. Strength lies hidden in prosperity, is shown in adversity, and is openly declared through patience. Strong of soul is not the man who avenges injuries brought upon him, but the man who patiently tolerates them; if he has received his adversary into his power, he considers that he could have exacted vengeance. The strength of monks is to despise the world, to conquer the flesh, to spurn every honor and pleasure of the world, and to place all hope and confidence in God alone. The measure of strength is to be neither timid nor rash, since the one walking on the right way turns aside neither to the right nor to the left. That monk must be judged strong who is temperate in all things, moderate, and just. Hence the most holy pope Gregory says: “The strength of the just is to conquer the flesh, to oppose their own pleasures, to extinguish the delight of the present life, to love the hardships of this world for eternal rewards, to contemn the enticements of prosperity, and to overcome the fear of adversity in the heart.” The saints are made strong in battle, because they subdue the flesh, are strengthened by virtues, fortify the spirit, despise earthly things, and seek heavenly things. They can be killed, but they cannot be bent, nor can they endure falsehood. Through weakness they fear [unclear], nor, when harmed, do they ever withdraw from the truth.
