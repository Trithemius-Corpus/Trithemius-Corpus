But the human intellect, which our preacher has called the hand of our soul, was indeed created in all ignorance from the beginning: it knows nothing coevally which the angelic intellect understands by nature. Those two intellects differ as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, habit and privation, beginning and end, simple and composite, substance and accident, nature and art, truth and likeness. For, as all divine Scripture bears witness, all the things that are present in the angelic intellect by nature from the beginning, the human intellect will attain through art at the end, as the Lord Jesus says in the holy Gospel: “In the resurrection of the dead they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but they will be like the angels of God in heaven.”

For as the mind is exercised toward understanding through many labors, as though through art, it has been placed for this time in the workshop of the body. If, turned toward God, it has duly completed its craft through love, then at last, departing from here, it becomes equal to an angel. But if, by the habits of the flesh and conscience, it has not kept its own order, it will depart from the body, like the troubled and wretched spirits, to be tormented forever. For since our mind, or soul, was created in ignorance and privation of all things, it must come to the art and knowledge of things by the movement of continual action. The end, indeed, makes the human intellect equal to the angelic, changing its potency into act; and from privation it makes habit, from empty full, from imperfect perfect, from deprived possessing, from dark luminous, from nocturnal diurnal, from lunar solar, from earthly heavenly.

This end, my dearly beloved brothers, is eternal salvation from its beginnings; no one can happily come to it who does not strive with all his strength toward the summit of perfection. But since in this troubled middle state it is difficult always to keep unbroken this necessary effort of making progress, where a thousand occasions for sinning are offered, the holy fathers, founders of the monastic order, taught us by word and example to flee the world, and often to withdraw from it not only in mind but also in body. And to those who were stronger in spirit they entrusted many things for the desert, so that they might become nearer to the Lord in mind the farther they withdrew from the world in body. Of these we read in the prophecy of Isaiah: “The wilderness will rejoice and blossom like the lily; budding, it will bud forth and exult, rejoicing and praising.” Others, wishing to be of use to many weaker men, founded cenobia, in which they placed a multitude of monks, so that their way of life would be separated from the people, yet they would nevertheless have fellowship with one another.

For it is not fitting for everyone to sit in the desert, for those for whom it is more suitable to serve the Lord in the midst of the brothers. For our monasteries have their own solitudes; and whoever wishes to be solitary can be so in the midst of the brothers, provided he has remained continually in the cell in which he dwells. Indeed a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is called solitary because he is separated in mind and body from the tumult of this world. Therefore, since today’s sermon is addressed to your charity, I admonish you with fatherly concern, and I ask that I not have spoken these things in vain, since I seek nothing other than your good, and desire nothing more than your progress in spiritual conduct. I ask you, I say, and exhort you in the bowels of brotherly charity, to love the quiet of the cell as the workshop of your salvation. In it much is ready for those who love God, and there is great advantage for bringing the intellect to perfection in the knowledge of the highest truth. For the prophet Isaiah says: “In silence and in hope will be your strength.”

Whoever does not love the solitude of the cell and the quiet of silence in no way loves purity of mind; but, wandering in body, he always draws in new vanities in his soul. Hence Jerome says: “Why do we desire the bustle of the city, we who are counted by our singularity? For to me the town is a prison; solitude is paradise.” If, brothers, the desert and solitude were pleasing to us, that place would offer great advantage to our purpose, where, apart from poor peasants, a guest rarely appears; no one disturbs our quiet, no one troubles us, no one brings in spectacles or empty rumors to disturb monastic renewal. But among you I see few lovers of inward quiet, and I grieve deeply, because I know very well that a monk cannot persevere in equal conduct if it is clear that he does not love the quiet of the cell. But one who knows how much good the deeds of solitude confer upon the soul loves his cell and does not readily go outside, always fearing amid the tumult to lose what he has gathered with great labor in secret.
