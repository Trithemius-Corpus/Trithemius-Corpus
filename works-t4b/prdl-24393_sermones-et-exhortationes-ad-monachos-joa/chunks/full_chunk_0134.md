*Sermon V.*
*Folio LXVIII.*

What shall we wretched ones say on that day, or where shall we stand before the strict Judge, if, as the apostle Peter says, scarcely will the righteous be saved? If Jerome, a righteous and most holy man, feared the judgment to come, O good God, where will Trithemius, an unhappy sinner, appear? If the righteous man has reason to fear, where will the sinner stand? What, then, shall we say, or what shall we answer, when we are gravely accused on every side? All the elements will stand as testimony against us; earth and heaven together will accuse us. And even if all things were silent, each person’s own conscience will be a grave accuser. He who knows all things is ignorant of nothing, for there all things will be manifest to the Judge. The evils we have done will be there, gravely accusing us; innumerable demons will stand around us as witnesses against us. We shall see beneath us that dreadful chaos lying open, above us the Judge angry against us, and on every side eternal danger threatening us.

My brothers, keep these dangers of the future examination always revolving in the memory of your heart, and diligently consider how dreadful it will be to fall under the Judge’s future sentence. In that terrible hour, pure hearts will be worth more than the artful words of rhetoricians; a clean conscience will profit a person more than the money of the whole world, since he who is to judge all things is not deceived; indeed, he is moved neither by price nor by prayers. O what incredible horror will invade all the wicked when they are about to hear that fearful sentence: “Go, accursed ones, into eternal fire, which has been prepared for the devil and his angels.” O worst of all evils, O deepest sorrow of all sorrows: to be separated from the fellowship of the saints and to be tormented forever with demons. Truly the day of judgment is dreadful and fearful to all, because it is exceedingly bitter: there the synteresis of conscience dies by the worm that does not die; there one once consigned to punishment is never freed.

Therefore the monk who desires to attain eternal salvation should, so far as he can, always keep himself clean from every sin, and that voice, “Go, accursed ones, into eternal fire,” should never depart from his memory.

Hence the third thing which we judge very useful for turning away from sin is to think diligently on the bitterness of the punishments of eternal damnation. For there is no temptation of carnal pleasure so vehement that it will not at once be overcome and fail, if the tempted person carefully recalls what the damned now suffer in hell. What do riches once possessed now profit the damned? What do past pleasures confer on the lustful placed in hell? What do the world’s vainest honors confer? What does power once broad confer on the proud? Think, brothers, what those already damned now suffer below; and lest similar things happen to you, persevere continually in good and holy works. If you fear hell, flee sin. For hell will harm nothing if a person has remained in the faith of Christ, free from sins; nor will he burn in punishment who previously here had not burned in guilt.

Hell is a place to be feared, in which there is no redemption; but whoever, once damned, enters there will never again come out to salvation. The damned, wretched in torments, in anguish and everlasting horror, say: “What now does our pride profit us: riches, honors, dignities, luxury, gluttony, and all past carnal pleasures? What do they now profit us, damned forever? Behold, all things have passed away like a shadow, and everything has vanished like the brief dream of a single night. But we have been seized by these eternal torments, in which we know most certainly that there is no redemption.” In hell, as Augustine testifies, even if there will be the goad of penitence, nevertheless there will be no correction of the will in the damned; their iniquities will be blamed by them in such a way that the equity of justice can in no way be loved or desired by them.

There, then, the consuming flame of hell burns those whom carnal delight now pollutes; and each person is burned in torments so much the more as here in the flesh he takes greater delight against God. In the lower regions, as Gregory testifies, there will be intolerable cold, inextinguishable fire, the immortal worm of conscience, intolerable stench, palpable darkness, pains beyond measure, lashes without interruption, the dreadful sight of demons striking them, confusion over committed crimes, and despair of all good things. But of all the punishments in hell, that one must be judged by far the gravest: that the damned are deprived forever of the vision of eternal light.

Here the most blessed father John Chrysostom says: “Gehenna is an intolerable thing, and all people abhor it. For who does not know that punishment of hell to be very horrible? Yet if anyone should set down a thousand, indeed ten thousand gehennas, the whole of what he says is nothing compared with this: for the soul, exposed before Christ, to be driven away from the glory of the saints, and to hear that terrible voice from the mouth of the Judge of all: ‘Depart from me, workers of iniquity, for I do not know you’; and, ‘Go, accursed ones, into eternal fire, which has been prepared for you and for the devil.’”
