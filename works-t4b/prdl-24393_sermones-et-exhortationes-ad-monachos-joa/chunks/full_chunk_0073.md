For laziness is the root of despair and the mother and nurse of all vices. For just as a garment both produces and nourishes the moth, so the most destructive plague of sloth both begets and fosters despair. This beast is accustomed to vex women more than monks, and especially those who lead a life withdrawn in solitude.

Therefore you must take the greatest care, O monks, against this enemy of the salvation of souls, and must fight not by fleeing but by resisting bravely. You must carefully consider that no one has attained the honor of holiness through sluggishness and laziness, nor has anyone earned the kingdom of eternal happiness without great and continuous labor. Therefore the mind must always be renewed by daily and fresh increases of the virtues; and this dangerous journey of life must be measured not by what has been traversed, but rather by what remains. Let no one promise himself security in this life, which is wholly called temptation, full of as many dangers as there are affections in the soul. Rather, let each person take care that he who could become better from worse does not become worse from better.

And especially in these holy days let us all take care, as much as we can, to become better, adding for ourselves, according to the counsel of our divine teacher, something to the usual measure of our service: namely special prayers, abstinence from food and drink, so that each one, beyond the measure appointed to him, may offer something to God of his own will with the joy of the Holy Spirit; that is, let him subtract from his body something of food, of drink, of sleep, of talkativeness, of ease, and with the joy of spiritual desire await holy Easter. For the monk who in these holy days is not found more religious, more fervent in the love of God, and more devout is shown to be only slightly religious at other times.

Hence the divine Pope Gregory says in a certain homily: “Lent must be observed with the greatest observance, so that the fast in it, because of the Lord’s days which are subtracted from abstinence, is by no means relaxed, unless infirmity prevents someone; for these days are themselves the tithe of the year which we are commanded to offer to the Lord.” Saint Ambrose also says: “He keeps Lent who by fasting and watching ascends to Easter. For just as fasting at the remaining time of the year is a reward, so in Lent not to fast is a sin. Those fasts are voluntary, but these are necessary; those come from choice, but these from law. To those we are invited; to these we are compelled.” And therefore he who passes over the appointed number by eating on one day is accused not as a violator of one day, but is charged as a transgressor of the whole of Lent.

In these sacred days all desires of the flesh must be restrained; the body must be chastised through abstinence, so that if perhaps at other times we have lived less temperately, we may in some way make compensation by this Lenten fast. Let us therefore follow the admonitions of our most holy father Benedict, dearest brothers, and with as much diligence as we can let us exercise ourselves in holy works, subtracting something each day from our body for the love of God in food and drink, so that we may prove ourselves true servants of God.

Next, our holy founder adds, saying: “Yet this very thing which each one offers, let him suggest to his abbot, and let it be done with his prayer and will; because what is done without the permission of the spiritual father will be reckoned as presumption and vainglory, not as reward. Therefore all things are to be done with the will of the abbot.”

The words of our holy lawgiver are clear, brothers, so that no one can have recourse to the covering of ignorance. For if all things are to be done with the will of the abbot, then without his permission absolutely nothing is to be done. Indeed, the more some good work is adorned by many virtues, the more there is no doubt that it will be of greater merit before God.
