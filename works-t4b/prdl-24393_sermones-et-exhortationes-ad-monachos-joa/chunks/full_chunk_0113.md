On continual preparation for death:

The continual meditation on necessary death makes a man never trust in earthly things, but await his own departure in every place and time with foresight. Meditation on death always expects, in every place, the end of life, because it is wholly ignorant of the moment of dying. Whoever meditates daily on the coming of future death disposes himself for dying, in every circumstance, with all the diligence he can. Nothing is more certain for us than death; all of us are ignorant of the hour of dying. Therefore, brothers, as earnestly as we can we admonish your charity that you always think upon death, and that with full foresight you diligently prepare yourselves for dying, so that when the hour of departure comes you may not fear.

Let no one promise himself a long life in this world, because nothing deceives mortals so much as hope of longer life. What does length of age accomplish in men except that it makes them liable for more offenses, and always less disposed for dying? The longer we live in this world, the more we fear death. Longer life accumulates greater debts of spiritual obligation, so that a man always fears the more to come before the sight of the strict judge, the more he recognizes himself bound before the Lord to render account in many matters. For the soul leaves its body uneasily when it is stirred there by the heavier stings of its conscience. But he who is pure from every evil of iniquity, and adorned for salvation with the necessary beauty of virtues, does not fear to die, because through death he goes to eternal life.

Therefore, O monk, live now as you wish to die, because death cannot be evil when the integrity of a good life has gone before. Whoever loves the Lord Jesus above all things, and despises all the goods of this world for love of him, and truly keeps his words, dies securely. He departs securely from this life who always lives in the love of God without stain of conscience. He dies securely whose life is defiled by none of the pleasures of this world. The death of such a one is a most sweet sleep, and a tranquil entrance into eternal life. But the death of sinners is very evil, for an evil life, contrary to the divine ordinances, has gone before. For one who lives in sins, contrary to God, death becomes a dreadful entrance into hell. He who has lived well does not die badly; rarely does he die well who is shown always to have lived badly.

He dies securely who loves God with a pure mind above all things, and keeps himself undefiled by this wicked world at all times. This hour is to be desired by all the upright in heart, because it brings an end to their distress and restores life in heaven. This hour is the abandonment of the body, the escape from a dark prison, and the laying down of a heavy burden. This hour is the end of present evils, the limit of sadness, and the grave of every earthly pleasure. A good life in the faith and charity of Jesus Christ makes this hour precious and most desirable to the servants of God. Now therefore, brothers, struggle to anticipate death with holy works, and by zeal for virtue make pleasant for yourselves what nature has willed to be necessary for all. Remember that we are all necessarily mortal, and that we shall pass from this world when we do not expect it. For, as a certain venerable bishop said: whether we wish it or not, all of us in the very midst of life are borne away, and we are drawn where life, when completed, calls us.

Therefore, brothers, while we have time, let us do whatever good we can, lest, overtaken by death, we grieve too late that we have been too negligent. Time alone is ours; all other things are another’s. The labor of our spiritual warfare, even if it seems somewhat hard to lukewarm minds, is nevertheless very brief, and with life it will quickly be ended; but the reward of good works that will be given to us remains forever. Let meditation on swiftly approaching death never desert you, but let the consideration of the strict judgment, which no one can escape, continually pierce your minds with compunction in the love of God.

Behold how many monks have gone before us in this monastery of Sponheim, living and dying, through almost four hundred years from its first foundation, of whom today no memory at all remains among us. Where then are the brothers who preceded us in this monastery? Just as there is now no memory of them among us, so too, after we have passed away, we shall be in everlasting oblivion among those who come after. Let us think on these things beforehand, dearest brothers, while we live, lest we fall into death unprepared. As we prepare daily for death, so daily, according to our strength, let us make ourselves ready; for he dies securely who in life is adorned with good and holy works.

Let not the hope of longer life deceive you, for the longer we live in this world, the shorter will be the life that remains. Behold, as we tend daily toward death, we die; daily one part of life is taken from us, and the longer our life advances, the shorter the remaining time of living becomes. All the time that we have lived is wholly lost when it has passed; what we live today we divide moment by moment with death; how much time for living remains to us in the future we do not know. Why then do we trust in the vanity of this life, when we are soon to die? Let us continually think, brothers, that we shall soon come to the place where all our elders have gone before us. We are all born under this law: that we may die soon.

Let death seem terrible to him whose whole life is polluted in the pleasure of the flesh and who stores up no good in heaven. But we who place all our hope in Christ Jesus our Savior, and who despise all the pleasures of the world for love of him, why should we fear death? Yet, since death is terrible, indeed most terrible of all terrors, we shall be able to drive out indiscreet fear of dying if we prepare ourselves daily as though we were to die. It is one thing to have fear of death, another to have trembling; for even the Lord Jesus himself felt the trembling of present death, to show that he had taken on the truth of both natures. Therefore continual meditation on death is altogether necessary for us weak ones, that a mind not yet perfected in the charity of Christ may be stirred, as if by a violent impulse, toward the grace of compunction.

For the memory of future death, like a nail driven into wood, pierces the mind with useful fear. The memory of death introduces difficult penitence and true sobriety of mind. The memory of death is necessary for beginners, whose conversion begins from servile fear, but is gradually lifted up through filial fear into charity. The memory of death and of the strict examination of God’s future judgment is especially necessary for those whom evil custom and long habit draw into sin. And if there are among you some who are too lukewarm and negligent in the spiritual warfare, we admonish and exhort them paternally to remember that they are mortal, and thereafter to live in such a way that they do not fear to die. For it is very dangerous to live in a state in which a man would fear to die.

One must live in such a way that we may await death securely; and this can happen only if, preserving ourselves in all purity of mind and body, we continually burn in the exercises of divine charity. Let us live, so far as can be done, innocently, according to the rule of our holy father Benedict, which we have professed, and we shall have good hope that almighty God will have mercy on us, for he does not allow a man of good will to perish. Let us always think of God as present to us everywhere: he sees all things, knows all things, understands all things, rewards all things. And let us live in that purity with which we promised to serve his most holy majesty.

The end of the twenty-fifth homily of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, to the monks.

Here ends the first book of exhortations to the monks, written with my own hand, Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, on the day of the month of June, in the year of Christians 1486, in the fourth indiction, in the thirty-third year of my age. I dedicated this work to my predecessor, now abbot of Seligenstadt, most dear to me.
