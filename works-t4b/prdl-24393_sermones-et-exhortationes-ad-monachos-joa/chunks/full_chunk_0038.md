It is a great thing to think nothing evil; but it belongs to no lesser prudence to be able to repel evil thoughts from their very beginning. For, according to the profound judgment of the human heart, it is mobile, changeable, manifold, and always unstable; and to observe its beginnings and restrain its movements is the height of prudence and a great sign of wisdom. If I say that the heart of man is driven by perpetual motion, I shall not stray from the truth, especially if anyone wishes to examine its restlessness more particularly. For what else is the human heart than a kind of mill, always in motion, grinding something? The human heart is never idle; it never rests without motion, but is always doing something. And just as a mill, set in motion while empty, consumes itself, so the heart of man shamefully runs down from itself when thought of the good does not constrain it. It is always moved toward some object with the desire of its own love; it is always grinding, always acting, always occupied with something delightful, whether to its good or to its harm. Just as the body is not moved without the soul, so the mind of man does not remain in the good unless it has been continually restrained by holy exercises. Therefore the wise man said in Ecclesiasticus: “The joyfulness of the heart is the life of a man and a treasure without loss of holiness, and a man’s exultation is length of life. Have compassion on your soul, pleasing God, and restrain yourself; gather your heart in his holiness, and drive sadness far from you.” What is the gathering of the heart, if not the careful custody of thought? For he gathers his heart in the holiness of the Lord who continually observes the movements of his thoughts in the fear of God. And therefore Scripture says: “With all custody keep your heart, for from it life proceeds.”

Now thoughts take the beginnings of their movements from those things which are offered by the senses; and the more dangerously thought strays, the less carefully they are considered from the beginning by the judgment of reason. Therefore whoever desires to be watchful over his thoughts, whoever wishes to gather his heart in the holiness of the Lord, must first of all guard his outward senses most diligently and place the bridle of prudence upon each of them. Hence Scripture says about hearing and sight: “Hedge your ears with thorns, and do not listen to a wicked tongue; make doors and bars for your mouth, and bolts for your ears.” “Blessed is the man who stops his ears lest he hear bloodshed, and shuts his eyes lest he see evil.” In the book of Job, too, we read: “I made a covenant with my eyes, that I would not so much as think about a virgin.” But against those who have fallen into the pit of sinners by the ministry of the senses, the Lord protests thus through the prophet Jeremiah: “For death has climbed in through your windows; it has entered your houses, to destroy the little children outside and the young men from the streets.” Death enters our houses through the windows whenever an evil thought is admitted into the mind through the ministry of sight or hearing. For the house is the conscience, of which Scripture says: “If you do not constantly hold yourself in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown,” that is, your conscience. This house is very fragile and quickly collapses unless it is upheld by the continual exercise of the fear of God. Unless the Lord guards the house, in vain does the man keep watch who guards it.

Therefore the house of conscience must be upheld by the fear of the Lord, and the senses must also be most diligently restrained, because he is not sufficient to guard his thoughts who does not turn his ears and eyes away from the occasion of evil. And so, if you wish to be a prudent guardian of your thoughts and truly to gather your heart in the holiness of the Lord, hedge your ears with the thorns of compunction and the fear of God, so that they do not hear evil, and turn away your eyes, lest through sight they introduce destruction. What it is not lawful for you to covet, do not look upon with desire; what it is not expedient to have, do not think about. Many have perished through the ministry of the senses, and because they rashly go on looking at outward things, they have often shamefully lost their inward goods. He who does not fear danger is already certainly near destruction; and he who presumes upon his own strength runs hastily toward the ruin of sin. He who fixes his eye on the radiance of lightning will be blinded for the moment; and he who considers the beauty of a woman will not be free from danger. For this reason I praise the prudence of the monk who, in the midst of crowds, is alone, and sets a doorkeeper over his senses, lest he admit any evil thing to the mistress within. Guards are stationed by the gate, who with sword and shield repel assailants rushing in. For the holy fear of the Lord never deserts the mind of a wise man, but, like a strong champion always keeping watch, stands at the praise of his prince, and so guards the mistress of the heart. He turns his ears away from all scurrility of words and shuts his eyes so that they may not see evil. He always walks according to the twelfth degree of humility, with his gaze cast down to the ground, praying to the Lord with the prophet: “Turn my eyes away, lest they see vanity; give me life in your way, O Lord.”

Likewise I admonish you, brothers: direct your senses in the fear of the Lord, so that you may always be able to keep the dwelling of the heart clean and inviolate. Remember that Scripture says: “Be clean, you who carry the vessels of the Lord.” Consider, I ask, with how great a cleanness and purity of mind you ought to shine, who not only carry the vessels of the Lord by reason of the priestly order, but, what is more, you yourselves are the vessels of the Lord. For the holy lover of Christ, Paul, says: “Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ?” And again he says: “Do you not know that your members are the temple of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have from God, and that you are not your own?” And elsewhere: “For the temple of God is holy, which you are.” And concerning Paul himself, in the Acts of the Apostles, the Lord said to Ananias: “Go, for this man is a vessel of election for me.”

The monk who performs priestly office in the Church of God is bound by a twofold reason to the observance of most holy purity. For Christ’s soldier, because of the vow he made to God, must be pure, holy, and continually tireless in good works; and by reason of the priestly ministry he must be circumspect and most diligently watchful. For it is written: “Cursed is the man who does the work of God negligently.” You are ministers of almighty God, who daily offer his Son at the altar; his work is committed into your hands, and therefore great purity of mind and body is required in your way of life. Therefore, so that chaste purity of mind may remain, the instability of thought must be restrained. The senses must be guarded at all times, for through them the integrity of the mind is corrupted. For you are dead to the world and have already begun to live for Christ. It is not lawful for you to think except holy things, nor is it fitting for you, ministers of God, to love anything earthly. For he whom you serve, O monks, is God; he whom you minister to, my brothers, is almighty. Live pure, holy, and innocent, because it is not fitting for you to be occupied with worldly business, you who have been called to God’s service. Let the love of God and purity of heart grow in you with time, so that the longer you live in this world, the holier you may become. Always turn yourselves away from every evil, so that you may be able to remain without failing in good thoughts. Always lift up the eyes of your mind to heaven, and do not occupy the sight of the flesh with perishable things. Close your hearing also, and yours, to every useless, vain, and foolish speech; always think with fear about the future judgment of God. Do not pollute your hands with unlawful and shameful contact with any things whatsoever, so that you may be able to offer the Son of God with a clean body and a pure conscience.

In food and drink it is not for monks to distinguish flavors, nor is it fitting for soldiers of Christ to love bodily delights. Nature requires little, and rustic foods are enough to nourish it; but appetite, unless it is restrained by the command of reason, is carried off toward unlawful things, and through it the simplicity of nature is violated. Therefore flee excess in all things, and in your manner of life observe regular temperance. Let none of you patiently listen to one who disparages his brothers or speaks ill of his neighbor. Let each one of you restrain his tongue from useless speech, and let a good monk blush to speak ill of any Christian. Let that threat of the Lord in the holy Gospel never depart from your memory, where he says: “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. A good man from a good treasure brings forth good things, and an evil man from an evil treasure brings forth evil things. But I say to you that every idle word which men have spoken, they shall render an account of it on the day of judgment.” O sentence greatly to be feared! If an account is demanded for idle speech, what do detraction, scurrility, and foul speech deserve? Or if a useless word is judged liable to the reason of strict examination, what answer will he give who so readily speaks what is evil and destructive? An idle word is every utterance of human beings that is brought forth without just usefulness and lacks the reason of legitimate necessity. Therefore, brothers, consider your words, and let no one speak a word not pertaining to the matter either of just usefulness or of necessary reason.
