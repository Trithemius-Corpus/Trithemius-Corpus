For thus the canon admonishes you, thus the monastic institution commands, thus reason necessary for salvation dictates. You are commanded to be content with every vileness and extremity, and always to be fearful in all things.

But I see that many of you overturn the rule of salvation and live in the monastery otherwise than you ought. Monks now are not afraid to seek delights in monasteries, though they came from the world to conversion poor and beggarly. For the things they could not have outside, if they are now lacking in the monastery, they rebel against humility as if they had suffered the gravest insult. Woe to the monks whom not love of God but want has made cloistered men, whose god is the belly, and who think of nothing less than poverty.

It is not yours, O monks, henceforth to seek delights in the monastery, which the rule commands you wholly to renounce. It is fitting for you to despise all pleasures of the present life, and to love absolutely nothing in this world except God. For your whole way of life ought always to be bound to divine services, since you promised to live according to the spirit and not according to the flesh. He who is a true monk never lives without penance; rather, whoever truly longs for the feasts of eternal happiness daily mortifies his body through abstinence. For it cannot be that a monk should enjoy the delights of the present life and the eternal ones; but if he longs for those, he must despise these.

For the monk who professes the cenobitic canon is commanded to be a disciple of Christ the Savior. If he desires to please him in truth, he must become his imitator. Thus he says to him in the holy Gospel: Whoever wishes to be my disciple, let him follow me. And what it means to follow him the knowing apostle John teaches in a certain epistle, saying: He who says that he remains in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. And the Lord himself again says in the Gospel: Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross daily, and follow me. Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me. And whoever loves son or daughter above me is not worthy of me.

Your master has not spoken obscurely, O monk, nor has he wrapped his meaning in riddles; rather, he has uttered a plain discourse, in which you can offer no excuse. For Christ himself is the pattern and rule of good and perfect conduct for every Christian, and especially for the monk. The cloistered man who does not follow him with his whole heart in these things boasts in vain of his profession of faith. For just as a Christian is called from Christ, so a disciple is named from imitation of the master. Therefore imitate the master in conduct, so that you may prove yourself a true disciple of Christ.

For the Lord Jesus Christ, whose disciple you profess yourself to be, O monk, though he was rich, became poor for us; but you, though vile and needy, seek abundance of all things in the school of poverty. And though the preceptor commands you to despise all things for love of him, you again desire to possess all things in the monastery. Your Lord and master was obedient to the Father for us, even to death on a cross; but you, full of pride and impatience, either refuse to obey your superiors for love of him, or obey unwillingly and with indignation.

For your salvation he endured not only the words and reproaches of wicked men with the greatest patience throughout his whole life, but also lashes, livid blows, and horrible wounds for love of you. He also did not refuse to undergo a most shameful death, so that he might recall your soul from the error of death to the life of eternal light. From the day of his birth he lived in the world poor and humble, filled with many tribulations and reproaches, so that by teaching and benefits he might restore you, a most ruined man, to recognition and love. For what would we be, if the Son of God had not appeared as man, except most utterly lost? And what are we, if we do not imitate the master according to our strength, except ungrateful?

Woe to us ungrateful mortal monks, who disdain to follow humbly our Lord and Savior. Let us examine our way of life, brothers, and compare the disciples with the master whose name has been invoked over us; and we shall plainly understand how far we have withdrawn from the paths of our most kindly preceptor. But let us return again with our whole heart, I pray, and do penance, because we have withdrawn far from the way of salvation, serving the flesh rather than the spirit. Let no one deceive himself with the vanity of hope; let no one wait until the last hour of this life, when he can no longer sin, for penance, because neither the length of life has certainty.
