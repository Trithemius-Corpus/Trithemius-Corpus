There are some among you who take correction badly. To them we declare what we have often said: that those who refuse to endure small and light things here must necessarily endure very grievous things in the future. But I, considering their weakness and ignorance, am grievously tormented within myself, because I see their minds wounded and yet am afraid to apply the necessary medicines. For if I apply remedies suited to their diseases, I fear that, made fainthearted, they may fall into despair, or certainly, given over to a reprobate sense, may withdraw altogether from their holy purpose. Therefore I use as much discretion as I can, as all of you will bear me witness, preferring to send the weak, through patience with charity, to purgatory, rather than, through just severity, to have them descend in despair to hell.

For who in this mortal life has ever been able to make satisfaction for sins who has not applied, or does not now apply, the good will of penance? For good will alone commends the works of penitents. But since many are punished unwillingly, they are still far removed from perfect satisfaction. Believe me, my dearest brothers, that satisfaction for sins is commended more by suffering than by acting. For even our Lord himself and Savior of us all, Jesus Christ, expiated the sin of the whole world by suffering on the altar of the cross. Would that you knew what an effective medicine it is for a soul stained by sins to receive proclamations and corrections with humble patience, and also, for the increase of perfect satisfaction, to bear injuries and insults with equanimity. I am certain that humble patience in adversity far surpasses every kind of satisfaction; and the readier the mind has been to bear insults and injuries for the love of Christ, the more easily and quickly it obtains from the Lord pardon for all its sins and a fuller indulgence.

For this place in which we now speak is the workshop of purgation and expiation; in it zeal for regular discipline holds first place, discretion presides over the examination, and charity directs and completes all things. Therefore in this very place patience is very necessary for you, for without its presence no satisfaction will be completed. Whatever monk is a zealous lover of his salvation and comes to this place as to a workshop of expiation diligently never goes away from it without having been made better than he was. For this place is to be feared and dreaded by demons, but dear and greatly venerable to the holy angels of God; in it the fault of humble monks is washed away, and whatever is committed openly is also expiated publicly before all. But if some hidden fault has been committed by any of you, the secret confession of the penitent will likewise purge it away. In both cases pure confession with humility is required, and both here and there patience is declared necessary for you.

Therefore, whatever monk desires to be good, prepare your soul in all patience, so that both in adversity and in prosperity you may keep the tranquility of your mind most constant. When you have been proclaimed by some fellow-brother for your negligences, bear it patiently, brother, with all humility, faithfully bringing to mind how innocently the Son of God was accused for you. For if that just and innocent one, who committed no sin, suffered insults, why should you not patiently bear reproof from a senior, you who offend daily in many things? He most patiently endured innumerable injuries for your salvation; and why should you not, for your sins, humbly and patiently endure brief and few admonitions? For if, when you are justly proclaimed in judgment, you are blamed for fault, you must necessarily be patient, since you yourself have deserved what you suffer; and humble toleration in correction will be imputed to you as satisfaction for sins. But if you did not deserve the proclamation in judgment, nevertheless bear it patiently for the love of Christ; then it will be reckoned to you not only as satisfaction for preceding sins, but much more as justification. Therefore whether a monk is proclaimed justly or unjustly, he has no just cause for defense, because if he has sinned, he suffers deservedly; but if he is innocent, he is the more bound to patience. For no one who is not patient and humble will be able to imitate Christ.
