On the Lenten fast of monks.

...to act against his command; let him omit or neglect nothing that has been enjoined upon him by him. Let him take the utmost care not to sadden or disturb his pastor by restless conduct, nor murmur against him, nor disparage him, nor say any evil whatever against him. Rather, let him revere him with the sincere affection of charity as Christ’s vicar, and let him not sadden his spirit by importunate or disorderly words or deeds. As far as he can, let him assist him in his labors, and intercede for him before God with continual prayers.

Monks sin greatly against God when they are not afraid to sadden their abbot with their importunate detractions and corrupt ways. Therefore whatever monk desires to be good, whoever truly wishes to be devout in the Lord, should always and everywhere venerate his abbot with pure love as the vicar of Jesus Christ in the monastery; he should humbly obey his commands in all things, and in no way depart from his direction.

The abbot, for his part, should strive to show himself in all things a worthy pastor to his monks, one who feeds the sheep of Christ entrusted to him by word and example alike, in all gentleness of charity. Let him not trouble the brothers without just and reasonable necessity; let him not be contemptuous of the holy community; let him recognize himself as the steward of the monastery’s goods, not their lord. Let him not be a mocker of his subjects, not turbulent, not importunate, not a tyrant, but their sincere lover: calm in speech, gentle and upright in conduct, holy in his way of life, clean in conscience, and always devout in mind toward God.

Let him at all times maintain solicitous care for the souls entrusted to him, and neglect nothing that seems able to contribute to their salvation. Let him not exalt himself on high because he has been placed as ruler of others, but let him remember that he must fear all the more, since he is certainly bound to render an account for all in the dread judgment of God. Let him correct the proud, rebellious, and obstinate as a lord; let him kindly cherish the humble, obedient, and governable as a most gentle father; and let him wisely distribute to each one his due without respect of persons.

Let him always consider himself the mother and nurse of the monks; and therefore those whom he can lead back to better things by gentleness he should not embitter with importunate words and harsh invectives. Let him always maintain solicitous care for the sick, and provide for all in such a way that they have no just cause for murmuring. In his own infirmities let him learn how he ought to have compassion on his subjects. For he cannot be a good pastor of souls who disdains to suffer with exact attention the needs of his infirm subjects.

But he can become a good abbot among monks who knows how to conform himself to the persons of the infirm in such a way that, whenever a brother asks something of him, he takes upon himself in his heart the condition of the one asking and says: “What would I wish to be done for me in this matter, according to the right judgment of reason, if I were in the person of the one asking?” Indeed, if the abbot imagines himself to be a subject, he will let no one go away from him saddened.

We have now said, brothers, what sort of men you ought to be toward us for love of God, so that, existing as good monks, you may please the Lord Jesus in truth. You have heard, nevertheless, what sort of pastor I ought to show myself to you; whether I do this or not, you yourselves will judge better than I. For we shall fast in vain and find no fruit of Lenten observance unless we all stand before God in true charity and in the concord of ecclesiastical unity. Or what good will it do to abstain from richer foods, and to defile the conscience with evil works or thoughts?

For the fasts we have begun to be pleasing to God, it is necessary that they be not only alien and remote from vices and sins, but also adorned with the full abundance of all virtues. For holy Ambrose says: “What good is it to fast in the belly and indulge in hunting, to abstain from foods and go astray in sins? Do you think, brothers, that the man is fasting who at first daybreak does not keep vigil at church, does not seek out the holy places of the blessed martyrs, but rising in the morning gathers his servants, sets out nets, brings forth dogs, and roams through groves and woods; who, I say, drags servants with him, perhaps more eager to hasten to church? For his own pleasures he piles up the sins of others, not knowing that he will be guilty both for his own fault and for the perdition of his servants.”

And Saint Pope Leo says: “The fast of our solemnity does not consist in abstinence from food alone...”
