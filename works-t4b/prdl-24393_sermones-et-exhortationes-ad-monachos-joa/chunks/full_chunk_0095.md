Homily XX.
Fol. LIX.

O lover and student of your own salvation, consider the form of obedience which you are to follow. The abbot bears the office of Christ’s vicar in the monastery; whether he is good or bad, he deserves the obedience of all his subjects, not by his own merit but in the name of Christ. For we understand the saying, “He who hears you hears me, and he who despises you despises me,” not only of the apostles, but of any prelate in the Church of God who has been appointed guardian and ruler of souls.

Therefore, if you have an abbot who is good, suitable, and holy, hear his voice as the command of God, since he bears God’s place; and whatever he commands you, be obedient to him in all things. If his teaching is for you a rule of life, and his holy conduct an example of purer discipline, then he is a friend of God and the vicar of Jesus Christ: for the one reason he deserves that you love him, and for the other that you obey him in all things when he commands.

But if you have obtained a shepherd who is rude, unlearned, or certainly less suitable, take care not to sin by wishing rashly to examine his actions. Rather, consider that your sins deserve to have a useless shepherd. Consider him not according to what he is, but according to what he is called; honor the shepherd for love and reverence of him whose vicariate marks him out. Remember at all times that the obedience which you promised to God must be shown purely and simply to his vicar; for if the abbot is bad in his way of life, nevertheless he does not lose the vicariate of Christ unless he is deposed in fact. Nor does the shepherd’s wicked and blameworthy life absolve you from the obedience promised in religion.

The sense of our saying must be maintained: bad monks do not deserve to have a good abbot. Do not think, beloved brothers, that this example is being hurled at you. For I do not believe myself to be a bad shepherd, although I cannot deny that I am rude and unlearned; nor have we admitted any suspicion of evil about you, since indeed we seek nothing more than to nourish and enlarge brotherly charity in all things.

Frequently monks come to us, wandering either with reason or without cause, who for the most part turn the matter of their own instability back upon their abbots. But I, who desire you to be instructed in all things holy and useful, exhort and warn your charity in fatherly fashion that hereafter you should not lend your ears to these wanderers, who slander the characters of their own shepherds and strive to hide their own crimes by accusations against others. This is not the necessary integrity of monastic life: to turn the cause of the rebellion and disobedience of subjects back upon the head of the shepherd.

He who is a true monk does not rashly judge another’s affairs; and he who is a son of humble obedience does not investigate causes for rebellion. But he who grants consent to the passions of the flesh can never be at rest; and he who scrutinizes another’s life is a despiser of his own salvation. A monk founded in the love of God does not pass judgment on the command of his superior; and he who always beholds Christ as present venerates his own shepherd as an angel of God. Boldness makes a monk degenerate, and presumption has cast many into prison.

A wanton monk, too lascivious and insolent, has lost the purpose of holiness; and unless he is recalled to his senses by the hard chastisement of the flesh, he has already set his foot toward hell. He who examines the command of his superior with curious rashness is not obedient; and he who resists an order is not a true disciple of Christ. For true obedience does not murmur, does not hesitate, is not sad, does not seek a byway, does not delay, does not excuse itself, nor show the least sign of lack of resignation. At the superior’s command, for the love of Christ, it always rejoices; it runs to meet it gladly, and obeys cheerfully with haste.

For what else is a monk than a constant combatant, who, armed with the virtues of humility and obedience, always keeps watch in the arena of struggle, and, victorious under a threefold battle-line, fights more strongly against himself? Blessed is that monk who, doing violence to himself, has learned to overcome the will of the flesh, and by the continual practice of spiritual warfare climbs the steep mountain of true obedience. Truly blessed is that monk who, having despised all the consolations of the world, has placed his whole self in the love of Jesus Christ, and now desires nothing more among visible things. It is not for monks to seek riches, honors, and pleasures in this world, nor is it fitting for the soldier of Christ to think of anything except Christ. O monk three and four times blessed, who for the love of Christ mortifies his own will until the end of life, and perseveres humbly in true obedience.

How great a good humble obedience is before God, my brothers, no one can sufficiently declare, for divine testimony wished to prefer it to all sacrifices. It is obedience that reconciles the soul to God, and through the shedding of blood crowns the martyrs.
