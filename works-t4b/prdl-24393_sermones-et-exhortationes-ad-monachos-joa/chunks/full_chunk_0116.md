*Sermon I. Folio LIX.*

In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ begins the second book of exhortations to monks, by Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, afterwards of [unclear]. How cautiously monks must live: Sermon One.

Many indeed, most beloved brothers, are the things necessary for you to consider in this life, if you desire to arrive at the glory of eternal salvation. For Scripture bears witness that your adversary the devil goes about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour; and we know that he must be resisted with great vigilance in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ through works of charity. Therefore let no one presume upon his own strength or trust in vanity, for unless you humbly keep yourselves in the fear of the Lord, you will quickly fall into the snare of the devil. “The greater you are,” Scripture commands, “humble yourself in all things,” for God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble.

A monk ought to be humble toward God and a true despiser of this world. A monk must always be solicitous in the observance of regular discipline, and continually zealous in the fear of the Lord. A monk must be chaste and modest in mind and body alike, so that he may serve God, just as he promised, in all purity. A monk ought not to live according to his own will, but according to the Rule and according to the direction of his pastor, conformed to the norm of cenobitic institution. A monk ought to love busy and holy silence, and to occupy his mind usefully in the reading of the Scriptures. A monk must at all times, even unto death, be solicitous that he may be perfect in the observance of the commandments of God and of the Rule.

But so that you may know what things are necessary for you who live in holy religion, for perfection, I shall describe for you a certain path of virtues, by which one must direct the way to the kingdom of heaven. All the virtues, however, cohere with one another by the bond of kinship, so that he who possesses one perfectly seems to have them all, and he who lacks even one is said to possess none at all.

The first of all virtues necessary for salvation is the most holy Catholic faith, separated from every pollution of error and heresy, and originally rooted in the doctrine of our Lord Jesus Christ. For, as Scripture bears witness, without faith it is impossible to please God; and he who does not believe in the only-begotten Son of God is already judged, and the wrath of God remains upon him. Therefore faith is the origin of all virtues, from which all pursuits of salvation arise.

True and right faith in God begets fear of the Lord, for if we believe that God is just, we shall rightly confess that he is to be feared. Right faith brings forth hope and confidence, because when we believe God to be merciful, we hope that by his goodness we can attain salvation. Faith begets charity, because if we believe God to be the highest good, without doubt we love him. For who would not love what he does not doubt to be the supreme good? Faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen, and the single foundation of our salvation. Faith joins the soul to God, and through the mediation of charity makes it the friend of the eternal King.

For the most holy bishop Augustine says: There are no greater riches, no treasures, no honors, no substance of this world greater than the Catholic faith, which saves sinful men, enlightens the blind, cures the sick, baptizes catechumens, justifies the faithful, restores penitents, increases the just, crowns martyrs, preserves virgins, widows, and married persons in chaste modesty, ordains clerics, consecrates priests, and places believers with the holy angels in the eternal inheritance of the heavens.

But where sound faith is not, there justice cannot be, because the just man lives by faith; and he who lacks the merit of justice will have no portion with Christ. Faith is the beginning of human salvation, without which no one can reach the number of the children of God, nor possess any share in the kingdom of God. Faith is the foundation of all virtues; whatever is done outside faith is without doubt built for Gehenna. Christian faith is a most beautiful lamp illuminating the soul, and through the observance of the commandments leading it into eternal happiness.

Faith first brings the mind back to the knowledge of God; afterward it gives the precepts of living unto obedience; and when a man has kept the commandments of God, hope of attaining salvation follows; then charity is made firm in hearts. Faith gives knowledge to the soul; knowledge brings forth action in charity; and to these two beatitude is promised in eternity. One must take the greatest care that there be no error in knowledge, because when right faith is corrupted by the falsehood of heresy, no action brings one to the harbor of salvation. Therefore let there be rectitude and truth in faith, justice and sincerity in action, and no iniquity will have dominion.

For faith without works is dead, and merits nothing toward the salvation of eternal happiness. To have faith without the works of charity is to imitate the demons, who, although they are far removed from all charity, nevertheless believe that God is just and tremble. Hence, concerning believers who do not show the works of charity, the Lord speaks through the prophet Isaiah and afterward through himself in the holy Gospel, saying: “This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.” He who praises Christ with his mouth but without works is like a man who doubts in faith. For if he truly believed, he would certainly fear God. Faith without works is dead, like a body without spirit, which, removed from the sight of the living, is hidden in a tomb. If you believe well and live badly, you slay yourself with your own sword, because for him who knows the good and does not do it, it is sin.

Beware, my brothers, lest you admit error in faith, and take the greatest care that there be no wickedness in action. Follow the faith which holy Church commends to us; live the life which the Gospel and the cenobitic norm command.

Then have hope in the manifestation of God at all times most certain, but never separated from fear of the Lord. Hope is the certain expectation of future goods through the grace of the merciful God, and the solicitude of meriting the crown of justice through the exercise of charity. Hope is the sure expectation of future beatitude, which the grace of God works in us, accompanied by the rectitude of a good will. Hope is that by which someone hopes to come to the life of eternal happiness which he desires, and, through the mediation of God’s grace, also composes and fits his own will to meet it.

Hope concerns goods that are difficult, future, and possible, of which no one hopes for the contrary. For no one hopes for evil, nor for what is easy, nor for what is present or past; nor do we fail to know that hope is not of impossible things. Hope is the moderator of all labors, and the one consolation in the midst of tribulations. For the hope of future goods brings rest in this life to those laboring for Christ, just as, for those contending in the arena, the expectation of the crown through continual victory lessens the pain of wounds. Hope strengthened the martyrs in torments, and made them, in the power of Christ, stronger than all their persecutors. Hope of eternal beatitude preserves the Church, and gave the holy confessors and virgins true charity in Christ. Hope powerfully raises up the soul cast down by grief; and the higher it has lifted itself through love, the less it feels the adversities of this world. Blessed is the man who trusts in the Lord, because hope does not confound, as the holy apostle promised, by which we have been saved.

But so that hope may not be corrupted into presumption, fear of the Lord is necessary for our salvation, so that him whom we believe to be kind and merciful we may not doubt also to be just and upright. For the most blessed Pope Gregory says: Nothing is safer for us than to tremble humbly under hope, lest an incautious mind either cast itself into the depths of vices by despairing, or, by exalting itself, fall presumptuously from hope. For the more humbly it trembles in hope before the eyes of the strict judge, the more robustly it stands in him.

Fear of God corrects the mind and keeps it pure from all malice. Fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom, because on the way of God one begins from fear, so that little by little one may arrive at the strength of charity. Fear of the Lord composes the monk, and makes him zealous in the observance of regular discipline. Fear of the Lord guards the Rule, and preserves true purity of mind and body. Fear of the Lord neglects none of the things that are commanded, but is solicitous for all. The commandments of God are walls. A city without the protection of walls is quickly captured by enemies, and a monk who does not have the fear of God is easily overcome by demons. For the fear of God alone is what corrects minds, drives away crimes, preserves the innocent, and makes the monk persevering in the purpose of holiness.

From fear, little by little, charity arises; and when, with God’s help, it has been truly rooted in the mind, overcoming servile fear, it now casts it out. The root of all good things is charity, since whatever good is done in this life is governed by charity alone, so that it may please God. Charity is the greatest of all virtues, without which all the deeds of mortals are nothing. For the most holy apostle of Christ says: “If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but do not have charity, I have become like sounding brass or a clanging cymbal. And if I have prophecy, and know all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith, so that…”
