On the nine degrees of ascent.

Hasten, my dearest brothers, to climb these degrees of the spiritual ladder by which one ascends, so that you may be able at last to reach the summit of perfection. And unless you, who have been placed under the obedience of monastic perfection, have advanced to this fourth degree, you will scarcely ascend the five that follow. For those who are placed on the fourth degree do good works when commanded, from their own will, though they have not submitted themselves by vow or by monastic profession to its constitutions. And in this one respect they are inferior to those who follow: because a good work is the more acceptable to God, the more it is commended by the merit of obedience.

The fifth degree, then, is climbed by one who guards himself with all his strength from sins, and, being placed under obedience, always remains strong and constant in his purpose of holiness. Loving God above all things with a pure mind, such persons do good works principally for love of him; and in their intention they neither fear punishments nor seek rewards. They humbly obey their prelates for the love of God, and do not withdraw from the purity of regular observance in the practice of penance. And yet there are some whom the weakness of the flesh sometimes makes less stable, and somewhat inclined to waver in their opinions against obedience. At times they try to defend their own exercises and private observances against the determination of their prelates, and to judge themselves more prudent in spiritual examination than their own rectors. Flee this, O monk, because the devil lies hidden in ambush, and those whom he cannot overcome by open assault he attempts to defeat by secret contrivance. For the more learned and perfect a monk has been, the more readily he should obey his prelate without discussion. But if a perfect monk, in matters lawful and honorable, contemns obedience to his abbot, though imperfect, he certainly loses the name of perfection. Therefore each person should take care to apply himself to the pursuit of progress in such a way that he presumes to violate obedience in nothing. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than sacrifices, and to listen is better than to offer the fat of rams.” Whatever you have offered is another’s; but, says our God [unclear].

The sixth degree, therefore, is especially fitting for those who, living in every purity of mind and body, love the Lord above all things and do every good work they undertake for God’s honor alone, and do not depart from the commands of their prelates. Great is the power of obedience, which doubles the deed of a good work and increases the merit of the one who performs it, as though trees were filled with fruit for God. Let not the words of the Lord concerning obedience trouble you, my dearest brothers, because he preferred the freedom of the will to the subjection of monks, and set a good work done in freedom before the merit of obedience. For this is the monk who obeys from the necessity of his profession: yet that will is good which, for love of God, bound itself to obedience, and this necessity commends it in many ways.

For there is one necessity by which a good person is compelled to do something, where the will has not consented but is always reluctant. This necessity diminishes the praises due to virtue, because the good work was never in the will. And there is another necessity, arising from an inward inclination of the mind, which increases rather than diminishes the praise of a good work, because it kindles the will intent upon the work itself. And there is a third necessity, which does not compel without regard to the will. For if you wish to cross a deep river, the fitting necessity requires an instrument. But the necessity by which a monk is compelled to obey his shepherd is not one of punitive coercion, nor of blind inclination, but rather of the third kind, which is ordered to its end from a preceding will. It is therefore necessary for a monk, on account of that end, to do what the abbot commands, so that he may be able to hope for the vow that he made for love of God. And since that end is most praiseworthy, by which the creature subjects himself to his Creator in obedience out of love, necessity in the monk does not diminish the praise of the good work, but makes it greater. O blessed necessity of monks, which draws from the treasure of a good will and orders it to the end which is God, the unchangeable good. It is an increase of praise, an excellence of merit, and an abundance of reward, to do a good work from obedience, rather than if it stood without vow and profession, in the freedom of one’s own will. For the monk has once committed himself wholly, and all his will, to God, reserving nothing at all for himself. Whatever good he has done under obedience he brings back to the end of the religious life he assumed, for love of keeping it; and no work is compared with this after martyrdom. One who is continent without a vow from the act of Venus performs one virtue, which is called chastity; but one who observes the same thing by reason of a vow proceeds otherwise, because the individual virtues are referred and ordered to the perfection of the whole religious life that he professed. The whole monastic religion is more than one particular virtue. For one who offers a good work that he has done without obedience offers only that, while the rest remains to himself, because he is still in his own will, with greater freedom of acting.
