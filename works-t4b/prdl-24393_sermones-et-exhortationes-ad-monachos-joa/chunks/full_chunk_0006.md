*Homily I. Fol. III.*

...that you keep the monastic ordinances which you have promised. “Vow,” says Scripture, “and render.” And again another Scripture says: “If you have vowed anything to God, do not delay to pay it. For an unfaithful and foolish promise displeases him, and it is much better not to vow than, after a vow, not to render what was promised.” And Saint Augustine warns us how necessary it is to render to God what has been promised, saying: “There are certain things which we owe the Lord even without vowing them, and there are certain things which we do not owe unless we have vowed them. But after we promise them to the Lord, we are necessarily constrained to render them.”

And now, my brothers, I ask you carefully to consider what you have vowed to the Lord your God, and diligently to weigh under what necessity of rendering what you promised you have been placed. For you have vowed to your God, not to man, obedience and the conversion of your conduct according to the Rule of our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and perpetual stability in this order of religion. The obedience which you promised to God you will show to the Lord’s vicar, whom you acknowledge, in the monastery and by the Rule which you profess, to bear the place of Christ. What sort of conversion of conduct ought to be according to the Rule, you see plainly set out in chapter four, “On the Instruments of Good Works,” where you find the summary of the whole of monastic perfection briefly comprised in a few words.

Indeed, all of you have heard what our blessed father commanded to be done concerning novices, and you know that it was done in each one of you at the beginning of your profession. For he says thus: “The one to be received, in the oratory before all, promises concerning his stability, the conversion of his conduct, and obedience, before God and his saints, so that if at any time he should act otherwise, he may know that he will be condemned by God, whom he mocks.” Consider now, therefore, brothers, under how great a necessity you have been placed for the observance of the Rule, since unless you fulfill in deed what you promised in receiving the monastic habit, you will be judged before the almighty Lord not as servants of God, not as friends, not as monks, but as mockers.

Therefore always be mindful of the things which the Rule commands you, according to which you promised to shape your conduct. Labor like true soldiers of Jesus Christ, always watching in fear of the Lord, since, as Scripture says, unless you hold yourselves steadfastly in fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown. Consider your calling, because God has not called you to idleness, nor to the pleasures of this world, nor to pursue the desires of the flesh, but rather to continual labor, to abstinence from all delights, and to the constant mortification of the flesh under the image of the Crucified, so that, as far as you are able, you may bear his signs marked in your hearts.

You have set your hands, therefore, to the plow of holy heavenly agriculture, in which you are commanded to sweat continually in labor, and not only never to look back, but always, by advancing from virtue to virtue, to strive toward the highest summit of perfection. For what else is it to live as a monk than to avoid the company of men, and in solitude to please God alone actively through the desire of most fervent love? Hence Scripture says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth. He will sit solitary and keep silence, because he has lifted himself above himself.”

Therefore, if you wish to be perfect, you who by regular institution are bound to love perfection, begin with the way of life which Christ taught; and since for love of him you have already renounced all other things, do not neglect humbly to conquer, overcome, and despise even yourselves. The servant of Christ has nothing besides Christ; he loves nothing, cares for nothing, seeks nothing except Christ, so that he may be perfect and please him whose justice he has chosen and professed. But the monk who has anything besides Christ cannot be perfect, since true love is not divided, nor is the mind truly united to diverse and contrary things.

Therefore the Lord says: “If you wish to be perfect, go and sell all that you have and give to the poor, and come, follow me.” You, indeed, most beloved brothers, as I trust, have now left the world and whatever you had in the world; and you have laid aside not only ownership of all the things which you possessed and could have possessed, but also every desire to possess. And you have come to this monastery of the blessed Martin, where the poverty of temporal goods is great, and this alone has been given to us in place of riches: not to find occasions for avarice. Yet just as punishment does not make a martyr, but the cause does, so want does not make a blessed poor man, but voluntary patience in that very poverty for love of Christ.
