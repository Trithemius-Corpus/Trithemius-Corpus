*Berno. I. Folio LX.*

“... so that I might move mountains, but if I have not charity, I am nothing. And if I should distribute all my goods as food for the poor, and if I should hand over my body so that it burns, but have not charity, it profits me nothing.” Consider carefully, now, my brothers, your own selves, and ask your consciences whether you have true charity; without it all other things avail nothing for salvation. For you have heard the clear judgment of the Apostle, by which he says that charity is necessary for salvation. Let no one trust in the wisdom of this world; let no one vainly exalt himself over the natural gifts he has received; let no one presume upon bodily continence, since, if charity is lacking, the observance of the other things contributes nothing to salvation. But lest anyone presume a false opinion of himself, the holy Apostle set forth certain signs and operations of charity, saying: “Charity is patient; it is kind. Charity does not envy, does not act perversely, is not puffed up, is not ambitious, does not seek what is its own, is not provoked, does not think evil, does not rejoice over iniquity, but rejoices together with the truth. It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Charity never fails.” Have the charity of God in you, my brothers; unless you keep it, you labor in vain by striving.

Humility is very necessary for you, as we have often said; it is not only a great virtue, but also the nurse of the other virtues and the foremost guardian of them all. For Pope Gregory the Great says: “He who gathers virtues without humility carries dust into the wind.” Humility is indeed the great virtue of monks, since they are bound to be so much humbler than the rest who live their lives in the world, as by profession they are bound to strive and tend toward greater perfection. Humility is the stable foundation of the virtues; whatever is built upon it will be overturned by no whirlwind of tempest. Through pride the devil fell from heaven; through humility man ascends. In vain are we called monks if we have not been humble, for just as fire is extinguished if it lacks the fuel of wood, so a monk without humility shows himself very unworthy of his name. By Christian humility the devil is conquered, and, as if struck by a nail, he is overcome as he flees. No more splendid jewel is seen in monastic adornment than humility; if a monk has despised it, he has denied both his profession and the humble Christ.

Prudence follows upon humility; it always directs all things to the standard of reason and does nothing except what is right, honorable, and praiseworthy. Prudence knows what it ought virtuously to seek, and knows well that it ought to live according to the right judgment of reason. True and perfect prudence is that by which we seek that highest, unchangeable good, and order all things to their due and right end. Prudence preserves the observance of regular discipline stable in vigor, and brings observing monks back to the complete restoration of the purpose they had lost. Prudence directs the monk in the right way of salvation, and does not allow him to decline to either side. Prudence preserves peace and concord among brothers, and orders right action in each matter. Prudence, O my brothers, is very necessary for you; by it you will know in every action what things are to be undertaken by you, and what in turn are to be avoided. True prudence, not of the flesh but of the spirit, teaches you to despise the world, and to desire only those things that are future and eternal. Prudence teaches a man to stand firm in himself, and neither in prosperity nor in adversity to decline from the purpose of holiness. Prudence teaches how to order present things beautifully; it brings past things back to memory for instruction; and it foresees future things, lest anything happen unexpectedly. Prudence corrects offenders usefully and fruitfully, and composes all that it either does, says, or thinks, according to the judgment of reason, toward its due end. Therefore be prudent as serpents and simple as doves, because prudence without goodness is malice, and simplicity without rational prudence is called foolishness. For the most holy Paul, Apostle of Christ, says: “The prudence of the flesh is death.” Be prudent, lest you be circumvented by the deceit of others, but simple, lest you circumvent anyone. For this is the duty of the prudent man: neither to be capable of being deceived, nor to wish to deceive anyone. A prudent man does not abandon the judgment of reason, and does nothing about which he must afterward be ashamed. For from foreseeing one is called prudent.

Justice accompanies prudence; it is the most splendid of all the virtues. For without justice all the remaining virtues are nothing. Justice is the preserver of religion, the mother of grace, the nurse of observance, the nourisher of truth, the begetter of peace, and the origin of piety.
