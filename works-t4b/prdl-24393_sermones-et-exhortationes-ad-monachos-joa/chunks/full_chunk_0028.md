Hence blessed Jerome also says: let action often be interrupted by prayer; then, in turn, after prayer has been made, let a pleasing alternation come to the soul. A monk who does not love the secrecy of the cell is not a true soldier of Christ, since he bears the name and habit of a monk without the works; and he who ought to have been solitary for the love of God, having in a sense returned to the world, loves the pursuit of vanity. Hence blessed Pope Gregory says: He who lives bodily removed from human beings, but through the disturbances of human intercourse plunges himself in thought into earthly desire, is not solitary, although he dwells in solitude. But if someone is bodily pressed by crowds of people, and yet suffers no disturbances of secular cares in his heart, he is not in the city, even if he lives in the midst of human beings.

And this is what Jerome says against the dreams of those who sleep: The wise man, he says, can never be alone. For he has with him all who are and who have been good; he has a free mind, and whatever he wishes he follows, and he carries himself wherever he wishes. For what he cannot do with the body he embraces with the mind; and if he lacks human company, he speaks with God. How, then, will a good man ever be alone?

And so, remain constant in your cell; let song always be in your mouth, since nothing so weakens all the powers of the mind as idleness, after it has produced acedia. If you love God ardently, you will never be idle, because the true love of Christ does not allow sluggishness to arise in the soul, but always drives the lover toward the will of the beloved. And just as a certain carnal-minded man truly said, “Let him love who does not wish to become idle,” so experience has taught us in spiritual love that, once it has begun to dwell in a mind, it does not permit that mind to grow torpid through idleness and sloth. For divine love always impels one toward better things, and the more it advances by burning, the greater increase it receives.

The companion of this love is a clean conscience; and if a monk desires both to have and to preserve it clean, it is necessary first of all that he cast off the vanity of the world and the business of temporal things. For it is not possible that twin loves should reside in one breast; nor can he prevail in loving Christ well who does not wish to despise the world. For God, though in the Trinity of persons he is one in the essence of substance, is either loved by us alone, or he is not loved with a partner. Therefore, so that conscience may remain clean in all purity, and so that the mind may be able to love God above all things, let the monk love the secrecy of the cell and never withdraw from the exercise of inward sweetness.

For in the cell of a good and wise monk there is the highest tranquility; there is, as it were, an earthly paradise in the monastery for those who are converted to the purity of the cenobitic institution. There are peace, piety, goodness, justice, virtue, and honor. There are joy, purity, sweetness, and great tranquility of life, where the devout monk speaks with the Lord in prayer, and the future sweetness of the heavenly kingdom is tasted beforehand by the contemplative soldier. O cell of monks truly serving God, paradise, indeed a little garden of most honorable delight, in which the mind is joined to God through love and sweetly raised up through desire. The monk who loves, venerates, and cherishes it is truly a soldier of Christ and serves the Lord in the fullness of virtues. But he who abhors and deserts the secrecy of the cell scorns the workshop of his own salvation. Therefore I beg you, my dearest brothers, begin to dwell in the secrecy of the cell with desire of mind; without love of it no one will be able to reach perfection of virtues in the cenobium.

On the Manual Labor of Monks. Homily VII.

“You shall eat the labor of your hands,” says the prophet of the Lord; “blessed are you, and it shall be well with you.” If all who live from the labor of their own hands are judged blessed, surely no one idle eats bread without guilt and fault. For almighty God imposed this upon Adam the first-formed as a penalty for the transgression of the original commandment, declaring to him that in the sweat of his face he would work food from the earth with his hands. Since, moreover, we all sinned in Adam, we are necessarily subjected to the same penalties to which it is clear that he was subjected. He labored for the food by which he might live, and he did not eat bread in idleness.

Hence our blessed father Benedict, detesting the idleness of monks, says in the Rule which he handed down to us: Idleness is the enemy of the soul, and therefore at certain times monks ought to be occupied in manual labor, and again at fixed hours in divine reading. For then they are truly monks, if they live by the labor of their hands, as our fathers and the apostles did. Hence also the holy apostle Paul says in the epistle: “He who does not labor shall not eat.”

In the beginning of the monastic state this was the custom of monks: that they admitted no one into their fellowship without labor, not so much for the necessities of food as for the salvation of the soul, lest through idleness an opportunity be given to destructive thoughts. At that time monks had no rents, no tithes, no revenues, no annual proceeds of any kind; rather, all living together in common procured food and clothing for themselves by their hands, burdening no one by demanding what was owed. The holy apostles of our Savior too, although they had the power to live from the preaching of the Gospel, preferred to labor with their hands, lest they seem to have burdened any of the faithful, and so that they might have not only the means by which they themselves lived, but also something with which they supplied necessities to the needy.

But after monks began to have rents and revenues, the former pursuits of simplicity soon failed, and little by little, along with wealth in temporal things, pride increased, to such a degree that in time monks refused all labors of the hands. As long as monks were poor and humble in birth, they worshiped God in truth; but after they began to abound in wealth, duplicity drove away simplicity, and the pride of nobles extinguished the humility of monks. Once the integrity of regular observance had been taken away, the wealth of temporal things soon also failed in many cenobia by the just judgment of God.

From their number was the present monastery of Sponheim, my brothers, neither the last nor the first, which for many years remained miserably desolate in both states, to such an extent that the five monks who alone lived there, leading a dissolute life, did not have the means from which they could obtain the necessities of life for themselves. Hence, at last, by the provision of God’s mercy, Frederick, duke of Transylvania, having called upon the counsel of the fathers of the reformed order, in the year of the Christians one thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, expelled the monks deformed in life, deposed the abbot, Otto by name, from Cologne, and placed in this monastery four others of the observance of Bursfeld, called from the cenobium of Schoenberg. Over them he set as abbot Johannes, from the noble family of Budisbach, which they commonly call “of the house of Carbonaria,” a man such as you have seen and heard, worthy of eternal memory. He found this place in extreme poverty, and, presiding over it for almost fourteen years in great patience, recalled it to the state in which we now live.

Therefore, since lack of temporal goods still presses us heavily, there would be need for continual labor, so that we might sustain ourselves with necessary food and free the house of God in which we dwell from debt. But what are your labors, or how much can they contribute to such great needs? We are few in number and unsuited to rustic labors; nor does the institution of the priestly order permit us to spend the whole day in the field turning stones with our hands and to interrupt divine service. We are wretched and weighed down by excessive poverty and lack of all necessities; and if we labor much, we gain little from it, yet if we work little, we certainly fail still more in spirit. For, as Scripture bears witness, man is born to labor as a bird is to flying.

Therefore, lest you eat your bread in idleness, you must always be bound to some honorable and useful labor, by which the mind may both be relieved from weariness with regular observance and our grievous poverty may at least in some small way be repaired. In the time of harvest, for hay, and in the days of autumn, for the vintage, we have been accustomed to employ you outside the monastery only when labor is clearly very necessary and the scarcity of hired workers increases the price and wage of servants. At other times we have decided to keep you in the cenobium, because your labors, compared with the expenses of the monastery, contribute little to its needs, and because, as it seems to us, very few of you willingly present yourselves voluntarily for rustic pursuits.
