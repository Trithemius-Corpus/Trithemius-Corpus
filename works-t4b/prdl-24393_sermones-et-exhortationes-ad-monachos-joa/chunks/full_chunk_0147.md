Sermon VII. Folio LXXIII

...to God and the power of acting. No one has offered more to God than the monk who has given himself. But some who ascend this sixth degree have hidden within themselves a certain defect of pride, because, while they look back incautiously upon themselves, they do not entrust themselves in all things to the divine will as they ought. This defect takes its beginning from a certain very subtle vanity, when the mind, trusting in its own purity, asks that some secret be revealed to it by the Lord. And in this one respect those who ascend the sixth rung differ from the seventh: those who are devoted to God flee signs of vanity. For signs were given not to believers but to unbelievers; hence the monk who asks a sign from the Lord shows that he has not yet perfectly subjected himself to the divine will.

Those ascend the seventh degree who most zealously keep all the prescribed things, and, subject to God in all things, live without the stain of vanity. They flee every sin, both venial and mortal, as far as they can; they do works of virtue for the love of God alone; they obey their pastors in all things; they seek no miracles of vanity. Whatever almighty God has ordained concerning them, they receive gratefully; they withdraw in no respect from the observance of monastic purity. Yet in one respect they are inferior to those who follow, because perhaps they suffer some defect unknown to them, by whose impulse they sometimes commit something through their own sensuality.

Whoever has deserved to ascend the eighth degree concedes nothing further to sensuality. Here is the mind’s sweetest rest; here is the perfect tranquillity of the soul. In this degree nothing vicious is left of beauty; nothing is found here that can sadden the soul. Here all things are clean, all things pure, all things burning most sweetly with the fervor of charity. O if we might strive to ascend to this degree; O if we might someday deserve to reach this harbor of tranquillity. The more one who stands on this holy rung begins to taste the sweetness of inward delight, the more, hastening toward what lies ahead, he desires still more. O desires of holy love always to be proclaimed; O venerable exercises of perfection, where the clean conscience of one loving God shines without sins, where the wicked battles of vices have ceased, and great tranquillity has followed.

Those ascend the ninth degree who, already consummated in the eighth, keep all the prescribed things purely and simply for the love of God, and desire nothing other than to praise, bless, and glorify God. Whoever has ascended to this degree of perfection, as though forgetful of himself, resigns himself wholly to God and humbly submits. He reckons himself unworthy of all God’s benefits; he cares nothing at all for himself, because he has already learned to entrust himself wholly to God and humbly to be subject in all things to his ordering. Hence he neither seeks miracles nor asks for any consolations in this life, but with the holy apostle of Christ truly cries: “For me to live is Christ, and to die is gain.” “The world is crucified to me, and I to the world.”

For the true monk, as a lover of Christ, has offered himself wholly to God out of charity, and has despised the world with all its riches, honors, and vanities. Hence the world is said to be crucified to him, in which there is nothing that seems able to pierce his mind. Happy are those monks who serve the Lord God with a pure conscience in true charity; who run without offense under the rule of humility and obedience; who ascend to this last degree of perfection. These are true monks, who seek nothing other than the honor of God.

Indeed, my brothers, although monks are not bound by necessity of salvation to be perfect, otherwise very few would be saved, yet we are all bound with good will to strive toward perfection, and with all the powers we can to long for this ninth degree. For it is very contrary to the monastic profession to be unwilling to make progress, and it is a kind of apostasy, after the vow of perfection, to wish to remain in imperfection. For the vices that attack us are enemies of our salvation, and until they either conquer or are conquered, they do not cease to pursue us. Let us imitate the history of the ancient battle, when the sons of promise destroyed their enemies not in one day but successively. For it is difficult to conquer all things at once, unless the charity of God suddenly overshadows the mind. Let us wage war in every way against all the vices that attack us. Today let us conquer gluttony; when it has been rightly laid low, let us overcome sloth; and so let us deal with the rest, that at last we may come out victors over all. The Lord will be present to us as we fight, and God will give victory to his lovers. Amen.

End of the second book of exhortations to monks by Johannes Trithemius, abbot, on the first day of September, 1486.

These two books were printed at Strasbourg by Johann Knoblouch, printer, at the expense of Johann Haselberger of Reichenau, diocese of Constance, in the year of the Lord 1516, on the twenty-fifth day of the month of August.
