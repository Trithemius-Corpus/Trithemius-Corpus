Homily XX. Folio XLIX.

For you who love your own salvation and are zealous for sound obedience, consider whom you are to follow. The abbot who governs the monastery as Christ’s vicar, whether he is good or bad, deserves the obedience of all his subjects not by his own merit, but in Christ’s name. For we understand the saying, “He who hears you hears me, and he who rejects you rejects me,” not only of the apostles, but of every prelate in God’s Church who is appointed guardian and ruler of souls.

Therefore, if you have an abbot who is good, suitable, and holy, listen to his voice as to the command of God’s representative, and be obedient in all things, whatever he commands you. But if his teaching is a rule of life for you, and his holy conduct an example of purer formation, he is God’s friend and the vicar of Jesus Christ: for the one reason he deserves to be loved, and for the other, to be obeyed in everything he commands.

But if your pastor is negligent, ignorant, or certainly less suitable, beware, I beg you, of wishing rashly to examine his actions. Rather, think that your sins deserve to have such a pastor over you. Consider not what he is, but what he is called, and honor the pastor for the love and reverence of him whose vicariate he bears. Remember at all times to show purely and simply to his vicar the obedience that you promised to God; for if the abbot is bad in his conduct, still he does not lose the vicariate of Christ unless he is removed from office. Nor does your pastor’s blameworthy life absolve you from the promised obedience of religion.

The saying of our word must be feared: reprobate monks do not deserve to have a good abbot. This example will not be turned against you, holy brothers in Christ; for I do not believe myself to be a bad pastor, though I cannot deny that I am negligent and ignorant. Nor have we ever admitted any evil suspicion about you, since we seek nothing more than to nourish and enlarge fraternal charity in all things.

Monks often come to us, wandering either with reason or without cause, who very often lay the grounds of their instability upon their abbots. But I, who desire you to be instructed in everything holy and useful, exhort your charity paternally and warn you in Christ not hereafter to lend your ears to those who detract from the character of their pastors and strive to hide their own crimes by denouncing others. It is not the necessary integrity of monastic discipline to turn against the head of the pastor a cause for rebellion and disobedience among subjects.

The true monk does not rashly judge another’s affairs; and one who is a son of humble obedience does not set forth causes of rebellion. But one who yields consent to the passions of the flesh can never be at rest; and one who scrutinizes another’s life has contempt for his own salvation. The monk founded in the love of God does not judge the command of his elder; and one who always beholds Christ as present venerates his pastor as an angel of God.

Audacity makes a monk degenerate, and presumption casts many into ruin. A pernicious and insolent monk has lost the purpose of holiness; and unless he is recalled to his senses by harsh chastisement of the flesh, he has already set his foot toward hell. One who discusses the elder’s command with curious rashness is not obedient; and one who is saddened by an order is not a true disciple of Christ. For true obedience does not murmur, does not delay, is not saddened, does not seek difficulties, does not tarry, does not make excuses, and does not consent to the least sign of irreverence. For love of Christ it always rejoices at the elder’s command, runs to meet it gladly, and obeys cheerfully with haste.

For what else is a monk but a constant combatant, armed with virtues, who always watches in the zealous contest of humility and obedience, and, under a triple battle-line, fights more bravely against himself after conquering? Blessed is that monk who, doing violence to himself, has learned to overcome the will of the flesh and, through continual practice of spiritual warfare, ascends the arduous mountain of true obedience.

Truly blessed is that monk who, despising all the consolations of the world, has placed his treasure in the love of Jesus Christ and desires nothing further among visible things. This is the divinity of monks: to despise honors and pleasures in this world, and to think of nothing else but Christ. O monk three and four times blessed, who, with his own will mortified from the beginning to the very end of life for the love of Christ, perseveres humbly in true obedience.

How great a gift humble obedience is before God, my brothers, no one can sufficiently express, since divine providence has willed to prefer it to all virtues. Obedience is what reconciles the soul to God and, through the shedding of blood, crowns martyrs.
