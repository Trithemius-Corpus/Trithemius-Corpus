On the knowledge of spiritual grace.

Young men, both in years and in monastic conversion, who consent to their own sensual appetites more than is expedient, are mocked by demons in various ways, as if under the appearance of good. They seek sanctity without merit; they desire to have merit without the necessary exercise of virtues. They seem to themselves great and outstanding before God in devotion, yet in their animal passions they are not mortified.

You are in error, O wretched men, and you shamefully deceive yourselves, who think to build the tower of sanctity without the foundation of most humble subjection and obedience. First the disordered desires of sensuality must be mortified; the animal passions must be restrained: anger, murmuring, the spite of indignation, and all impatience. For as long as vices dominate you, whatever gifts of private singularity you offer are not pleasing to God. All things are empty where conscience is not pure.

The way of life of a young monk is suspect to us, however devout it may seem, if the humility of voluntary subjection does not commend it. For a monk without humility and obedience cannot be good, even if by fasting and prayer he hunts for sanctity every day. The devil deceives many in the secrecy of elation and pride, whom he permits continually to keep vigil, fast, and pray. For just as no works without humility and charity are pleasing to God, so the fasts, vigils, prayers, and studies of monks without these are not to be feared by the devil. This does not move the devil, because he is not tormented by it. He does not reckon much the vigils of the self-exalted, nor does he heed the fast of the swollen with pride, nor fear the prayers of the proud.

But if he finds you humble; if he finds you cheerfully obedient in all things to the commands of your elders; if he observes that you do great things and think humble things about yourself; if he weighs that charity toward God and neighbor burns in you, then certainly he is moved against you, because his envy is tormented by your virtues. He turns against you all the weapons of his cunning that he can, and with all his strength he tries to overturn your purpose of holiness. For nothing in a monk displeases demons more, nothing torments them more violently, than if for love of God he has truly humbled himself in all things and has always kept the service of monastic institution under the guard of obedience with a cheerful spirit.

You must beware the promptings of demons, you who think yourselves something great without the merit of true virtues. I see that you are unwilling toward the commands of your elders, impatient and murmuring under correction, quick to rebuke another's life, but exceedingly slow in considering your own defects. These are the surest signs of the promptings of demons, and manifest indications of necessary mortification despised and of self-will. You believe yourselves to be perfect and holy practitioners of great religion and inward devotion, yet you have neither brotherly charity nor true humility, which are necessary in you as monks. You seek sensible devotion in prayers, and with tearful eyes already think yourselves God's friends.

What then? Do tears in prayers make a person holy, when prayer is made with a certain elation of mind and without true humility? God gives tears; the soul struck by adversities gives them; the devil, deceiver of the self-exalted, also gives them. God makes the mind humbled in true charity fruitful, like a meadow, with sweet tears; nature moves one to weep in adversities; the devil pours little streams of waters upon the proud under the likeness of devotion.

Humility is the one virtue which, in the breadth of charity, makes human hearts truly devout. Nothing that you do in your private singularity profits you, O monk, if you do not have true humility together with the fervor of twofold charity. For to rise before others for the nocturnal lauds, to say long prayers in private beyond the custom of the order, to continue a fast beyond the rule's prescription while others are lawfully eating, to remain in seclusion while the rest go out to common labor: two things make such acts meritorious. If they are lacking, all will be altogether fruitless. Indeed, whatever work of a monk charity and humble obedience do not commend will be judged empty of merit before God and will lack reward.

But you who scorn the precepts of your elders with haughty brow, who do not do what they command but what pleases you by your own rashness, who set your own inventions before the common holy exercises of the order, are still far removed from the way of life of the saints. It is not permitted you to abandon by your own rashness the things that belong to monastic institution, and to prefer to necessary things the new and empty things that please you. Whatever work you do is ungrateful to God if you despise that to which you are bound.

The inspiration of divine grace teaches humility, persuades obedience, and follows true charity in all things. It keeps the commandment; it admits no pride; it despises no one, but pursues the perpetual integrity of regular discipline. But the prompting of the devil, under the appearance of good, craftily trips up the unwary monk and makes him subject in all things to his own judgment. For everything that pertains to the preservation of monastic integrity, he falsely says is badly instituted and contrary, and therefore to be set aside in favor of his own pursuits.
