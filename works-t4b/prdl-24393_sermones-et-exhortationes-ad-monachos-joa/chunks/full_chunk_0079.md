Homily XVII.
Fol. XLI.

Since, when we sing psalms, we believe God to be present everywhere, great reverence is needed in prayer, so that our mind may agree in every respect with our voice. Let us therefore consider, brothers, before whom we stand, and to whom, in the sight of the angels, we recite the psalms in prayer; for the monk who, at the time of prayer, admits vain or even destructive thoughts into his mind is like one who, when matters very necessary to him are being discussed with the king, is summoned by his own servant, leaves the king, and turns aside to the servant’s vain and useless conversations. Truly, whoever did this would be judged foolish by everyone, and would rightly seem deserving of the gravest punishments.

But what does the monk deserve who, standing before the highest king of all and speaking with him about those things that concern the salvation of his body, admits harmful or even destructive thoughts into his mind? If one who mocks and despises an earthly king is punished by beheading, what does one deserve who despises the heavenly emperor? Therefore I admonish you, brothers, and with all the sincerity I can I ask that, having purified your hearts, especially at the time of prayer, from the stain of wicked thought, you receive in the mind only what you sing aloud with the mouth.

As the angels always stand before God with fear and the greatest reverence, singing an everlasting hymn of praise, so let us also take care, my brothers, to stand before him with a pure heart, a clean conscience, and most fervent devotion, so that we may sing praises to him much more in spirit than in body. Let us therefore separate our minds from all earthly affairs; let us cleanse our consciences of all enticements of the flesh; let us put on the fervor of the Holy Spirit; and let us drive all sluggishness and idleness far from us. For just as a heavy burden weighs down the back of a slothful man, so drowsiness at the time of psalmody is accustomed to oppress the minds of monks.

Nothing harms monks more than sloth, which little by little weakens all the powers of the mind and extinguishes the whole fervor of the spirit. For as soon as a monk has begun to fall away from the fervor of holy devotion, countless evil thoughts enter the mind; and he can repel them so much the less, the farther he is known to have withdrawn from his former fervor. Hence drowsiness arises in the performance of the prescribed services; the mind is burdened by sadness and melancholy, which the monk, growing lukewarm in spirit, strives with all zeal to relieve not by spiritual but by earthly and fleshly little consolations. And when he advances too long in this alienation of mind, he is hardened, now almost insensible, against the exercise of compunction; and the more he pants after the consolations of the flesh, the farther he wanders from the inward fervor of the spirit.

For what makes you drowsy at the time of psalmody except sloth? For the mind that has fallen into torpor soon makes the body doze. But again I admonish you, brothers, as I have often been accustomed to do, that you especially flee idleness and drowsiness at the time of prayer, since, just as drunkenness brings forgetfulness into the soul, so also drowsiness makes the mind unsound and foolish before God. Wash away this vice of drowsiness, O young men, by which we grieve that some of the elders have long been infected through long habit; among them dozing in choir is familiar, and compunction of mind toward God is exceedingly rare.

Beware, I say, this evil habit of sleeping, which, after it has once led a monk away captive, rarely allows him to be free. Therefore, at the beginning of conversion, young men especially must fight against this most pernicious enemy of religion, as someone said in verse: “Resist at the beginnings; medicine is prepared too late when evils have gained strength through long delays. But for that reason do not put yourself off to future hours; he who is not ready today will be less ready tomorrow.” For it is difficult to cure a sickness that has become old through long duration.

Let us see with how much diligence and attentiveness servants stand before earthly kings and princes, so that we may not be ignorant of how it befits us to stand in prayers before God, who alone is king of all and lord of the universe. The eyes of all look to the lord, and all and each hang most attentively upon his nod, lest anything commanded by him escape those standing by through negligence. From this, then, let us consider with how great purity we ought to stand before the Lord God in the assembly of prayer, day and night, from whom we desire to obtain the kingdom of eternal happiness.
