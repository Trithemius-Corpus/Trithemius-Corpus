On the true humility of monks.

Humility obeys the commands of God with a willing and zealous heart; pride resists the precepts of the Most High. Humility accumulates the merits of everlasting happiness; pride, however, prepares for itself the very heavy torments of hell. Humility grants security at the moment of death; pride brings dreadful fears and calamities to the dying. Humility frees the souls of its servants from every anguish in the fearful judgment of God; pride, however, accuses before that same judge all the wretches whom it has deceived in the world. Humility will place God’s servants at the judge’s right hand; pride will set the damned on the left. Humility will plead the causes of its own and prevail in judgment; pride will hand over its guilty followers to the devil. Humility will repay its friends with the rewards of eternal happiness; pride will render to all its wretches the punishments of perpetual damnation. Humility will be crowned in heaven without end; pride will suffer inexpressible torment with the demons forever among those below. Humility, crowned with Christ, will reign in heaven; pride, condemned with the devil, will be tormented in punishments. Humility, joyful and glorious, will sit forever at the right hand of God; pride, sorrowful and disgraced, will lie without end in the torments of hell.

Then the humble will exult in glory; all the proud, under excessive pain, will cry out forever in punishment. The humble will stand in the sight of God and of all the saints in great joy and everlasting exultation, receiving for the voluntary poverty of this life unfading riches; for lowliness, a kingdom; for sorrow, joy; for the love of God, dominion; for contempt of the world and voluntary chastening of the flesh, heaven. But when the proud and arrogant, set in hell, see the great glory of the humble whom they despised in the world, they will marvel at the sudden change of the saints, and will be troubled with dreadful fear; groaning from the excessive anguish of spirit, the proud will say in their fury: “These are the poor and humble monks, once clothed in vile garments, cast aside by men, abstaining from meats and from every impurity of the flesh, whose habit we once held in derision and as a likeness of death, and whom we counted wretched, without reputation and without honor. Behold how they have been numbered among the sons of God, and their lot is among the saints. Therefore we have strayed from the way of truth; the light of justice has not shone upon us, and the sun of understanding has not risen for us. What now has pride profited us, by which we once despised the humble? And what has the boasting of divine things in the habit brought us in this world? All things have passed away, and because of our stubbornness we have been numbered among the demons, since we always hated humility in the habit, and now suffer inexpressible torments.”

But the humble will live forever; with God is their reward, and their thought is with the Most High. My dearest brothers, always think beforehand that these things will undoubtedly be true in the future; therefore take up humility of mind, flee every pride, and strive to preserve purity, so that you may merit to obtain eternal rewards. Amen.

On the true obedience of monks. Homily 11.

If the humility of monks is true and perfect, it brings forth every fullness of the holy virtues. But if it lacks the splendor of virtue, true humility was never present in the mind. For first of all it brings forth true obedience, and profound subjection in all things for the love of God. For the monk who is truly humble while he journeys in the flesh strives to show himself in all things a disciple of our Lord Jesus Christ; for love of him, content with every lowliness and extremity, obeying his abbot in all things, he rejoices to be subject. Humble obedience for the love of Christ is a great virtue: it will open heaven to the monk and by its strength close hell. For obedience restores the peace of paradise between God and man. True obedience in a monk is this: it is rendered to the commands of superiors not from slavish fear but from the inward affection of charity; not from fear of punishment but from love of justice, persevering even to death and working good in cheerfulness of mind. These are those most beautiful wings of the mind by which a monk flies from earth to heaven: one wing humility spreads, the other the obedience of subjection carries. A monk who has not been adorned with these wings has utterly lost the dignity of his name and merit. He should not be called a monk, but a lying and most deceitful apostate, who, living in the monastery with the others, is without the obedience of humble subjection.

For a monk without obedience is a monster of the devil; and he who resists the commands of his superiors will inherit the punishments of hell. From the beginning the disobedience of the first man destroyed the way of eternal happiness, which the obedience of Jesus Christ restored. For he was made obedient to the Father for us, even unto death, and by his most innocent passion he restored to us the happiness we had lost. Hence the way to salvation is humble obedience, as our holy lawgiver Benedict cries out in the Rule: “Listen, my son, to the precepts of the master, and incline the ear of your heart; willingly receive the admonition of a loving father and carry it out effectively, so that by the labor of obedience you may return to him from whom you had departed by the sloth of disobedience.”

Obedience is the tree of eternal salvation, which grows in the garden of monastic humility. Obedience is the dwelling-place of the Holy Spirit and the quiet tomb of one’s own will. Obedience is the merit of heavenly happiness and the single lesson of monastic discipline. Obedience is the destruction of all arrogance and pride, and the pursuer of all vices. Obedience conquers every malice and extinguishes every disordered pleasure of the flesh. Obedience makes the conscience pure and clean, and preserves true humility in the mind. Obedience is the remedy for the soul’s wound and the foundation of all monastic observance. Obedience is the faithful guardian of all good works and the discipline of a sincere mind. Obedience is the beginning of mortification and the glorious ornament of monks. Obedience is the preserver of peace and concord, without which the vigor of monastic discipline perishes. Obedience always directs the keen edge of the mind toward God; and he who is truly mortified in spirit despises all the consolations of this world. Obedience makes the monk close to God and far removed from every defilement of soul. Obedience is the adornment of the soul and the garment of beauty; without it no one will come to the kingdom of eternal brightness. Obedience is the mother of victory; and whoever knows how, for the honor of God, to be subject in humility will obtain the crown of martyrdom in heaven. Obedience distinguishes the monk from the world; and he who follows it with his whole heart will be great before the Most High. Obedience is the consummation of Christian perfection; in its humble workshop, the more a monk advances, the holier he becomes. Obedience is sure security at the hour of death, for when a monk is about to depart from here, he will fly forth the more securely, the more ready he has been for obedience to commands. Obedience is the rewarder of the humble and the chief emblem of merits. Obedience is the rule of Christian gentleness and the strongest inner nourishment of charity. Obedience is the shelter of chastity and the ornament of most pure innocence. Obedience is the wisdom of monks; and to submit to the commands of prelates is the highest philosophy of cloistered men.

Obedience and humility are the wealth and riches of monks; and to possess continually in the mind the memory of the Lord’s passion is a sweetness greater than all the feasts of the world. Obedience and humility make the monk; but he who is rebellious and proud wears the monastic habit without cause. Obedience in the mind is the voluntary mortification of living members; its merit is of such dignity that a man has nothing greater that may be offered in honor of God. Let each one offer for the love of God what he can: without doubt, he has offered more than all who has slaughtered his own will for God through obedience. It is indeed a great good to distribute one’s resources for Christ’s love to the use of the poor, and to relieve by alms the poverty of the needy. But by as much as the whole outweighs the part, as free choice of the will is nobler than necessity, and as heaven stands apart from earth, by so much more has he offered who, for the love of God and by laying aside his will, has forever denied himself. For whatever temporal substance you have offered to God is not yours but another’s, and is of less value than what is proper to you, both in esteem and in reward. You have one thing proper to yourself which, offered to God, is pleasing above all things. What is this, if not free will, which the almighty Creator placed in your power? A good will is greater than all riches, for nothing richer can be offered to God, nothing will be judged more acceptable.

This most precious gift, my brothers, you once offered to God when, denying yourselves and all worldly things for Christ’s honor, you put on the monastic habit.
