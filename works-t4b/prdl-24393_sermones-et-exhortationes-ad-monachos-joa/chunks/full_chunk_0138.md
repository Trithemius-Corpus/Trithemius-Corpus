How Sin Is to Be Fled.

He does not imitate God, but the devil, the first author of sin. Let us love God, and sin will not have dominion in us; let us love Christ, and we shall do nothing contrary to our salvation. Nothing is better, nothing stronger against sin, nothing safer, than to love God, who first loved us and washed us from all sins in the blood of his only-begotten Son.

For we are bound to God, and we are obliged to love him with all the strength of our heart for many reasons. First, God is to be loved supremely by us for no other cause than for himself, who is the highest and only good; in him alone is rest and salvation for the soul, and without him there is no salvation, no rest, no blessedness, no happiness, either in heaven or on earth. For since God is the cause, maker, and beginning of all things, he alone is the object of the soul's blessedness; eternal enjoyment of him is reached by charity alone, and one comes to love of him by purity of mind alone. Therefore, since God is the soul's one and highest good, he is to be loved by us for his own sake with all our strength. Outside God there is nothing good, nothing holy, nothing by which the soul can be gladdened forever. For God alone is the center of the soul's blessedness.

Second, we are commanded to love the Lord our God because he deigns to show himself to us as a most loving father. For thus he taught us to pray: "Our Father, who are in heaven, hallowed be your name." Truly God is the father of us all: he created us all, nourishes us, feeds us, and preserves us in being lest we perish. There has never been any father on earth so indulgent that he loved his only-begotten son with as much charity as God almighty, our Father, loves, cherishes, directs, and attends us with fatherly affection. And therefore we are bound to love above all things, with a pure heart, him who gives us both being and life. For he is our Father; and Adam, who was created first of all human beings, did not know us.

Third, we ought to love God above all things because of the likeness: man was made in the image of God, following his Creator by a certain likeness in intellect. God, however, is perfect, infinite, and everlasting intellect; by participating in his goodness through grace, our intellect is made blessed in him who created all things. For although there is no comparison between God and man, nevertheless the rational soul itself has, from the nobility of its created condition, a likeness to its own principle that is of no contemptible dignity. For the prophet says: "I have said: you are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High." The Lord God also said in the beginning: "Let us make man in our image and likeness." Great is the condescension of divine kindness toward our race, and we ought to remember it at all times with a pure mind in the observance of charity.

Fourth, we ought to love our God above all things because of his inestimable munificence, when we recognize that he has bestowed so many benefits upon us, who did not deserve them. When we did not exist, he first established us in being by his goodness, and ordained us for a share in his glory, which will never end. Thus Scripture says of man's nobility: "You made him a little lower than the angels; with glory and honor you crowned him, and you set him over the works of your hands." Do you wish to know, brothers, in what respect man is less than an angel? Attend carefully to what we say.

There are two intellects created from the beginning, the angelic and the human. The angelic intellect, immediately from the beginning of its creation, was made full and perfect in the actuality of all knowable things, and it knew and comprehended by nature all that it ought to know. But the human intellect first exists in potency toward all knowable things, ignorant of all things; in the end, however, through many actions, it finally acquires knowledge of all things, like the angelic intellect. For the perfect consummation of nature which belongs to the angelic intellect from the beginning will be granted to the human intellect at the end. Hence between these two created intellects there is plainly only this difference: the angelic intellect is what it can be, while the human intellect is to receive, at the end, that is, after the death of the flesh, the likeness of angelic perfection.

For the angelic intellect, which was created first, was created without any action of its own, immovably, substantially, and by nature, full and perfect in knowledge of all things. Those angels who adhered by love to their principle, namely God, have been confirmed in eternal happiness; but those who, glorying in themselves, withdrew in affection from God are perpetually miserable and unhappy. The human intellect, therefore, because it was created at the beginning in ignorance and privation of all things, must necessarily come at last, by action, motion, and art, to the knowledge of things set before it.

As long as we are in the body and are pilgrims away from the Lord, our intellect, because of its excessive commerce with the body, is confused as though placed in the darkness of ignorance, and is by no means illumined to perfect knowledge of future things. But after the soul has been separated from the body, then the intellect will rejoice in angelic perfection. Therefore we are distant from the angels only in proportion as we share in bodily impurity.
