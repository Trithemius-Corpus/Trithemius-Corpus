*Concerning monks sent on the road.*

As the man to whom the Lord speaks, when he wakes from sleep, remembers nothing of what he had dreamed, so too the monastic canon commands. Therefore no one should presume to report any other things he has seen or heard outside the monastery, for this is a very great distraction. If anyone presumes to do so, let him undergo regular punishment. The same applies to anyone who presumes to go out beyond the cloisters of the monastery, or to go anywhere, or to do anything, however small, without the abbot's command.

Since we have voluntarily undertaken this rule of life and have sworn by his words, it is not lawful for any of us to turn back or to attempt anything to the contrary. What he has seen or heard outside, let him leave outside when he returns from the road, and not bring novelty in among the brothers, lest he produce various distractions of mind. He who keeps the commands of the Lord's rule is a lover of discipline. The man who always multiplies words and walks on the road speaking vocabulary after vocabulary is [unclear]. But the humble brother, speaking with God in the hidden silence of the heart, likewise walks rejoicing in his simplicity. Whatever the father's ordinance has commanded, he considers most diligently; and wherever he is, he carefully preserves everything.

The monk who is sent on the road, if he is preserved pure and keeps himself from every stain of fault, returns joyful to the monastery, and, as though he had escaped the danger of the sea, hastens with longing to the harbor of rest. But he who has lost shame, having become insolent, returns sad and deserving punishment, as though to a prison.

The true monk of Christ the Lord neither seeks nor loves the friendship of secular people, but despises it; but those who multiply familiar relations with people unlike themselves gradually fall away from their purpose of holiness. Cursed is the cloistered man who puts his confidence in human beings and withdraws his heart from the Lord God. Do not seek friendships with men, but take care to obtain the fellowship of the invisible angels. Frequent friendships with mortals do not suit a monk; through them the vigor of the mind is loosened and empty occupations are introduced. For just as dripping water over a long time hollows out stone, however hard and solid, so too excessive familiarity with secular people gradually softens monastic rigor. Have charity toward all, but familiarity with few.

He who has God as his friend easily disdains human familiarity; and he who speaks with angels takes very little delight in the conversation of mortals. Whenever on the road you see a sign of the Lord's Passion or of the Crucified One, do not pass by unless you say: I give you thanks, Lord Jesus Christ, for the pain of your wounds, for the shedding of your most holy blood, and for your most innocent death, which you endured for us on the altar of the cross. I beseech you, by your bitter Passion, have mercy on me now and in the dreadful hour of my death. Repeat this most blessed prayer often as you go, and never pass an image of the Crucified One unless you have given thanks to the Savior.

Do not quarrel with the one who walks with you on the road, lest, when he has returned to the monastery, he remember some evil thing against you. He who provokes his companion on a journey to impatience is like one who drives a rabid dog to bark by striking it. The wicked man is overcome by gentleness, and the humble of heart calms one stirred up in mind. Among secular people do not pour out speech, lest perhaps they take occasion for mockery against you.

We permit good things to be spoken of [unclear] among young men; but let the one who is lower in rank listen to him. The weary ploughman hastens to rest; and the monk who is sound in mind returns with longing to his cell, hastening by a direct way. Let the word be weighed in the mouth until reason brings forth a judgment with equity; and speech that can instruct its hearers should not be hidden.

Do not trust the songs of flatterers, for [unclear] turns the innocent man away, and they soothe his ears with disordered speeches. A monk who is fed on contentions is like [unclear], and like a spasmodic man who stirs up quarrels under the name of religion. Do not presume to speak before the learned, lest perhaps, when you do not understand the parable, you be laughed at by those standing around. The monk who recognizes himself as such does not use profound speech; and the one who teaches little ones makes questions plain.

When you go to people unlike your profession, do not boast in anything; but if you are questioned, answer humbly in few words.
