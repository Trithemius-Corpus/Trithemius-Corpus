Sermon V. Folio LXIX.

Sixth, you will draw your mind back from sin very strongly if you keep it always occupied, as a matter of habit, in meditation on the Lord’s passion. For who will be able to devote himself to the pleasures and works of the flesh, if he considers that Jesus Christ, ruler of the world, suffered on the cross for him? Christ, by his cross, freed the world from perpetual death; whoever has fixed so great a benefit in his memory will easily abstain from every wicked deed. Finally, if anyone has understood and considered with his whole inner mind that God is present to him in every place, knowing and understanding all things, he will without doubt always abhor every sin. And indeed God is everywhere present to us, not only most present, but truly most intimate and most near; no secret is hidden from him, nothing remains concealed from him, but everything is present and manifest to him. He searches the depths of all things; he knows the most secret thoughts; he alone has true knowledge of all things. What mortal, in the presence of so great a majesty, which he believes sees all things, would dare without the greatest shame to be anything other than he judges that he ought to appear? Therefore, whenever some troublesome thought disturbs the mind, whenever disordered movement of the flesh unsettles the soul, think that the Lord God, who is the avenger of evils, is present everywhere; recall to memory the bitterness of the passion of Jesus Christ; fill your soul with the sweetness of heavenly grace. Harder than stone is the man whom these things, diligently considered, cannot call back from readiness to sin.

The seventh thing that recalls the mind of a holy monk from the desire of sin is the continual reading of the passions, deeds, and martyrdoms of the saints. For truly, whoever often calls to memory the unconquerable constancy of Christ’s holy martyrs cannot fail to become readier and stronger for resistance. Therefore the brave deeds of the saints were once committed to writing so that we, weak men, reading them, might rise up to imitate those who have gone before. Let the brethren not regard those who, with proud rashness, deny faith to the histories of the saints and think the glorious things narrated about Christ’s most blessed friends to be fables. For the virtues and good things that they do not find in themselves, they refuse to believe that God’s saints possessed. Indeed, many, and especially those among them who judge themselves great because of their learning, rashly reject the histories of the holy fathers; and since they themselves cannot enter upon the hard road of virtues, they claim with reckless loquacity that no one, or only very few, could do so. Whatever they read about the lives of the saints as arduous or great, they cry out that it is invented and fabulous. As they themselves live, placed in the darkness of vices, so they suppose those histories which they mock to have proceeded. But a prudent monk and lover of honor is not moved by the sloth of the proud, since he well understands how great an efficacy for exhorting holiness lies within history.

Meanwhile, it is clear enough that, if I may say so, I am moved by the devoted affection of holy fervor toward one thing: that, amid the many writings of the curious, the deeds of the saints should be read. For, as most holy Pope Leo says, examples are stronger than words, and it is more to teach by deed than by voice. But just as not all things please everyone, so not all things suit everyone. Each person should be advised frequently and diligently to read that by whose reading his mind is stirred to greater progress.

The eighth thing that most strongly draws a man back from sin is charity, concerning which the holy apostle says: Charity is patient, is kind; it does not act perversely; it is not puffed up; it is not ambitious; it does not seek what is its own; it is not provoked; it does not think evil; it does not rejoice over iniquity, but rejoices with the truth; it bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Charity never fails. Whoever loves God flees sin; but whoever is a servant of crimes certainly does not love God. Love of God does not cause a man to sin; and whoever truly loves his Lord and always turns back to him in repentance does not work iniquity. For God is the highest and unchangeable good; for love of him sin is to be fled and detested. For whoever, through fear of long-lasting punishments, is afraid to sin, has not climbed to the summit of charity. A servant does not sin through fear of the blow, but a son withdraws from evil because of perfect love, lest he offend his father’s heart.

Let us flee the snares of sin, my brothers, and with as much purity of soul as we can let us live in the sight of the Lord, because just as literal venom, when taken, kills the body, so sin, when committed, kills the soul. Hence the wise man admonishes us in Ecclesiasticus, saying: Flee sin as from the face of a serpent, for if you come near it, it will seize you. The teeth of a lion are the teeth of sin, killing the souls of men. All iniquity is like a two-edged sword; there is no healing for its wound. In Ezekiel also it is said: The soul that has sinned, that soul shall die. With all solicitude we must always keep watch, lest by meditating on sin we be divided from God.
