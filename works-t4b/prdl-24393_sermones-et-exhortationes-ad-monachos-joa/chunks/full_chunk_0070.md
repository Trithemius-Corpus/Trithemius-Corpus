*Homily XV.*
*Fol. XXXVI.*

[unclear] For what condemned our first parents except the transgression of the commandment? For God’s concern was not with food, but with obedience. For if man had abstained, as almighty God had commanded him, then surely through this obedience he would have merited, in his innocence, the enjoyment of eternal happiness. For it is established by the tradition of the Scriptures that man cannot be saved without the merit of free will, since, although the grace of God works all things in all, nevertheless it will save no one without the cooperation of his own will, except infants baptized in the name of the divine Trinity, and those who cannot have the free use of reason.

For just as free will accomplishes nothing toward salvation without the grace of the Savior, so the grace of God will not save an adult without the merit of a good will. But the first man, placed in paradise, and having transgressed God’s commandment through intemperance, did not have the merit of obedience in a good will; therefore, wretched and unhappy, he was cast out. But the compassion of almighty God determined from eternity to restore him who had fallen through weakness, whom he knew to have been deceived by the envious suggestion of the serpent rather than by his own malice. He assigned this guilt to the passion and death of his only-begotten Son, born in time of the Virgin, so that he who had fallen by his own rashness might be restored to salvation through the obedience of the mediator. For it was impossible for the human race, lost through disobedience, to reach the mercy of the Creator except through the obedience of such a mediator, who would be God and man, in whom there would be both the strength of power and the innocence of nature. For we were blind through ignorance and mute through silence. The mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus, came, sent to enlighten every man coming into this world; he delivered the knowledge of God to mortals, and, by dying for the lost, sealed for the redeemed the greatest cause of love. He instructed the intellect through faith, by which we know God; he moved the affection through his passion, by which we rightly love him above all things.

And so, if we love our Lord and Redeemer with the whole integrity of sincere charity, it is plainly fitting that we chastise our body for love of him. For our abstinence will conduce to merit for us in two respects: namely, when by fasting, through obedience to the statutes of the fathers, we make satisfaction, and when by abstaining through the charity of God we show ourselves grateful to our Redeemer, who endured death for our sake. Abstinence increases merit; commends obedience; supplies wisdom and instruction; purges the affection; enlightens the intellect; grants victory against demons; perfects religion; obtains forgiveness of sins; tames the unlawful motions of the flesh; wins efficacy for prayers; and displays to one’s neighbor an example of virtue. Abstinence purifies the mind, adorns the soul with virtues, and heals the weak little body. He who loves abstinence is wise, and he who loves fasting is a strong ruler. A sober mind is the dwelling-place of wisdom; but he who pursues banquets will die in poverty of soul. It is difficult for a man to become wise who lives his life amid pleasures. Holy nature is content with little, and the health of life is preserved by modest things. Greediness long continued has harmed many, and more have perished by gluttony than by the sword. For habit seems to alter nature, and the longer it has prevailed, the more firmly rooted it will be in any matter.

There is among the Helvetians a man named Nicholas, whose abstinence turns the men of all Germany to admiration; and today, as you have heard, he is named in everyone’s mouth with the title of sanctity. Once a farmer in a certain village of the Helvetians, he was accustomed, in the manner of country people, to pronounce judgment among villagers in disputes. It happened at one time that he could not revoke a decision of his companions which he thought unjust; and, fearing danger to the salvation of his soul, he left his house, wife, children, friends, worldly affairs, and possessions, and withdrew alone into a nearby wood for the love of God. There he lives a solitary life. And behold, today is the twentieth year since he entered the solitude, without having taken human food. I speak of things known to all, nor do I think there is any man living in Germany who has not heard the report of this miracle. And lest perhaps in future anyone believe that we have been deceived by pretense, we cite as witnesses the Helvetians, together with the prelate of the church of Constance, who observe his ways and life with the greatest care.
