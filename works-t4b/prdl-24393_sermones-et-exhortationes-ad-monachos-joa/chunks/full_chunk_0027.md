Hence Jerome again says: “Let prayer frequently interrupt reading, and let a pleasing alternation of holy action come to the soul that is continually intent.” A monk who does not love the privacy of the cell is not a true soldier of Christ, because he bears the name and habit of a monk without the works; and he who ought to have been solitary for the love of God has turned back in mind to the world and loves the pursuit of vanity. Hence the divine Pope Gregory says: “He who lives bodily removed from human beings, but plunges himself in thought into the tumults of human dealings through desire for earthly things, is not solitary, although he dwells in solitude. But if someone is bodily pressed by crowds of people, and yet suffers no tumults of worldly cares in his heart, he is not in the city, even if he lives in the midst of human beings.”

And this is what holy Jerome says against Jovinian: “The wise man,” he says, “can never be alone. For he has with him all who are and have been good; and with a free mind he brings forth whatever he wishes and transports himself wherever he wishes. What he cannot do in body, he embraces in mind; and if there is a lack of human beings, he speaks with God. Therefore a good man will never be alone.” And so, while remaining in your cell, always beware idleness, because nothing so enervates all the powers of the mind as idleness, after it has produced acedia from itself. If you love God ardently, you will never be idle, because true love of Christ does not allow sluggishness to arise in the soul, but always impels the lover toward the pleasure of the beloved friend. And just as a certain carnally minded person truly said, “Let him who does not wish to become idle love,” so experience has taught us in spiritual love that it does not permit the mind which it has begun to inhabit to grow sluggish with idleness and laziness. For divine love always impels one toward better things; and the more it advances by burning, the more it takes on increase of itself.

The workshop of this love is a clean conscience. If a monk desires to have it and to keep it clean, it is necessary first of all that he despise the vanities of the world and the business of temporal things. For it is not possible for twin loves to reside in one breast, nor can a person prevail in loving Christ who is unwilling to despise the world. For God, who in the Trinity of persons is one in the essence of substance, is either loved by us alone, or is not loved with a partner. Therefore, so that the conscience may remain clean in all purity, and so that the mind may be able to love God above all things, let the monk love the privacy of the cell and never withdraw from the exercise of inner sweetness. For in the cell of a good and wise monk there is the greatest tranquility; and if there is any earthly paradise in a monastery, it belongs to those who live according to the purity of the cenobitic institution. There are peace, piety, goodness, light, virtue, and honor. There are joy, purity, sweetness, and great tranquility of life, where the devout monk at prayer speaks with the Lord and the sweetness of the future heavenly kingdom is foretasted by the contemplative soldier.

O cell of monks truly serving God, paradise: you are surely the little garden of most honorable pleasure, in which the mind is joined to God through love and sweetly lifted up through desire. The monk who loves, venerates, and cultivates you is a true soldier of Christ and serves the Lord in the fullness of virtues. But he who abhors and deserts the privacy of the cell despises the workshop of his own salvation. Therefore, my dearest brothers, I beg you to begin to inhabit the privacy of the cell with desire of mind; without love for it no one will be able to attain the perfection of virtues in the cenobium.

On the Manual Labor of Monks. Homily VII.

“Because you will eat the labors of your hands,” says the prophet of the Lord, “blessed are you, and it will be well with you.” If all who live from the labor of their own hands are counted blessed, surely no one eats bread idly without guilt and crime. For almighty God imposed this penalty on Adam, the first-formed, for the transgression of the original commandment, declaring to him that in the sweat of his face he would obtain food from the earth by working with his hands. And since in Adam we have all sinned, we are necessarily subject to the same penalties to which he is known to have been subjected.
