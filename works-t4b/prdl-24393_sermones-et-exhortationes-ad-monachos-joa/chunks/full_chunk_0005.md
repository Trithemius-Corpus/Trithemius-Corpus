*All things. I.*
*Folio IIII.*

...that you also carefully keep the monastic ordinances which you promised. “Make vows,” says Scripture, “and fulfill them.” And again another Scripture says: “If you have vowed anything to God, do not delay to fulfill it.” For an unfaithful and foolish promise displeases him, and it is much better not to vow than, after a vow, not to fulfill what was promised. And Saint Augustine admonishes us how necessary it is to fulfill promises made to God, saying: There are certain things which we owe to the Lord even without vowing them. But after we promise them to the Lord, we are necessarily constrained to fulfill them.

But now, my brothers, I ask you carefully to consider what you have vowed to the Lord your God, and diligently to weigh under what necessity of fulfilling your promises you have been placed. For you vowed to your God, not to a man, obedience and the conversion of your morals according to the Rule of our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and perpetual stability in this order of religion. The obedience which you promised to God you owe to his vicar, a man, whom from the Rule you profess you have learned to hold the place of Christ in the monastery. But what the conversion of morals ought to be according to the Rule is plainly seen in the fourth chapter, headed “On the Instruments of Good Works,” in which you do not fail to recognize the very pattern of all monastic perfection.

Indeed, all of you have heard what our divine father commanded to be done concerning novices, and you know that this was done in the case of each one of you at the beginning of his profession. For thus he says: “The one to be received, in the oratory before all, shall promise concerning his stability, the conversion of his morals, and obedience, before God and his saints, so that, if he should ever act otherwise, he may know that he is to be condemned by God, whom he mocks.” Consider now, therefore, brothers, under how great a necessity of regular observance you have been placed, since unless by deed you fulfill what you promised when you received the monastic habit, you will have to be judged before the almighty Lord not as servants of God, not as friends, not as monks, but as mockers.

Therefore always be mindful of the things which the Rule commands you, according to which you promised to form your morals; and labor like true soldiers of Jesus Christ, constantly keeping watch in the fear of the Lord, since, as Scripture says, “unless you hold yourselves steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown.” Consider your vocation: because the Lord has called you not to idleness, not to the pleasures of this world, nor to pursuing the desires of the flesh, but rather to continual labor, to abstinence from all delights, and to the constant mortification of the flesh under the image of the Crucified, so that you may bear his marks in your hearts.

Certainly you have now put your hands to the plow of holy heavenly agriculture, in which you are commanded to sweat in continual labor, and not only never to look back, but, by always advancing from virtue to virtue, to pant toward the summit of highest perfection. For to live as a monk is nothing other than for a man to withdraw from fellowship and, in solitude, actually to please God alone through the desire of most fervent love. Hence Scripture says: “It is good for a man when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he shall sit solitary and be silent, because he shall raise himself above himself.”

Therefore, if you wish to be perfect, you who by regular institution are bound to love perfection, begin from the way of life which Christ taught; and you who have already despised all things for love of him, do not neglect humbly to conquer, overcome, and despise even yourselves. The servant of Christ has nothing except Christ, loves nothing, cares for nothing, seeks nothing except Christ, so that he may be perfect and pleasing to the one whose soldiery he has voluntarily professed. But the monk who loves anything besides Christ cannot be perfect, since true love is not divided, nor is the mind truly nourished by diverse and contrary things.

For this reason the Lord says: “If you wish to be perfect, go and sell all that you have and give to the poor; and come, follow me.” You indeed, most beloved brothers, as I trust, have already left the world and whatever you had in the world; and not only all the worldly possessions which you owned and could have had, but you have also laid aside every desire of having. And you have come to this monastery of the divine bishop Martin, in which the poverty of temporal goods is great, and this alone has been given to us in place of riches: not to find occasions for avarice. But just as punishment does not make a martyr, but the cause does, so want does not make a poor man blessed, but voluntary poverty for the love of Christ
