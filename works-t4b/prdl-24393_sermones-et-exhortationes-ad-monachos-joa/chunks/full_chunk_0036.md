For the Lord is the hearer of a righteous prayer when integrity of life commends the prayer, and no guilt of conscience makes the one praying hesitate. Friendship will not stand between dissimilar persons, and there can be no communion between God and a sinner. If you wish to be heard when you pray, take care first that you do not disagree with the will of God. Live in spirit very like to God, and you will suffer no rejection in prayer.

Indeed, purity of conscience in the fervor of divine love makes a human being very like to God in understanding. Likeness causes love; love introduces familiarity; familiarity brings confidence; and confidence in petition readily obtains its request. For just as there is no union between things unlike, so prayer profits nothing between God and an evil man. Therefore whoever among you desires to gain the effect of his prayers should first cleanse the dwelling of his heart from every defilement through true repentance; then he should strive to inflame himself by the exercise of holy compunction; afterward he should continually feed his mind in the practice of the love of God, so that he may never thereafter interrupt the good that he has begun.

But after, with the desire of divine love always increasing in him, he has by long practice brought the exercise of holy purity into habit, and the mind has acquired the necessary firmness in a good purpose, then at last the likeness of divine communion is gradually introduced, and prayer is shaped into its proper state. For the more the mind, stirred in the love of God, advances, the sooner prayer will obtain from the Lord what it asks. For only the love of God makes prayer powerful; and the more fervently the mind burns in it, the more easily it will gain the effect of its petitions. For if you love much, you can do much. God is charity, and he who remains in charity remains in God, and God in him. But he in whom God remains has nothing stronger than himself, except God alone.

There are among you some weak and feeble in spirit who already consider themselves strong and perfect in the way of the Lord. Their devotion is rare and bound to the hour; they boast that they have approached the kingdom of heaven without the wedding garment, although they still lie far below. To these I say again what I have often said: no one suddenly becomes the highest; and unless a monk has subdued all his animal passions by mortifications of the flesh, he does not reach the lofty mountain of divine contemplation. For whoever, placed in a monastery under authority and obedience, follows his own will contrary to monastic institutions is not worthy to be heard by the Lord in his prayers.

Those of whom we speak are great and wise in their own eyes. Loving their own will, they murmur at everything, and whatever they themselves have not arranged they think badly done. They examine the actions of their brothers with curious inquiry, but neglect and despise the discernment of their own negligences. In their conduct they are loose and disorderly, in speech frivolous and noisy, unwilling for obedience, and always slow and full of sloth in good works. They neglect the common exercises of the order and give themselves to private ones according to their own will. And as long as they remain in their own rashness, they do not rise up to the true love of God.

Let monks who love their own will hear what the Lord says: ‘I have not come to do my own will, but the will of the Father who sent me.’ Brothers, attend to this teacher; unless you live in imitation of him, you will pray without fruit. For the Lord gladly hears the prayer of the one who keeps his commandments in truth. But the one who transgresses God’s commandment, and follows with desire the pleasures of the flesh and the comforts of the world, his prayer is not heard. For God, just and upright, is believed to hear your prayer gladly when mind and good life pray together. But if someone’s way of life displeases God, how will his prayer please him? For God is spirit, and the man who walks not in the spirit but in the flesh certainly does not draw near to God.

Therefore you, monk, who do not walk according to the purity of the Gospel and the Rule, who love your own will and not God’s, know that you will not feel the efficacy of your prayers unless you first amend your life. For unless the conduct of your life agrees with your prayer, you will not obtain what you ask. Thus you cry to the Lord with a loud voice when, even with the tongue silent, you cry out by good works. For good prayers and an evil way of life merit nothing good. Therefore do what a certain wise man advised: if you have sinned, do not add to it again, but pray also concerning your former sins, that they may be forgiven you. Prepare your soul before prayer, and do not be like a man who tempts God.

Let the mind be pure and burning in the love of the Lord; let the conscience be clean and strengthened in the purpose of holy conduct, and prayer will be pleasing to divine ears. If you pray otherwise, you will obtain nothing. He prays well who lives well, and he who prays with holy works obtains easily. Finally, prayer is made very efficacious and pleasing in the sight of God if at all times it is consonant with the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. For Christ is God and man, the mediator between God and us, through whom we have secure access to the Father, as he himself bears witness in the holy Gospel: ‘No one comes to the Father except through me.’

Therefore let no day pass on which, in prayer, you do not give thanks to Christ at least once: he healed the illnesses of your soul by the pain of his wounds; he poured out his blood for you; and by his most innocent death he redeemed you, wretched as you were, from the destruction of eternal death. A man is certainly most ungrateful and unworthy of the name Christian if he is so forgetful of such great benefits of Christ that he does not give him thanks daily, at least once. But the monk, who is commanded to be dead to the world and to live for God alone, should continually carry the memory of the Lord’s Passion in his breast, always giving thanks to his Redeemer, always blessing, always adoring, and daily, indeed as often as possible at every moment, turning over with tears and compassion the bitterness of Christ’s wounds.

Every day, after each of the regular prayers which the Greeks call canonical, with knees bent and mind lifted to heaven, let him say: I give you thanks, and blessing you I adore you, O mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus, sweetest God and man: for your most holy incarnation, for your birth from the Virgin, for your circumcision, for the flight into Egypt and the seven years in exile that you endured for us. I adore you, Lord Savior Jesus Christ, for your labors, for hunger and thirst, cold and nakedness; for your journeys, for your teaching and preaching, for your miracles, for the calling of the nations, and for the justification of sinners. I give you thanks, Lord, Son of the living God, for the pain of your wounds; I bless you for the outpouring of your most holy blood; I adore you for the death that you endured most innocently; and I beg you, through all your Passion, mercy, and clemency, have mercy on me now and in the hour of my death.

A devout monk should recite this very brief prayer at least seven times a day, and whenever he passes before an image of the Crucified, he should adore Christ in the same way, with fervor of mind. Whoever is a true lover of Christ always thinks of Christ, speaks of Christ, and venerates Christ; he does not doubt that Christ is everywhere present to him. The Passion of Christ is never absent from his memory, and because of the greatness of love he forgets himself and is wholly occupied in Christ in mind and desire. Whoever burns in spirit with the love of Christ despises earthly things, conquers the flesh, and desires only heavenly things. Awake, he always thinks of Christ with desire; asleep, he dreams of Christ; and sighing continually, he cries out with Saint Ignatius: ‘My love has been crucified.’ He joins sigh to sigh, and Jesus, whom he loves with his whole heart, he sees present not with the eyes but with the spirit.

When the desire of love presses upon him, as often as he looks upon the Crucified, he is at once dissolved into tears, and, burning with the fire of charity, cries aloud: O love stronger than all strengths, how sweetly he burns who is inflamed with desire for Christ. He spoke rightly who said, ‘Love is strong as death.’ For what can be stronger than the love of Christ, by whose ardor the holy martyrs, despising so many thousands of kinds of death, overcame them? O my crucified love, have mercy on me; grant me to feel that sweetness of your love of which I speak, so that I may always carry your most bitter Passion in my heart.

I know, my brothers, I know that there is no more efficacious manner of praying, nor one more useful for us who are still weak, than in the memory and power of the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore, since it is clear that without a mediator there is no ascent of the mind to God, every good work must be begun in the name of Christ, because then we pour out prayers to God profitably, when with a purified conscience we pray through his only-begotten Son.
