*On the True Penitence of Monks.*

... let us invoke him with supplications. Let us preserve charity inviolate in all things, because it is charity that covers a multitude of sins. Each day the good soldier of Christ, who desires to be faithful to his Lord, should examine his conscience by discussion at least once, and consider more carefully how much on that day he has advanced or fallen back in holy desire. Whenever he finds that he has sinned in anything, let him at once do penance and with tears ask mercy from the Lord. Let him not wait for the doubtful morrow: salvation is for today. He who can immediately find pardon should by no means introduce delay. Let him at once grieve in his heart that he has sinned against the Lord; let him also renew his holy purpose with new fervor; and let him judge that he has utterly lost that day on which he has not become better by making progress.

Prayer should always be on the monk's lips; continual compunction should be in his heart; tears of devotion should continually flow from his eyes; and he should never withdraw from the inward exercises of love or from their remembrance. Let the very bitter remembrance of the Lord's Passion often be in the mind of the cloistered man; and let all the prayers that he makes to the Father be brought forward in the power of the blood and wounds of our Lord Jesus Christ. Let there always be on his lips words that show the sign of a compunct mind: either let prayer be offered to God, or let meditation on his words be renewed. Let the devout monk turn away from evil and do the good that he can, with cheerfulness and with the discretion of the Spirit of God. For, as has been said, to do penance it is not enough to change one's conduct for the better and withdraw from evil action, unless one also strives to adorn his conscience with good works.

Therefore, my brothers, as you know, four things are required for true penitence. The first is true contrition for sins, which is sorrow of heart voluntarily assumed in detestation of the evils that one has committed. Of this Scripture says: “A contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.” And again: “Those who sow in tears shall reap in exultation.” For every sorrow of penitence is sorrow of contrition; the greater it is, the more fruitful penitence will be. True contrition is that which both sorrow of mind commends and continual cleansing by tears attests. Great is the usefulness of tears, because there is no sin so grave that contrition, joined with tears, does not wipe it away.

Hence the holy doctor Augustine says: “O humble tear, how powerful and virtuous you are, because yours is the kingdom, yours is the power. You do not fear the gaze of the judge; you impose silence on accusing enemies. Alone you enter before the king, but alone you do not depart; you conquer the unconquerable; you bind the omnipotent.” He likewise says: “The holier anyone is, and the fuller of holy desire, the more abundant is his weeping in prayer.” True compunction has sorrow; sorrow is shown by tears; and penitence is adorned by these as by certain ornaments. True compunction makes the soul shudder at sins; and when it has begun from fear, increased by its own advances, it gradually rises into love. Great is its virtue and truly admirable its power; before it every enormity of sins soon yields. Whoever true compunction of heart has made to supplicate, the merciful Lord soon hears him.

O happy tears, saving affliction and truly sweet bitterness, which the devil fears, through which the compunct soul rises to the mercy of God. For, as the holy pope Gregory says, the more richly guilt falls away, the higher the knowledge of truth is attained, because, for seeing the inward light, the conscience stained by former fault is renewed when baptized with tears. The second thing that pertains to the truth of penitence is a firm purpose of amendment, because whoever has the word of confession in his mouth and does not have the purpose of amendment in his heart is either deceitful or vain. But whoever has it in his heart and not in his mouth is either proud or timid, and in no way fit for true penitence. For Scripture says: “With the heart one believes unto righteousness, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.” This judgment, lovers of Christ, must be understood principally of faith in our Lord Jesus Christ; nevertheless it seems applicable to true penitence. For without confession of the mouth, contrition of heart alone is not enough for penitence, especially from that time when the statutes of the Church decreed confession necessary for salvation. The third, therefore, is confession of the mouth, necessary for the truth of penitence; without it neither contrition nor the purpose of future amendment is enough. But what this saving confession ought to be, I know that none of you can be ignorant.
