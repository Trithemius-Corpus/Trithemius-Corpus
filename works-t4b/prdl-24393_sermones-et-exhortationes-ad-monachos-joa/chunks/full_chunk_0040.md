Homily IX. Fol. XXI.

...of necessity. You have heard in these words the dread sentence of the Lord, and you are not ignorant of the command of our holy lawgiver in the Rule: “As for scurrilities and idle words, or words that provoke laughter, we condemn them to perpetual exclusion in every place, and we do not permit the disciple to open his mouth for such speech.”

Here the holy father Benedict forbids monks three kinds of words, and shuts them out everywhere by perpetual exclusion: namely scurrilous words, idle words, and words that provoke laughter. What “scurrility” is, however, is made clear to us by grammatical interpretation. For scurrility is the opposite of urbanity and modesty; it is a kind of wanton jesting that provokes laughter, an immoderate amusement in words and gestures without reverence or propriety, not in accord with reason, nor befitting a virtuous and good man, nor becoming to the wise. It is called scurrility from the scurra, the buffoon, who frequents the houses of the wealthy and, by wanton and dishonorable words and gestures, provokes his hearers to laughter for a reward or small coin.

This kind of speech the holy father Benedict forbids to monks and has utterly condemned, because those who serve God ought in his sight always to abstain from shameful and dishonorable words. Between scurrility, idle speech, and much speaking there seems to be this distinction: the first sins by dishonorable jesting, the second by lack of usefulness, the third by excess. The first is shameful and dishonorable; the second is superfluous and without use. Foolish speech is rightly joined to these, since it is forbidden not only to monks but also to all Christians. Hence the holy apostle of Christ says: “But fornication and all uncleanness or covetousness, let it not even be named among you, as befits saints; nor filthiness, nor foolish talk, nor scurrility, which has no purpose, but rather thanksgiving.”

Likewise every idle word is forbidden to all monks, because, as you have heard, it is prohibited by the Lord in the holy Gospel. Words that provoke laughter are also not becoming to Christian men, and much less do they befit cloistered monks. For the monk, who is dead to the world, is commanded always to think upon his last things; and for the love of God he is ordered everywhere to despise laughter and every insolence of the present life. Laughter is the outward loosening of the mouth that reveals an immoderate inward gladness of the mind. But nothing that exceeds modesty and temperance is permitted to monks, whose life and conversation does not follow flesh and blood, but is compared to angelic purity.

Untimely laughter, poured forth with immoderate levity, is a sign of an undisciplined mind and shows a man forgetful of moderate propriety and modesty. But the monk, who is commanded to imitate Christ and to renounce the world on the condition that he may live in greater purity, must beware of all things that overthrow the manly steadiness of the mind. He must despise all the vain joys of this world for desire of the heavenly homeland, and keep himself always unconquered in every maturity of mind and body. And just as immoderate laughter is forbidden to monks in every circumstance and place, so too they are forbidden to speak words that provoke laughter in their hearers.

Therefore now, dearest brothers, attend to yourselves, and carefully consider how you ought to restrain your tongues. Recall often to memory what the prophet says in the psalm: “I said, I will guard my ways, so that I may not sin with my tongue. I set a guard upon my mouth; I was mute and humbled, and kept silence even from good things.” Recognize that in every place you stand before the Lord God, who is present everywhere, who sees all things, hears all things, knows all things, understands all things, and from whose knowledge nothing is hidden; to whose exact examination all things are reserved. Live in his sight in such a way that you may be able to come securely to his judgment.

Whenever, with our permission or that of the prior, you come together for conversation, take the greatest care, according to the command of our lawgiver, to avoid scurrilous words, idle words, and words that stir up laughter. Let your speech be full of modesty: not loud, not frivolous, not rash, not contentious, lest exact accounts be demanded for them. Likewise flee much speaking, because it is not without sin and makes a monk wearisome to all people. Let the seniors speak, not just anything whatever, but words of saving instruction, by which they may instruct the minds of hearers for the seed of righteousness and kindle them more fully to the love of God. For our holy lawgiver says: “It befits the master to speak and teach, and the disciple to be silent and listen.” And therefore let the seniors speak useful things and teach; let the juniors, however, listen in silence and learn.

And if anything must be asked of the prior, let it be asked with all humility, reverence, and subjection, lest anyone seem to speak more than is fitting. A man is known from his words, and he who multiplies speeches in his mouth is foolish in heart. A man’s speech is the witness of his conscience; and whoever loves modesty and innocence of heart ought to have modest words, free from vanity. For you are ministers of the living God, my brothers. Let no vain, useless, or idle speech go out of your mouth, but only what is good for the edification of hearers, so that it may increase charity in their minds and enlighten the heart in the knowledge and love of the highest truth.

Precious is the tongue of a monk that says nothing at all except what is holy; and worthy of blessing is the mouth from which nothing proceeds except what is divine. Let each person keep his heart clean from every wicked thought, because one who is good in heart does not speak an evil word; and one who truly loves God does not utter idle speech. Apply all care and consideration to your thoughts, because evil must chiefly be watched there where it takes the beginning of its progress, so that as soon as it has begun to arise it may be cut off at the root. For there is no thought of evil so weak that it will not grow with time, if it is nourished by the consent of the thinker.

Therefore evil must be conquered immediately at the beginning, while it is still weak, because when delay intervenes, evil, however small it seemed in the beginning, gains strength. A great fire often arises from a small spark, and from a little thought a great sin sometimes rises up. When thought gives birth to delight, it soon tends to break out into consent, and afterward, if it can, to cast itself headlong even into deed. But when the will has given consent through delight to an evil thought for action, even if what the soul desires is not accomplished, the sin is already consummated before God. But what does Scripture say? “When sin has been consummated, it brings forth death.”

For almighty God looks upon the will of a human being, whether he wills good or evil. Therefore in the holy Gospel he says: “Whoever looks at a woman to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart.” God counts the will as the deed whenever a person cannot bring into effect what he wished. Therefore again and again I admonish you, brothers, and with all the diligence I can I bear witness, that according to the holy apostle you should walk carefully, not as fools, redeeming the time, because the days are evil. Watch most carefully for the snares of the evil demon, and resist evil and unclean thoughts immediately from the beginning, because remedy is considered much too late when the disease has grown strong through long duration.

Accustom yourselves at all times to dwell upon good and holy thoughts, and you will easily be able to overcome the insolence of evil ones. For just as vessels filled with good liquids admit no external corruption, so minds filled with good thoughts do not admit the intemperance of evil ones. For the eye of the heart is that by which God is seen, and through purity of conscience one comes to the glory of eternal blessedness. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God.” A clean heart is the dwelling place of the Holy Spirit; and where an evil thought is not admitted, God without doubt abides. A clean heart is the seat of wisdom; and where purity of mind is guarded, no dangers of adversity will do harm.

Be mindful of the Scripture which says: “Perverse thoughts separate from God, but proven virtue corrects fools. For wisdom will not enter a malicious soul, nor dwell in a body subject to sins. For the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee deceit, and will withdraw from thoughts that are without understanding.” A perverse thought is any thought admitted contrary to evangelical purity; by contact with it, the mind of the one thinking is defiled whenever it delights in thinking over and turning over those things that are opposed to God. These perverse thoughts separate the thinker’s mind from God and little by little cast it down into the depth of evils. For an evil and perverse thought, once it has penetrated, first poisons by the venom of delight, then clouds reason, afterward draws the will into consenting to the evil suggestion, and finally consummates sin in deed. From then on, an evil once presumed is done more freely, and all shame or fear is removed by habit. Sin is joined to sin, and little by little the habit of doing evil is strengthened.
