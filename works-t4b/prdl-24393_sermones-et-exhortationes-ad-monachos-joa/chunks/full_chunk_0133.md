Sermon V.
Folio LXVIII.

What shall we wretched people say on that day, or where shall we stand before the strict judge, if, as blessed Peter the apostle says, even the just man will scarcely be saved? If Jerome, a just and most holy man, feared the judgment to come, O good God, where will unhappy Trithemius, a sinner, appear? If the just man fears, where will the sinner stand? For what shall we say, or what shall we answer, when we are gravely accused on every side? All the elements will stand against us as testimony; earth and heaven alike will accuse us. Even if all these were silent, each person’s own conscience will be a grave accuser. He who knows all things is ignorant of nothing, for there all things to be judged will be made manifest.

There the evils we have done will be present, accusing us gravely; around us on every side there will stand countless demons as witnesses against us. We shall see open before us the dreadful abyss below; above us we shall behold the judge in wrath; and from every side the utmost danger will hang over us. My brothers, always turn over in the memory of your heart these dangers of the examination to come, and carefully consider how dreadful it is to fall under the future sentence of the judge. In that terrible hour pure hearts will avail more than the sharp words of rhetoricians; a clean conscience will profit a person more than the money of the whole world, for he who will judge all things is not deceived; indeed, he is moved neither by price nor by prayers.

O what incredible horror will invade all the wicked when they are about to hear that fearsome sentence: “Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels.” O worst of all evils, O deepest sorrow of all sorrows: to be separated from the fellowship of the saints and to be tortured forever with demons. Truly the day of judgment is dreadful and fearsome to all, because it is exceedingly bitter; there the remorse of conscience does not die like a worm; there one once damned to punishment is never freed.

Therefore the monk who desires to obtain everlasting salvation should always keep himself, as far as he can, clean from every sin, and that voice, “Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire,” should never depart from his memory.

Hence the third thing that we consider very useful for turning away from sin is the careful thought of the bitterness of the punishments of eternal damnation. For there is no temptation of carnal pleasure so violent that it will not immediately be overcome and fail, if the one tempted recalls in diligent meditation what the damned suffer in hell. What do the riches once possessed now profit the damned? What do past pleasures contribute to the luxurious placed in hell? What do most empty honors contribute to the proud, or once-broad power to the arrogant? Consider, brothers, what those already damned now suffer in the lower world, and, lest similar things befall you, persevere continually in good and holy works.

If you fear hell, flee sin. For hell will harm no one if a person remains in the faith of Christ and free from sins; nor will he burn in punishment who had not first burned here in guilty desire. Hell is a place to be feared, in which there is no redemption, but whoever has once entered there damned will never again come out to salvation. The wretched cry out in torments and in anguish, placed in everlasting horror, saying: “What now does our pride profit us? What do riches, honors, dignities, luxury, gluttony, and all past carnal pleasures profit us, now that we are damned forever? Behold, all things have passed like a shadow, and all have vanished like the brief dream of a single night. But we have been assigned to these eternal torments, in which we know most certainly there is no redemption.”

In hell, as Augustine testifies, even if there will be the goad of penitence, nevertheless there will be in the damned no correction of the will; in them iniquity will be so blamed that the equity of justice can in no way be loved or desired by them. There the devouring flame of hell burns those whom carnal delight now pollutes, and each person is burned so much more in torments as he delights more here in the flesh against God. In the lower world, as Gregory bears witness, there will be intolerable cold, inextinguishable fire, the immortal worm of conscience, unbearable stench, palpable darkness, inestimable pains, scourges without interruption from those who strike, the dreadful sight of demons, confusion over crimes committed, and despair of all goods. But of all the punishments in hell, that one must be considered by far the gravest: that there the damned are forever deprived of the vision of eternal light.

Here the most blessed bishop John Chrysostom says: “Gehenna is an intolerable thing, and one that all people abhor. For who does not know that that punishment in hell is very horrible? But if someone were to posit a thousand, indeed ten thousand, Gehennas, all that he says is nothing compared with this: for the soul to be exposed to Christ, to be thrust away from the glory of the saints, and to hear that terrible voice from the mouth of the judge of all: ‘Depart from me, workers of iniquity, for I do not know you,’ and, ‘Go, accursed ones, into the eternal fire prepared for you and for the devil.’”
