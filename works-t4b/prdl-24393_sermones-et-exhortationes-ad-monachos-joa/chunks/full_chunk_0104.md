On the brotherly charity of monks.

What is immortal is what one is bound to love in another, and the regard of charity must be directed toward the soul rather than toward the body. Since by the use of reason and understanding we are distinguished from brute animals, let the gaze of our love be referred to the mind, by which we understand, not to the body, which we have in common with beasts. And let all affections of the flesh be far from holy monks, who are not ignorant of the mutual and sincere rule of charity. Let love toward one another be pure, upright, and conceived from the fear of God alone, so that no advantage of any corruptible thing may intervene, but the simple intention of charity may remain.

True charity, simple and pure, does not seek the byways of evil conspiracies, nor arm itself with the murmur of contention for plots of iniquity. Whoever possesses the true charity of brotherhood in his heart keeps peace in all things and raises no matter or causes of destructive dissension. Charity, rendering what is uniquely its own, does injury to no one, and whatever it does not wish done to itself, it transfers to no one else’s person.

Do not suppose that you have true brotherly charity when you love one another for the sake of the flesh or for the advantage of any temporal thing; for where the cause of love is not the Lord, the affection of the one who loves is always either partly or wholly vicious. But he will rightly love his brother who loves him for the love of God and because of the common sharing in the kingdom which we await.

Therefore, as Saint John the bishop says, we have been baptized into one body of the Church, so that we may not be dissolved from that harmony and friendship toward one another which belongs to one body, but may preserve charity and mutual agreement perpetually. Just as no one outside the unity of the catholic Church can obtain salvation, so it is believed that no one can be saved without brotherly charity.

Charity, then, is necessary for you before all things for salvation; without it the remaining works of the virtues are altogether fruitless. For there can be no good, no saving merit for life, that has not taken its origin from it. Charity is a certain road to the compendium of virtue; if anyone enters upon it by the straight path, he will need no guide to direct him. Pure, simple, and upright charity is not accustomed to injure a neighbor or to inflict evil on anyone.

Charity is accustomed to expend upon all the affection which it grants to its own interests, and whatever it hopes will conduce to its own salvation, it desires this to be common to all people in Christ Jesus. Charity performs the office of love without any disturbance, and although it loves all people most greatly, it is nevertheless provoked for no one into bitter zeal. It is always solid, always constant, always stable and firm, because it is neither touched by passions nor agitated by movements of unrest. It presents itself as common to all, and so bestows its love upon each that it seems to view its own things as belonging to all.

In those things that pertain to the reason of the mind and soul, charity does not neglect itself; but in those things that concern the advantage of the body, it often sets the causes of others before its own utilities. Great indeed is the command of true charity, which compels a person to make another’s misfortune his own.

And so you, my brothers, who have solemnly pledged yourselves to Christ’s service, who through the steep path of obedience pant after eternal life: if you do not wish to run in vain in the arena of so laborious a contest, take care before all things, we exhort you, always to preserve brotherly charity inviolate within yourselves. For unless you have sincere and perfect charity toward one another, you labor without fruit in regular observance. Abstinence from meats will confer nothing upon you for the salvation of souls; the renunciation of all private property will profit nothing toward obtaining eternal happiness, if brotherly love does not hold a place in your hearts.

Desiring, therefore, to be saved with Christ in truth, love one another mutually; and, as Christ loved you, so we beg you to love one another. Whatever each one of you desires to be done for himself, let him, without envy and for the love of God, always bestow that very thing upon his brother. Let divine love conquer among you all quarrels of the flesh and all contentious disputes, and let destructive dissension of the flesh find no place where the fullness of brotherly charity ought continually to dwell.

Unless all of you in the monastery are united according to the law of Christian institution, you must always necessarily abstain from injuring one another. But if through contentions and insults you wound one another mutually, you are certainly neither monks nor true Christians. For it is fitting that all cloistered men and monks be outstanding in the observance of charity, and be far removed from envy, hatred, and rancor.
