On the Lenten Fast of Monks.

One who beholds the death of Jesus Christ with devotion is healed of the diseases of sins. But if you trust that you have been justified from sin, Christ hanging on the cross must be contemplated by you all the more and more fervently; without the merit of his passion no one lives righteous on earth. For outside faith in Christ there is no righteousness, and however religiously a person may live, if he does not believe in our Lord Jesus Christ, he will in no way attain eternal salvation; rather, the wrath of God remains upon him, and he will necessarily perish forever. The death of Christ opened the gate of salvation, through which no one can enter except one who faithfully believes in Christ. Therefore Christ humbly subjected himself to the condemnation of death, so that he might free believers from the yoke of perpetual damnation.

O infinite charity, O loving-kindness without measure: who among those who believe in you, O eternal majesty, would not marvel at such astonishing benefits granted to us? For your passion, Lord Jesus, is the price of our salvation, the final refuge of sinners, the singular remedy of infirm minds, the fountain, origin, and end of all good things. Therefore now, dearest brothers, let us recall and diligently bring back to memory the bitterness of the Lord’s passion, so that, since we necessarily acknowledge ourselves to be sinners, we may at last be justified through the merit of this same Savior of ours.

For meditation on the Lord’s passion surpasses all exercises of this kind, if it has been continued for a long time with the fervor of inward devotion. That mind of a monk which, kindled by zeal for divine love, has become accustomed every day, at least for a little hour, to keep the memory of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ with tears, does not easily lie open to the suggestions of demons. For that heart which the love of Jesus possesses always remains inaccessible and closed to all evil thoughts.

Truly blessed is that monk, and never to be named except with honor, who meditates day and night with loving zeal on the teaching and passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; who never forgets his Savior; who, whether he eats or drinks or does anything else, always beholds with the eyes of the mind the Lord Jesus hanging on the cross. No pernicious thoughts move about in his heart; no evil or idle word proceeds from his mouth; he thinks of nothing except Jesus crucified for us, and whatever he says sounds of Jesus or of righteousness.

Moreover, when he sees a painted image of Jesus crucified, he melts into tears, and, as if stunned by the excessive desire of his mind, he is filled with a most sweet bitterness. He can admit no consolations of the present life, because when he thinks of his Lord dwelling in heaven, he laments his own prolonged exile on earth. He loves nothing in this world except Jesus; he seeks nothing except the crucified one. If riches are at hand, he utterly refuses to set his heart on them; if they are not at hand, he by no means seeks to find them. For he is eager to recognize himself as a disciple of the poor Jesus, and he understands very well that the riches of this world are a great impediment to Christian perfection.

Having slender food and poor clothing for the body, he is content with his Lord, who became poor for us. That monk can truly be called blessed who can say with Theophorus: “My love has been crucified; therefore my heart receives no consolation of this world.” And that saying of Paul, the lover of Christ: “The world has been crucified to me, and I to the world.” Therefore, my dearest brothers, hear this counsel of mine, and by continual remembrance of the Lord’s passion sanctify the fast you have begun.

For I tell you that among all the exercises of monks none can be holier, none more acceptable to God, none more salutary for you, than that which is practiced in daily meditation on the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, with compunction of inward love. This exercise of the saving passion quickly makes a penitent out of a sinner; by wonderful compunction it makes the lukewarm and dry soul devout; it makes the impious person righteous. It overcomes the sweetness of sins, gives strength to the spirit against demons, sets an end to vices, and confers a strong increase on the holy virtues of the soul.

Devout and continued memory of the Lord’s passion purifies the conscience of the one meditating, enlightens the intellect in every knowledge of truth, and sweetly inflames the affection in the love of God. Devout meditation on Christ’s passion causes one to despise all the delights of this world and to fear nothing, however hard or adverse. It drives out lusts, tames luxury of both mind and body, puts envy to flight, restrains gluttony, calms avarice, mitigates sloth and anger, and uproots pride by the roots. Moreover, no passion for any carnal thing wearies the mind of a person whom the passion of Christ truly occupies.

O how sweetly burns the one whom the memory of Jesus’ passion sweetly inflames. O how blessed is the mind that embraces continual meditation on Christ’s death. O good Jesus, my crucified love, who are the salvation and health of faithful souls, grant us to keep continual memory of your passion; grant your servants always to commend the fasts begun with good works, because then our abstinence will be most pleasing to your uncircumscribable majesty, when it has been adorned with meditation on your precious blood; you who live and reign with God the Father in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for all ages of ages.

Here ends Homily XVII.

On the Sleep and Wakefulness of Monks. Homily XVII.

The monk who always thinks that he is going to die should allow no time to pass idly. For Scripture says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” Against the forgetful and heedless, however, it is written: “They are a nation without counsel and without prudence. Would that they were wise and understood and provided for their last things.” The remembrance of death brings watchfulness into the soul and extinguishes every disordered thought of the mind. He who rightly fears death never grows sluggish in good work. For the wise man says: “Whatever your hand is able to do, do earnestly; for neither work, nor reason, nor wisdom, nor knowledge will be among the dead, to whom you are hastening.”
