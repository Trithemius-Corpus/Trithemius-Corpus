*On Avoiding the Company of the Depraved.*

... he who by rash speech infects the minds of youths is like the impious Herod, who killed the boys. The evil that a young man receives from a foolish and negligent old man, even when he has become a priest, he will not correct. Every vessel that is filled with vinegar is spoiled; and a monk who is neglected in his novitiate is not corrected when he has grown old. Cursed is the old man by whose evil way of life youths perish, because he who has left evil tracks for those who follow him is like one who with his own hand has hurled a blind man into a pit. Before the inner Judge each person is worthy of as many deaths as the innocent people who have perished by his depraved example.

Young man, seek an old man as the moderator of your life: not one who has passed many years in a dissolute and lukewarm way of life, but one whom the fear of the Lord and knowledge of God’s Scriptures commend. Better is the company of a young monk who fears the Lord than the fellowship of an old teacher whose life lacks the light of divine love and the integrity of regular observance. An old man disordered in morals, lacking cleanness of mind and without discipline, is like one injured in the leg, led lame to the grave. An old and dissolute monk is like a foolish man who, not knowing the end, is taken among robbers at a drinking feast. An elder without maturity is like a little elder-pipe of sambucus, crushed under the feet of players.

He boasts vainly that he has been a monk for many days who has not perceived the beginning of the interior life. He who loves the cleanness of his heart guards himself at all times from the company of undisciplined brothers. Just as a spot of pitch clinging to the garments of dancers grieves virgins, so a good young man blushes at the shameful words of elders. Lascivious speech is not the speech of a man who fears God, because the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart; and he who brings forth shameful words is not clean before the Most High. If sinners begin to hate you because of the fear of God, do not be saddened, because unless you displease the wicked you will not attain the perfection of the good. For to displease the wicked on account of righteousness is praiseworthy, and is a clear sign of divine love. If you love your soul, flee the counsels of the undisciplined, and do not associate with mockers. Walk with the good, and let him who fears the Lord be your counselor.

*On Monks Sent Out on the Road. Homily XII. Fo. xxvii.*

It belongs to monks to flee the world, and, for love of solitude, to avoid the crowds of men as much as possible at all times. A monk is so called from solitude, just as one is named from unity. He cannot be a monk who follows after the frequenting of peoples; and he who delights in cities has not yet tasted monastic sweetness. If you are a monk of a cenobium, you ought not abandon its solitude without reasonable cause; and if you are bound to silence, you ought not be pleased by the conversations of worldly people. Whoever loves wandering excursions in the city has abandoned his conscience; and he who is often outside his monastery does not preserve the purity of inner progress. He who goes around the houses of relatives and delights in the feasts of neighbors does not consider himself a monk; and he who goes to the banquets of drinkers kills his own soul. He who sleeps in the house of his kinsman seeks comfort, and he who avoids the assemblies of the religious follows destructive liberty.

A monk in the house of his father and mother is like a lamb in the midst of wolves, and like a hare surrounded by hunters’ dogs. If you are a monk, if a servant and soldier of God, what are you doing in the house of your parents? Or what are you seeking in the midst of peoples? A monk remaining for a long time outside the cenobium among secular people is like a man who has buried his only-begotten son beneath the depths of the waters. For just as fish have no life on dry land, so monks have no salvation of soul in the world. Outside the river the fish dies, and the purity of a monk’s conscience is quickly violated outside the monastery. The life of monks is in the cenobium, and the death of souls is amid secular business.

It is a great virtue of the soul to flee the crowds of men, or, when it is altogether necessary to be among them, nevertheless to avoid them in mind. At times we are compelled by necessity to make a journey through the world; but the purity of conscience is polluted all the less by encounters with men, the less the desire of the mind is drawn away from the secrecy of the cell. Therefore every good monk must be warned that whenever, by the command of the elder, he is sent out on the road for the necessity of some matter, he should never wander outwardly in his mind. He who does not guard his senses is quickly corrupted when evil is set before him; and he who casts off the fear of the Lord necessarily returns impure to the monastery.
