But the human intellect, which our teachers have called the soul, was indeed created at the beginning in complete ignorance, and knows none of those things which the angelic intellect understands by nature.

These two intellects differ as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, habit and privation, beginning and end, simple and composite, substance and accident, nature and art, truth and likeness. For all things, as divine Scripture testifies, which belong to the angelic intellect by nature and from the beginning, the human intellect will attain in the end through arts, as the Lord Jesus says in the holy Gospel: “In the resurrection of the dead they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but will be like the angels of God in heaven.”

For the mind has been placed for a short time in the workshop of the body, so that it may be trained toward understanding through many labors, as though through arts. If there, turned toward God, it has duly completed its craft through love, then at last, when it departs from here, it becomes equal to an angel. But if, consenting to the motions of the flesh, it has not kept its proper order, then, disordered and wretched, like the fallen apostate spirits, it will leave the body to be tormented forever.

For since our mind or soul was created in ignorance and privation of all things, it must come to knowledge of things by continual action, movement, and art. The end, indeed, makes the human intellect equal to the angelic by changing its potency into act; it will make from privation a habit, from emptiness fullness, from imperfection perfection, from one deprived one possessing, from darkness light, from night day, from lunar solar, and from earthly heavenly.

This end, my dearest brothers, is the beginning of eternal salvation. No one can happily come to it who does not strive with all his powers toward the summit of perfection. But since it is difficult, amid crowds where a thousand occasions for sin are offered, always to keep unbroken this necessary striving for progress, the holy fathers, founders of the monastic order, taught us by word and example to flee the world and to avoid its crowd not only in mind but also in body.

Of those who were stronger in spirit, many hastened into the desert, so that they might become nearer to the Lord in mind the farther they withdrew in body from the world. Of them we read the words of the prophet Isaiah: “The wilderness will rejoice and blossom like the lily; it will bud forth abundantly, and will rejoice with gladness and praise.” Others, wishing to benefit many, established cenobia, in which they placed a multitude of monks, whose way of life would be separated from the peoples and nevertheless would be shared socially with one another.

For it is not fitting for everyone to sit in the desert, when for some it is more suitable to serve the Lord in the midst of brothers. For even our monasteries have their own solitude, so that whoever wishes to be solitary can do so in the midst of brothers, provided he keeps the privacy of the cell in which he dwells. For a monk, according to the interpretation of his name, is called solitary, because in mind and body he is separated from the many things of this world.

Therefore, as I am about to deliver today’s sermon to your charities, I wish to admonish you, since I desire your progress in spiritual life. I ask you, then, and exhort you in the bowels of brotherly charity, to love the quiet of the cell as the workshop of your salvation, in which there is great peace for those who love God, and great advantage for perfecting the intellect toward knowledge of the highest truth. For the prophet Isaiah says: “In silence and hope will be your strength.”

Whoever does not love the solitude of the cell and the quiet of silence by no means loves purity of mind; rather, wandering in body, he always drinks in new vanities in his soul. Hence blessed Jerome says: “Why do we desire the crowdedness of cities, we who are reckoned by our singularity? For to me a town is a prison; solitude is paradise.”

If the desert and solitude delighted us, brothers, this place would offer great convenience for our purpose, where, apart from poor peasants, a guest rarely appears. No one disturbs our quiet, no one troubles us, no one introduces spectacles or rumors of momentary novelty. But I see few among you who are lovers of inward quiet, and I grieve deeply, because I know very well that a monk cannot make progress in spiritual life if he is known not to love the quiet of the cell.
