Homily XI. Fol. XXV.

Let him judge that he has spent uselessly any day on which he has not sent ahead some good work for which he may expect reward. A good monk should strive at all times to cultivate the field of his heart, and to root out completely, so far as he can, all the thorns and briars of evil affections. Let him learn to love the Lord Jesus above all things with a pure mind, and to keep with the greatest diligence whatever he has commanded. Let him sow in the tears of devotion and constancy what he may reap with all the saints in exultation. Let not the continual difficulty of labor frighten his mind; let not the harsh mortification of the flesh break his spirit, since he who labors little gathers little fruit at harvest, and he who now, for the love of Christ, sows much in tears will reap most abundant fruit on that day. Nor is it part of our delight, brothers, what we do in this present pilgrimage, but rather that, by the continual exercise of labors and adversities, we may be purified here and come clean and pure to our homeland.

On Fleeing the Company of the Wicked: Homily Eleven.

The company of the wicked has drawn many away from the way of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Therefore, my most beloved brothers, we do not cease to admonish you to walk in the way of the Lord with the good and holy, so that by their instruction and example you may daily become better. For as the divine apostle of Christ says, imitating a certain Menander, a Greek writer: “Bad conversations corrupt good morals.” He does not truly desire to be good whom the companionship of the wicked delights. For no one speaks wicked things unless he is first found inwardly corrupted and depraved in his heart against the purity of evangelical tradition. As each person is, and as he turns over and thinks upon things in the secrets of his heart, so speech more often proceeds from his mouth. For speech is the most truthful witness of the heart, as our Lord and Savior says in the holy Gospel: “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.” For a good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and likewise an evil man brings forth evil things.

Since, therefore, he is not good who speaks evil, we must beware of all who use depraved, scurrilous, and destructive speech, in whose conversation certain shameful things frequently resound, while words of holiness are either never heard or very rarely. Shameful speech is the sign of an impure heart, even if deeds do not follow. Therefore you, my dearest brothers, who have promised according to the rules and statutes of the divine fathers of the Church not only to preserve chastity and every kind of modesty in deed, even to death, by an implicit as well as explicit profession, but also to be far removed from every unclean thought, must flee the company of wicked men; avoid at all times the assemblies of the malicious and those who speak wicked, shameful, or idle things.

Remember the Scripture saying: “He who touches pitch will be defiled by it, and he who associates with the proud will put on pride.” Hence holy Pope Gregory says: “The weak must avoid the fellowship of the wicked, lest they take delight in imitating the evils they often see and cannot correct.” And the divine bishop [unclear] says: “If a good man joins himself to an evil one, either both are quickly made equal, or they are quickly separated from each other. For friendships either seek equals or make them.” For the nature of things is such that whenever a good person is joined to an evil one, it is not that the evil is made better by the good, but that the good is corrupted by the evil. For the diversity of things can never have concord, and wicked fellowship troubles many.

There are among you certain brothers, I say with grief, professed men very forgetful of regular discipline, for whom the cell is a prison, the choir a siege, prayer nausea, silence a punishment, and the reading of Scripture disgust. The love of God never, or only rarely, lifts their hearts upward, since their whole way of life is fixed on lower things. Whenever I happen to be absent, they soon contend among themselves; not about Christ, nor about the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom, nor about the integrity of regular observance, but they draw out long speech about the vain and useless affairs of this world, and by talking together they involve themselves in tales and wars of kings, things about which it is not lawful for a monk even to think.
