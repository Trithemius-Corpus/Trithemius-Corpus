Fine garments, beautifully and elegantly arranged, do not befit a monk, but rather the most useful ones, by which he may defend the body from the harmful inclemency of the air. All fastidiousness is forbidden to cloistered men, who, by reason of the profession they have made before God, are bound to contempt of the world. Humility flees high things; it seeks what is lowly.

The twenty-third sign of monastic humility shows the monk servant-like toward the brethren and willingly given to acts of service, and ready for all things whatsoever he has been commanded. For true humility obeys even inferiors with a most ready will, and is never indignant against the one who commands. As far as he can, he desires to serve all for the love of Christ, who faithfully loves God with his whole heart. Whatever service he has bestowed on the brethren, he firmly believes that it has all been shown to Christ.

The twenty-fourth sign of humility a monk then truly attains when, for the love of God, he delights to attend in the monastery to the meaner offices, which the others despise. We read that many once ascended to this degree of humility, and we do not doubt that they are of great merit before God. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Everyone who humbles himself shall be exalted; for God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble. The humble monk imitates Christ; the proud one imitates the devil.

The twenty-fifth sign of monastic humility is always to seek the lower place for sitting in the assembly of any persons whatever, and not to prefer oneself to either an equal or an inferior. It is not for monks to contend proudly about precedence of place, nor does it befit a soldier of Christ to wish to surpass others in honor. For the true monk who loves humility, content with the lowest place in every respect, does not desire to excel anyone.

The twenty-sixth indication of humility especially befits him who, although learned and educated, because of the abasement of his own humiliation hides all learning and knowledge. For the truly humble monk is always found readier to listen than to speak. For the divine priest Jerome says: The monk has the office not of teaching but of lamenting, who either mourns himself or the world, and fearfully awaits the coming of the Lord. The arrogance of monks is hateful both to God and to men, and always liable to sin.

The twenty-seventh sign of humility is perfectly attained by him who, for love of regular discipline, delights more in silence than in speech. The humble lover of Christ loves silence, and, lest he offend in speech, is accustomed to say very little. Humility is rarely found among the talkative; and he who is quick to speak casts down his own soul. A talkative and garrulous monk is not without pride, and the fool multiplies words. Whoever in the spiritual contest desires to overcome his adversary through humility must not neglect to restrain his tongue from much speaking and from every idle word. It is written in the book of Proverbs that in much speaking sin is not lacking.

Whoever has attained the twenty-eighth sign of perfect humility will avoid laughter and all the unruly gestures of those who guffaw. Lamentation most befits a monk, not laughter, since he is dead to all the levities of the world. And the wise man says: A fool raises his voice in laughter, but a wise man will scarcely laugh quietly.

The twenty-ninth indication of perfect humility takes its beginning from the fear of the Lord; for his honor, as far as he can, he flees and detests every sin. For everyone who does not fear to sin has not truly been humble. But the monk who, according to his strength, follows Christ in true humility is accustomed to flee every sin and, as far as he can, to exercise himself in the pursuit of virtues. Humility confesses its sin, and whenever it has offended even in the smallest matters, it does not rest until it has amended everything through true penance. He who is truly humble is always found zealous for preserving cleanness of conscience. For the nourishment of humility is charity, which does not allow the one who has it to be idle.

The thirtieth sign of true humility shows a monk confirmed in the purpose of holiness, and makes him subject to all the brethren, even the younger. Whatever the pastor has commanded, he obeys simply; he never debates the commands of his superior; he does not ask the reason why he commands thus. If impossibilities are commanded, he is ready in spirit; he is prepared without contradiction for whatever is hard and arduous. He is directed by the counsel of his pastor; he believes him, trusts him, obeys him, and willingly is governed by his command. You have now heard, my dearest brethren, the thirty indications of true humility. O good God, where are the works?
