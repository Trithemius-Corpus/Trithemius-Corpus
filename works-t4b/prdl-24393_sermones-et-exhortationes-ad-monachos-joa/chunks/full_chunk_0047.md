On the Daily Struggle with Temptation.

Unless you impose upon its unlawful movements the yoke of continence and subjection, you will go wholly with it, as it leads the way, into the abyss. For we know, as the holy Apostle says, that the flesh lusts against the spirit; and unless by the spirit we mortify the works of the flesh, we shall die in its corruption. Nothing casts the soul more headlong into the pit of sin than the insolence of the flesh, which, when it is permitted to live by its own will, struggles back like a contumacious servant against every command. Unhappy flesh, which through the concupiscence of vanity casts the soul down, it will not pass unpunished for you if it has undertaken to suggest any evil to you, for God will judge both. You too will be punished when you have sinned with it, because when Adam, having transgressed the commandment, is driven from paradise, Eve, the instigator of evil, is necessarily joined with him in misery.

Be subject, O flesh, to the spirit, for the Apostle says: 'Those who are Christ's have crucified their flesh with its vices and concupiscences.' And you, O men and brothers, who profess the service of Christ, who have willingly sworn yourselves to the law of monastic life, who have promised to imitate the footsteps of the Savior: attend to your calling, that you may walk carefully, not as the unwise, but as true lovers of Jesus Christ, who by the spirit mortify the deeds of the flesh. You, I say, my most beloved fellow soldiers, always keep watch in good works, mortifying for Christ's love our bodies that are upon the earth, constant in fasts and prayers, and continually occupied in the meditation of the divine Scriptures. Always stretch yourselves toward what lies ahead; each day renew with fresh zeal the fervor of your holy purpose. Flee idleness and every sloth, and diligently consider how brief is the time of this life. Let the brief and moderate labor of a hard struggle not frighten you; let the enemy's importunity not make you fainthearted, since every labor is quickly overcome by willing persistence, and the devil, however fiercely he rages, is conquered as soon as you invoke Christ.

Remember the words of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, who says in the holy Gospel: 'Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me.' But what is our cross, my brothers, except a life full of temptations and adversities, and the burden of regular discipline under which we are wearied day and night? But let us gather our hearts, most dear brothers, and for the love of God gladly take up whatever he wishes us to endure, so that in his judgment we may deserve to say: 'We have passed through fire and water, and you have brought us into refreshment.' O how glorious a reward is laid up in the kingdom of heaven for the servants of God, who now struggle in this brief course and are wearied for his love. Eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man, the immensity of that good which almighty God has prepared for those who love him, to remain without end in eternal felicity.

The highest happiness of this good is acquired by humility and possessed by purity of heart and the fervor of divine love. Let us hasten there, most dear brothers; let us run there with all the devotion of mind that we can, where the spirit is joined to its Creator through the enjoyment of sweetness, where the intellect is perfected in the knowledge of the highest good, which is God. There is the joy of everlasting happiness, which does not diminish; there is gladness which does not end; there, after every labor, is perpetual rest; there is the glorious reward for tribulations and injuries borne for Christ. Whatever good we now do, there without doubt we shall find it stored up; whatever adversity we patiently endure for the love of Christ, there we shall receive it back, rewarded with a most abundant prize.

O monk, monk, who spend fruitlessly the time mercifully granted to you by the Lord God for doing good, what do you deserve, or what reward will you receive in that terrible hour, you who are now so sluggish and unwilling to sow? Have you not read in Scripture concerning the labors of the saints: 'Going, they went and wept, casting their seeds; but coming, they shall come with exultation, carrying their sheaves'? Now is the time, O monk, to work the field of your heart; now is the hour to sow good seed with the blessing of cheerfulness, because he who now sows sparingly will also reap sparingly in that future recompense of reward. We must labor now and never cease from good work, since whatever we have now done here, there without doubt we shall receive. Therefore, if there is among you any faithful monk, if anyone is a zealous lover of his own salvation, let him always think on the hour of future reward, which no one can escape; let him always prepare himself to render the account of his stewardship. Let no single day perish in which he has not done some particular good work to send ahead for future recompense.

Let him consider that he has spent uselessly any day on which he has not sent ahead some good work for which he expects to be rewarded. A good monk should strive at all times to cultivate the field of his heart, and, so far as he can, utterly root out all the thorns and briars of evil affections. Let him learn to love the Lord Jesus above all things with a pure mind, and to keep most diligently whatever he has commanded. Let him sow in the tears of devotion and constancy what he may reap with all the saints in exultation. Let the continual difficulty of laboring not deter the mind; let the hard struggle with the flesh not break the spirit, since he who labors little gathers little fruit at the harvest, and he who now sows much in tears for the love of Christ will bring back most abundant fruit on that day. For this, brothers, is the destination of our journey which we make in the present pilgrimage: that, purified by the continual exercise of labors and adversities, we may depart from here clean and pure for our fatherland.

On Fleeing the Company of the Wicked: Omnia, the Eleventh.

The company of the wicked has drawn many back from the way of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Therefore, my most beloved brothers, we do not cease to admonish you to walk in the way of the Lord with the good and holy, so that by their instruction and example you may become better every day. For as the blessed Apostle of Christ says, imitating a verse of a certain Greek, Menander: 'Evil conversations corrupt good morals.' He does not truly desire to be good who delights in the way of life of the wicked. No one speaks wicked things unless he is first found corrupt and depraved in his heart against the purity of the evangelical tradition. As each person is, and as he goes over things by rumination and thought in the secret of his heart, so speech more often proceeds from his mouth. For the speech of the one speaking is the truest witness of the heart, as our Lord and Savior says in the holy Gospel: 'Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.' For a good man brings forth good things from the treasure of his heart, and an evil man likewise brings forth evil things.

Since, then, he who speaks evil is not good, we must beware of all who use wicked, scurrilous, and pernicious speech, in whose talk certain shameful things frequently resound, while words of holiness are heard either never or very rarely. Shameful speech is the sign of an immodest heart, even if deeds do not follow. Therefore, my dearest brothers, you who have promised, by profession both implicit and explicit, according to the rule and the ecclesiastical statutes of the holy fathers, not only to preserve chastity and every form of modesty in deed until death, but who also ought to be far removed from every unclean thought: flee the company of wicked men and the assemblies of the malicious, and at all times avoid those who speak unjust, shameful, or idle things.

Remember the Scripture saying: 'He who touches pitch will be defiled by it; and he who associates with the proud will put on pride.' Hence holy Pope Gregory says: 'The weak ought to avoid the society of the wicked, lest they delight in imitating the evils which they frequently see and are unable to correct.' And the blessed bishop John says: 'If a good man is joined to an evil one, either they are made equal, or they are quickly separated from each other. For friendships either seek equals or make them.' For the nature of things is such that, whenever a good man is joined to an evil one, the evil man is not made better by the good, but the good man is contaminated by the evil. For diversity of things can never have concord, and wicked society tempts many.

There are among you certain brothers, I say with grief, professed men very forgetful of regular discipline, for whom the cell is a prison, the choir a siege, prayer a nausea, silence a punishment, and the reading of Scripture a weariness. The love of God either never or only rarely lifts their hearts upward, for their whole way of life rests in lower things. Whenever I happen to be absent, they immediately gather among themselves, not to speak of Christ, nor of the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom, nor of the integrity of regular observance, but they produce a long discourse about the vain and useless affairs of this world; and by conversing they busy themselves with tales of kings and wars, matters about which a monk is not permitted to think.
