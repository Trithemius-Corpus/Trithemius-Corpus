Blessed Father Benedict, detesting the idleness of monks, says in the Rule which he handed down to us: “Idleness is the enemy of the soul; therefore at fixed times the brothers ought to be occupied in manual labor, and again at fixed times in divine reading.” For then they are truly monks, if they live by the labor of their hands, as our fathers and the apostles did. Hence also Saint Paul says in the Epistle: “Whoever does not work should not eat.” In the first day of the monastic state this was the custom of monks: they admitted no one into their fellowship without labor, not so much because of the necessities of food as because of the soul’s salvation, lest through idleness an occasion be given to destructive thoughts.

At that time monks had no revenues, no tithes, no rents, no annual income from anyone; but all who lived together in common procured food and clothing for themselves by their hands, burdening no one by the exaction of what was owed. The holy apostles of our Savior too, although they had power to live from the preaching of the Gospel, labored with their hands, preferring not to seem to burden any of the faithful, so that they might have not only what they themselves could live on, but also what they might provide as necessities for the needy.

Again, afterward, when monks began to have estates and revenues, the first love of simplicity and study failed, and pride gradually increased with the wealth of temporal things, to such a degree that all withdrew from the labor of their hands, with time, after monks had become rich. As long as monks were poor and humble in birth, they worshiped God in truth; but afterward, when they began to abound in riches, duplicity drove out simplicity, and the arrogance of nobles uprooted the humility of monks. For the integrity of regular observance gave birth to an abundance of temporal things, but little by little the riches of monks uprooted the purity of the order from its foundations. But once the integrity of regular observance had been removed, in many cenobia even the riches of temporal things failed by the just judgment of God.

And among their number was this present monastery of Sponheim, the last among my brothers, and not the first, which for many years remained wretchedly desolate in each condition, to such a degree that five monks lived in it, leading a dissolute life, and did not have the necessities of life to support themselves. Therefore, by the mercy of God at last providing, Frederick, count palatine across the Rhine, having called upon the counsel of fathers of a reformed order, in the Christian year one thousand four hundred sixty-nine, expelled the monks deformed in life, deposed the abbot named Otto of Cologne, and placed four others in this monastery from the observance of Bursfeld and from the cenobium called Mount Speciosus. Over them he set as abbot Johannes, from the noble family of Buchenbach, which they commonly call “of the Carbonaria house,” a man such as you have seen and heard, worthy of eternal memory. He found this place in extreme poverty, and, presiding for almost fourteen years in great patience, restored it to the condition in which we now live.

Therefore, since lack of temporal goods still presses us heavily, there was need of continual labor, both that we might support ourselves with necessary food and that we might free the house of God in which we live from another’s debt. But what are your labors, or how much can they contribute for us toward such great needs? We are few in number, and our priestly order’s institution does not often permit us to spend the whole day in the field turning stones with our hands and to interrupt divine service. We are few, and extreme poverty necessarily weighs us down with lack of all things; and even if we have labored much, we accomplish little afterward; and even if we have been able to work only a little, surely we fail all the more in spirit. For man, as Scripture bears witness, is born for labor just as a bird is born for flying.

Therefore, lest you eat your bread idly, you will always have to be assigned to some honest and useful labor, by which both the weariness of regular observance may be relieved and grave poverty may at least in part be repaired. At certain times of the year, in old age, and in the days of autumn for the vintage, we have been accustomed to occupy you outside the monastery, that is, when labor is both very necessary and the cost and wage of hired workers increases the price of servants. At the remaining time we have decreed to keep you in the cenobium, partly because your labors contribute little to the monastery’s needs in comparison with its expenses, and because, as it seems to us, very few of you come gladly as volunteers to rustic tasks.

There is also another consideration which moves my mind not to occupy you easily, without reasonable cause and manifest necessity, in external works of the hands. For I have learned by experience that labors of this kind do not suit you in regular observance, but rather destroy the integrity of monastic discipline among you than advance it. I am ashamed to say it, and nevertheless I am compelled to utter it: your labors outside the monastery and the cell bring more destruction to the purity of the soul than salvation. For what do you labor at, and what are your pursuits in your labors? Is it not to wander about, to run here and there, to indulge in conversation and cups, and to spend the rest of the time in vain and useless talk?

You do not have in yourselves that fervor of the Holy Spirit of God by which, as we read, the ancient monks of our institution were once inflamed. For the common necessity they all and singly labored with their hands with such desire, such zeal, and such exertion that you would have thought the business of the whole monastery was placed in each one’s prudence. For each labored at that time with his hands as if he alone ought to nourish the whole congregation. Amid these things they paid the services of divine worship with such cheerfulness by day and night as if they had in no way been occupied in penitence by the labors of their hands.

Who among you, brother, is an imitator of such men, so that he reckons the whole of our common want as his own, or strives according to his strength to relieve it by laboring in the sweat of his brow? They, laboring with their hands, prayed to God with continual hearts; but you, intent on stories, satisfy neither the duty of the hands nor the minds of monks. Likewise, in the time of the regular fast, they either diligently kept the measure of abstinence appointed for them, or, if they could not because of the magnitude of their labors, they ate for this reason: that they might be made fit and able for labor. But you do not eat in order to labor; rather, you labor in order to eat, though by the Rule you ought to be fasting. You take occasion from labor to break your fasts.

As for the clothing which some of you treat foully and most disgracefully, what zealous lover of regular discipline could bear it with equanimity? These are, my brothers, things which are ruinously tended. Everything that belongs to the monasteries of the servants of God is the patrimony of Christ; the whole is dedicated to God, and no one may misuse the things of Christ, or turn them to some other use. Do you not know what our holy lawgiver Benedict says to you in the Rule: “If anyone treats the things of the monastery filthily or negligently, let him be corrected. But if he does not amend, let him be subject to regular discipline.” And in the chapter on the cellarer of the monastery: “Let him regard all the substance of the monastery as if it were the sacred vessels of the altar.”

Finally, I cannot and ought not be silent about how filthily and negligently many of you treat everything, as if either you were not seen by God, or no loss came to us from negligence in these matters. Know this, brothers, that I announce it to you beforehand: for these things, as also for your other sins, you will have to render account to the Lord God. For this reason I have reduced your labors outside the monastery, lest by acting badly you sin more gravely; and manual work is to be enjoined upon you: writing books, binding them, and things like these, which you can accomplish within the enclosures of the monastery with tranquillity of mind and body.

If only you would do at least these works of the hands, or of the pen, diligently for the love of God, so that you might not eat bread altogether idle. There is no manual work which, in my judgment, befits a monk more than writing books of sacred reading and preparing the necessary materials for others who write. It will very often be permitted to interrupt this holy labor with prayer, and, as food for the mind, it is no less watchful for the body. Necessity also compels us to apply ourselves diligently to writing books, if we wish to have at hand the means by which we can usefully occupy ourselves with one another in spiritual study. For you see that the whole library of this cenobium, which was once distinguished and large, was scattered, sold, and alienated by the deformed monks before us, and that no more than fourteen volumes were found here by us.

Indeed, the industry of the printing art, recently invented in our days at Mainz, daily brings many volumes into the light; but it is by no means possible for us to buy all new books, since this house is still oppressed by the greatest poverty. Therefore I admonish and exhort you: since you do not come gladly enough to external manual labors, at least labor diligently in writing volumes for the honor of God. For just as idleness is the enemy of the soul, so moderate labor is the spiritual preserver of life. Since a monk is a soldier of the eternal king, he must never be idle.
