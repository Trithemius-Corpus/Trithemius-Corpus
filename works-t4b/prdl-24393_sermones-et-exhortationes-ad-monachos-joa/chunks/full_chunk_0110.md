On the Serenity of Conscience.

From this there is no doubt that many have erroneous consciences, because they love the pleasures of their own flesh too much. Every proud, lustful, unclean person, and everyone entangled in great or public crimes, clouds his own conscience with deliberate perversity; and how erroneously he thinks about the future repayment of merits he often makes clear both by words and by deeds. A malicious affection perverts the judgment of uprightness, and the disordered desire of the flesh clouds the understanding.

Sometimes, moreover, excessive and immoderate faintheartedness makes the conscience err; this evil is born partly from ignorance, and partly generated from one’s own sensuality. In such scrupulosity, however, the monk should follow the counsel either of the abbot or of some more experienced elder, and should not rashly defend the opinion of his own head. Excessive scrupulosity is dangerous, because it hurls the conscience into error and unhappily trips up many. For everything that is done against conscience is said to build toward hell.

Excessive perplexity of mind has also harmed many and has cast them headlong into various errors. But he who is truly a humble monk brings himself to the examination of his ruler. For whoever, in doubtful matters that arise, makes use of the counsel of his superior is not easily tripped up by the snares of demons. Indeed, the more the consciences of monks have been exposed to the examination of a wise prelate, the more secure they will be, and the more clearly they will shine.

Therefore, dearest brothers, care must be taken above all that you have consciences free from every error, then pure from all vice, and finally adorned with the holy works of all the virtues. Let the monk, then, who desires to advance in truth guard his heart with every vigilance, and as far as possible place a barrier before his senses, nor allow anything deadly to enter. But if, by any occasion, he recognizes that he has fallen into some sin, let him at once strive to erase it by true repentance, and not grant time to the wicked seed in which it may grow stronger for the worse. While the wound is small, let a remedy be applied, for if it is neglected it grows without measure.

Therefore, my brothers, resist strongly the first movements of demonic suggestion, and overcome the enemy while he is small and weak. But if the sensuality of the body has become altogether wanton, let it be brought back to obedience by harsh mortification, just as we know our holy father Benedict did, when he rooted out the slippery insolence of the flesh with the stings of nettles and brambles. Then, if you diligently call back to memory the dreadful future punishments of the wicked, you will easily overcome the illicit movements of the flesh. For there is no one who does not fear pain more than he loves pleasure.

You should also often recall to mind the joys of heavenly happiness, to which you desire to come; for if you think on them faithfully, you will strongly quench the fire of the flesh. Whatever delights in the world is corruptible and momentary; but the joys of eternal delight remain without end forever. It is foolish, therefore, and a matter of extreme madness, to choose a passing advantage and to despise that good which remains forever. He is judged foolish among men who prefers dung to gold; but before God he is held most foolish who puts temporal pleasure before everlasting goods.

He who loves a serene conscience should flee idleness, since, as Scripture says, every idle person is in desire, and idleness has taught much malice. The soul must be occupied with good exercises, and the fire of holy conduct, which burns with charity, must be nourished by continual practice. For the idle man lives neither for himself nor for his neighbor, but is always given over to his belly or to sleep; he opens his mind to vices, and keeps it closed and empty of virtues. Idleness, the kindler of lust, quickly extinguishes the fervor of spiritual devotion.

Abstinence also is especially necessary for the monk above all, so that his conscience may remain clean. For, as Saint Jerome says, a full belly easily foams into lust, and he who feeds his servant delicately will find him rebellious. The lover of serenity of conscience should be constant in holy prayers, since, as dogs flee a stone, so demons flee devout and continual prayer. Let him also diligently beware of acedia, together with sadness of mind; and as soon as he feels any temptation creeping in, let him have recourse to the consolation of the Scriptures. Truly, the mind that the word of God has filled, base thought will not enter.

Whatever we do, whatever we think, whatever we undertake, let us always know that the presence of God is there, and we shall easily overcome disordered delight. A good conscience never forgets the presence of God, but always meditates that its judge is present.
