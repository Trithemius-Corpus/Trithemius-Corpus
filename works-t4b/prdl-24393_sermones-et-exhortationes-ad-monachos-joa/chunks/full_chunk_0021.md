On the daily renewal of holy purpose.

...the more studious a monk has been in learning the Scriptures, the less he is inflamed by the ardors of carnal pleasures. For although, according to the true opinion of Saint Jerome, a monk seems to have the office not of teaching but rather of lamenting, nevertheless, since he professes the military service of Christ, it is fitting, indeed necessary, that he know the well-pleasing will of almighty God, in whose words and arms he has sworn allegiance. But just as a soldier cannot know the will of his lord, who is far absent, except through messengers and letters, so a monk will not understand the commands of the eternal King except through sacred readings and sermons, the evangelical Scriptures.

The reading of the Scriptures opens the gate of salvation to those who do not know it; it leads the diligent reader by the straight path to the knowledge of God; it makes the lover of itself despise all worldly things and love nothing other than true wisdom. Nothing, however, is more shameful than for a monk in spiritual life to be unwilling to advance, and knowingly to wish to be ignorant of the wills of God. Let no one flatter himself on account of his weakness or ignorance, since he who is ignorant will be ignored, and he who hates saving knowledge is deservedly condemned.

When the Lord was about to send out his preachers into all lands, he admonished them beforehand, saying: “Be prudent as serpents and simple as doves,” so that through ignorance you should neither be craftily deceived by anyone nor wrongfully lay a snare for anyone by fraud. For there is little difference in fault between being able to deceive and being deceived. Therefore I beg you, dearest brothers, to attend to the Rule of our holy father Benedict, which admonishes you in very many places to sweat more frequently over the readings of the divine Scriptures. For in the Scriptures we are taught and instructed for all piety, how in this most wretched pilgrimage of our sojourning we ought to live, so that we may be able to please the Lord our God above all things.

For it belongs to monks to despise the world and the filthy pleasures of the present life for the love of God; usefully to restrain the unlawful habits of the flesh; to devote themselves also to prayer, reading, and meditation on the Scriptures; and continually to lift the mind from earthly things to heavenly things. Blessed is that monk who has not gone away in the counsel of the wicked, and has not stood in the way of sinners, and has not sat in the chair of pestilential men, but meditates on the law of the Lord day and night. He is shown wisdom in the Scriptures of God, the fruit of which, after the end of this pilgrimage, he will receive as eternal life.

On the Daily Renewal of Holy Purpose. Homily Five.

We have heard, dearest brothers, the reading just recited to us from the book of holy Job, who laments the misery of the human race and the calamity of this life, and at the same time had said: “Man born of woman, living for a short time, is filled with many miseries; he comes forth like a flower and is crushed, and flees like a shadow, and never remains in the same state.” “Behold,” blessed Job says, “my years pass by, and I walk a path by which I shall not return.” “All flesh will fail together, and man will return to dust.”

What do you observe among these things, brothers, or what do you think? Is there any man among us who does not know that he is mortal, who can truthfully say that he will remain forever? God, my brothers, how blind we are, while holding our eyes open; how forgetful and sluggish travelers we are, who certainly discern and know that we are going to die, and yet we trust in the vanity of the present life as though we were not going to become mortal. But who is the man who will live and not see death, and who will rescue his soul from the hand of hell? Behold, Scripture says: “We are all dying, and like water we flow away upon the earth.”

Therefore remember, my brothers, that death does not delay, and all that passes by is short when reckoned; for, as the holy lover of Christ Paul says, “It is appointed for human beings to die once,” and no one will be able to flee or escape beneath this common law of necessity. O if only we set daily before the gaze of the mind that hour of coming death, which each one of us must necessarily undergo shortly, and arranged our life and conduct in such a way that we might always be found ready, how happy and secure our departure could become. The judge of the living and the dead, Christ himself our Lord, admonished us in the holy Gospel always to be ready for death, saying: “Therefore you also must be ready, because at the hour when you do not think it, the Son of Man will come.”

How much frequent meditation on death avails for purity of mind, the wise man in Ecclesiasticus admonished beforehand, saying: “In all your works remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” The thought of future death induces fears; fear in turn necessarily subdues the mind, and like a nail driven by a hammer fastens the proud movements of the flesh to the wood of the cross. In consideration of this view many of the pagan philosophers used to say that the whole life of a wise man is a certain continual meditation on death.

Hence Saint Jerome says: “Nothing will benefit you as much for temperance in all things as frequent meditation on this brief and uncertain life, which fails like a leaf carried off by the wind, and when it is not feared falls into death.” Therefore keep in mind the remembrance of your death, which by no counsel can you avoid, and you will easily be able to overcome all the movements of the flesh. For the one who sometimes thinks that he is going to die despises present things and, unless he is wholly foolish, will prepare himself for future things.

For I know, and I confess that I have partly experienced it, that the will of the human mind cannot long remain uniform in the holy fervor of spiritual life. Therefore it has pleased me to recall the memory of death before your eyes more often, so that the mind, shaken by fear of future things, may daily be renewed in a holier purpose. The eternal human spirit, bound to the body by the exercise of itself, is slight, fluctuating, and unstable: now it is snatched toward this thing, now moved toward another.

For this reason the Wisdom of God says through Solomon in Proverbs: “My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes keep my ways.” It is as though he said: Unless you have devoted the whole love of your heart to divine Wisdom, you will never be stable and confirmed in the keeping of the Lord’s commandments. For just as the heart is the first principle of movement in a human being, the greater is its guarding for the preservation of life, since if it is struck by an assault of diseases, the whole life of the body is already in danger. Hence it is written: “With all watchfulness keep your heart, because life proceeds from it.”

By the name of the heart, however, we understand the affection, whenever it inclines toward love of some thing and cleaves to it so strongly that it involves the whole will. Hence the psalmist says: “If riches abound, do not set your heart on them,” that is, do not love them with the desire of your will. What is more changeable than the human heart, which is altered by almost every single thought and can scarcely remain in the same purpose unless it restrains itself by a certain forceful intention? Saint David, not ignorant of this, said to the Lord: “But I said in my abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Lord, in your will you gave strength to my beauty. You turned your face away from me, and I was troubled.”

Therefore, my dearest brothers, if there is anyone among you who desires to serve our Lord and Savior in true purity of heart, if any man wishes to be a monk in deed as well as in name, let him learn first of all to trust not in himself but in God, and let him take care to renew the good purpose of holy life always by a new beginning, not only daily, but, as far as he can, at every hour and at every moment. For the fervor of spiritual life, to whose practical operation the institution of cloistered life is known to be wholly ordered, cannot long remain at the same point; because if it does not always advance toward what lies ahead, it necessarily runs back toward lower things and, what is worse, toward weaker ones.

In the wrestling-ground of spiritual exercise there is no stationary and lingering place of standing still, since it is the property of the human heart by its own natural motion always either to advance toward better things or to fall back again into what is evil. Hence if you do not advance in spiritual life, you necessarily fail; if you do not go forward toward holier things, you certainly go backward. Since experience has made this clear to us, I do not cease to admonish your charities to stretch out your desire in good life always toward what lies further ahead, lest you dangerously incur the peril of falling away there, where you did not know that the opportunity of becoming perfect lay open.

The fervor of spiritual purpose must be directed toward the love of God, and it cannot otherwise be preserved except in him. For the love of God is not absurdly compared to fire: just as fire, when the fuel of wood is withdrawn, is necessarily extinguished, so the love of God, if continual exercises of good works are lacking, is endangered as it dies away. Hence there is that saying of the Savior in the Gospel: “I came to cast fire upon the earth, and what do I wish except that it burn?” O monk, happy is that conscience in whose breast nothing burns except the love of Christ, which loves him above all things and despises all the studies of vanity. Now consider, brothers, your consciences with the greatest care, and examine exactly whether you love God above all things.
