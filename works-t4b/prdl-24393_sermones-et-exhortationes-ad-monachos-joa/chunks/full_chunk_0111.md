Hence there is no doubt that it comes about that many have erroneous consciences, because they love too much the pleasures of their own flesh. Every proud, lustful, and impure person, and anyone entangled in great or public crimes, clouds his conscience by deliberate perversity; and how erroneously he thinks about the future repayment of merits he frequently shows both by words and by deeds. A malicious affection perverts the judgment of rectitude, and the disordered concupiscence of the flesh clouds the understanding. Sometimes, moreover, excessive and immoderate faint-heartedness makes the conscience err; this evil is born partly from ignorance and partly from one’s own sensuality.

In this scrupulosity, however, let the monk follow the counsel either of the abbot or of some more experienced elder, and let him not rashly defend the opinion of his own head. Excessive scrupulosity is dangerous, because it casts the conscience into error and unhappily causes many to stumble. For everything that is done against conscience is said to build toward hell. Excessive perplexity of mind has also harmed many and driven them headlong into various errors. But he who is truly a monk submits himself in humility to the examination of his rector. For whoever, in doubts that arise, makes use of his superior’s counsel is not easily overthrown by the snares of demons. For the more the consciences of monks have been exposed to the examination of a wise prelate, the more secure and more brightly shining they will gleam.

Therefore, dearest brothers, care must be taken first of all that you have consciences free from every error; then pure from all vices; and finally adorned with the holy works of all the virtues. Therefore let the monk who desires to advance in truth guard his heart with every watchfulness; and, as far as can be done, let him place a barrier before his senses and not permit anything deadly to enter. But if by any occasion he recognizes that he has fallen into some sin, let him at once strive to wipe it away by true penance, and let him not grant time to the wicked seed in which it may grow stronger for the worse. While the wound is small, let a remedy be applied; for if it is neglected, it grows beyond measure.

Therefore, my brothers, strongly resist the first motions of demonic suggestion, and overcome the enemy while he is small and weak. But if the sensuality of the body has become altogether wanton, let it be brought back to obedience by harsh mortification, just as we know our holy father Benedict did when he rooted out the slippery wantonness of the flesh by the prickings of nettles and thorns. Then, too, if you diligently recall to memory the dreadful future punishments of the impious, you will easily overcome the unlawful motions of the flesh. For there is no one who does not fear pain more than he loves pleasure.

Also bring often to mind the joys of heavenly blessedness, to which you desire to come; for if you faithfully consider them, you will strongly lessen the fire of the flesh. Whatever delights in the world is corruptible and momentary; but the joys of eternal pleasure remain without end forever. It is therefore foolish, and a matter of extreme madness, to choose a passing advantage and to despise that good which remains eternally. He is judged foolish among men who prefers dross to gold; but he is held most senseless before God who sets temporal pleasure before everlasting goods.

He who loves a serene conscience should flee idleness, since, as Scripture says, every idle man is in desires, and idleness has taught much malice. The soul must be occupied with good exercises, and the fire of holy conduct, which burns with charity, must be nourished by continual use. For the idle man lives neither for himself nor for his neighbor, but is always given over to the belly or to sleep; he opens his mind to vice and keeps it closed and empty of virtues. Idleness, the kindler of lust, quickly extinguishes the fervors of spiritual devotion.

Abstinence also is especially necessary for a monk, so that his conscience may remain clean. For, as Saint Jerome says, a filled belly easily foams into lust; and he who nourishes his servant delicately will find him disobedient. The lover of serenity of conscience should be constant in holy prayers, since, as dogs flee a stone, so demons flee devout and continual prayer. Let him also carefully beware of acedia together with sadness of mind; and as soon as he senses some temptation creeping in, let him have recourse to the consolation of the Scriptures. Indeed, no foul thought will enter the mind which the word of God has filled. Whatever we do, whatever we think, whatever we perform, let us always know that the presence of God is at hand, and we shall easily overcome disordered delight. A good conscience never forgets the presence of God, but always meditates that its judge is present.
