Now it is your part, O Nicholas, to censure the vices of others with diligent admonition, and to show yourself free from vices, lest, if you censure crimes while yourself vicious, you make yourself deserving of condemnation. For no one can confidently rebuke in another what he remembers, with conscience as witness, that he has committed himself. Therefore he who judges the sins of others must be irreproachable, because one who commits in himself what he censures in others does not amend but provokes.

But today many among priests are found resembling hypocrites: they are enraged at those who offend in lighter matters, yet do not wish anyone to be indignant at them. Wretched is the condition of human frailty: sinner condemns sinner, and in the same crime passes an unequal sentence. I lie if it is not very often that an adulterer censures his neighbor for fornication; a judge vicious in many things condemns a few vices in another; and, so that he may be able to hide his own depravity, he makes public the offenses of all. Against him it is said by the Lord: Hypocrite, first cast out the beam that you carry in your own eye, and then you will see the splinter in another's. Who rebukes a thief, if he is sacrilegious? Why does a wicked man censure one who is vicious?

Every correction will be altogether fruitless when an equal baseness stains the life. Therefore, if you wish to avoid the reproach of hypocrites, beware of sinning in the same fault as the one you correct. Correction will never be useful among subjects while the one in authority bends his neck under public sins. Indeed he loses the right of correction when the conduct of the corrector is defiled by the vices of his subjects. The dignity and authority of the priesthood are debased if public iniquity has consumed the honorableness of one’s way of life. Therefore let every correction take its beginning in its own author, and only then proceed securely into public. Let him suspend the sentence of correction for a time if his own conscience reproves him in a similar crime.

The condition of believers is now corrupted for the most part, and as the people are, so also is their priest; all pursue avarice, from the greatest to the lowest. Woe to the sinful nation, which is always slipping into heavier things, while our judges are held fast by an equal offense. Ecclesiastical authority has now brought itself down to such misery that we are commanded to embrace the words of priests, not their deeds. For unhappy priests say of themselves, bearing witness to their own defilement: “Attend, O faithful Christians, to the words of our instruction; beware of our examples.” O words most worthy of everlasting confusion: as much as they ought to have instructed the people of God by teaching, so much by their perverse works they cause scandal. Untaught, crude, and now without discernment, they come to the priesthood.
