It is now your duty, O Nicolaus, to reprove the vices of others with diligent admonition, and to show yourself free from those vices, lest, if you reprove crimes while vicious yourself, you make yourself liable to condemnation. For no one can confidently rebuke in another what he remembers, with conscience as witness, that he himself has committed.

Therefore the man who judges the sins of others must be blameless, because one who commits in himself what he reproves in others does not correct but provokes. But today many among priests are found like hypocrites: they grow angry at brothers who offend in lighter matters, and do not wish anyone to be indignant at themselves. Wretched is the condition of human frailty: a sinner condemns a sinner, and in an equal crime he pronounces an unequal sentence.

I would be lying if I said that very often an adulterer does not accuse his neighbor of fornication; that a judge vicious in many things does not condemn a few vices in another; and that, so that he may be able to conceal his own depravity, he does not make known everyone else’s offenses. Against such a man it is said by the Lord: “Hypocrite, first cast out the beam which you carry in your own eye, and then you will see the speck in another’s.” Why does the sacrilegious man rebuke the thief? Why does the criminal accuse the vicious man? Every correction will be altogether fruitless when an equal foulness of life stains it.

Therefore, so that you may be free of the reproach of hypocrites, take care, as one who corrects another, not to fall into a similar sin. Correction will never be useful among subjects while the one who presides bends his neck beneath public sins. Indeed, the right of correction is lost when the conduct of the corrector is stained by the vices of his subjects. The authority of the priesthood grows cheap if public wickedness corrupts the honesty of one’s conduct. Therefore every correction should take its beginning in its own author, and only then will it proceed securely into public view. Let him whose own conscience gnaws at him over a similar crime suspend for a time the sentence of rebuke.

The condition of believers is now for the most part corrupt, and as the people are, so also is the priest: all are devoted to avarice, from the greatest down to the least. Woe to a sinful race, which is always sliding toward graver offenses, while it beholds its own judges guilty of the same offense. Ecclesiastical authority has now come to such misery that we are commanded to embrace the priest’s words, not his deeds. For unhappy priests teach this about themselves, bearing witness to their own defilement: “Attend, O faithful of Christ, to the words of our instruction; beware of our examples.” These words are worthy of everlasting shame: as much as they ought to have instructed the people of God by doctrine, so much do they scandalize them by perverse works. Unlearned, ignorant men, and now boys without discretion are advanced to the priesthood.
