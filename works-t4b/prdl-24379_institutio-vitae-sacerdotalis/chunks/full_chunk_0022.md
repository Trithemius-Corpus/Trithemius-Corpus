He who has been appointed ruler of souls must teach no less by example than by speech. For only the man who does what he teaches can rebuke avarice and luxury in word. He is a delicate master who disputes about fasting with a full belly. Therefore life must be sought before doctrine, because it teaches more fully by deed than by speech. For the demonstration of example is the most excellent kind of teaching. And Jesus himself first did, and afterward taught. For he teaches with the greatest authority whose speech is confirmed by the witness of his work. The ruler’s speech easily benefits his subjects if it is supported by honorable conduct. But he who teaches well and lives badly destroys himself by the sentence of his own mouth, and does not turn his subjects away from evil instruction.

Consider also how dangerous the care of souls is, especially in these times, when iniquity abounds everywhere and the charity of many has grown cold. For the days are evil, and men are slow toward justice; the whole world lies in wickedness, and faith is rare on the earth. Amid these things the word of God is despised by the people; even among religious persons discipline is neglected; priestly authority is contemned. Lay life, liable to many sins and crimes, neglects the remedies of divine mercy and, intent on deceits and frauds, despises the conduct of priests. And unless the ruler of souls diligently announces to the people committed to him their crimes and the penalties due for sins, he will also be guilty of their blood before God. And after these things God will bring all things into judgment, where he compels pastors of souls to render accounts for each one, and he will condemn more heavily those who were negligent in this world.

Therefore it is safer to flee humbly from the care and rule of souls than to usurp it arrogantly. For if each person in that dreadful judgment will scarcely suffice to render an account of his own life, how will he answer for so many thousands committed to him? O how well he lives who lives for himself. You see that bishops, abbots, pastors, or any prelates hold a good place among men in this age; but it is enough to consider how terrible the end of a negligent man will be. For a most severe judgment will be made upon those who are in charge, and the mighty will mightily suffer torments.

He passes well who lives simply, to whom nothing of earthly honor is committed, to whom no care of souls is entrusted. For pastoral care is never without danger as long as one lives in this world. And in this, too, there is a very great sea of dangers: when the soul has been separated from the body by death, the earthly pastor must be presented before God, the supreme pastor, to render account. A hard point of final damnation threatens, when an account of stewardship badly administered is demanded, and the blood of many sheep is required from the hand of one pastor. What, then, will an unskilled pastor be able to reply to the charges, since he was negligent in the care of the sheep? He must fall silent, because he has no answer.
