The opening of the treatise of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, of the order of the holy father Benedict, On the Life of Priests, addressed to Nicolaus Mernicensis, priest, begins. Chapter 1.

Although, dearest brother Nicolaus, you ask me and press me with frequent letters to commend to you, in writing, a form of living praiseworthily in the priesthood, you compel me to labor beyond the powers of my understanding, and expose me to be torn by the bites of rivals, who will judge me presumptuous for this: that I should wish to show you a way on which I myself have not yet learned to walk. And although the matter stands thus, I would rather undergo the judgment of rivalry than suffer loss in my charity toward you. For I have always cherished you with such affection that I have never been accustomed to deny you anything you asked.

Desiring, therefore, to satisfy your requests, I have wished to commend to you a rule of life from the writings of the saints: not because it will be wholly sufficient for your state in every respect, but so that, as some kind of beginning, it may lead you, if you wish, to honorable conduct. First, then, I admonish you to weigh the state of your appointed way of life from the definition of the name. For we are called clerics, all of us who have been specially chosen into the Lord’s portion. Let the word of our name, then, be interpreted, and we shall understand the character of the honorable conduct enjoined upon us. For cleros in Greek is interpreted in Latin as “lot” or “portion.” From cleros, therefore, come clerici: those chosen into the Lord’s portion.

If, then, we recognize that we have been called into God’s portion, why do we still serve the world? Why do we seek on earth the inheritance of dying men, we who await God, promising himself to us as inheritance? For in the Old Testament we read that the Lord commanded the priests and Levites not to receive an inheritance among the children of Israel, their brothers, because he himself was their inheritance. Therefore, if you consider yourself to be a cleric, you ought to walk more honorably than other people by the excellence of your conduct, and by no means love the perishable riches of this world. But if you subject your soul to the inheritance of the age, you will be placed outside the Lord’s portion, among the foolish.

For the Lord will not deem you worthy to receive among his elect if, going after gold, you have resolved to love the world. Greed is most shameful in a priest of Christ, for it makes a servant of God into a minister of idols. If it has once seized your soul, it never again lets it go in possession of itself. For no one can serve two discordant masters. “You cannot,” he says, “serve God and Mammon.” You will not be able to be a good cleric if you are greedy. But I know, and have learned by long experience in living with you, that nature has made you sparing. Beware lest you turn the gift of grace into

fuel for greed. For those who wish to become rich in this world often fall into the temptations of the devil. The minister of God must be set apart from care for the world, because the mind cannot go freely to God if it has been entangled in earthly cares. For this reason we wear shaven crowns on the head, so that, once superfluities have been cast away, we may prepare a free ascent to God in thought. For the hairs of the head signify the superfluities of earthly activity. And just as the hairs of the head arise from an excess of moisture, so the anxieties of the world grow from the love of money.

Therefore, when we shave the hairs of the head in the form of a crown, we are admonished that, being applied to God’s service, we should despise all cares of the world. And just as the head is the more eminent part of the body, so the mind is the highest power of reason. Therefore we wear a crown on the head so that, released from earthly cares, we may be lifted more freely in mind to the contemplation of God. Hence we also who are cloistered, who are called monks, adorned with very broad crowns, leave fewer hairs on the head, so that we may show all the more that, dead to the active life, we are free for the contemplation of heavenly things.

Now attend to the definition of the name, and take care to correspond, with worthy merits of life, to the lot of the heavenly Creator. Guard your heart with all vigilance, lest desire sink you into the depth of greed; lest the burning of lust drag your soul down with sinners into the filth of wantonness; lest the arrogance of pride draw you with itself to hell, to be tortured without end. Beware lest through the cares of this world you fall away from the portion of God. Beware lest you lose the reputation of a good name. Beware lest you go on the way of those who despise God. Consider the falls of many priests around you, and always fear a similar danger for yourself. Nowhere among mortals is one lived more safely than if danger is always feared. Hence it is written: “Blessed is the man who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil.” For truly, the one who fears danger takes care; the one who neglects it falls.

The minister of Christ, the priest of God, must be a man, not a child, so that he may know and understand what sort of burden he has taken up, for the administration of which he will render an account in the strict judgment of God. Let him therefore be prudent, sober, chaste, and quiet. Let him be modest, kindly, and gentle, so that he who is appointed teacher of an untaught people may suffer no contempt because of his own conduct. It belongs to a priest, when questioned, to answer concerning the law; steadfastly to rebuke the vices of those subject to him through the words of holy preaching; and to fear no adversities for love of the truth.
