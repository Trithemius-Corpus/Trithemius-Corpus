On Dwelling in the Cell and the Works to Be Done in It.

There are three places in which a devout monk and lover of God ought often to be found: namely, in church, in his cell, and, at the proper time, in the refectory. But he does not permit wandering about the monastery, because it is written of the wicked, the reprobate, and the gyrovagues: “The wicked walk about in a circuit.” But of the just man Jeremiah says: “It is good for a man, when he has borne the yoke of the Lord from his youth; he will sit solitary and keep silence, because he has lifted himself above himself.”

There are five chief works of the cell. The first is to attend to commands; the second is to read usefully; the third is to apply oneself at suitable times to meditation; the fourth is to pray frequently; the fifth is to rest fittingly. We shall speak briefly of these in order.

In the cell, first, you should attend to commands.

First, then, while you are in the dwelling of the cell, learn to attend to commands and not to prefer your empty devotions to obedience. For Scripture says: “Obedience is better than the sacrifices of fools, and to hearken is better than to offer fat.” Therefore, if something has been enjoined on you to do in the cell, do not transgress the paths of obedience on any occasion, but strive diligently to perform the work imposed on you for the love of our Lord Jesus Christ and the merit of holy obedience. Hence Hugh, a lover of quiet, says: “Be gladly in your cell, and always do something in it that may build you up, and that has been enjoined by your superior. Consider that it is not within your right to go out of it without

Second, in the cell one ought to read usefully.

It is especially fitting for a monk who is in his cell always to read something good, and to occupy himself usefully in all things. For sacred reading overcomes the temptations of the devil, calms the fires of the flesh, makes one despise the world, and raises the mind to heavenly things. For Jerome, lover of divine Scripture, truly said: “Love knowledge of the Scriptures, and you will not love the vices of the flesh.” Not all things are to be read by all monks, since one who is still weak in spirit must be instructed only in simple teachings. And Hugh says to the monk who loves simplicity: “Do not read in order to be considered more learned, nor read curiosities that do not build up, because vain reading begets vain thoughts and extinguishes the devotion of the mind.” Therefore simpler monks should read simple things, not lofty ones; devout things, not curious ones, so that those who, because of slowness of understanding, are not learned in higher matters may not become dissolved in vain things. But we do not forbid suitable studies to those who are stronger in spirit, because all things are clean to the clean, and nothing that is read harms those who are truly devout. Nevertheless, let no one claim for himself a strength of mind that he does not have, but let it be placed in the abbot’s discretion, so that he may see who is fit for freedom in reading.

Third, in the cell you should apply yourself at suitable times to meditations.

Just as a flower springs from a root, so meditation is generated from reading. If the reading has been vain, the meditation too will be fruitless and useless. For the fruit of reading is meditation, and the offspring of meditation is prayer. Therefore take care to apply yourself at suitable times to holy meditations, so that you may be able also sometimes to raise your mind, through the fervor of divine love, into contemplation. Fourth, in the cell you should pray frequently.

The holy apostle admonishes us to devote ourselves to prayer without interruption, because just as breath is always necessary for us for the life of the flesh, so continual prayer is altogether necessary for the health of the mind. For as we are instructed toward good by readings, so by prayers we are delivered from evil. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: “Pray, that you may not enter into temptation.” Prayer feeds the hungry mind, extinguishes the fire of carnal desire, instructs morals, and drives away demons. If you wish always to be with God, always pray. Let your prayer be pure, simple, and clean; let it be poured out to God through the fervor of love, and it will not be able to be fruitless.

Fifth, in the cell you should rest fittingly.

He rests best who commits no evil. He rests well who despises all the occupations of the world. Be quiet in your cell, so that you do not wander here and there in any thought. Be pure and tranquil in mind, so that you may deserve to obtain tasteful wisdom. For holy Prosper says in verse: “Wisdom imbues a tranquil mind, free from cares, and will be the guest of a peaceful breast.” And again he says: “Those who are quiet can search the law in whatever way they can, if hearts free from the clamor of the world are empty. Therefore, that the mind intent on pious studies may be helped, let divine love conquer fleshly quarrels.” Remain, then, quiet in your cell, and by loving the truth you will be able to know it. For a tranquil mind cannot be receptive of divine wisdom when the body is restless. Hence Hugh says: “Be quiet in the dormitory, so that another may not be disturbed by you.
