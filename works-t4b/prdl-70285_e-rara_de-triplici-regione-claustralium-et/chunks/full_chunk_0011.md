He is also to be called obstinate who casts aside the fear of the Lord; who trusts too much in his own judgment; who, in any matters to be done, admits no counsels of the wise. And indeed, among pastors of souls, very many are disordered by this sickness of mind: they either spend the resources of the monastery too improvidently, or preserve them with too much tenacity. For their flatterers they prepare the most sumptuous feasts; but to others, to whom they are averse, they deny even the smallest things. In the guesthouse or in their abbacy, pastors depraved by disordered conduct sometimes eat to the point of gluttony and always drink the best wine; but to the poor monks, who by watching and singing bear the burden of the day and the heat, they scarcely and meagerly grant necessities.

Sixth, a prelate of monks is shown to be very disordered if, bold against God, he is lifted up in pride by making light of his judgments. For indeed, since the pastor cannot be corrected by his subjects, he is referred by the holy father Benedict to the judgment of God, as is found in many places of the Rule. “Let the abbot know,” he says, “that he must render an account to God for all his judgments.” And again: “The ordained abbot should always consider what burden he has undertaken, and to whom he is going to render an account of his stewardship.” And again he says: “As soon as vices begin to arise, let him cut them off with charity, not with tyranny.” And in another place he admonishes, saying: “Yet the abbot himself should do all things with the fear of God and the observance of the holy Rule, knowing without doubt that he will render an account of all his judgments to God, the most just judge.” But the disordered ruler of monks, like an asp with stopped-up ears, despises hearing this threat of the most holy lawgiver; he scorns the patience of God’s long-suffering; he thinks nothing of the examination to come, until at last the judge, angry with him, says to his face: “These things you have done, and I was silent. Now, therefore, render an account of your stewardship. Give back to me the sheep of my inheritance, and restore to me without delay the souls once entrusted to you in the body. Return their number entire. If even one of them has perished through your negligence, your soul will never behold my face forever.

What then will you answer to the supreme pastor, useless ruler, when not only is the blood of those who perish required from you, but an account is also demanded for those who made too little progress? Behold, now you walk in the worst security, despising God’s examination; you think that in the body all things that please you are lawful for you, and you never consider to whom you must someday render an account for all things. Soon the Lord of all will come, to whom you do not fear to show yourself an unfaithful minister, and whose honor you do not fear to usurp for yourself. Unless meanwhile you do penance, unless you amend your life for the better, you will burn without end with the demons in hell.”

Seventh, it happens that a pastor is disordered toward his subjects if, neglecting the common utility, he seeks his own advantage with a vicious intention; if, presiding in the monastery, he serves not God but his own interests, while he rejoices that he is prelate in order to obtain ruinous liberties of the flesh, and fraudulently provides for himself in the future from the resources of the monastery. This disorder is especially visible in those who resign their office on some pretext, to whom, as payment for their stewardship, just as if they had been bishops, an annual pension from the resources of the monastery is assigned. This is not the way of those who seek the Lord; this is not the path of the ancient monks. When you resign the pastoral office for God’s sake, resign it simply. Do not demand anything beyond food and clothing, if you do not wish to sin with proprietors. You are a monk; you were a monk before you became abbot; you will remain a monk after the office. Why, then, do you demand a pension, when you know that you are a monk? For just as the vice of simony is committed by buying prelacies, so by demanding a pension for the resignation of an abbacy, one sins against the Rule. What then? Do we condemn those who, resigning the office of pastoral care without fault, have received annual provisions from the resources of the monastery? Far from it. We do not forbid necessities, since we deny superfluities. Nevertheless, one who is a true monk and lover of God is content with the regular provision even after an abbacy.
