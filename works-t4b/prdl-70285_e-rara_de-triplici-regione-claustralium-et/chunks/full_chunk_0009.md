Second, a pastor of souls becomes disordered toward his subjects if his mind is too greedily occupied with temporal affairs, if he has greater concern for secular business than for the souls committed to him. This excessive care for temporal things holy father Benedict forbids in the Rule, and he rightly judges rational souls to be preferred to all the things of the world. “Above all,” he says, “let the abbot not, by neglecting or making little of the salvation of the souls entrusted to him, have greater concern for transient, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always consider that he has undertaken to govern souls, for which he must render an account.” Then, when the pious father gives assurance about bodily necessities, provided that he loves God with a pure mind, he says: “And lest perhaps he complain of lesser means, let him remember what is written: First seek the kingdom of God and his justice, and all these things will be added to you. And again: Nothing is lacking to those who fear God.”

But a disorderly pastor of monks is seduced by the desire of greed; blinded in mind, as though without perception, he casts the fear of the Lord away from himself; with Ahab, sold under sin, he runs to the aid of wickedness. He is deprived of confidence in the necessities promised by the holy father as consequences; he is occupied in external things more than befits a monk. Driven henceforth by the goads of greed, he despises the commandments of God and of holy mother Church; he does not observe feast days; he is wholly immersed in earthly affairs; he meditates on nothing except temporal things. Foolishly, with Saul, he transgresses the commandment of God; foolishly, contrary to obedience, he thinks himself expiated by sacrifices. Finally, grown gross and enlarged either by secular possessions or by secular cares, he commonly slips into every kind of wantonness and dissolution; he detests the observance of regular discipline; and, while he fears being restrained from evil pursuits by the authority of the chapter, he seeks the dissolution of holy union. Then, made reprobate with Saul, he calls upon the counsel of the pythoness; he declares the obedience of reform damnable for his monastery; he desires to be absolved as quickly as possible from submission to the holy confederation; to this end he tells many things falsely and mendaciously. that monastic observance suffer shipwreck, since the love of God regularly keeps the good within discipline, and fear of correction restrains the wicked from evil works. Whoever strives to evade the unity of the chapter imitates the devil. Thus the emperor of the Bulgars forbids the Christians subject to him, on pain of death, to hold an assembly in church, although he grants them license to believe whatever they wish. For that crafty man knows that the sincerity of Christian faith cannot long endure if the office of public exhortation happens to be absent. The disorderly and carnal abbot strives, as far as he can, to imitate this adversary of the Christian faith, for he resists the authority of so necessary a synodal chapter. For the authority of the chapter alone is what maintains in the order the observance of regular discipline; if it is despised, no integrity of monastic life will remain.

But the inhabitant of the lowest region, the disorderly abbot, devises innumerable excuses against regular observance: he falsely claims that the conditions of his monastery are contrary to it; he complains of ancient privileges of his own; he is indignant that his monastery should be subject to anyone. But woe to disorderly men through whom even one monastery should happen to be lost from this most holy congregation. Finally, this plague of greed makes an abbot not only disorderly toward his subjects, but also opposed to himself and wholly turned away from God. For cupidity is the root of all evils; taking away the servant of Christ, it makes him a minister of the devil. Therefore the abbot who has submitted his mind to this vice shows himself to be not a pastor but a hireling.

He always pants after earthly profits; he cares nothing for the progress of souls. A book of sacred reading is rarely, or never, seen in his hands; registers of revenues are turned over more often. He joins field to field; always burying gold and silver in a chest as he gathers it, and while he desires too greatly that his monastery abound in temporal goods, he prepares plunder for robbers. Often [unclear] devours the monies of monasteries, which the excessive greed of the monks brings into the cenobium. Woe to you, greedy pastor of cloisterers, who have greater concern for brute animals than for those entrusted to you
