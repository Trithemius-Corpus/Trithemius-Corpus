The ninth degree of pride is described as murmuring or impatience, which for the most part rules carnal monks. For truly, when a man whose confession is feigned is touched by one slight reproach or penance, he at once rises up into open pride; the deceit of feigned humility is uncovered, and the false mind can no longer hide itself, but is compelled to bring out into public whatever lies hidden within. From then on, having lost command of himself, the proud monk in the monastery murmurs at everything; he contumaciously accuses the judgment of the pastor, however just it may be; he gnashes with his teeth; when stirred, he grows angry; without reason, like a madman, he is thrown into disorder. But the proud monk, given over to murmuring, having despised the fourth degree of humility, falls into the tenth descent of pride, through which he runs toward hell.

The tenth degree of pride follows murmuring, and is called rebellion. Proud and carnal monks fall into this, who, still dwelling in the lowest region, devote themselves to murmurings. Hence Bernard says: Unless divine compassion looks upon the murmurer, so that at last, converted, which is very difficult for such men, he quietly acquiesces in the judgments of others, he soon becomes brazen and shameless, and falls the more disastrously into this degree, in proportion as, in despair, he does not restrain the movement of his soul. He always advances toward worse things; and he who before had arrogantly despised his brothers in secret now openly shows what is still more serious: that he despises his master.

But the censure of divine judgment does not allow this degree of pride to pass long unpunished. For monks rebellious against their pastors often apostatize from the order; others, because of grave crimes, occupy prisons; some, expelled from monasteries, wander and live as miserable exiles; many, horribly struck by the Lord, hasten in despair to the lower world. For it is written: He who resists authority opposes the ordinance of God.

And again the Lord says concerning the prelates of the faithful: He who despises you despises me. What will you say to these things, rebellious monks? Will you cite laws to me, by which you corrupt the truth? But I dare to say what perhaps you are not ashamed to hear. In the strict judgment of God, presumption of law will not save you, rebellious monk, who now proudly despise the obedience of subjection. Law commands obedience; it requires humble subjection in the order.

Rebellious monks consequently fall into the eleventh degree of pride, which is the freedom to sin: those who despise not only the corrections and exhortations of the brothers, but also those of the prelates. For truly, when rebellion is taken up against the teacher, boldness in sinning is generated, because one who despises the authority of correction now commits every crime as if by license. The love of God voluntarily restrains good monks in the monastery from sins, and fear holds back the weak. But where neither the love of God nor fear of correction penetrates a monk, the mind, through rebellion and the habit of sinning, is already approaching hell. For Scripture says: When the impious man has come into the depths of evils, he contemns. What does he contemn? He contemns God; he despises men; in despair he does not know how to seek the salvation of his soul.

Therefore they fall into the twelfth degree of pride who, through boldness in sinning, run into an evil habit. This is the nearest descent to hell; this is the path of hell; this is the swift road to the region covered with the darkness of death. Whoever has once fallen into this degree of the habit of sinning rises again only by the strong hand of God’s mercy, because when habit is not resisted, necessity of sinning is generated. For he always advances in worse malice, and always, falling, drops more deeply.

Behold, these are the twelve degrees of pride through which one descends to hell. Whoever desires to escape them should always strive to keep himself in the observance of humility. In these twelve evils every indignity of vices overshadows the monk in the monastery; it causes him to be disordered in himself; in his conduct it changes a man into a most savage beast. The end of these things is destruction; and unless, before the death of the flesh, the remedy of repentance intervenes, the progress of the evil degrees will leave the monk to burn without end.
