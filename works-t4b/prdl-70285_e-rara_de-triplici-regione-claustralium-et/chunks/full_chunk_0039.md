The first root of the fear and love of God is intent meditation on the incomprehensible divine majesty: the more curiously it is investigated by man, the less it is comprehended. For the prophet says: Truly you are a hidden God. This meditation begets wonder at divine perfection; wonder causes reverence; from reverence arises fear of majesty; and from fear worship is produced. Meditation on the highest goodness is divided, according to our purpose, into seven considerations, which are not unfittingly adapted to the seven days of the week, or even to the seven canonical hours. And although that highest and most perfect goodness abundantly suffices for us as matter for meditation, nevertheless, because it belongs to few to be able to ascend to invisible things without visible things, we wish through known things to raise the minds of the weak to things unknown. For the holy Apostle says: The invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are perceived, being understood through the things that have been made; his everlasting power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.

First, therefore, the wondrous power of God is praiseworthy in the principal work of creation: for he who was able to bring forth all things out of nothing created, by his will alone, things so great, so solid, so varied, and so noble. From this consideration arises wonder at divine power, through which man is moved to the fear of God.

Second, the provident wisdom of God is praiseworthy, because, knowing all things, he orders the universe and governs by his providence all things that he has created. He knows all things, understands all things; by his counsel he disposes all things; by his command he contains all things. For, as a certain lover of worldly wisdom said, God is a sphere whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere. He is within all things and is not enclosed; he is outside all things and is not shut out; he is beneath all things and is not oppressed; he is above all things and is not borne up. He includes all things, for although he is wholly everywhere, he is confined in no place; great and immense, he is bounded by no quantity. He orders all things as he wills; stable in himself and always immovable, he changes the vicissitudes of things; without passion of mind he mercifully prepares eternal rewards for the good; without disturbance he condemns the unjust in wrath. Great without magnitude, immense without quantity, he has set a limit to things; governing the world, he disposes all things.

Third, the merciful clemency of God is praiseworthy in our redemption.

It is praiseworthy because the man whom he created in his own image, fallen so wretchedly into the pit of damnation, he restored so mercifully. O what shall I, a little worm, indeed nothing in comparison with my maker, render to the supreme and incomprehensible majesty, who, when I did not exist, created me by his power, and, when I had perished, became man for me and, dying on the cross, redeemed me? What shall I repay to you, mediator of God and men, Jesus, savior of the world, who, though you are God exalted and inestimable, became man for me, the vilest, and were willing to undergo things so vile and unworthy? Behold, my heart speaks to you; all my inward parts bow toward you, because no voice can express what, concerning your most innocent passion, you grant your faithful inwardly to feel in their hearts.

Fourth, in the work of our justification, the kindly patience of God is praiseworthy, because he not only mercifully bears with sinners, but also calls those who are to be justified to repentance by the promises of eternal joys. Who could suffice to praise this clemency of the most indulgent father, who so mercifully justifies sinners returning to him, gives after a fall the grace of meriting, ordains manifold occasions for living well, and supplies his sacraments to the penitent as certain vessels of grace? Finally, for compunction, instruction, consolation, and thanksgiving, he pours the Holy Spirit himself into his faithful, making friends and sons out of enemies. He does not consider what sort of sinner a person was before; he seeks only that thereafter he remain faithful to him. Great is the clemency of divine compassion, which so mercifully receives the penitent to pardon, and even promises them great rewards after guilt.

Fifth, the deep justice of God is praiseworthy in the damnation of the reprobate, because he permits no evil to pass unpunished, depriving the sinner of the good which, by sinning, the fool does not fear to lose. Finally, to speak in our manner, the supreme purity of God is shown praiseworthy in this: it abominates every sin and vice as a putrid corpse.
