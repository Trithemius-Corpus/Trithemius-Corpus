From these, some judge and despise their abbot in secret; others contradict him to his face. And while they neglect to show reverence to their shepherd, they throw the whole observance of discipline into confusion. Pride is always familiar to them, and shamefulness, the friend of the flesh; for those who voluntarily withdraw themselves from affection for their shepherd fall into abominable temptations and the worst crimes. For whoever despises the vicar of Christ in the monastery hands over to the devil the power to rage against him, and disrupts the union of the mystical body.

Fifth, carnal monks, disordered toward their shepherd, derogate from his authority, when they think that nothing is permitted to him in the monastery unless they themselves have consented. With neck held high they despise the virtue of humility; they spurn regular subjection; they disdain to yield to their betters. They wish to thrust themselves into all business and causes, and to usurp for themselves the chief place in counsel, so that the abbot dares to do or arrange nothing unless the matter first proceeds from their consent. But what does the lawgiver Benedict say in the Rule? “Let the abbot,” he says, “after hearing the counsel of the brothers, consider it within himself, and do what he has judged more useful.” And again: “Let the brothers give counsel with all humble submission, so that they do not presume insolently to defend what has seemed good to them; rather, let it depend on the abbot’s judgment, so that all may obey what he has judged to be more salutary.” And again, concerning the appointment of the prior, he says: “We have thought it expedient, for the preservation of charity and peace, that the ordering of his monastery depend upon the abbot’s decision.”

This ordering indeed concerns the appointment and removal of all officials of the monastery; it requires not the consent of the brothers but the counsel of those who fear God. Finally, the Rule commands that all things be done according to the will of the abbot, and that absolutely nothing be presumed which has not been enjoined by him. Carnal monks, rash usurpers of their own will, try in various ways to break this authority of his

and to disturb the order and charity of the whole monastery, since they think the rector should do nothing except what they themselves consider right. But tell me, proud cultivators of the lowest region, you who always establish your own will, you who usurp for yourselves another’s right: is this the virtue of obedience, to refuse to yield to the command of a superior, but to cling obstinately to your own judgment? The holy father did not establish in the Rule that you should live in this way, for he commanded you in all things to follow the will of the abbot. For obedience is the monk’s greatest virtue, which our divine father wished to commend to such an extent that he said a good work lacks merit without the abbot’s will. Hence, giving command, he concludes thus: “Therefore let all things be done with the abbot’s will, and let no one in the monastery follow the will of his own heart.”

But if the prudence of the rector does not seem sufficient for the ordering of the monastery, this must be imputed to the carnal monks, who voluntarily set over themselves someone unfit. Folly always hates prudence, and wicked monks detest a wise abbot. Therefore those who knowingly set a fool over themselves will necessarily be subject to his ordering and will. Finally, no incompetence of a shepherd ought to extinguish the obedience of subjects, since the Rule orders us to obey even an unprofitable one. For it says: “The commands of the abbot are to be obeyed in all things, even if he himself, which God forbid, acts otherwise.”

But when we rebuke disobedience, carnal monks object: “We do these things with a right and good intention, since we see the substance of the monastery perishing through bad governance.” But the Apostle contradicts you, since evils are not to be done so that good may come, nor is the subjection of humility to be abandoned because of damage to a temporal thing. For where one must undergo either the loss of temporal substance or danger to regular obedience, it is better for the substance to perish than for the purity of obedience to be harmed.
