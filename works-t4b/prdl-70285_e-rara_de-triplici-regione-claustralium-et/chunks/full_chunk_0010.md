An ox and an ass fall sick, and you show them compassion; a brother is swallowed up by sadness, and you do not come to his aid. You, then, are a hireling, not a shepherd: you despise the infirmities of your brothers with a stubborn heart; you do not help those set in bitterness. Third, a ruler is disordered toward his subjects when he places outside, not without danger, those whom he ought carefully to have kept within the enclosures of the monastery. For truly, when a weak cenobite is exposed to the governance of nuns or to secular churches, the integrity of regular observance is violated, because one who, still dwelling in the lowest region, has not learned to subject the flesh to the spirit does not know how to preside over others without danger to himself.

But the disordered shepherd who, for the sake of some advantage in temporal affairs, is not afraid to expose his monks to so great a danger is guilty of all the souls struck down on such an occasion, and before the inward judge he is bound to make restitution for all of them. It does not belong to a monk’s profession to govern secular churches; it is not expedient for a weak cenobite to preside over nuns. But what then? Ought a monk to oppose the command of his shepherd if he wishes him to reside with nuns or in secular churches? For the Rule commands that nothing is to be preferred to obedience. I truly do not know what I should answer to this question, so that I may not incur the danger that is seen to threaten on both sides. For if I speak the truth, I shall seem to weaken the subjection of obedience; if I consent to exposing monks to danger, I confess that I sin. Yet I shall say briefly what I think, although I know that the truth will give birth to hatred. Just as a monk, by the necessity of his profession, is simply bound to obey his shepherd in all things that are according to the monastic Rule, so a prelate cannot compel a monk, without that monk’s own will, to things that are beyond the Rule, especially when danger to salvation intervenes. But to govern churches, to preside over women, to reside outside the monastery: this is not to have obeyed according to the Rule. Do not be angry with me, fathers, if I have spoken the truth; do not contract your brows

if I have expressed what seems right to me. You, nevertheless, will do what seems right to you, even if I protest. Yet I beg you: do not expose your monks to dangers to chastity; do not violently compel anyone to the governance of nuns and parish churches, since you can find many who ambitiously seek these offices. Obedience is indeed good, but it must in no way be preferred to truth and to the salvation of the soul. For when danger to salvation is at issue, it is better to set obedience aside by humbly excusing oneself than to suffer shipwreck of chastity. Woe to the prelates of monks who incorporate secular churches into their monasteries, who widen the bosom of desire with the torches of avarice, because while they think they are benefiting posterity, they damn many through the occasion.

Fourth, a ruler of monks becomes disordered toward his subjects if he is found turbulent and anxious. In a shepherd of souls, pious and prudent discretion is very necessary, lest either zeal for discipline be excessive or the measure of compassion be relaxed by too much laxity. But a disordered ruler sometimes, through indiscreet fury, considers himself a tyrant of monks and does not know himself as the father and companion of the humble. What can be more savage than this kind of rule, what more criminal than this kind of power, where not God, not reason, not understanding, but a beast holds sway? For truly, where God and understanding govern authority, the judgments of those who preside do not depart from the rectitude of the law of nature. But a disordered and anxious ruler is infected by the various passions of beasts; he always suspects evil of the monks, and while he directs the manner of his rule wrongly, he is always anxious and, with Herod, greatly fears losing his kingdom.

Fifth, a shepherd of cloistered men is said to be disordered toward the monks if, in dispensing the goods of the monastery, he is considered either excessive or obstinate. He must be called excessive if, in distributing, he has been prodigal, or too lavish through indiscreet generosity.
