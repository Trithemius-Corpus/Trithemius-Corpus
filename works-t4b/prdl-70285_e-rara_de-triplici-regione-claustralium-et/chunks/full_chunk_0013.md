For Scripture says: “He who is evil to himself, to whom will he be good?” The right order of the human condition established that the flesh should be subjected to the spirit; the spirit is commanded to be subject to him from whom it received the form of being. But among carnal minds the order of reason is perverted, since the flesh dominates the spirit; and reason, which according to the power of understanding implanted in it ought to have governed everything, is suffocated by the pleasures of the flesh and does not hasten toward uprightness of judgment through command.

Carnal monks, therefore, are disorderly toward themselves through the vice of curiosity, because by prying they curiously judge another’s life, and do not amend their own, stained with every vice. They do not know how to correct great crimes in themselves, although they proudly rebuke others who sin in the smallest things. They carefully consider the speck in a brother’s eye, and excuse the beam clinging in their own eyes as though it were nothing. They subject all their affections to sensuality, and, despising the judgment of right reason, live carnally like beasts; passion often stirs them to anger, and the fury of envy inflames them against their neighbor. With an anxious mind they always demand vengeance against the brothers; blinded by excessive rage, they often bring it back not without harm to themselves. But disorderly citizens cannot remain long hidden in secret, because the more they remain unknown to themselves through elation of mind, the more they are made manifest to others through blind judgment. For every proud and disorderly man is said first to be ignorant of himself, since if he truly knew himself, he would in no way be proud. Since, however, disorder of oneself takes its origin from pride, we cannot describe this plague better than if we take care to disclose its roots, descending by degrees to hell. For just as the perfection of the spiritual life is contained in twelve degrees of humility, so the source of all monastic disorder is contained in twice-six degrees of pride.

The first degree of pride, then, is called curiosity. By it monks badly ordered toward themselves neglect their own life and morals, and continually inquire into the deeds or words of others. At every time they accuse the negligences of the brothers and rebuke the life of the pastor, because the more curiously they desire to know external matters belonging to others, the farther they withdraw from themselves. But the vice of curiosity is most pernicious to monks,

because once it has invaded their mind, stripping it of all virtues, it makes it a dwelling place of vices. It is the surest sign of infirmity to examine the deeds of others and, apart from the reason of one’s office, to be occupied with others’ affairs. For the monk who, according to the definition of his name, is commanded to care for himself alone, searches out the deeds of others not without manifest danger to his salvation. Whoever subjects himself to the vice of curiosity soon descends into the second degree of pride. For one always disposes a person toward another, and no single sin rules alone over proud monks.

The second degree of pride, therefore, is lightness of mind and the unstable occupation of a wandering heart. Carnal monks fall into this degree of disorderly conduct through various affections, which they have sensually contracted from the vice of curiosity. But the true cenobite must remain steadfast in his holy purpose, because when lightness of mind is admitted, the constancy of virtue is scattered in the heart. It is the mark of a disorderly mind’s judgment to run about among various images of things, and always to beget new forms of sensual affections.

The third degree of pride is called empty joy, which is begotten from the lightness of a disorderly man’s mind. Carnal monks compose for themselves various images of affections; when these occur pleasantly to sensuality, they rejoice, but when it turns out otherwise, they contract grave indignation and slip into various temptations. But that joy is not true which does not take its beginning from decorum, nor does a joy long persevere which does not have its source in the love of God. And therefore disorderly men, tossed about by vain joy, fall into foolish boasting, when they falsely ascribe great things to themselves.

The fourth degree of pride, then, as we have said, arises from empty joy; it is called foolish boasting. Carnal monks fall into this when they magnify and exalt themselves before others. If they have done anything good, they boldly bring it forward in public, often falsely inventing even things they have not done. They desire to seem holy, learned, prudent, and noble, and they invent many things by the craftiness of their wit, so that they may appear glorious outwardly to men, though inwardly they know they are empty of the merit of virtue.
