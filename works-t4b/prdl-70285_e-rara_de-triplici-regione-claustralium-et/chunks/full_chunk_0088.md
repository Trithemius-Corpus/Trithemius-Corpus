For whoever loves God with his whole heart does not admit displeasure against his faithful brother. The monk, indeed, ought to be established in such elevation of mind that, whatever a brother may have committed against him, by weighing the nature of that brother’s soul he cannot be moved.

Let the monk’s conduct toward those who injure him be gentle.

Third, the monk’s conduct toward those who injure him ought to be gentle, because only then is it well done, if regard is always had for the common future happiness and companionship. To attain this gentleness of soul, let our cenobite learn often to recall to mind the conduct of the holy fathers, and faithfully consider with how much joy they received injuries. Let him strive above all, as far as possible, to imitate our Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who not only endured the injuries and insults of the wicked with a most gentle heart, but also prayed to the Father for his persecutors, that they might not perish. Imitating this example of the Savior, the holy fathers were accustomed, for the love of God, to receive injuries patiently and gently, and, what is more, to love those who injured them. To this end let the cenobite recall to mind the degrees of gentleness, which are briefly contained and included in these verses:

Adapt. Strengthen. Being gentle, bear contrary things.
Preserve. Restrain. Rest wholly within yourself.
Correct prudently. Be merciful. Give wisely.
Pray by setting yourself there, by desiring martyrdom.

For if humility has possessed the mind of the cenobite, it makes him love injuries. The degrees of humility, however, are contained in these words:

The twelve degrees of humility.

The first of the degrees of the righteous fears the Almighty.
The second puts reins on the enticements of the flesh.
The third reverently subjects himself to the abbot.

Let the monk’s conduct toward secular people be modest.

Fourth, the monk’s conduct toward secular people ought to be modest, so that neither his familiarity with them is excessive nor his withdrawal indiscreet. Hence Hugh says: With respect to people, as far as lies in you, avoid everything that can be a bad example to them, just as it befits a faithful servant of God to advance the honor of his Lord in all things, and to guard against dishonoring him. And do not be familiar with any woman, even a holy one. For if they are discreet, you will please them more by this; but if they are importunate, they are all the more to be avoided. Do not be suspicious. Be attentive to the weak, humble in acts of service; be very truthful in words. Let your words be as an oath, and you will edify others. Love poverty. Be chaste in all things, and everywhere fearful of God.

But in founding all virtues and good morals, set before yourself that most brilliant and most perfect example of all brightness, of the life and conduct of our Lord Jesus Christ, who

was sent from heaven for this, that he might lead us on the way of virtues and teach us by his own example, just like himself. Therefore, the more a monk has taken care to conform himself to him by imitation on the way of virtues, the more like and nearer to him he will doubtless be in the glory of eternal happiness. Whatever you do, always look to him, and see how he conducted himself in all things according to the Gospel and your estimation. For since he is best, he conducted himself best in all things. Say, then, to yourself: If my Lord Jesus were present, what do you think he would do in this matter, or what would he now say? Surely he would both act and speak in the best way; and I ought to imitate him in all things according to my strength.

General conclusion of all spiritual exercises.

Consideration XII.

Wishing at last to put an end to this work, we say that the completion of all spiritual exercises consists in two things. First, in the knowledge and feeling of one’s own imperfection, so that the monk may always be conscious of his weakness and remain rooted in humility. Second, in the desire and effort to come to a higher conduct, so that he may always desire to advance in spirit toward better things and not withdraw at all from this effort. Toward this effort of advancing, every cenobite ought to extend his mind at every moment; if he ever sets it aside, he incurs transgression of his vow and very grave danger to his soul. But let the monk take care that consideration of his own weakness does not cast down the effort to advance; let him take care that the effort itself does not despise the consideration of imperfection. Rather, let these two so cling mutually to each other that they never withdraw from one another. But if excessive consideration of one’s own imperfection has tempted the mind toward despair of God’s mercy, let him recall to memory what the holy abbot Bernard said: Let us breathe again, brothers, because although we are nothing in our own hearts, perhaps something in us may lie hidden in the heart of God. These two considerations, of our weakness and of divine estimation, stir up great devotion in us. Thus the Psalmist said: I am poor and sorrowful. Behold the first. Your salvation, O God, has received me. Behold the second.
