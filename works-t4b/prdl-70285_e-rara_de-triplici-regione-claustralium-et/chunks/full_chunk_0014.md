The fifth degree of pride is taken to be singularity, which is generated from the swelling of boasting. Proud cloistered men fall into this degree, who, through boasting, have presumed that they surpass others either in merit or in knowledge. Hence, lest they should appear like the others in their manner of life, out of mere pride they make themselves singular in everything. But this singularity is reprehended in a monastery, where all are commanded by the rule to live in common. Proud monks, as Saint Bernard testifies, when they prefer themselves to others in virtues and wisdom, fall into such arrogance of rashness that they flatter themselves more over one fast while the others are eating than if they had fasted seven days with the others. Finally, one little singular prayer seems more suitable to them than the whole psalmody of one night. During the meal they often carry their eyes around the table; and if they see anyone eating less, they groan, and begin to grieve that they have been outdone, cruelly withholding from themselves what they had provided for necessary sustenance. These proud cloistered men are vigorous in their own singular practices, but sluggish in common ones. They keep watch in bed; in choir and at the regular reading they doze.

The sixth degree of pride takes its beginning from the fifth, and is called the arrogance of the proud. For indeed, when disordered cloistered men, through the pursuit of singularity, begin outwardly to appear different from others, they burn with a greater madness of arrogance; and whatever is said by men in their praise, the fools think true. O pitiable folly of the proud: since in all other matters they believe no one more than themselves, about themselves alone they believe themselves less than another. Whatever praise they hear about themselves from the inexperienced, they attribute not to the ignorance or goodwill of the praiser, but to their own merits. But whoever is agreeably fed on human praises is held before the Lord to be empty of every virtue and full of arrogance.

The seventh degree of pride is born from the preceding one, and is called the presumption of the proud. All the arrogant fall into this, who individually believe themselves to be something great.

Concerning these Bernard says: He who thinks that he surpasses others, how should he not presume more about himself than about others? He sits first in the assembly; in councils he answers first; he comes forward uncalled; he thrusts himself in unbidden; he rearranges what has been arranged; he rejects what has been done by others. Whatever he himself has not done or arranged, he does not think rightly done or beautifully ordered. He judges those who judge; he prejudges those who are going to judge. For the one for whom God is not enough in the heart seeks comfort in outward things in vain.

The eighth degree of pride is called the defense of sins, which is always generated from presumption. For a presumptuous monk, always thinking himself better than everyone, if he commits something either obstinately denies it, or, if he has been convicted, excuses it in many ways. For how will he be able humbly to confess his fault, who does not acknowledge himself guilty? If you wish to chastise such a man for his offenses, you always make him worse. Therefore whoever, when corrected for any matter, falls into malicious words; whoever opposes excuses for sins to the one reproving him: he falls into this descent of death, which is set opposite the fifth degree of humility. The presumptuous man who is found to have fallen into this degree of pride tries in various ways to excuse his sin. For he says either: I did not do it; or: I did it indeed, but I did well; or, if badly: not very badly; or, if very badly: not with evil intention. And if he has been convicted of malice, immediately in excuse of himself he does not fear to bring forward the counsel of another or of his abbot; and sometimes, contrary to the rule, he will reveal the secrets of his abbey. But when he comes to confession, he will cloak the truth with a feigned mind, because, so that he himself may escape confusion, he strives to bring the negligences of others into the open. Inwardly disordered, proud, and presumptuous, in confession he cloaks his own faults and confesses those of others. Always restless within himself, with one sentence he condemns the examinations and deeds of others; he complains that no one lives rightly in the monastery; he thinks that there is no charity among the brothers.
