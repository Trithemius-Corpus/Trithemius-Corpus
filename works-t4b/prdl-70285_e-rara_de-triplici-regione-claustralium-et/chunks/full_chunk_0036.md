The eighth degree of humility, then, is set down in the Rule as follows: if the monk does nothing except what the common rule of the cenobium, or the examples of the elders, encourage. In this degree, singularity of outward conduct contrary to the Rule is forbidden to monks; but inward singularity of greater charity and humility is forbidden to no one. Therefore let no one prefer his own inventions to the holy Rule; let no one put his own customs before the examples of the elders, since it is not enough for one to stand in the seventh degree if he does not keep the rule of the community.

The ninth degree of humility follows from the Rule; through it excessive talkativeness is forbidden. For it befits a monk to keep silence, especially while he is seated in the midst of the elders. Indeed, one who speaks when not questioned is deservedly regarded as a fool.

The tenth degree of humility forbids readiness to laughter and levity, since whoever delights in laughter is shown to be foolish and estranged from the fear of God.

The eleventh degree of humility enjoins modesty and maturity upon the monk, so that, when the occasion and permission to speak are granted, he should speak with humble gravity, without laughter or anger. Let the monk’s speech be rare, brief, and reasonable, because it does not befit one who wishes to please the Most High to say useless things.

The twelfth degree of humility teaches the monk to order the outward man, in appearance and dress, from a humble heart, and to show by his outward conduct also that he is inwardly devout. And although this degree is last in the order of them all, nevertheless in the practice of those beginning to make progress it is first. And therefore Bernard, describing these twelve degrees of humility, begins from this one and, ascending to the first, descends in order, so to speak. For no one easily advances inwardly to compunction of heart and the fear of God if he does not order his manners outwardly.

On the devout fitting of the powers for entrance into the third region.

Article VI.

Since, by the six degrees of humility, the inner powers of a person have above all been purged, grounded, and reformed, it is now said to be very necessary in monks that they be more devoutly prepared for entrance into the third region. Now this diligence in striving toward higher things, as we have said, is necessary for the cenobite, since where the effort to make progress is laid aside, acquired virtue is altogether dissipated. No one is permitted to stand still in this second region; rather, we are all commanded to ascend to the third through continual effort. Indeed this continual motion casts every slothful and lukewarm person down into the first region of the flesh, but it gently impels the humble and those fervent in desire always toward the third, without violence.

Therefore whoever has deserved to obtain from the Lord, through humility, the reformation of his powers is already near the third region of the spirit. In order that he may be able to enter it effectively, it is necessary that, by most diligently fitting the powers of his soul, he order them to their several operations. The intellect is then said to be rightly fitted for entrance into the third region when it is illumined for knowing the highest truth according to its capacity. Memory is ordered to the same end when it is fully absorbed in holding and cherishing the highest truth. But if the will is disposed to love the highest good, which is God, it is well prepared for the entrance of the spirit.

In order, then, that the cenobite may be able to attain purification of the intellectual powers, it is necessary that, dying to the world, he seek a mind made peaceful from every inner and outer disturbance, so that, buried within the bosom of divine love, it may remain there and never withdraw from the sweetness of inward union.
