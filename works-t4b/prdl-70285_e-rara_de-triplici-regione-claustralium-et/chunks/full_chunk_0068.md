If the name of a confessor, or of a virgin, or of a saint of some other condition has been recited, the devout lover of God will readily vary, according to the ardor of his affection, a prayer to any saint extemporaneously. For affective prayers are more effective than all others.

*At the Reading of the Rule*

When the reader recites one passage from the Rule, as is customary, let the devout monk say in his heart: Help me, most sweet Lord Jesus Christ, so that I may be able to live according to this norm of your religious servant Benedict, and to remain faithful at all times in your most holy service.

Then let him consider that, just as the Rule is read daily for the progress of the good and devout, so it is recited to the lukewarm, the bad, or the negligent so that they may be inexcusable, to the increase of their condemnation. Finally, let him know that he is bound to make a faithful effort toward all the things that the Rule contains, as the tenor of his profession states. For thus he said in his profession: I promise obedience and conversion of my morals according to the Rule of Saint Benedict.

*When the Names of the Dead Are Recited*

But when, after the reading of the Rule, the names of the dead have been recited, the monk shall pray as follows for these and for all other faithful departed, both commended and not commended to him, no less from charity than from duty.

*Prayer*

I beseech you, most sweet Lord Jesus, through the pain of your wounds, through the shedding of your most holy blood, and through the power of your most innocent death: have mercy on these souls, and on all those commended to me in prayer, and on all the faithful departed; and grant them eternal rest. It is indeed a great work of mercy to intercede before the merciful Judge for souls placed in the prison of purgatory, and out of charity to aid them through the remembrance of the Lord's passion. For I firmly believe, and with pious confidence trust, that the prayer poured out for the dead from charity alone returns doubled into the bosom of the one who prays. For the devout person who here, through the fervor of divine love, by praying particularly for all the faithful departed, suffers with them in charity, is honored at his death with an inestimable reward. For the Lord says in the holy Gospel: Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.

*On the Chapter of Faults*

Since nothing in human actions is perfect in every respect, let the devout monk rejoice that he has found a remedy for his imperfection, because whatever is committed from human frailty in well-reformed monasteries is corrected daily. For the chapter of faults, continued on individual days, washes away public offenses; sacramental confession, repeated daily through the grace of contrition, remits hidden negligences. We discern four chapters in the monastery, concerning which we shall speak in order.

*The First Passive Chapter Is When a Brother Proclaims Himself or Is Proclaimed by Another*

The first chapter is called passive, in which our devout monk proclaims himself for his negligences, or is proclaimed by another. In this chapter the lover of God, who has denied his very self to himself, humbly both accuses his own negligences and allows them to be accused by others, lest he be judged more severely by the Lord after this life. For the holy apostle of God says: If we judged ourselves, we would certainly not be judged. And the prophet Nahum says: Affliction shall not rise up a second time. For truly, the one who here is now voluntarily afflicted for his sins, for the love of God, is worthy, as one well purified, to be freed by the mercy of the Creator from future affliction.

If only all cloistered persons knew how much the proclamations of chapter, when borne voluntarily, profit them for the merit of eternal life, they would bear all accusations and injuries for the love of God with inestimable joy. For we merit more when we patiently suffer injuries and adversities for the love of God and fraternal charity than if we devote ourselves to good works.

Therefore, standing in judgment, let the devout monk not fear any proclamations whatever, but desire to be accused by others in remembrance of the Lord's passion, knowing that before him has been set the loftiness of glory, to be bestowed in heaven upon humble patience.
