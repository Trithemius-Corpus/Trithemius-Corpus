Preface to the third treatise of the first part, which describes the supreme region of the spirit of cloistered persons.

As I am at last about to write, with the Lord's help, about that supreme region of the spirit, I am shaken at once by fear and by shame: for I, a small man, fear to speak of the greatest matters, and I blush deeply to teach what I have not sufficiently experienced. For what intellect of any man, however well exercised, could know the state of a mind sweetly resting in God through love? Or what speech, even of the most eloquent orator, could explain the secret sweetness of inward contemplation? Indeed, the exercise of this third region surpasses human understanding for the greater part. In it the mind of a purified man, through the fervor of divine love, is carried above itself; and, while its natural essence remains, it is lulled with wondrous sweetness and absorbed in the abyss of divine contemplation. For it is oppressed by so great a sweetness of inward majesty that it seems unable either to understand or to express its own state. Having left behind the sensitive operation, it also passes wholly beyond the intellective operation; and, what is more, leaving itself behind, without imagination, reason, or understanding, it is lifted above itself by affection to that supreme summit of divine darkness.

But when it has altogether failed from itself, it does not act but rests; nor, when it returns to itself, can it understand what it has undergone, or reveal to anyone the manner of its elevation. For this elevation of the mind into God is so profound and so sublime that not only can the inexperienced person not understand it, but even the experienced person cannot fully explain in words what it is like. But what will I be able to say about the mountain of divine darkness, which I have not yet deserved to penetrate? For the well of inward contemplation is deep, and I am weak, since I have brought no vessel fit for these waters, nor have I prepared steps suitable for ascending to heights. Composed words do not befit a fool; nor ought an unskilled and cold man to speak of the secrets of divine contemplation,

one who still remains with the weak in the valleys. For only he speaks fruitfully about profound matters who is illuminated by the twofold ray of the spirit. Nevertheless, since I am a man placed under obedience to the annual chapter, I am compelled to take up the mandate of that synod and to complete, according to my powers, the work set before me. Therefore I enter upon the third region of cloistered persons under the guidance of the venerable father lord Johannes of Bursfelde, the devout abbot, so that, by reducing as far as I can into my own manner of speaking what he described in learned and truthful discourse, I may appear to have obeyed the chapter.

Here begins the third region of the soul, which is called spiritual, in which the highest tranquility of mind is possessed.

The third or supreme region of the soul is called spiritual; in it the highest rest and tranquility of mind are found. This region is compared to the empyrean heaven, in which there is the true rest of all and the true happiness of souls. The spirit abiding in this region leaves behind what is below reason, and, lifted up by love, ascends wondrously into God. For whatever it does in this most holy region is judged to be wholly divine. Here the human spirit, caught up above itself, receives the divine inrush; and, lulled in the darkness of divine contemplation, withdrawn from all creatures, it sweetly rests. In this region almighty God directs the spirit of the contemplative person; and, with the intellectual powers of the soul simplified and called back from every phantasm into the inner chamber, he perfects them according to their directed tendencies. And leading the mind, stripped of every multiplicity, to himself as the one principle, he also makes it one in himself, not by nature, but by gracious participation. The citizens of this supreme region are truly spiritual persons, who, having despised all things and affections of the world, love nothing in this world except God, covet nothing inordinately, but, absorbed by inward affection

by the divine spirit, thirst vehemently for God alone and burn with ineffable desire for love of him. For they are driven into God by so great a fervor of love that, lifted above themselves, they commonly leave behind all intellectual operations, and, caught up into the abyss of divine contemplation, admit nothing whatever except God. Existing there insensibly through the grace of love, they become one with God, while the property of their natures is preserved, by the gift of participation. For the holy apostle of Jesus Christ says: He who clings to the Lord is one spirit. And again he says: Those who are led by the Spirit of God are the sons of God. The same apostle also shows himself to be a citizen of this most holy region when he cries out to the Philippians: But our conversation is in heaven. For as long as we are in the body we are exiles from the Lord; but we are sweetly led, as though through sleep, to our homeland when in mind and spirit we have our conversation in heavenly things. I have said “through sleep” not properly, but by a remote comparison. For in dreams we go forth amid images of things intelligible to us, with their forms preserved in the imagination; but in contemplation, beyond every imagination, we are lifted up pure in mind and spirit to God. And therefore this supreme region of the soul is called spiritual; from it all religious persons obtain the name of spirituality. Woe to us. We are called spiritual from this most blessed region, and we are named religious lovers of God, though nevertheless we desire to attain the summit of this mountain through contemplation. In this region charity holds command, wisdom the consulship; in it nothing is loved except God, nothing is committed inordinately. This most holy region, moreover, belongs to the empyrean heaven, because those whom it receives spiritually as citizens it leads to the joys of heaven.
