Inflated by the spirit of pride, they boast that by foundation they possess distinguished monasteries, rich in temporal goods, remarkable for treasures, buildings, and privileges. In what way the citizens of this lowest region are disordered toward penitence.

Article VIII.

Since man, born of woman, living for a short time and filled with many miseries, cannot remain long in the same state, it is necessary that with all zeal he turn himself daily, by reforming himself, toward his due end; for as long as anyone is permitted to live in this world, he is shown to be changeable in either direction. Therefore whoever, placed in this mutability, desires to come to the joys of immutability should humbly acknowledge his frailty, should always, after the fall of sin, flee to the remedies of penitence, and should fear to lie in crime even for a moment. For the life of a monk must always consist in saving penitence, and he must continually await the coming of the judge with holy fear. For blessed Jerome says: “A monk has the office not of one who teaches but of one who laments, who should mourn either himself or the world, and should await the Lord’s coming in fear.” But the disordered cloisterers of the lowest region despise this saving remedy; they consume the acceptable time in vanity; and because they do not fear to be badly ordered toward all things, they show themselves very disordered even for doing penitence.

First, indeed, they are badly ordered toward penitence through negligence of mind, when they neglect to understand how greatly sin displeases almighty God, against whom, by the decision of a depraved will, they do not fear to rebel. Just as black night is opposed to day, so the sinner, turned away from divine goodness, is opposed and hostile to it.

What a putrid corpse is accustomed to do in the sight of men, sin in its own way does incomparably in the sight of angels. But carnal monks despise the honor of God and therefore do not notice the disgrace of sin. They neglect to amend venial sins, as if they might lawfully commit mortal ones. For Scripture says: “He who neglects the smallest things gradually falls.” Hence Bernard’s sentence warns us, saying: “Let no one, brothers, say in his heart: These are light matters; I do not care to correct them. It is no great thing if I remain in these venial and very small sins. This, brothers, is impenitence; this is the blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, which is unforgivable.” For no one can escape greater sins if he has disdained to amend the lesser ones. Nothing is worse than negligence of mind.

Second, the carnal monks, lovers of the lowest region, are exceedingly disordered toward penitence because they do not perceive the contagion of sin. For just as a healthy man is often infected by a contagious disease, so from one sin a man is inclined into another. For one sin is always said to dispose toward another, unless it is amended as quickly as possible through contrition. For the prophet says: “Jerusalem has sinned a sin; therefore she has become unstable; let her be defiled still.” We have sufficiently shown the contagion of this disease in the degrees of pride; for just as we said that one always arises from another, so we perceive that through negligence a man becomes slippery from one sin into another.

Third, carnal monks, disordered toward penitence, contract a very bad sickness through the habit of sin, when they incur a pernicious blindness of mind. Every vice, often committed, is held cheap and is judged lighter through habit. Just as grace enlightens the mind, so the frequency of sin blinds reason. For it is written: “Blind the heart of this people and make its ears heavy, so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand, lest they be converted and I heal them,” says the Lord. For when the wicked man has come into the depth of evils, he despises. No sin is so great that it does not seem light and small through habit.
