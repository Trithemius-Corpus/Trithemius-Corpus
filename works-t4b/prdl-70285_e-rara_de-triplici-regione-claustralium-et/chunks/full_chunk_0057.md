The first of them, namely the intellect, is raised up to know God, but it is not sufficient to come to the mountain of divine darkness, because it proceeds burdened with the phantasms of sensible things. Therefore affection, the simpler eye of the soul, enters the dark mountain without any images whatever; it contemplates God through love; separated from all creatures, it thinks of nothing sensible; resting in mystical silence, stripped bare, it is free. This is the single eye by which the whole body is made bright; this is the eye of which the bridegroom boasts in the Canticles: “You have wounded my heart, my sister, my bride; you have wounded my heart with one of your eyes.” For affection alone, through the fervor of love, enters the secret place of divine contemplation without the guidance of the intellect; and therefore the bridegroom is wounded in one eye and not in both.

Whoever, then, through the exercise of devotion and purity, has deserved, by Christ’s gift, to arrive at this region of union and simplification, should learn to remain prudently at all times in deep humility, lest through pride he lose the gift he rejoices to possess. For Bernard, that deep and most cautious contemplator of divine things, says: “One striving toward higher things must lay humility deep and think humbly of himself, lest, while he is vainly lifted above himself, he fall from the height to which he seemed to have ascended.” Therefore one who remains in this region should prudently return at times to the degrees of humility described in the second; the mind should learn humbly to remain in deep resignation of itself, because as soon as it has despised self-denial, it falls from the highest mountain into a monstrous precipice.

For many have been deceived by trusting in the jubilation of their sensual devotion, who ascended this very high mountain without the foundation of humility. Others, with their heads injured and their senses destroyed, have become foolish, because, not yet mortified in their passions, they presumed to enter the darkness of mystical silence. Others, raised up in pride while they were still given over to sensuality, descended through the vice of self-will into the depth of hell. For Scripture says concerning the ascent of this

peak: “The beast that touches the mountain shall be overwhelmed with stones.” A beast is said to touch the mountain when a human being, not yet purified through the exercise of the second region, attempts to ascend to the third. For as long as affection has not been purged of the passions of its sensuality, the beast still lives in it. But if the mind of man, with its beastliness, presumes to approach the mountain of God, it is overwhelmed by as many stones as the motions of the passions by which it is wounded. For the wise man says: “The sluggard was stoned with a muddy stone, and all will speak in contempt of him.” By the muddy stone carnal affection is expressed, because man is compared to mud. For it is written in Job: “Remember, I beg you, that you made me like mud, and you will reduce me to dust.” And again he says: “I have been compared to mud, and have been likened to embers and ashes.” Thus the sluggard is overwhelmed by the muddy stone when the mind, still unclean with carnal and sensual passions, tries to ascend to the mountain of contemplation; there, as unworthy, it is forced to suffer repulse and ruin by the hard stones set against it. For no one will be able to reach this most tranquil region of the spirit who has not first purged himself through long exercise in the furnace of the middle region. Whoever, still given over to carnal passions, attempts to approach this supreme region of the spirit is trying, as it were, to draw water in a sieve. Indeed this region does not admit carnal minds into itself, for it is prepared for hearts purified from every affection.
