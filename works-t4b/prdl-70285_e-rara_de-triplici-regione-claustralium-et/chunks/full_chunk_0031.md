Indeed, this most perfect resignation of oneself, by which the cenobite truly subjects his own will to God, shows without intermediary the straight road to the highest good. Here there is the greatest tranquility of mind; the greatest security is present in the heart, because a soul that strives to subject itself perfectly to God will no longer be able to fear any opposition in this world except sin alone. Whatever adversity comes upon him, with the Lord’s help he has learned to overcome it; whatever advantage he desires, he understands that he possesses it in the Lord alone. He despises all adversities for love of him; he has placed all goods in love of him. For that devout man wisely said: “Let go of everything, and you will find everything. Abandon desire, and you will find true peace.”

Finally, in order that this resignation of one’s own will may receive the fullness of consummation, it implores, so to speak, the support of a threefold aid.

The first is placed in the virtue of perseverance: that the will, once denied, is never taken back. For blessed Bernard says to those who despise this aid: “Why do you again take charge of yourselves, you who once, by mature deliberation, handed over your will to us?” For the monk who has once handed over his own will to God into the hands of the abbot does not take it back without the crime of sacrilege.

The second thing required for the perfect resignation of the will is that it never be slackened. Hence in the Apocalypse the Lord says to the lukewarm soul: “I have a few things against you, because you have abandoned your first charity,” that is, your fervor.

Third, resignation of the will is perfected if it is never cut off. For the Savior says: “He who has persevered to the end will be saved.” Therefore, when you see a cenobite inquiring anxiously

The third step of humility is to subject oneself to a superior in all obedience for love of God, and to show oneself an imitator of Christ in deeds, not only in words. Concerning this the holy Apostle says: “He became obedient unto death, even death on a cross.” This step takes its beginning from the second and raises its ascent up to its own third step. For just as the second mortifies one’s own will by denying it, so this one lays the foundation of a holy work through humble subjection. The former subjects the whole self to the divine will; the latter, for love of God, also obeys human beings.

The keeping of one step without the observance of the other is not sufficient in a monk; nor is the will of a cenobite subjected to the divine ordinance if it deems it beneath itself to obey the command of a superior.

Therefore the true cenobite places all his joy in the virtue of holy obedience. He considers it the greatest gain to fulfill in all things the will of his shepherd. He believes it a grave loss to the soul if he has found himself to have committed even a little thing outside obedience. For he always considers with faithful devotion what the Lord says in the Gospel: “He who hears you hears me, and he who despises you despises me.” Hence blessed Bernard says to the monks: “Whatever a man commands in God’s stead must be received no differently than if God himself commanded it in person.” Therefore let the true monk prefer this subjection of obedience to every sacrifice; let him do absolutely nothing against its integrity; and let him continually say within himself what is written: “Obedience is better than sacrifice.” The Rule says: “Therefore all things are to be done according to the will of the abbot.”

But this step, as we have said, arises from the second, because he who perfectly forsakes his own will for fear of God now seeks nothing else in this world than to follow that will in all things.
