Either you are accused justly, or unjustly. If justly, why would you not endure it? It is better to be purified here in small matters than after this life in many. And he who sins publicly must be corrected before all. But if you are accused unjustly, remember that you are a monk, and you will find that you are bound to suffer injuries. For when you are accused justly, know that you must be purified through humble patience; when unjustly, you show yourself a true monk and gain greater merit. Therefore, when you are accused, call to mind the fourth and sixth degrees of humility; and if you are a true monk, pray even for those who accuse you. For a true lover of God loves the one who accuses him all the more, and always on that same day offers him either some prayer or some special service. If you are good and wise, the accusation cannot displease you; but if you are wicked and foolish, you deserve to be accused at all times. For Scripture says of the wise man: Correct a wise man, and he will love you. But of the wicked and foolish the prophet says: They hated the one who corrected them in the gate, and they abhorred the one who spoke perfectly.

The second chapter, active, is that in which one person accuses another.

We call the second chapter active: that in which we no longer accuse ourselves, but accuse another. For the act of this accusation, charity is supremely necessary; compassion too, no less than discretion, must be applied. Therefore, when you wish to accuse your brother by reason of charity, always bring in the fear of God; and, mindful of the fellowship of eternal life, say at the beginning: Our beloved brother committed this or that; I ask that he be willing to amend himself for the Lord’s sake. Whatever must be said, let him put it forward in few words and with compassion; let him state the fact simply; let him not increase the fault of the accused by aggravating it; but let him silently commit to the president’s judgment whether what was done was good or evil. For the person who alone, among many, aggravates a brother’s fault by passing sentence commonly carries away the suspicion of bitter zeal and displeasure.

Therefore, whoever strives to accuse another should beware that he is not moved against his brother by bitter zeal; for if the charity of the accuser does not intervene, resentment does not amend the accused. The accusation of an accuser never bears fruit in a delinquent when the love of God and fraternal charity do not commend it. For almighty God, since he is supremely good, will not grant to your accusation the purpose of compunction in your brother’s heart when he finds that you have been moved to accuse not from charity but from displeasure. But where he finds the root of his charity, he will not deny good fruit. If, however, you sense that you have been moved by some indignation against your brother, it is better to postpone the accusation for a time than to wound yourself so cruelly with another’s wound.

The third chapter is that of private humiliation.

The third chapter is called that of private humiliation: whenever a brother is rebuked by a senior for his negligences. For our most holy father Benedict commands in chapter 72 of the Rule that a monk, accused in whatever place or time, as soon as he senses that his senior’s mind has been moved, even slightly, should cast himself down at his feet, and there lie humbly until his command. Here he is in no way permitted to excuse himself or to place the fault on another; rather, the monk is commanded to be silent and humbly acknowledge himself. For one who is a true monk neither murmurs when corrected justly, nor excuses himself when accused unjustly. In the divine offices also, and in the other conventual places, as soon as a devout cenobite has offended in some matter through negligence, humbling himself according to the custom, he makes satisfaction there; and he chooses rather to undergo the greater penance in doubtful cases than to wound his conscience with a lesser one, or to cause scandal to others. But whoever still understands this humiliation, to be undertaken at every time and place, as burdensome and difficult, does not walk according to the spirit but still lives carnally; and unless he makes this Jebusite tributary to himself by subduing it, he will never be a true and devout cenobite.
