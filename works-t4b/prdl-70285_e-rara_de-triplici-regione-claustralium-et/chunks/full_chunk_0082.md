And concerning secret matters, in tomorrow's confession; concerning public and regular transgressions, in the next chapter meeting, he should faithfully and from his heart resolve to clear himself by accusing himself. Let him grieve over what he has committed, make satisfaction for what he has repented of, and beware of what he may commit.

Next, let him consider the grave danger of offending God, whether the sin has been venial or mortal. For Jean Gerson, chancellor of Paris, says in the treatise On the Spiritual Life: "Even venial sin is so much against God that, according to his justice, he could damn a man eternally for it. But that he does not impute it to a man unto damnation proceeds not from justice but from his infinite goodness." And the holy bishop Anselm warns us that a man ought not knowingly to commit the smallest sin against God's will for the whole world. For the most blessed pontiff Augustine says: "No sin is so venial that it does not become mortal while it is pleasing." You may say: this statement cannot be true, because if a sin is venial, it can never become mortal; but if it becomes mortal, it was not venial. But listen and understand. I grant that venial sin, considered in itself, cannot become mortal. But one and the same sin, which can stand together with charity, becomes merely venial for one person by negligence and weakness alone; for another, however, through contempt and aversion from God in the same sin, it is without doubt imputed as mortal, because it is pleasing even insofar as it is sin and contrary to God. Blessed, therefore, is he who is always fearful; but he who is hard of mind will fall into evil. Whoever neglects the smallest sins when he can amend them gradually falls into greater ones.

Indeed, how much sin displeases almighty God is shown by the punishment that follows. For so often in the sacred books and in the deeds of times past we read of sinners against the Lord being gravely punished, that we rightly fear the pit of sin. How much even the very briefest thought of an averted spirit displeases him is declared to us by the fall of the first angel. For as soon as he showed himself disordered against the Lord by pride, he was cast

How contrary to the Lord idle and useless words are, the account in the Lives of the Holy Fathers about the infernal pigs appearing among those speaking useless things and gnashing their teeth warns us plainly enough.

How much evil and pernicious words offend almighty God was evident in the five-year-old boy who blasphemed; the devil snatched him alive from his father's lap and, as is believed, carried him off to hell.

How much deeds and negligence concerning one's office displease him is clear in the sons of Eli, who were negligent and less careful about the rite of sacrifice.

How much evil deeds offend God, since we can understand it from, as it were, countless histories, need not be proved by a few examples.

When, however, our monk in his devout recollections finds himself burdened by the aforesaid or by any other negligences or sins, however grave, let him assume such contrition for the past as is possible for him, a purpose of amendment by firmly avoiding them in the future, and penance for what he has committed. Therefore let him take up the counsel of the aforesaid chancellor, and, being compunct, say:

Lord my God, I confess to your most holy goodness that I have sinned in this way or that against your will. I grieve vehemently over it, and because I have offended you, who are wholly good, I humbly do penance.
