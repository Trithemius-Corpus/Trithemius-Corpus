Blinded by sin, they do not come to the grace of true compunction; they approach confession superficially, from habit alone; they do not abandon their depraved will; they do not truly take up a purpose of amendment. Woe to those who, buried in the habit of sin, do not fear the judgment of a strict examination; who do not withdraw from blindness of mind by the remedy of contrition. For just as they now despise the long-suffering of God’s goodness, so in that terrible hour they will undergo the sentence of eternal death. What can confession of the mouth profit where there is no true contrition of heart? Without contrition there is no saving confession, nor can there be contrition without a purpose of amendment. The test of contrition is to be unwilling to sin any more.

Fifth, the disorderly citizens of the lowest region, made contemners of almighty God through the habit of sinning, always fall into graver dangers of soul, since they always approach badly the most holy sacred mysteries of the body and blood of the Lord. They make a superficial confession from habit; they allow things that they think well ordered to run on in their consciences. And thereafter, as if confederates of hell, they do not fear God; they despise the paths of a more amended life; they think themselves well ordered; they do not care how many vices they are inwardly full of. Sometimes, sensibly affected by a fantastic devotion, they think that the Holy Spirit is now present; they affirm that they are beloved by God and holy. They also thrust themselves shamelessly upon the divine mysteries; not discerning the body and blood of the Lord, they take it to themselves for judgment and condemnation.

Finally, such people, communicating unworthily, are hardened more gravely, because, while they think themselves very secure and well conducted, they do not take proper care to cleanse their conscience of vices. Thus Judas the apostate, the evil of betrayal which at first, as someone says, he was afraid to commit against the Savior, after the morsel had entered and the devil had entered into him, did not fear to carry out boldly and without shame. O how many carnal monks are citizens of this lowest region: exalted, proud, envious, murmuring, and entangled in various sins, whose conscience is rarely or never made clean by true contrition, and who nevertheless are not afraid to approach the most holy altar almost every day.

Behold how evil is the society of all who dwell in this lowest region, whose end is destruction, for whom descent into hell is prepared through bodily death. This is the far-off region of beastliness and unlikeness, into which that prodigal son went away, who squandered the portion of his inheritance luxuriously with prostitutes. In this region the carnal citizens dwell, concerning whom the holy Apostle declares: The animal man does not know the things that are of God.

In this dark region, covered with the gloom of death, no spiritual good dwells. In it not peace but beastly discord reigns, as the prophet says: There is no peace for the wicked, says my Lord.

*Concerning those who are neighbors to this lowest region and relapse into it rather often through inconstancy of mind.*

Although between the first region of the bad and the second of the good there is properly no middle place, nevertheless there are some who often move between both, since from weakness they are at one time tepid, and at another time, from contrition of mind, seem to be devout. These are weak people. Although they are not submerged in the lowest region, nevertheless through negligence they are very near to it; and indeed they frequently fall into it through bad habit, since they do not withdraw, as far as they can, from the affections of the flesh.

Concerning them Bernard says in a certain sermon: Harmful tepidity, because it is dangerous, because it is most troublesome and full of misery and sorrow, and plainly nearest to hell, is rightly judged the shadow of death. Indeed, one may find fainthearted and remiss people, failing under the burden, needing the rod and spurs, whose joy is brief, whose sadness is fainthearted, whose compunction is brief and rare. Their thought is animal; their way of life tepid; their obedience without devotion; their speech without circumspection; their prayer without the heart’s attention; their reading without edification. Finally, as we see, fear of Gehenna scarcely restrains them; shame scarcely holds them back; reason scarcely bridles them; discipline scarcely confines them. But if people of this kind remain long under the burden, either they are crushed and fall, or in some way they are in hell, so that they never breathe fully under the sweet yoke and light burden.
