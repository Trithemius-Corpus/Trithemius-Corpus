Sixth, God’s abundant munificence is praiseworthy in the glorification of the blessed, since for a small merit he rewards his elect with so ineffable a recompense, and bestows on them, as immortals, a gift together with the holy angels.

Seventh, God’s incomprehensible excellence in himself is praiseworthy: he orders all things according to his providence, and, always without motion or time, remains unchangeable in his most consequent perfection. The contemplation of this seventh consideration pertains to the Sabbath of eternal blessedness, in which there is no longer the work of laborious conduct, but the happy blessedness of an eternal dwelling and of rest. For just as God, the creator of all things, is read to have completed all his work and rested on the seventh day, so in the heavenly Sabbath not work but eternal rest will be laid upon the just. Therefore, if we desire happily to reach those heavenly mansions, let us rest in God alone, in whom the rational creature is perfected by resting; and, perfected through the adherence of love, it rejoices with the Lord in ineffable union and joy.

Now, for the praising of God, let pure devotion seek nothing henceforth outside God, since almighty God himself, perfect and most wise, suffices for all as the matter of his praise, and exceeds every praise by his infinite perfections. For Scripture says: “Praise the Lord as much as you can; he will still surpass it.” For he is greater than all praise, and therefore he is to be praised by us most humbly rather in himself and for his own sake than from his creatures.

From the aforesaid considerations concerning God there arises a manifold and varied affection of devotion; through it, too, a more worthy effect of prayer is generated according to the desire of the affection. For when meditation of the heart is taken up concerning God’s majesty or power, reverent wonder is produced. But when we begin to meditate on his ineffable wisdom, confounded by our own imperfect justice, we accuse ourselves. Thus, in consequence, from consideration of God’s mercy the hope of pardon is taken up;

Concerning Meditation on the Work of Our Reparation in the Passion of Our Lord Jesus Christ.

Root II.

The second root of the fear and love of God is placed in meditation on our redemption, which is generated, through diligent exercise, in the life, deeds, teaching, and passion of our Lord Jesus Christ. Among all meditations we believe this one to be more useful for us, for in it we know that our salvation and the sum of eternal blessedness are contained. And note that the Lord’s passion, with respect to its proceedings, was unjust; with respect to its manner, shameful; with respect to its pain, bitter; and with respect to its effect, fruitful. Therefore it must always be held in memory.

First, with respect to its proceedings, it was unjust, because he who was born of a virgin neither contracted sin nor committed it, nor was deceit found in his mouth. For death is inflicted on us for sin; but he could not justly die who was always just and innocent, without any sin. Finally, a man is killed contrary to human law if a sentence of death is brought against someone neither confessed nor convicted of a crime. For before Pilate the Savior was accused on three counts: that, calling himself a king, he forbade tribute to be given to Caesar; and third, that he made himself the Son of God. The Gospel speaks about the first two, and the accusation is shown to be false. For regarding the first, the contrary was plainly established, since Christ said that his kingdom was not earthly. Nor was the falsity of the second hidden from Pilate, for he knew that Jesus had commanded that the things that were Caesar’s should be given to Caesar.
