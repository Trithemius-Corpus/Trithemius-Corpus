*On Manual Labor. Tenth Consideration.*

Since our most holy father Benedict, in the Rule, commends as true monks those who live by the labor of their hands, and declares them blessed, it seemed fitting to us, after the end of the day, to say something about this most holy exercise. Indeed, we read that this work is commended in many ways by the holy fathers, and we know that in it they profited before God and human beings in no small measure. For the holy apostle says: “He who does not work, let him not eat.” What Augustine, that teacher among the most learned teachers, thought about this manual exercise he shows most clearly in the book *On the Work of Monks*. We also read that those ancient and most holy fathers dwelling in solitude labored not lukewarmly at the work of their hands, so that they might not incur the danger of idleness, and, living from their own labors, after the example of the most blessed teacher of the nations, burden no one. Would that we too, brothers, might deign to take up this holy exercise of laboring with the same intention and goodwill as they, so that after the end of our labor we may deserve to rest with them without end in the heavenly homeland.

*There Are Four Things for a Monk to Observe in Labor.*

When called to labor, a monk should strive to show himself such in all things that he does not lose the merit of bodily labor through dissolution of mind. For we see many among monks going out to labor in the wrong way, whom we see seeking nothing other than the comforts of the flesh. Some of these take up useless and less necessary labors so that, in the time of the regular fast, they may have an occasion for eating.

For they labor so that they may eat; whether it benefits or harms the monastery, they do not care. Others have been accustomed to arrange various labors so that, under this pretext, they may be able to run about here and there without the bridle of discipline. Others procure labors useless to the community so that they may freely indulge in vain talk and frivolities. Others love external labors so that, as if by a suitable opportunity and without suspicion, they may be able to see and speak with people whom I will not call seculars, known and friendly to them according to the flesh and not according to God. Others, because of instability of mind, unable to rest in the monastery, always seem to pant after labors. The intention of all those laboring in this way is not sincere, and therefore they do not attain the excellent reward of labor.

But you, brother, when you are called to labor, take care to submit your intention to the regular institution, so that you love nothing other than the honor of God, the merit of obedience, and the usefulness of the monastery, and intend nothing except the profit of the soul. While you are at labor, do not imprudently extinguish the fervor of the spirit; do not omit the exercise of spiritual conduct, but take care diligently to observe everywhere the four things that follow: in outward conduct be faithful, serviceable, and kindly; inwardly, humble and devout; in speech, sparing and edifying; in manners, composed and mature. If you keep these four things in labor, you will always return to your cell better.

*Outwardly the Monk Should Be Faithful, Kindly, and Serviceable.*

The first thing to be diligently observed by the cenobite in labor is that, in working, he always show himself faithful to his monastery; that he anticipate the brothers out of charity in serving and ministering, or also in carrying heavier things; and that in all words and deeds he show himself kindly and tractable.
