*On the Ordered Institution of True Monastic Progress and the Degrees of Humility.*

Article V.

The greatest virtue of a monk is humility, and the gravest crime in a cenobite is pride. For a monk who is proud with a swollen mind becomes an imitator and servant of the devil, and is stripped of all virtues, if he had any, since no virtue can subsist in a mind in which pride has come to dwell. Therefore the cenobite who desires to make progress in this middle region should learn to order himself toward the true progress of monastic institution through humility, because no one truly attains humility unless he spends time in spiritual pursuits.

True progress in regular institution, excluding all adulterous muses, arises from the degrees of humility, which are set down as twelve, by their roots, in the Rule. The roots of this progress come from the study of sacred reading, which gradually leads a person to the fear of the Lord. From reading spiritual meditation arises, from which holy compunction is generated. The more useful mode of cenobitic meditation consists in the memory of the Lord's passion and in the consideration of God's benefits. But those who, going beyond this middle region, come to tranquility of spirit render their meditations more sublime through contemplation of the divine majesty and excellence. We shall speak of these in their own place.

These two roots receive their increase from the watering of constant prayer, and grow strong into the robustness of a very firm plant. For no meditation makes progress without prayer, and study without it will not save.

From these three roots of spiritual progress arises the fear of the Lord, which is the first degree of humility; from this the other degrees, succeeding in order, are generated. But monks who neglect the saving study of the Scriptures and holy meditations, together with constant prayers, by no means reach even this first degree of humility. of humility, how will he be able to arrive at the others? For the second is never ascended if the first is neglected. Therefore whoever neglects one ascends none.

The study of sacred reading must not be taken up for the vanity of the world, nor for the pleasure of a seduced mind, because reading never advances toward compunction when delight of the flesh or of vanity corrupts it. A monk cannot reach devotion through the study of sacred reading unless he strives to change every curiosity into a simple spirit. Therefore whoever desires to advance from the reading of the Scriptures to compunction, whoever ought to ascend to humility, must choose reading, but only that which can more fully stir the mind to the love of God.

For this purpose the reading of the body of the Lord seems to me sufficiently suitable, because through it the memory is often recalled to love of him. We also gladly admit miracles and accounts of the torments or joys of separated souls, provided they conform to the catholic faith; by these we have found that sluggish hearts are rather often stirred to compunction. Finally, the passions of the holy martyrs awaken the fervor of devotion in no small measure; in them, while we bring to mind the unconquerable charity of Christ, we are moved by the example of such great constancy and, as if touched by a certain fire of love, sigh over our own imperfection.

From reading the matter of meditation is taken, and whatever the former is like, the latter will be like as well. Not the shameful songs of poets, not the curious doctrines of philosophers, not the swollen writings of rhetoricians: rather, they suffocate fervor. Therefore if you wish to persist in good meditations, do not occupy your mind with any readings except holy and devout ones. For indeed not one good meditation is generated from such study.
