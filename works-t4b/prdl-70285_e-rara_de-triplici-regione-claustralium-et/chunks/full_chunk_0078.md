On the Regular Meal.

Consideration V.

When the signal for the meal is rung, have heartfelt compassion for the sick, the needy, and the poor who suffer want; and when you can do nothing else for them, entreat the Lord on their behalf.

Consider that among Christians there are many far better than you whom harsh poverty oppresses in this life, to whom neither the food necessary for sustenance is offered nor even the thinnest clothing supplied for covering the body. But you, a monk, though you are a despiser of the world, are clothed in excellent garments and sit at table like a prince. Food is set before you to satiety by brothers better than you; all necessary things, even to the point of surfeit, are provided for you, an unworthy sinner, by the servants of God. Consider, therefore, what end this abundance of goods will have in a monk, since by entering religion you professed poverty, and now in religion you feast more splendidly than worldly rich men. Take care, then, not to be ungrateful for such great benefits of God. Live according to the rule which you have professed. Pray for those who contribute temporal substance to your monastery, that almighty God may mercifully grant them eternal life.

On Reading at Table.

When seated at table, be composed while eating. Do not look this way and that, but take care to eat the food set before you only for the necessity of nature, with humility and thanksgiving. Consider diligently what is read at table, so that, just as the body is refreshed by its food, so the soul may not be deprived of its own refreshment. Learn, while eating, frequently to raise your mind to heavenly things, and by the word of sacred reading, which is never lacking while we eat, sweetly to inflame it with divine compunction.

For the true and inward worshiper of God, whenever he hears anything read, even at meals, about the bitterness of the Lord’s passion, the fervor of divine love, or the kingdom of the heavenly homeland, immediately groans from excessive desire; his affection is kindled in the fire of love; compelled by exceeding sweetness, he weeps. Indeed, the mind that truly loves the Lord Jesus, as soon as it hears his name or looks upon the image of the crucified one, is dissolved in compunction and by affection is raised to his feet or to his embrace.

At Thanksgiving.

When the meal has been completed, give thanks to the Lord; and while you go to the church under the psalm, direct your mind upward. Intercede for benefactors, living and dead. If after the thanksgiving you add some particular prayer for the dead, you do well.

On Regular Reading.

On leaving the refectory for your cell, if it is not a fast day, complete the customary manner of praying; then read something good for edification, meditate, do whatever work has been assigned to you, or write. But if it is a fast day, take up the volume for your study and enter the place of regular reading. Sitting with the others, read in silence whatever you wish. But if you are asked by the presiding officer to say something good, answer humbly, briefly, and usefully, without curious citations. Use plain speech, and do not bring forward the vain curiosities of secular people. Do not entangle the brothers sitting around you, or your superiors, in your words, but express the thought of your mind simply in a few words, unless perhaps the presiding officer, for some definite reason, orders you to make a longer address.
