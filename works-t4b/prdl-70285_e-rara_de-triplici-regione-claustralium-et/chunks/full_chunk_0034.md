For although no one does good by his own holy powers, still no one does good unwillingly. Thus the grace of God and freedom of the will are related to one another in such a way that one without the other cannot suffice for the salvation of a human being; rather, both must necessarily come together for the working of virtue. Since, then, without the grace of God we can do nothing good, we ought to esteem ourselves vile and unworthy for all things, so that, while we submit ourselves to God in humility, we may always obtain greater things. And therefore this sixth degree of confession is meritorious, and the sole foundation of all the preceding degrees. For as long as the cenobite remains in this degree of humility, he will stand firm in the earlier ones.

The seventh degree of humility is not only to believe oneself, from the inmost affection of the heart, lower than all, but also to desire with a simple spirit that others believe and say this about him. This condition of holy humility is set on high, and in this age is loved by few monks. For the first condition of this profound humility seems to consist in this: that the monk ought from the heart to esteem himself lower than all. The second is that he rejoice to be esteemed vile and abject by all, and desire this from the inmost affection of the heart without pretense. The third condition is placed in a higher dwelling: namely, that the monk, from the same affection, delight to be treated most vilely and trampled on by all. Rooted in this humility, the cenobite loves every reproach and insult; he does not fear to be despised and cast away most vilely, because, humbly submitting himself, he places his whole self in the love of God, and hastens to insults as though to feasts, with the greatest cheerfulness. But so that you may understand the truth of this seventh degree, read the following considerations.

The first consideration directs the mind toward hope in divine love, which arises from a certain pious confidence. For although a person does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless anyone can in some measure recognize, from the disposition of his own heart,

what he ought to think about his state, whether good or bad.

Second, it must be considered that this good disposition of the heart, and of a will ordered toward God, is a sign of the approval of God’s love and of good works. For thus, when the Lord was born in the flesh, the angels sang: “Peace on earth to men of good will.”

Third, it must be noted that just as a good disposition of the heart always orders and rouses the mind through holy exercises toward the fear of God and the working of virtues, so the negligence of an evil disposition inclines it especially toward vices and acedia. The consideration of this difference, moreover, gives rise to conjecture.

Fourth, it must be considered that an evil disposition of the heart impedes and empties out the grace of God and merits in a person, since it makes the soul vile, ignoble, and degenerate, and always disposes it toward graver and worst things. Indeed, the root of evils of tongue and deed comes from an evil heart, as is clear in Saul and in many similar cases.

The fifth consideration is that the evil of deed and tongue is never properly examined unless the evil of the heart is first truly known. Each person, however, can examine his own heart better than another can, and consider what sort of person lies hidden within himself. Therefore it is enough for the cenobite, to whom care of another has not been entrusted, to inspect his own heart diligently, and in no way to busy himself with the scrutiny of another’s heart. But the shepherd of monks, to whom the care of souls is entrusted, is also commanded to know the face of his flock.

Sixth, it must be noted from these things that it is very rash to wish to examine another’s heart, and to judge the inner thoughts of brothers from their words and deeds. The Lord knows those who are his; nor is it granted to a human being to become a rash judge of another’s heart without the reason of office.

Seventh, it must be noted that a small evil of one’s own heart, known to the person himself, harms him more in his own heart than a great evil suspected doubtfully about another’s heart. For the former is to be wept over because it is certain and known; the latter is to be piously excused because it is unknown.
