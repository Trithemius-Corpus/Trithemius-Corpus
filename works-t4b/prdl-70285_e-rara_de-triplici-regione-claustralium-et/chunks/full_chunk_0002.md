If it pleases you, praise the author’s talent; but if it displeases you because of its worthlessness, correct my imperfect work. For I had excellent material for a spiritual building, supplied at another’s expense, but through my own inexperience I have constructed an unskilled structure. If I have become a fool, you compelled me. Therefore, whether the work pleases or displeases, it was easier for me, by writing ineptly, obediently to undertake your command than, against obedience, to keep silence. Yet I trust not in my own powers but in the merits of others, for a work that charity has produced will displease no one who loves God. I pray that your paternities may always remain happy in Christ. From Sponheim, the fourteenth day before the Kalends of September, in the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1497.

Here begins the book on the threefold region of cloistered persons, and on spiritual exercises.

When I recall to mind the state of the monastic life in this time, I grieve deeply over its calamity, because the fervor of spiritual exercise, which once produced the richest fruits in the order of our holy father Benedict, has now largely failed among monks. For we see cloistered persons returning in their ways to the world; we see the institution of regular observance being despised by many; and religion, which from the beginning had been instituted for contempt of the world, is now being recalled again by wicked monks to the pomps of the world. The crown has fallen from our head; the integrity of regular discipline has been corrupted; and the former fervor of holy life has perished. We see many monasteries of our order lying buried in the worst deformity, and very few restored to the purity of the rule.

The cause of this destruction of regular institution is negligence of spiritual exercises. For as soon as the spirit of fervor has grown faint in the minds of cloistered persons, the flesh, formerly dead through denial of itself, has revived; and the world, which before seemed dry and contemptible to devout hearts, has begun again to flourish in carnal minds. Indeed, the thought of the human heart, prone to evil from youth, unless it is daily spurred on to fervor of spirit by the bridle of spiritual exercise, soon falls back into its former negligences and grows lukewarm from its holy purpose. For just as fallow land, deprived of the careful attention of the cultivator, quickly returns to its former wasteland as thorns spring up, so the human mind, however pure or holy it may be, is quickly reduced to thickets of vices unless it is daily renewed to fervor of spirit by the exercise of devotion.

Led by this consideration, the fathers of our Bursfeld observance, devoted cultivators, commanded us and posterity by word and example that, since because of our frailty we cannot remain constant in fervor of spirit, at least by an evening recollection after Compline, accusing our daily negligences, we should renew the state of our mind to the fervor of our purpose.

Whoever neglects the exercise of daily institution falls into a deep torpor, as the wise man says in Ecclesiasticus: “If you do not hold yourself constantly in the fear of the Lord, your house will soon be overthrown,” which is understood as a good conscience. The rule of our father Benedict seems sufficient for a monk, since it is agreed to contain all material for exercises, both spiritual and bodily, necessary for guarding regular discipline among monks. And therefore all exercises of monks must be humbly submitted to the constitutions of the rule, because whatever is commanded either by us or by others for the instruction of monks is wholly assigned to the observance of the monastic norm.

For the sum of our entire religious life, which is acquired by the continuation of spiritual exercise, is contained in compendious form, as in a root, in the rule of monastic tradition. Therefore every exercise of monks, whether spiritual or bodily, by which the mind is led from love to a humble abasement of itself, is conformed to the institutions of the holy rule. The more it is known to present the monk to God as good, humble, and very near, the more it opposes the zeal of proud contempt.

The holy mysteries of this exercise direct the mind of their cultivators by an invisible teaching toward the love of God, as toward their proper end, along a secure path; they wonderfully obtain the grace of inward devotion; and they make the monk despise all earthly things as nothing, and burn sweetly with every time of the lower spirit [unclear]. The animal man does not grasp this wholesome exercise of devotion; the vain and curious monk does not understand this pursuit; the proud man does not admit this knowledge, to be preferred to all things. Happy is he who has learned to penetrate the secrets of this exercise; happy is he who has deserved to receive from the Lord this gift of holy compunction. Whoever has once perfectly obtained it will fear no adversities of this world.

But that exercise which, lacking its proper end, does not direct the minds of cloistered persons to observance of the commandments of the rule does not pour in the holy spirit of discipline, because where the obedience of subjection is despised, no gift is accepted by almighty God.
