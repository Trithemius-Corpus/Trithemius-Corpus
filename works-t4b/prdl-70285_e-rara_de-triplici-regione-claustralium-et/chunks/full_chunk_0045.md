God created you in his goodness, not because your condition was necessary to him, but because it was advantageous to you alone. Whoever considers this goodness of God with intent meditation rightly praises his Creator.

Second, so that you may not be lifted up into pride, O man, consider the vileness of your condition: that you were made from the mud of the earth, and after a little while, consumed by worms, you will return into the earth as dust. Why then are you proud, earth and ashes? Your conception has guilt; your birth, misery; your life, punishment; your dissolution, the distress of death. For a man born of woman, living a short time, is filled with many miseries; and then he is especially miserable when he does not recognize that he is miserable in the flesh.

Third, consider the origin of your conception, and you will find your soul stained by the foul ugliness of original sin. For we are born children of God's wrath, but, obtaining the grace of baptism in the blood of Jesus Christ, we are cleansed from the original stain. Rightly, through this consideration, we love our Savior, by whose mercy we have been cleansed from the original stain.

Fourth, faithful soul, consider how exceedingly vile you have become by repeating your ways in the act of sin, for you have shown yourself an adulteress to Christ, your bridegroom, though he joined you to himself by his own blood, wholly unstained. What are you doing, O miserable one? Why do you abandon your most beloved bridegroom, or why, foolish one, do you withdraw from your beginning? Know yourself to have been placed in the flesh, not so that you may consent to its vices and desires, but rather so that you may resist them by the command of reason. For as often as, by dissembling, you have yielded your consent, so to speak, to the disordered movements of the flesh and to things contrary to divine rectitude, so often, turned away from the highest good and from your bridegroom, you show yourself an adulteress. Beware, beware, O soul redeemed by the blood of Christ, lest the love of the flesh deceive you; for just as the first man, deceived by love for the woman who was his companion, fell into death, so any soul that consents to the pleasures of its flesh shamefully withdraws from its beginning, which is God.

But alas, miserable one, where have you fallen? You have dwelt in a foreign land; you have been defiled with the dead; you have been counted with those descending into hell. You have abandoned the fountain of wisdom; you have turned backward; you have committed adultery.

But hear my counsel, and return as quickly as possible to the guide of your youth, Jesus; submit yourself wholly and humbly to him. He is merciful. He will gladly receive you as you return to him; he will forgive your sins, and, if you ask, he will bestow the grace of his love. But you must withdraw from the affections of your flesh if you desire to come into the sight of your Savior. Therefore, if you are converted to Jesus, be converted in truth, because the highest Truth cannot love anything except what is true.

There is also another mode of self-knowledge, which is taken up with respect to God, whenever an incomparable comparison between God and man is contemplated. Concerning this mode, Bernard, clear in contemplation, says: Never does the measure of human imperfection perceive itself better than in the light of God's countenance and in the mirror of divine vision. For there, one who is more in the day and sees more clearly what is lacking in himself amends day by day, by likeness, whatever he has lost through unlikeness, drawing near to him by likeness from whom he has been made distant by unlikeness. Therefore let us raise the sharpness of the mind to the most luminous mirror of divine perfection; let us submit our affection to that immutable good, so that in God's wisdom we may be able to perceive our own foolishness, and in his power consider our own weakness.

On Keeping the Memory of One's Sins. Root V.

The fifth root of the fear of God is the memory of sins, and the foulness of sin itself when it is often contemplated. For truly, whoever genuinely understands the ugliness of mortal sin always fears the pit of this evil. But so that you may in some way recognize the gravity of sin, you must consider the seven points that follow.

First, then, consider the beauty of God's justice, to which sin is wholly contrary and therefore most to be fled. For through sin the soul is wholly turned away from the highest good, and the love that is naturally within it for its beginning is changed into hatred.
