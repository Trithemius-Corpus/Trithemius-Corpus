Therefore every monk must examine himself diligently and carefully cleanse himself of the faults he has committed according to the ordinance of the Church. But he who, on examining his conscience, has found it clean from mortal sin should offer humble praises to God and should not neglect to add the examination of the things that follow.

The second thing necessary for a good man, then, is to intend God principally in all his works, and to order all things, as far as he is able, to his praise and honor. But if, because of the multitude and variety of works, a man is hindered and cannot always actually keep God before his eyes, let him at least habitually conform his intention of living according to the most gracious will of God to this desire.

The third is that in all things he obey God, holy mother Church, and the prelates set over him, and not depart from the path of right and enlightened reason; for whoever despises this threefold obedience has denied the faith. It is therefore necessary to show equal obedience to these three in the right order, according to the ordinance of reason, so that the monk’s life may remain blameless in its actions and free from fear.

These three things, possessed with humble sincerity, make a good man and show the state of a conscience that hopes well in the Lord. But the man who lacks one of these is not perfectly good, even if he appears to be good. If, however, repenting of his sins, he has resolved henceforth to fulfill these three things, he is now made good and pleasing to God, however evil he may have been before. Moreover, in order that from a good man a good religious, or spiritual man, may be born, three other things are required, according to the aforesaid Castellensis.

The first thing required for making a good religious is that purity of heart be cleansed by the practice of discipline from every rivalry or incitement of the flesh. This purity is never better acquired than if the world, together with all its things, is contemned.

The second thing through which a good man becomes a good religious is called spiritual freedom in God. This freedom, once the phantasms of all things have been excluded,

can raise itself, unencumbered, upward to God in all its exercises, and continually lift itself without impediment in thanksgiving together with devout prayers.

In the third way a good man becomes spiritually good when, through the desire of love, the spirit is united with God by grace. These last two make our cenobite spiritual and often introduce him among the citizens of the third region. Therefore whoever desires to have good confidence in the testimony of his conscience should humbly strive to exercise himself in the aforesaid things, because security is never prudently presumed when danger is not avoided. And what is so dangerous as always to live in negligence and sins, and to be unwilling to amend the errors one has committed? The confidence of the just, arising from the testimony of a good conscience, is always best, and is continually subject to God in humble service. But the impious and sinners, disturbed by their own actions, raise themselves against God and do not trust in the testimony of conscience, but rather presume vainly.

Here follow the seven roots of divine fear, which are laid fundamentally in the first step of humility.

Since this second region principally instructs the cenobite in due order through the steps of humility that proceed from the fear of God, it is necessary for him clearly to know the roots of that fear, if he wishes to reach the love of God by ascending. Therefore, so that we may be able to construct this spiritual house to perfection, we must place those roots in order in the foundation. We did not set them out in the first step of humility for this reason: that we should not confuse the order of the things to be said with excessive prolixity. There are, then, seven roots of the fear of God, through which the cenobite is led to compunction and, by making progress in true humility, is more strongly rooted.
