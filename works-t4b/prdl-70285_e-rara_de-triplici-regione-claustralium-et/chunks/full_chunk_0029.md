Therefore let frequent reading about the last things that await us take its beginning; then let the deeds of the holy fathers be read, so that the memory of future things may shake the mind with fear of God, and the exemplary way of life of the saints may kindle the desire to imitate them. For the wise man says: “Remember your last things, and you will not sin forever.” The way of life of the holy fathers is set before us as an example, and the destruction of the wicked is recounted to us for our instruction, so that virtue when rewarded may stir desire, and iniquity when punished may forbid license to sin.

Therefore the devout cenobite should always recall to memory the examples and virtues of the saints; he should consider the brief pleasure of the depraved, but their eternal torment, so that by fearing unhappiness he may deserve to escape it, and by cleaving to virtues obtain their perpetual joy. The distinct condition of the good and the evil is no slight matter, since each is described as destined to remain forever, and is ended by no limit. O eternity, always to be feared by all mortals: in it, after this life, there is no fear of consummation for the just, and no hope of liberation for the perverse. Let him who can think what eternity is think it; where there is no end, who dares imagine a boundary? And when he has found nothing in eternity except eternity, let him understand how nothing is all the time in which he lives in this world.

Indeed, once the spirit has been stripped of the flesh, it is necessarily compelled to enter the abyss of eternity; and the condition which it has once obtained according to its merits it will bear without end and will not exchange for another. For he who dies good in the love of God will obtain eternal life according to his merits. But he who is evil and, without the penance of reconciliation, once departs from this life will remain to be tormented without end with the demons in hell. From these meditations servile fear arises in the mind, through which one gradually arrives at love. But so that we may be able to lead the cenobite successively through these roots to charity, it is our pleasure to explain briefly, in order, the degrees of humility described in the Rule.

The first degree of humility, therefore, is described by Saint Benedict in the Rule as the fear of God, which is called the beginning of wisdom. The fear of the Lord is handed down under a threefold distinction: namely, servile, initial, and filial, which is also called chaste. From this threefold distinction three roots are said to proceed, according to which the beginning cenobite ought to order his meditations. For each root has its own proper meditations; and when one is rightly disposed, another, better one is always generated.

From servile fear, then, when it is assumed not only servilely but prudently and virtuously, come meditations on horror and punishments, which, restraining the soul from sin, gradually introduce filial fear. Indeed, when by consideration of punishments the mind is turned to horror, boldness in sinning is restrained, because the servant who fears blows keeps the Lord’s commands. This servile fear by itself is not sufficient for meriting the paternal inheritance; nevertheless, as an initial effect, it disposes one toward filial fear, which is sufficient.

Initial fear is placed midway between the two, and is easily changed from servile into filial. This initial fear receives meditations on the last things, and dreads both incurring punishments and losing the sweet homeland. And this step is placed in the Rule, where its roots too are described.

Filial fear, however, embraces its meditations in love, since it fears nothing except that the beloved Father may be offended. Of this fear the wise man says: “The fear of the Lord is glory and boasting, joy and a crown of exultation. The fear of the Lord will delight the heart and will give gladness and joy, and length of days.” And again: “It will be well with the one who fears God at the last things, and on the day of his death he will be blessed.” This fear is also called chaste, because it detests evil not from fear of punishments, but from love alone.
