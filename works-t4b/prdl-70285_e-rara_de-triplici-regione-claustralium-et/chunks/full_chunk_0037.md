For the soul that, consenting to the law of God, is subjected to God through an upright will, even if it still seems weak, is ordered toward the path of virtue. For when the flesh resists, virtue brings it to completion, and how upright the will is is proved by the violence done by the spirit. Yet a will is often called upright and good even when it is found to be less strong. The good that it loves it cannot always carry out; the evil that it hates it grieves that, through weakness, it has committed. Through the weakness of the flesh it is very near the lowest region, yet by the uprightness of its intention and by the effort to make progress it is continually kindled toward the freedom of the spirit. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling depresses the sense that thinks many thoughts. O who could describe this battle between spirit and flesh, where the spirit itself desires to subject the flesh to itself through an upright will, and the weak flesh always resists the spirit through contrary desires?

The second degree is a will strong in uprightness, so that it both vigorously flees the evil that it hates against the impulses of the flesh, and firmly grasps, by doing it, the good that it loves, although with labor.

The third degree is a devout will, by which the mind, with enlarged heart, embraces the way of God’s commandments and delights in them as in all riches. For indeed the will does not long remain strong if it has not been devout in the commandments of God.

The fourth degree is a full will, so that it does not divide its powers at all, but continually stretches itself wholly toward its God through the renunciation of all things. For this degree three things are necessary, so that the good will may be made full in us.

The first is the complete renunciation of oneself; for a will is proved still to be weak if it is not mortified through the total renunciation of self.

The second thing required for a full good will is held to be perfect resignation. If anyone desires to apprehend it, let him fervently ascend the third degree of humility.

The third member necessary for the fourth degree of good will is called the total extension of the will into God. Through it the soul, admitted into divine love and sweetly burning, is in some way transformed into that same love, and is frequently admitted into the third region.

Finally, this transformation into divine love, according to the opinion of Bernard, is love, affection, adhesion, union, charity, or fruition. No one can attain it unless he has learned to mortify all his lower affections of spirit. For the spirit of a person who has his heart lifted up to God is called a perfect will, and it is united with God in the sweetest bond. Therefore in these degrees of good will the faithful cenobite ought without interruption to direct his effort upward, and always to examine with prudent care how much he is making progress or falling short. For Saint Bernard says: Just as one who remains in a vicious will is condemned, so one who does not strive to make progress in other things is blameworthy. For where the effort to make progress is laid aside, the danger of falling away is incurred.

On the Example and Experimental Knowledge of All the Things Mentioned Above

Article VII.

Although a person does not know whether he is worthy of love or hatred, nevertheless each person can, by diligently examining his conscience, recognize what he ought to think about the condition of his state. What sort of person each one lies hidden as within himself is made manifest by the testimony of his own conscience. Therefore, so that the cenobite may clearly discern the condition of his state, according to John of Kastl on the Rule he ought to observe three things in his conscience, without which no one becomes a good Christian, and no one is judged a good man or monk.

The first, then, that is required for a good man is the good testimony of conscience, namely that no mortal sin lies hidden in the conscience. For the mind that is polluted by the guilt of mortal sin is testified by conscience to have an evil state.
