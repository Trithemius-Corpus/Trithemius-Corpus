Preface to the Second Principal Part of the Spiritual Exercise of Cloistered Persons. It begins happily.

Since, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have recently completed the little work on the threefold region of cloistered persons, assigned to us by the annual chapter, it now remains that, taking up according to our strength the second principal part of the work, we describe the manner and form of the daily spiritual exercise. For among cloistered persons there are many weak and feeble people, and some of them perhaps would gladly ascend to the contemplation of divine love if they had a manner suited to beginners.

For all who have been established in holy religious life, great care must be taken that the mind not grow sluggish in idleness, since whoever withdraws from the continual labor of spiritual exercise gradually falls into the depth of torpor. For just as this visible fire of ours cannot subsist in its operation without fuel suitable to it, so the love of inner compunction cannot remain in a person’s mind without the continual exercise of devotion. For as soon as the labor of spiritual study has been interrupted through negligence, all compunction of devotion gradually vanishes. No one, indeed, can remain stable and firm in a holy purpose unless he strives daily to renew the fervor of his mind through some exercise. For as soon as the fuel of divine love is withdrawn, devotion is changed into torpor.

For Scripture says, “Every idle person is in desires.” Therefore, lest the mind gradually slip away through laziness from the holy fervor of the spirit, it must learn to renew itself each day through some spiritual exercise, so that, while the fuel of love is supplied day by day, the desire for making progress may be further kindled. Thus, we read, the most holy fathers once made progress in the order; thus we know that they remained in devotion, because they knew very well the instability of the human mind, and therefore they were accustomed daily to stir themselves to a new

fervor of spirit. One drove his mind toward compunction of devotion through the study of sacred reading; another stirred up the sweet seed of Passion through meditation; another, through the continuation of a holy purpose, placed before his inner eyes each day the image of approaching death; another, by a fixed manner in prayers, daily offered himself to the Lord with tears.

It is believed that none among them was without a daily spiritual exercise of the mind; none was found given over to idleness, because the kingdom of God is promised to those laboring in the vineyard of the Lord, but denied to the idle. Considering this, the devout fathers of the annual chapter unanimously decreed that the present exercise should be taken up and offered for practice to their subjects who did not have better things.

And although so great a multitude of monks subject to the aforementioned chapter neither can nor ought to be confined to the formula of a single exercise, nevertheless it is set forth for all in common, so that anyone who does not have a better exercise may use it. It is especially fitting that novices converted to the order should exercise themselves in the manner set forth below, until, grounded in humility, they grow through compunction to better things. But those who consider that they have already, for a long time, been accustomed by devotion to dwell in higher things should at least allow these things, without envy, to the little ones. For I do not doubt that many persons devoted to better modes of spiritual exercise are living in the order, who know by long practice how to remain in the supreme region of the spirit through the ray of divine love. We in no way force our small offerings upon these people, whom we know to be higher than the stages of the present exercise. For we, imperfect as we are, write for beginners who are making progress; we are humbly prepared not to teach the perfect, but rather to hear them as our teachers.
