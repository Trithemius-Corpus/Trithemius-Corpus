For, as it seems to me, it profits little to describe the beauty of contemplation unless we also know how to show those who desire it the manner of arriving at it. Therefore, anyone wishing to ascend through desire of love to the pleasantness of this highest region should take care to exercise himself diligently in the labors of the middle region; for unless in that region he has been purified from sensual passions through the pursuit of holy compunction, he approaches this one not without danger. For the completion of this exercise let him often recall to memory the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ; let him weep from delight in divine love; let him learn, frequently and attentively, to pass through the text of that Passion in meditation, and to rouse himself to compassion for the Savior. And because the body, which is corrupted, weighs down the soul, so that the mind may be able to lift itself powerfully in this meditation on the Lord's Passion, let him take care, if need be, to present to his thoughts images truly sensible. Therefore, withdrawing from crowds in silence, let him begin to turn over the sequence of the Passion by remembering it; let him see himself with the eyes of the mind as present at each event with the suffering Lord; let him hear Jesus speaking in imagination; let him go with him as he passes through all the places of the Passion. Then, at each article, let him apply his affections from the desire of compassion; let him gaze with faithful eyes upon Jesus suffering, set before him; weeping from anguish of heart, let him grow accustomed, in spirit, to address him as though bodily present, and to embrace him wounded at the column and on the cross through desire of love. For the mind will soon be able to be kindled in the love of the Lord Jesus, if it truly grows accustomed to suffer with him through tears of meditation. But I remember that I have written elsewhere at greater length about the most effective exercise of the Lord's Passion; therefore here I have treated it briefly. For those who do not know how to meditate, it is enough to have given these beginnings, since those who know have higher things.

Therefore, when affection has been purified from every passion and concupiscence that impedes it, the mind, with the intellect assisting, begins to think somewhat upon the goodness and beauty of the highest good; then it desires to comprehend the immutable good. Contemplation, however, is sometimes accomplished by dilation of the mind, because when the soul stretches itself more vehemently by its own effort in holy meditation, meditation is changed into contemplation. This mode of contemplation usually rises by three degrees: art, by which we learn what must be done; exercise, by which we carry out what has been learned; and attention, by which we fulfill the duty.

Sometimes contemplation is taken up by an uplifting of the mind into God, because when the mind has been divinely irradiated above the human, it passes as if by an unwonted rapture into the contemplation of divine things. This mode also is raised into the height of contemplation by three degrees: for sometimes the understanding of a person, divinely inspired, receives something that wholly exceeds the measure of its own knowledge. The second degree raises the mind above its own effort, when, divinely illumined, it perceives something to which no human effort attains. In the third degree the mind is raised on high, when it is divinely illumined in contemplation above the bounds of human nature and effort.

At times, too, contemplation directs itself into the mountain of divine darkness by alienation of the mind, when, the memory of present things lulled to sleep, the soul passes, through the illumination of divine working, into a state unknown to human knowledge. And this mode especially belongs to this highest region of the spirit, in which the mind of the true lover of God is seen to be kindled with such fervor of love that it is often proved to be alienated from itself. For sometimes, because of the greatness of devotion, it is rapt outside itself; sometimes, through excessive wonder, it is lifted above itself; at times, because of immeasurable exultation, it abandons itself, and through the ray of love passes into the highest elevation of contemplation. But no mortal person understands the pleasantness of this loftiest and highest region unless, purified through the experience of love, he ascends to it.
