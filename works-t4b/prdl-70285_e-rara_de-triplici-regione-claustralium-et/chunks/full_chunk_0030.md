Servile fear carries out its exercises in one way; initial fear applies itself to the virtues in another; and filial fear proceeds in its works in yet a very different way. For just as the affections of hearts arise in various ways, so the exercises of one fear take place in different ways. The publican, mindful of his own crimes, feared the sentence of the judge in one way; Jerome said that he heard in another way the sound of the trumpet calling the dead to judgment. For the holy Gospel says of the former: “Standing far off, the publican would not lift his eyes to heaven, but struck his breast, saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner.’” But that blessed Jerome says: “Whether I drink or eat, I always think I hear that trumpet sounding in my ears: ‘Rise, dead, come to judgment.’” The compunction of both arises from fear, but each is not produced from the desire of one and the same affection. For the publican, terrified by the guilt of his sins, feared the sentence of the just judge, and therefore, pierced with compunction, said, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” Jerome, however, is not shaken by fear from the guilt of conscience, but, founded in charity and regarding himself as a useless servant, he reveres the abyss of divine justice.

The second degree is to relinquish one’s own will for the love of God, and to love one’s own desire in nothing contrary to the rule.

This degree arises from the first ascent of humility and raises one to the third. For truly, one who fears God and truly fears being separated from him and undergoing punishments hates nothing more than his own will. For the faithful cenobite knows that every sin arises from one’s own will alone, and therefore he detests it as the gate of hell. For that blessed man spoke truly: “Take away one’s own will, and there will no longer be hell.” For nothing burns in hell except one’s own will; nothing loses the grace of God except voluntary fault. Therefore whoever truly fears God and fears losing his grace greatly dreads his own will as the ambusher of his soul. Consideration of the danger of one’s own will makes the cenobite despise his own desire and deny himself to himself. And this denial of one’s own will and of oneself is accomplished in three ways, as will be said in what follows.

First, then, he denies himself to himself who strives to live according to the teaching of eternal wisdom and not according to his own habits, and to obey Christ in all things as he counsels him to humility. Therefore let the Savior say to the cenobite: If you wish to deny yourself to yourself and follow me, turn away your eyes, lest they see harmful things; turn away your ears, lest they hear vain things; and in place of the sweet things of this world, take up bitter things. Learn to despise the delights of your body, to love tranquillity and peace of heart, to receive all tribulations patiently and joyfully for love of me, and desire to appear vile and contemptible to all. Consequently, learn to break your own wills in all things, to mortify the desires of the flesh, to despise all earthly things, and, as far as possible, to conform your life according to your strength to the manner of life of your Savior. For whoever says that he remains in Christ ought himself also to walk just as he walked. Whoever has done these things has well denied himself.

Second, the denial of one’s own will is rightly accomplished when the advantages of others are placed before one’s own. For the holy Apostle says: “Not seeking the things that are their own, but the things of others.” For according to Bernard’s judgment, the soul is laid down for the brothers as often as their advantages are preferred to one’s own benefits for the love of God.

Third, the resignation of one’s own will receives the fullness of perfection when the cenobite, pierced with compunction and wholly given over to God, desires out of love to serve him freely, without regard for reward. And this most holy resignation of one’s own will makes a person very near to God and always rests in his love.

But just as this resignation of oneself receives its substance from the second degree of humility, so too it necessarily requires an inward and complete resignation to the will of God in all things.
