Whoever, already disordered in himself, has once fallen into the first degree of pride, unless he strives to rise again as quickly as possible through penitence, soon casts himself down into the second, and consequently into all the others in order. The full measure of the disorder of carnal monks in themselves is expressed in these degrees of self-exaltation, through which the disordered citizens of the lowest region are shown forth. What we have said about disordered and proud monks must also be understood of the prelates of the lowest region.

In What Way the Citizens of the Lowest Region, Carnal Monks, Are Disordered with Regard to the Goods of the Monastery.
Article VI

Whoever, while established in the monastery, neglects to order his inner affections toward the purity of his holy profession, also frequently exceeds measure grievously in outward matters. For monks disordered toward God are consequently shown to be disordered toward everything. For just as the good cenobite, united by love to the divine will, considers everything with a simple eye according to the right order of reason, so one turned away from God through disordered affections sins even in good things when he uses them badly. Therefore carnal monks, inhabitants of the lowest region, are badly disordered not only with regard to the soul, but also with regard to the temporal substance of the monastery. For outward disturbance of the body follows the inner confusion of the mind, and the one is shown by the other.

First, then, the citizen of the lowest region is badly ordered with regard to the goods of the monastery when he demands that they be granted to him in an improper order, or in any way usurps more than the use of regular observance permits. For our rule, which commands that all necessary things be given to monks, utterly forbids superfluities.

Second, those who do not fear to give and receive against the prohibition of the master use the goods of the monastery badly; so do those who act unfaithfully, with deceit and fraud, in the things entrusted to them. For the monk who, at his own pleasure, without reasonable cause, against the rule, and without permission, dissipates the goods of the monastery in any way,

sins against the rule like an unfaithful and false proprietor.

Third, monks or abbots show themselves disordered toward the goods of the monastery when they devote them to unlawful uses. Theft and sacrilege are committed together when the goods of the monastery are converted to illicit uses. For the substance of the monastery, dedicated to God, should be kept like the vessels of the holy altar, lest it be dissipated in any way through negligence. But whoever forbids the goods of the cloister to be spent uselessly would by no means grant that they be squandered sinfully on base uses. What answer will monks and rulers of the lowest region give to the lawgiver in the strict examination, when they do not fear to dissipate the goods of the monastery on parents, relatives, minstrels, and shameful people, as occasion and reward for sin?

Fourth, carnal abbots and monks sin more gravely in the goods of the monastery when they gather them with excessive greed, to the detriment of divine charity and of their own salvation. For those made subject to the tribute of avarice think of nothing but profits and money; violating feast days, they do not observe them; they always desire to increase temporal goods; they do not notice the losses of charity. They grieve more over a dying beast than over a sinner perishing. Indeed, all disordered cloistered men are so blind that they consider avarice prudence and think wealth is reform. They join field to field; they call gain piety; and where they see temporal things abound, there they say the best discipline is observed. They refuse to spend even small sums for the common good of the order, because they have truly lost the order of charity. They raise useless, curious, and damnable structures on high; they keep costly vestments and ornaments less necessary for divine service; they clothe sacred things in gold and leave the poor of Christ naked. This avaricious curiosity does not befit monks, since we are commanded to imitate the ancient saints and are ordered to sell these things for the support of the poor. But a covetous monk and an avaricious abbot are as far from the purpose of holy religion as the devil is from heaven.
