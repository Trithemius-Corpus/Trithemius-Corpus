And insofar as they disfigure the soul, let him despise himself as more vile than others, even if he knows their public crimes. For since the root of their heart is utterly unknown to him, he cannot know how the divine election concerning them was ordained from eternity. Who would have believed that the thief crucified with the Lord was elected from eternity, if he judged him according to his works? All things turn out for good for the elect, since where sin is seen to have abounded, often, by the will of divine election, greater grace is found. For divine love regards and embraces its elect from eternity, and considers them elect not according to the misery in which they now are, but according to their future glory. Let the cenobite therefore beware of disparaging others; let him beware of judging others, if he desires to escape eternal death. For who are you, that you judge another’s servant? He stands to be judged by his own Lord alone.

The disposition of this seventh degree, moreover, because of that clause “from the innermost disposition of the heart,” possesses a threefold root of its origin: namely truth, hope, and charity. For when the faithful cenobite sees that he does not have his being from himself, he truly considers himself, insofar as he is from himself, to be nothing; and consequently, in the same respect, he considers his works, insofar as they come from himself who is nothing, to be nothing, since he is not ignorant that nothing is made from nothing. Therefore the monk greatly rejoices in the truth when, in this respect, he esteems himself and his works as nothing, because he fears falling into the vice of falsehood if he believed himself to be something great. And so, withdrawing from himself, he fails in himself, and places his being, living, and power in God alone. Secondly, this disposition is said to arise from hope of acquiring greater grace, because when a person fails in himself through humility, he comes nearer to God’s grace. Thirdly, the humility of this disposition takes its beginning from the love of God, and receives continual increase in it. For whoever loves God perfectly seeks nothing other than his honor in all things, and does not know how to exalt himself in anything, but desires to be subject to all for God’s sake. And the more he is cast down by insults and injuries,

the more, rejoicing, he is glad in the Lord. For he sees that he is wretched, so that in himself he may proclaim the Lord glorious. He who truly loves his Lord seeks nothing other than his glory.

It is clear from what has been said that the perfect charity which is promised to those ascending the degrees of humility is displayed in this seventh degree. For the other five degrees that follow in the Rule add nothing to the perfection of the inner man existing in the seventh, but rather compose the outer man, lest he disturb the inner. Finally, from this seventh degree we can erect certain new ascents of humility and raise all the other degrees higher in them. For a more fervent filial fear resides in him who is able to make further progress in the breadth of the others.

Now that the common ascents of the heart to the degree of charity have been known, let the cenobite consider with what zeal, what effort, what perseverance he ought to exercise himself in these things, so that he may reach this seventh degree of love. But whoever does not always strive to advance in these degrees of humility should know that he sins against the Rule; and unless he recovers his former holy effort by repentance, he incurs the sentence of damnation. For the Rule says thus: “But if he should ever act otherwise, let him know that he will be condemned by the one whom he mocks.” So if a person making less progress in religion believes himself guilty before the divine judgment, what else does one who abandons the effort to advance deserve but to undergo the sentence of damnation? For where an account is rendered, according to the Rule, even concerning a lesser fault among those striving to advance, monks who despise every effort to advance are without doubt condemned by the sentence of the Judge. Therefore, if you do not wish to incur the danger of damnation, strive always to preserve the effort of advancing under the Rule.

Since the other five degrees of humility are said to pertain to the guarding of the inner man, let us also briefly explain them. For a monk who neglects to compose his outward conduct according to the Rule will not long remain well composed in inward things. For just as an unclothed body is corrupted, so humility without conduct is emptied.
