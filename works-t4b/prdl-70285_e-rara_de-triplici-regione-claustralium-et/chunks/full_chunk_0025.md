For I think that no one is unaware, especially among monks, how useful and necessary it is for us to restrain the forces of sensual passions. Indeed, whoever desires to reach, in this region, the due end of regular conduct that we have described must subdue his disordered natural habits and movements with prudent constancy, because no one is truly able to resign himself to another’s command who does not firmly restrain the forces of sensual passions. For one who desires to make progress must, with steadfast mind, abandon both the movements and the prudence of the flesh, and show himself to be a son of divine grace, not of flesh and blood. For Scripture says: “If you live according to the rule of the flesh, you will die.” Indeed, those who sow in the flesh will also reap corruption from the flesh. But flesh and blood will not possess the kingdom of God. Yet what does the Savior say to us? “A man’s enemies,” he says, “are those of his own household.” And who are these household members, except the sensual powers that oppose the spirit? For concupiscibility and irascibility, our enemies, stir up every evil in us against the spirit, and continually disturb the house of conscience with various passions. For as long as these sensual powers pursue their natural movements and courses in a person, they do not allow him to ascend to the liberty of the spirit. Concupiscibility takes pleasure in delights through the sense of the flesh, while the contrary irascible power detests bodily hardships. Meanwhile, as long as this war of the flesh is being waged in a person, the monk is considered restless, carnal, and a cultivator of the lowest region. Therefore whoever desires to come to eternal life must firmly restrain these disordered powers of the senses in all things.

Therefore let the faithful coenobite take care to direct the force of concupiscibility toward hardships that are acceptable to God, and to reform all his affections according to God’s good pleasure. If he does this, he will confidently pray to the Lord: “My soul has longed to desire your justifications at all times.” And that other verse,

he will often cry out sweetly with tears: “As the deer longs for the springs of waters, so my soul longs for you, O God.” Then, cleaving to God, he must prudently set the irascible power against sensual pleasures, and diligently reform it toward hatred of vices. Thus the soul, ordered toward its own principle, will not fear to endure any hard things for the love of God, and through the irascible power it will begin to struggle firmly against all carnal desires. And then the devout coenobite, as the spirit lifts him up, will cry: “My soul refused to be comforted.” And that saying of the Apostle: “I have counted all things as dung.” For the soul that truly loves God despises everything except him.

Now from these two powers every disordered affection arises; and, similarly, good affections are generated from these by way of opposition. For from the concupiscible power joy and hope take their beginning, and the irascible power gives birth to sorrow and fear. Saint Bernard speaks of these in this way: “The whole heart of man is turned about by four affections; if we bring these back to God, the whole heart of man is converted.”

On the faithful cleansing of the powers.

But we are by no means able to restrain the sensual powers unless we also faithfully cleanse the intellectual powers. For this cleansing great and continual diligence is needed, since the coenobite who is loosened by the sluggishness of negligence is never cleansed from vicious affections. For he carries corruption back from his own errors. Therefore the coenobite, a cultivator of this middle region, must correct his inner powers from every disorder, if he wishes to remain constantly in the pursuit of progress. These intellectual powers must be cleansed in this way.
