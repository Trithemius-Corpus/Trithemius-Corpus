In insults of words, patience preserves the purpose of humility in a prudent man who loves God; in the removal of possessions and temporal advantages, it receives increase; and in bodily injury, with conscience silent, it preserves the rule of virtue. Consider now, O faithful soul, how your most loving Savior Jesus endured these three things most patiently for your redemption; and if you believe that you love him, do not find it burdensome to bear similar things patiently for love of him.

From the three degrees immediately preceding we have established one sum of the cenobitic goal, which, as it seems to us, we reasonably call the three-degree ascent. For by three degrees, namely the second, third, and fourth, the goal of the cenobitic institution is perfected, and the denial of one’s own will is brought to completion. For where patience, the preserver of virtues, is joined to the denial of one’s own will and to obedience, every pleasure and concern of the body is mortified. Whoever has deserved to ascend this three-degree ascent has fulfilled the Lord’s command, where he says in the Gospel: “Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself,” through the second degree of humility; “and take up his cross,” through the third; “and follow me,” through the fourth.

The fifth degree of humility is true confession of sins. The faithful cenobite is said to ascend into it if he confesses to his abbot, with humble confidence, all evil thoughts that come into his heart, or any evils committed in secret. Indeed, if anything is committed through impatience, at the suggestion of the adversary, in the preceding three-degree ascent, it is restored in this one through contrition and humble confession. For no one is found so perfect that he does not contract some stain from earthly conduct, as Scripture says: “In many things we all offend.” For no one is clean from sin, not even an infant whose life upon earth is one day. Therefore this degree necessarily follows the fourth without an intervening stage; in it all the negligences of the preceding degrees are corrected. True confession raises up the soldier falling in combat, heals him when wounded by the enemy, and strengthens the weak and fainthearted. Therefore whoever desires to stand in battle and bravely conquer the enemy should often have recourse to the defenses of true confession.

For it is impossible for one wounded by the enemy to recover health if he neglects to disclose the wound itself to the physician. You, therefore, faithful cenobite, who desire to save your soul and to be a good soldier of Jesus Christ, do not omit humble confession, because you will never be able to conquer the devil better than if you take care faithfully to disclose to your abbot all the thoughts that he suggests to you, and all your sins. It is certain to me, and proved by the destruction of many, that the cenobite who refuses to keep this degree commits himself to being conquered by many temptations of the enemy.

The sixth degree of humility arises from true confession: that the monk should consider himself base and unworthy for all things. According to the Rule, we believe him to be a true monk whom we see, from humble patience, to be content with every baseness and extremity in food and clothing. This baseness is placed in the inconveniences of ordinary use. The former gladly embraces patience in cheaper garments, foods, and any necessities of the body; the latter delights in serving the sick, lepers, and the outcast, in being put to shame, degraded, and despised in all things. Therefore the monk who is truly dead to the flesh and the world should be content with every baseness and extremity, should show himself a faithful worker in all things, and yet, because of the corruption of nature by which he offends in many things, should believe himself base and unworthy.

Indeed, whatever good is done in this world, however holy and religiously one lives in this age, it cannot suffice for anyone to merit eternal life unless God’s great mercy deigns to assist. We receive the greatest things for the smallest; we await the kingdom of God not as owed to us from our own merit, but as promised from grace. For the holy apostle says: “The sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that will be revealed in us.” For if we receive God’s justice without mercy, we are worthy of hell.
