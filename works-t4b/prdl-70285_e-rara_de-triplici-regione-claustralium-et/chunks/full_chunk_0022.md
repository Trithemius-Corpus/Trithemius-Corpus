For just as motion itself is always ordered in heaven by the order of the first mover as its cause, so continuous labor is carried on in the middle region, so that each intellectual power may perfect its own object and in no way depart from the order naturally implanted in it. There are three powers of the soul of this kind: memory, intellect, and will.

Of these, memory gathers the likenesses of things and phantasms, and extends itself toward what is difficult. The intellect, by distinguishing those phantasms, considers them, and there investigates the true. The will embraces what has been approved and ordered by the intellect, and is occupied with the known good. When these three powers of the soul rightly preserve their own order, they lead the spirit in faith toward heavenly things.

This second region is compared to the ethereal heaven, in which, just as there is the continuous motion and position of the stars, so in this middle region there is continuous motion and labor against the struggles of vices. And just as the motion of the stars accomplishes the order of its office implanted in it by nature, so too in this region the equity of rightness requires that it be done: namely, that each power of the soul, when it is subjected to its own object, may be perfected toward the end appointed for it, so far as can befall wayfarers. Yet none of these powers can simply be perfected to fullness in this region, since no one in this pilgrim condition is so perfect that he cannot be more perfect in his own order. For even if memory, in its own object, which is the difficult, could behold the gathered likenesses and phantasms of all creatures, it would not be satisfied unless it could apprehend their principle, which is God. But this is altogether impossible for this region, in which we can form no phantasms or likenesses of God. The intellect too, the counselor, as I might say, of the blind will, extends itself toward the true; but however much it considers its object, it is not satisfied until it can apprehend all truth and come, without the opinion of fantastic speculation, to knowledge of eternal, highest, and infallible truth. But this perfection is possible for no wayfarer so long as he dwells in the body of mortality. The will also cannot be satisfied and truly

delighted in its own object, which is the good, unless it reaches that immutable and highest good which no instability of change overshadows. The highest truth and immutable good is God, who said to Moses: No man shall see me and live. And the disciple of the highest truth says: No one has ever seen God. But the prophet too, addressing the Lord, says: Truly you are a hidden God. Although, then, the three aforesaid powers of the soul cannot perfectly comprehend their object in this life, nevertheless none should cease from its office, lest it run into the danger of failing precisely where it has laid aside the undertaking of striving.

Therefore the citizens of this middle region mortify evil affections in themselves, adorn themselves with continual virtues, direct all their works with a right intention, and do not depart in anything from the office of reason.

The citizens of this region are scholastic, contemplative, and active men, devoted to various and indeed honorable arts: those who subject the sensual or carnal powers to reason; those who do not neglect to cleanse these three powers of the soul from all unlawful objects; those who always raise their intention to the end of honorable usefulness; those who reform the will, in act or in habit, toward its proper end. Whoever desires to make progress in this middle region according to the vow of his profession should diligently take care to observe the following articles. If he has neglected to keep them, he shows that he is still a citizen not of the middle region but of the lowest. For, as we said above, no one can remain in this middle region without a continual effort to make progress; here, not to have advanced is to fail, and not to have ascended is to descend.
