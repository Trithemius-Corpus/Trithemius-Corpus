Next, let our faithful cenobite not neglect to cleanse his memory from every occupation with unlawful things. Let him admit no forms of shameful or less useful thoughts, and let him strive to repel the phantasms of irrational passions by holy meditations, and, so far as can be done, to think nothing foolish.

The will also must be cleansed from every disordered affection, so that it wishes nothing except what right reason suggests should be wished, and rejects nothing except what reason, according to the judgment of truth, forbids it.

But this manly restraint and cleansing of the powers, as we have said, is granted to absolutely no one except through great, discreet, and continual diligence in a strong struggle, knowing that the kingdom of God suffers violence, and that no one can subject the enemy to himself unless he has chosen to fight against him fiercely and perseveringly. For, as blessed Bishop Augustine testifies, we know that a person does not die well unless he has first died to vices and concupiscences. But no one truly dies to these among human beings unless, resisting his own wills through continual labor, he sows in tears. Therefore the mind must be fortified with salutary teachings; the strength of patience must be taken up in all things. For just as someone unarmed and impatient of labor cannot overthrow an armed enemy, so a monk cannot cleanse his intellectual powers without spiritual weapons and a certain continual force in fighting. Although the weapons of spiritual warfare are very numerous in one’s progress, nevertheless three must chiefly be taken up by the monk.

First, indeed, vigor is necessary for the cenobite in everything he does, so that he may dare to attack courageously the things contrary to the flesh, and may never grow lukewarm in his holy purpose. For the exercises of the spirit are hard and wholly contrary to the flesh;

and no one is able to persevere in them constantly who refuses to put on the strongest armor of vigor. Therefore, if you desire to subject all your affections to the spirit, if you wish to fight successfully against the vices of the flesh, take care to do prudent violence to yourself and to arm yourself bravely against all things.

Next, a certain holy severity is set forth as something to be assumed by you; through it all contrary and adverse things of this world are borne as though they were nothing. For whoever, with a kindly lightness of mind, has once truly taken up anything for the love of God and for desire of heaven will thereafter fear no adversities of the flesh, but will be filled inwardly with so great a consideration of divine reward that he seems to delight more in adversities that occur than in prosperity.

But severity is by no means pleasing to the weaker unless it has been tempered somewhat with the sweetness of kindness. Therefore, consequently, kindness itself must be taken up with it, so that when, because of the weakness of the flesh, a person tempted in battle, or even wounded, sometimes falters, he may soon rise again through penitence, to fight once more with greater strength. For it is necessary that the soldier of Christ, if he has fallen in war, rise again as often as needed through contrition and the other remedies of penitence, to fight again, until at last, at some other time, by the mercy of God, he is able to overcome the adversary. If the just person falls seven times in a day and rises again, how shall the sinner, now penitent, not hope for pardon? Rise, therefore, to fight again, O soldiers, as often as you have fallen when the adversary has prevailed. Always renew the warfare afresh with greater strength, so that you may finally overcome by perseverance the enemy whom for the time being you are not strong enough to destroy by force. Do not despair of victory, O faithful cenobite, because you have often been conquered by temptation or have fallen into the snare of vice. Do not lie long prostrate because you feel yourself weak, but rise again at once as often as you have fallen, and trust that you have a merciful Lord, by whose help you will at last be victorious against the adversary. For no one suddenly becomes supreme, nor is victory over the enemy granted at once to inexperienced soldiers, lest, doomed to perish, they fall more gravely into pride.
