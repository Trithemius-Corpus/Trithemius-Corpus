Therefore whoever despises the vicar of some king has without doubt despised the king himself. But tell me, good brother: why do you withhold due reverence from your prelate, whom you hear is Christ’s vicar? Perhaps you think him evil and wholly unworthy of such an office, and for that reason you think it is permissible for you to despise him. Not so, brother, not so. Rather, consider whose place it is in which Christ’s vicar presides, and know that you honor the sender, not the one sent. If you wish to be Christ’s disciple, always consider why you have come, and you will never deny reverence even to an unworthy prelate. For why have you come? Surely so that, according to the Gospel and the Rule, you may deny yourself for God’s sake; so that under another’s command you may serve Christ as a soldier; so that with patience you may await the eternal reward for these things. Therefore do not, brother, do not lose the crown of eternal blessedness prepared for you in heaven because of one man’s displeasing conduct, a crown you ought not exchange for the whole world.

Remember that you have nothing except from God, to whom you owe wholly what you are and what you know, so that you may fulfill his will and live according to his ordinance. But since you cannot understand God’s will by your own consideration, you must humbly obey your rector as his vicar, to whom you have subjected yourself; nor must you examine either his life or his command, since it is not permitted you to judge your shepherd. Indeed, in your profession you swore that you ought not carry out your own will, but that you wished to obey the abbot in all things according to the Rule. Therefore the abbot is lord of your will, not by his own assumption, but by the ordinance of God, whose vicar he also is. But handling another’s property against the owner’s will is theft, and you know that thieves will have no share in the kingdom of Christ and of God.

Thus the monk who has been professed according to the Rule to God and to his abbot no longer thereafter has lordship over his own will. If afterward he usurps that will for himself, he commits theft and sacrilege. Nor will permission obtained save the presumptuous man, if it has been obtained by threats and insults. Your abbot, O brother, is God’s vicar; to him you must render the obedience promised, not as to a human being but as to God,

and show humble reverence in God’s place. For whatever is given by subjects to the ruler is without doubt referred to God, whose vicar he is. Therefore, since you have subjected yourself to your prelate for God alone and for no other cause, strive at all times to present yourself toward him in such a way that he may freely be able to command you to do or leave undone whatever seems expedient to him, and that he may have no fear at all of any insolence or disturbances of your spirit. For if your abbot is compelled to fear you, his monk, the order of justice is violated; and where a disciple strives to be above the master, he sins against the Gospel.

Take care, therefore, lest you withhold reverence and honor from prelates; take care lest by saddening them you offend the Lord. Rather, see to it that, whatever sort of men they may be, you meet them reverently and obey their commands in all things according to the Gospel and the Rule. Believe me, brother, because the fault committed by a subject against a prelate will be punished most severely, either in the present life or in the life to come. For it is written: “Do not touch my anointed ones.” Whatever your prelate commands you, you must receive with such trust that you believe God has inspired it in him.

Let the monk’s conduct toward his brothers be charitable.

Second, the conduct of a monk toward his brothers is commanded to have charity, through which alone peace and concord are maintained in the monastery. Let our faithful cenobite always think, and turn over in his heart, the common joy of souls in eternal blessedness; and let him continually direct the eye of the mind toward the equal condition of souls, so that his thought may not withdraw from love of their future fellowship. If a brother has offended you in any way by word or deed, if he has been troublesome or opposed to you in some matter, do not weigh the disorderly movement of sensuality from this; do not consider his present weakness, but lift the eyes of your mind to the nobility of his soul, and consider what kind of fellowship in eternal blessedness he is to have with you. To this end you ought often to recall to memory the chapter of the Rule on the good zeal of monks, so that you may more easily rouse yourself.
