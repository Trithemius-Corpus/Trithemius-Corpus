This obedience, however, is owed strictly to prelates according to the Gospel and the monastic Rule; for although mutual obedience among brothers is good, it must never be preferred to the command of a superior. Monks are indeed commanded to render discreet obedience to one another, and, while preserving the precepts of their superiors, to anticipate one another in mutual services. And, to bring in an example from our own custom: obedience is to be given to all according to order. If a brother summons you, obey gladly; if a senior commands something else, leave aside the command of the former and obey this one. Again, if the prior of the community interposes his own command, all mutual obedience among the brothers will cease. But if the precept of the abbot intervenes, no obedience to anyone else will prevail against it. For he is believed to hold the first place in the monastery in Christ's stead, and therefore he is preferred to all.

There are, moreover, seven graded circumstances of the virtue of holy obedience, which blessed Bernard described thus. The first degree of obedience is to obey gladly, so that, evidently, you have no will of your own at all, but believe the good pleasure of your pastor to be entirely your own. The second is to obey simply, so that you do neither more nor otherwise than what has been commanded you, nor, by doing less, omit anything. The third is to obey cheerfully, so that you display serenity in your face and show sweetness in your voice. The fourth is to obey swiftly, so that the hand is not slower in the work than the command is in the voice. The fifth is to obey manfully, lest one withhold his strength from the command and pretend that he is weak and unequal to the precepts. The sixth is to obey humbly: not proudly, not haughtily, not contentiously; for unless obedience is humble, it will receive not reward but punishment. The seventh is to obey perseveringly, because obedience that is interrupted before completion is not true obedience, and he alone who perseveres to the end is rewarded.

The fruit of obedience is everlasting, and is limited by no end at all. God almighty obeys our prayers only as much as we obey our superiors. Do not flatter yourself, stiff-necked monk, about your persistence in prayer, for Christ does not hear a man praying when that man despises Christ's vicar in the monastery. Humility raises prayer up; obedience commends it. For obedience, as one of the fathers said, is the salvation of all the faithful, the mother of all virtues, the finder of the kingdom of heaven. Obedience opens the heavens, closes the gates of hell, becomes a co-dweller with the angels, and is considered the food of all the saints. But the monk who rejects subjection to obedience runs toward death.

The fourth degree of humility is patience in adversities. Of this our holy father Benedict speaks in the Rule: if a monk under obedience embraces patience with silent conscience amid hard and contrary things, or even amid any injuries inflicted on him. This degree too takes its beginning from the third, for no one should truly be called obedient if he is found impatient. For, as that divine Chrysostom says, he cannot be pleasing to the king who has been subject to the enemy; nor is he truly subject to Christ who has been enslaved to passionate and impatient flesh. No one will be able to conquer a fierce enemy unless he has first learned to fight bravely against him. Patience is very necessary for us, because no virtue is brought to perfection lawfully if patience is lacking.

Indeed, as blessed Gregory bears witness, it is of greater merit to endure adversities patiently for God's sake than to toil in good works. And Saint Jerome says: He who believes that tribulation has come upon him for his sins summons God's mercy to himself so that he may do more good. Therefore let the cenobite learn to set the virtue of patience against all things that befall him, if he desires to triumph over his enemies. For he who suffers conquers; he who murmurs is overcome.
