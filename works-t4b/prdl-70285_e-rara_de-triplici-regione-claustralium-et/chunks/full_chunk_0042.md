He suffered in his whole body, since from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head there was no soundness in him. He suffers in his friends, since he is betrayed by a disciple and, deserted by all, is even publicly denied by Peter. He is also afflicted in compassion for his mother, whose most holy soul is remembered as having been pierced by the sword of sorrow. He suffered from the ingratitude of many, because he foresaw that the death which he endured in compassion for all would be lost in many of the reprobate.

Finally, he suffered in all the senses, as we are taught from the Gospel. He suffers in his eyes, which, with many tears poured out, are veiled with a cloth. He suffers in his hearing, when, mocked with many insults and reproaches, he is blasphemed. He suffers in smell, since he is defiled by the foul spittle of the Jews. He suffers in touch, since he is tormented in all the members of his body. He suffers in taste, because, thirsting, he is given gall and vinegar to drink.

Sixth, because of the cause of the passion, which was solely the injury done by the Jews, since he suffered entirely without guilt. For whoever suffers unjustly is tormented all the more in spirit. But the innocent Son of God dies for our sins and is tormented for our crimes, as is read in Isaiah: Truly he himself bore our infirmities.

Seventh, because of the lack of every consolation, since Christ found no one consoling him in his passion. For he says in the Psalm: You have put friend and neighbor far from me, and my acquaintances because of misery. Indeed, his acquaintances stood far off.

Fourth, the passion of Christ was fruitful with respect to its effect, and this is principally to be considered in three things. First, the remission of sins was accomplished; second, the bestowal of grace; third, the display of glory. These three things are indicated by the title affixed to the cross, which, as the evangelist says, contained the cause of Jesus’ passion: Jesus of Nazareth, king of the Jews. For Jesus, saving sinners, pertains to the first; Nazarene, bestowing the flower of grace, to the second; king of the Jews, granting the heavenly kingdom to those who confess him, to the third.

On Meditation on the Benefits of God. Root III.

The third root of divine fear and love is fixed in meditation on the benefits of God, through which one comes to the grace of devotion. For the beginning of devotion is placed in meditation on the benefits of God, from the memory of which love is born. Its middle, as progress in that meditation itself, desires nothing other than to please God above all things, from whom it knows that it has been anticipated in many benefits without merit. Its end loves God above all things with burning desire and pure fervor of mind, and longs to cling to him alone there continually, without intermediary.

And although merciful God has bestowed many things specially upon each one of us, nevertheless, because we are writing this synthema not for one person but for many, we ought to discuss at length, not the particular benefits allotted to individuals, but the general benefits conferred upon all. Therefore we include the more important ones under the number seven, so that through the seven days of the week, or even in the canonical hours themselves, we may have ready at hand the material for daily meditation. But if anyone is unable to ascend to that summit of contemplation, let him strive to exercise himself more fervently in these meditations on the benefits of God, so that, while he diligently continues the lower things, once purified he may ascend stronger to the higher. Therefore let the monk devoutly consider what he has received, so that he may be able to show himself grateful. Let him render to God what he has received, if he desires to be good.
