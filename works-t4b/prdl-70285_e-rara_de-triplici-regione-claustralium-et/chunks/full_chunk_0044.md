Sixth, the Creator has bestowed on us time for meriting eternal life, and has given the necessary nourishment of the body. For here indeed we are debtors to ourselves, since in this time we owe to ourselves the acquisition of merits. Why, then, do we spend the days of salvation so negligently and without the fruit of merits, when we have nothing more precious than time? Behold, within this brief present time we can merit the joys of eternal blessedness, and yet we all dissemble when it comes to doing what is good. Woe to us, foolish and mad, who accept nothing in this world as more precious than time, and yet are accustomed to spend nothing more cheaply. For now an acceptable time is granted to us; now the days of salvation are given, in which we ought to persist in holy works with the greatest fervor, and always to have regard for the hour of death.

Seventh, God has set before us heaven, filled with all joy, and has promised himself, with all the things contained in it, as a reward. For this generosity of our Savior we ought to devote ourselves to holy works of mercy, and show ourselves imitators of God according to our strength. To this he exhorts us in his sacred Gospels: “Be merciful, just as your Father, God, is merciful.” At the end of the world also, when the day of judgment has come, by his own promise he will send the merciful into eternal life, since whoever has shown mercy will obtain mercy. And lest any monk should say, “I can show mercy to no one, since my religious life allows me to possess absolutely nothing,” let us know that among the necessary works of mercy the spiritual are the more powerful. If you wish, as a monk, to be merciful, pray to God for the wretched; give saving instruction to the ignorant.

The sum, then, of meditation on the benefits of God rests on three exercises, in which it is chiefly and wholly contained. In the first, we must attribute all things to God, not to ourselves; in the second, by remembering God’s benefits, we must exercise ourselves in the pursuit of love; in the third, for the benefits received,

we must give thanks with a pure heart. But if through these exercises concerning benefits we wish to kindle our heart to the love of God, we ought to consider six conditions in those gifts just mentioned. First, consider that God’s gift is altogether noble; second, that it is especially fruitful and valuable for you; third, that it is varied, manifold, and repeated very often. These three consider the gift as ordered in itself; the two that follow have regard to the person of the giver; the last notes the condition of the receiver. Fourth, then, consider the great affection of the giver toward you; fifth, his inestimable dignity; sixth, that you, the receiver, are lowly and contemptible, and unworthy of all things, though you receive great things from the greatest one. And by these considerations of benefits, the love of God is accustomed to rise up in the mind of a devout person, and, advancing by sure steps, to lift the spirit toward contemplation.
