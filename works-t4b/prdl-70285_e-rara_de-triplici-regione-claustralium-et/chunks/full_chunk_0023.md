The first article, then, is the diligent intention of the cenobite’s end; the second is the proper restraint of the sensual powers; the third, the faithful cleansing of the faculties; the fourth, the attentive reformation of the works of the lowest region; the fifth, the ordered establishment of the progress of a true cenobite; the sixth, the adaptation of the faculties for entry into the third region; and the seventh, exemplary or experiential certainty concerning all the aforesaid matters.

On the continual and diligent intention toward the end of cenobitic life. First Article.

Since the end is first in the intention of the wise person, and each thing in its own kind is judged the more perfect the more it approaches its own end, the monk who cultivates this middle region should always consider why he entered religious life. If he finds his intention well ordered, he should strive to continue it; but if he finds it corrupted, he should not fail to reform it according to the rectitude of the proper end.

But note that in spiritual exercises the end is described as twofold, and through it the virtue of monastic conduct is increased in merits. The first is charity, which is called the principal and ultimate end; toward it all monastic exercises are ordered as toward their first cause. Whatever good we do must always and principally be ordered toward this end, since no work of virtue receives merit unless charity always supplies the proper foundation.

God, says John, is charity; and he who abides in charity abides in God, and God in him. Everyone, moreover, who abides in God does not sin. The second end, as a dispositive end, is directed toward the first and principal end as though through some fitting means, and it is called secondary or less principal. The monk must always attend to this end and through it order his exercises toward the ultimate end. For no one can arrive at the principal end of charity if he neglects to observe the means.

This dispositive end directed toward the ultimate end, so far as it concerns cenobites, according to John Cassian in the Conferences of the Fathers, is to cast away from oneself all cares of the world completely, to mortify one’s own wills, to entrust every disposition and care of oneself to the superior, and to think nothing about tomorrow. For the cenobite who wishes to make progress in this middle region must wholly take up self-denial, and then resign himself totally into the hands of his superior, so that inwardly, within himself, he may be clean from every dust of self-will, and outwardly free from every secular occupation for serving the Lord. Indeed, unless our cenobite reaches this end, he is in no way a true monk, since he still sows corruption in corruptible flesh and by no means observes the virtue of his profession.

This monastic denial of self-will appears to consist principally in three things, without which no one can truly be said to have denied himself. First, the mortification of self-will requires in the monk the eradication of private judgment, since whoever relies on his own judgment or counsel disdains humbly to be subject to another. For the root from which the defense of self-will arises is called attachment to one’s own counsel or judgment. Whoever, being placed in a monastery, desires to cling to his own judgment, sins gravely against the rule as a usurper of self-will.
