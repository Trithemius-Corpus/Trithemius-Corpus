The devout and faithful cenobite who truly loves God does not spare himself in labor, but is accustomed to apply himself to the manual tasks imposed on him with such fervor, cheerfulness, and fidelity, as if by his labor alone he had to gather together and feed all who are established in the monastery. For the most prudent soul knows that it is devoting itself wholly to God, whatever bodily necessity for his servants it happens to labor for. For just as spiritual things cannot long endure in religion without the cooperation of temporal things, so I do not hesitate to call one who ministers bodily necessities to those serving God, for God's sake, a servant of God. Therefore whatever a good cenobite can acquire for the monastery by his labors with right intention, let him know that he is offering all that to the Lord. Nevertheless, the monk must always understand that he is to labor in such a way that he in no way omits monastic prayers, masses, and the other services of holy mother Church.

Manual work, however, undertaken out of curiosity and self-complacency is judged before the inward judge to be deprived of the merit of obedience; and work presumed as idle and without the superior's permission is held liable to account. But the good cenobite, who desires to please God alone, should say to himself that saying of Bernard in every stealthy intrusion of an idle word, deed, or thought: If you were about to die now, would you do in this way what you are now doing, thinking, or saying? But if, were you now about to die, you would not do what you are doing in the way you are doing it, you are most foolish, since, destined to die shortly, you so boldly send the works of death on ahead to death for yourself. But if, since it is necessary, you desire to die well, do at no time what you would not do if death were already present. For whatever you do now, whether good or bad, you will find after death.

Let the monk be inwardly
humble and devout.

Second, in the time of labor the monk must be inwardly devout and humble, since if he abandons the progress of inward fervor, what he acquires outwardly through labor is of no help.

Therefore, so that the soul may truly profit while laboring, let him bring back to constant memory the degrees of good will written in the first section. Let him arrange meditations on the Lord's passion with fervor of devotion, in which he may conform his labors, as far as possible, to Christ's labor. Let him always be humble in heart by self-denial. Let him be devout in affection through the fervor of holy compunction, so that the labor of his hands may outwardly advance the usefulness and advantage of the community, while his mind does not withdraw from the inward sweetness of divine love. If labor wearies the working cenobite, if heat burns him, if untimely thirst oppresses him, let him call to mind what the Lord of labor endured for us, and he will bear everything lightly. And so let him say this prayer to the Lord.

Prayer of one laboring.

Most sweet and most lovable Lord Jesus, king of heaven and earth, who for my redemption were born of the most pure Virgin Mary, and for thirty-three years lived among human beings as God and man; who had great labors in walking, teaching, and preaching; and who willingly and patiently endured thirst, hunger, heat, and cold, for all of which I give thanks to you: receive, I beseech you, these very small labors of mine, however useless, which in humility of your most holy testament I offer to you in memory and honor of all your labor and of your most holy passion. And may they be pleasing to you in that union by which your most holy labors were pleasing to God the Father. Amen.

During the time of labor let our cenobite turn over in mind the chief articles of the Lord's passion: first, as though commemorating them before Christ in his passion; then, giving thanks at his knees; third, asking for mercy in himself according to the likeness of them.

Let him also recall to memory the ways of life of any holy fathers, or the passions of the martyrs, and let him consider what reward and glory they now receive in the fatherland for their labors.

Let the monk be sparing and edifying in speech.
