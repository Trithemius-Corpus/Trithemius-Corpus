All receive the alms of the faithful cheerfully, but few bestow the support of prayers upon those who give them. From these things many citizens of the lowest region, when they have come to the order, serve not God but the belly. They desire more food, drink, and clothing to be given them than necessity requires; and those who in the world could scarcely have bread and vegetables for food, now, having become monks, refuse eggs and little fish. They importunately demand that large, varied, different, and delicate fish be given them. Murmuring from the concupiscence of the flesh with the children of Israel in the desert, they cry out to their leader, saying that they cannot eat eggs and peas; they always desire more delicate things.

How the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered toward contempt of the world.

Article VII.

The kingdom of God suffers violence, and the violent seize it. Therefore whoever desires to enter the heavenly kingdom by the arms of holy obedience, whoever chooses to live for God under regular conduct, must do violence to the pleasures of the flesh and firmly contemn the world with all its vanities. But the carnal monks, citizens of the lowest region, who are badly ordered both toward God and toward their neighbors, are also badly ordered toward contempt of the world.

And first, they are infected by concupiscence of the flesh, for they always burn with the fire of pleasure. In food and drink they are found excessive; in beds and baths, against the rules, self-indulgent; they prefer the desires of the flesh to the salvation of the soul; they do not seek the sweetness of inner conduct. They nourish their flesh too delicately, through which they continually burn in various concupiscences. For no one can truly contemn the world who neglects to restrain the appetite of the flesh even in the smallest things. If you eat to satiety, you do not truly contemn the world. For the first commandment imposed upon contemners of the world is grounded in abstinence alone. But if you fill your belly with food to the point of satiety, you at once incur either lust or grave acedia.

Therefore concupiscence of the flesh draws back the mind of the disordered cenobite, so that he cannot contemn the world.

Second, they are disordered toward contemning the world because of the vain concupiscence of the eyes. For the monk who forgets the true honor of faithful souls delights entirely in worldly pomps and curious ornaments. Indeed, what each person is inwardly, hidden within himself, his outward attire makes manifest. To carry rings on the fingers, corals on scarves, scent-vessels in the hands, and pleats in cowls does not show a monk but a soldier; it presents not a humble cenobite but a proud secular man. This ornamentation in monks cannot please anyone except the reprobate, and it is often found to have displeased even the proud. Woe to you, disordered cloisterers, who descend to hell through the double concupiscence of the eyes, while you desire to see in others what is not fitting, and present yourselves to be seen by others otherwise than you ought.

What have you to do with the trinkets of the world? What with worldly curiosities? All these beauties, as the divine Bernard bears witness, make a masculine soul effeminate; these outward honors quickly weaken a manly purpose. Such men are like ragged boys who, because of one thin red strip fastened to a torn sleeve, think themselves very handsome and elegant, and insolently mock others standing before them, though they are very well dressed, as if they were naked. These pompous cloisterers see others, walking in humble habit, contemn the world in the spirit of poverty, and for that reason please both God and human beings; but they see themselves despised by the wise and by princes, and sometimes not even admitted to conversation. Seeing these things, the proud, even if they grieve heavily as if over an injury, nevertheless utterly refuse to lay aside their arrogance.

Third, the citizens of the lowest region are badly ordered toward contempt of the world because of pride of life, since they are not only infected by vain pleasures of the flesh, but also proud through swelling of mind. For all who follow the desires of the flesh are puffed up in spirit; they do not know how to endure equals; they do not admit superiors. These men rejoice that a great retinue of servants attends them.
