The fourth chapter is that of deliberations and counsels.

We call the fourth chapter deliberative, when we summon the brothers to counsel over some difficult matter. How this chapter ought to be held, Saint Benedict makes clear in the Rule, in the section on summoning the brothers to counsel. Therefore the humble and true cenobite, when he has been called to counsel, approaches humbly, with reverence and modesty. When asked what he thinks, he says little. He is in no way offended if his own counsel is disregarded and another’s approved. What he thinks, indeed, he states humbly; but always preferring the counsels of others to his own, he entrusts himself and his opinion to be examined by the one presiding, with true submission. For to such a degree does he annihilate himself in deep humility that he always believes more should be credited to others than to himself. He trusts that sound counsel is divinely inspired in those better than himself, and in his mind he is accustomed to set his prelate before all as Christ’s vicar. A monk makes the greatest progress, indeed, toward the mortification of his own will when, in giving counsels, he submits his own opinion to the abbot at all times with cheerful humility. But when the devout cenobite perceives that his counsel is disregarded or rejected, let him give thanks to almighty God from true humility, and recognize that an occasion for pride has been taken away from him.

At Private Masses.

Since human nature, by its own strength, cannot of itself undertake the entrance into the kingdom of heaven, it pleased God the most merciful Father, through the death of our Lord Jesus Christ, his only-begotten Son, mercifully to come to our aid. Thus, because on account of our weakness we cannot approach the invisible majesty, we may have a mediator approaching on our behalf, one who is both God and man. For the merit of the most holy Passion of our mediator opened for us the entrance to the kingdom of heaven, gave us spiritual nourishment of life, and, just as the vine supplies nourishment of life to the branch, so in Christ our mind truly lives. This spiritual nourishment, however, we receive with special sweetness in the sacrament of the body and blood of that same mediator of ours, whenever we devoutly offer it to God the Father as an odor of sweetness. And this most sacred oblation of the body and blood of the Lord not only profits us for the pardon of sins, but also wonderfully aids the souls of the departed who are placed in purgatory. Therefore this noble sacrifice is to be offered with great and continual devotion, for all our good works, which in themselves are very worthless, are most pleasing to God the Father through this most holy oblation. Therefore, so that our devout cenobite may be able to attend more fervently at the offering of this precious sacrifice, let him note four things in order.

Of the Brother Serving at Mass.

The devout brother who is going to serve at Mass ought to come to this with the greatest cheerfulness and fervor, and prepare himself to serve the priest most humbly. For Saint Hugh says: Serve freely at Masses, because it is the office of angels; and when you serve at that sacrifice, you make familiar to yourself those whose coworker you stand as. If, according to the Savior’s promise, one who receives a righteous man in the name of a righteous man receives the reward of a righteous man, who will deny the reward of the celebrant to the one who devoutly serves at Mass?
