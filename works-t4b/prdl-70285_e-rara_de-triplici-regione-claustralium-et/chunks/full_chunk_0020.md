Now such pernicious lukewarmness flows from the fact that their will has not yet been purified, and they do not will the good as they know it, being drawn away and enticed by their own concupiscence. For in their flesh they love little earthly consolations, whether in word, or in sign, or in deed, or in something else; and even if at times they interrupt these things, still they do not break them off completely. Hence it is that they rarely direct their affections toward God, and their compunction is not continuous, but occasional. But a soul that is subject to these distractions cannot be filled with the visitations of the Lord; and the more it is emptied of the former, the more fully it will be filled with the latter. If much, much; if little, little. Or perhaps, if you prefer, these things will never be able to be mixed with those forever. For spirit and flesh do not dwell in one home, nor fire and lukewarmness, especially since lukewarmness itself is accustomed to provoke the Lord to vomiting.

Therefore whoever desires to flee from the midst of Babylon, whoever longs to come to the region of the spirit, should always take care to drive torpor of mind away from himself, and in his holy purpose to burn continually through the fire of divine love. Nothing harms those wishing to serve God so much as lukewarmness of mind. It always arises at first from some small negligence, but unless it is immediately treated, it grows wonderfully by degrees. Moreover, one placed near the lowest region of religion often falls back into that same region, because the dregs of his former carnal way of life still subtly occupy his will, and by an easy occasion disturb the weak fervor of his devotion.

But no one can rise from the depth of hell with a lukewarm will; no one can overcome a habit without a contrary habit, because unless some violence intervenes, the habitual practice of sin will always strengthen vice. This lowest region is rightly called hell, for by a certain likeness of disturbance and aversion it is compared to hell. After this life there are three dwelling places of souls, which receive their citizens emigrating from this world from the three regions. The first dwelling place is called hell, and it is compared to the lowest region. For just as in hell no order, but everlasting horror, dwells, so in this lowest region all things are disturbed by the frenzy of a mind blinded by carnal affections.

In hell all are evil, and in the lowest region good people are nowhere found. Whoever falls into hell has come from the lowest region. For the lowest region always sends its citizens to hell, unless, led by repentance while alive, they have taken care to renounce dead works.

The second dwelling place is called purgatory, and it is compared to the second, rational region. For just as purgatory purges and perfects souls, and sends those purged on to the kingdom of heaven, so this second region, through continuous exercise, washes the citizens persevering in it from the stains of sins, and disposes them for the ascent of the third region, which is called spirit. In this dwelling place all are good, but still to be purged so that they may become better; and in the second region no evil people are found, but they must always be exercised so that they may be made better. This region sends its citizens who die in it to purgatory, because, even if all are good, they do not yet seem fit for the kingdom without temporal purification. For indeed, those who inhabit this middle region, although they seem to be very near the highest, have still not yet been entirely removed from the dust of the lower region.

The third dwelling place of separated souls is called the kingdom of heaven, and it is compared to the highest region of the spirit. For just as in that heavenly homeland there is the highest rest and eternal peace, so in the region of the spirit there is continual tranquillity of mind.

Here ends the first region.
