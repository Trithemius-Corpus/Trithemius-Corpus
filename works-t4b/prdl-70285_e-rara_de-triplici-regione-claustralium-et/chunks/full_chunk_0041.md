His Passion also was contrary to custom, because it received no delay of time. He is not, according to custom, placed in prison; nothing is deliberated in his case; but, once taken, he is at once dragged before the judge to be subjected to death. Scourged, spat upon, and afflicted with many injuries, blows, and mockeries, he is nailed to the cross. Third, it was contrary to the law of nature, and procured from the obstinate hatred of the heart. For nature forbids raging against the dead, and shudders at tearing with new wounds a body already slain. For our redeemer, dead on the cross, is pierced through with a lance; and, contrary to the law of nature, though dead, he is wounded by the impious.

Second, the Passion of Christ is said to have been ignominious as to its manner for the six causes that follow. First, because of the paltry price: for blood of incomparable worth is sold by a disciple for thirty pieces of silver, for which no one would deign to sell his little ass. Second, because of the shameful office: for, full of wounds and half dead, he is compelled to carry his own cross, something found to have been done to no robber. Third, because of the unworthiness of the place: for he is crucified outside the city in the place of Calvary, where robbers and criminal murderers are punished for their deserts. Fourth, because of the shameful punishment: for he is hung naked on the cross, a penalty owed only to robbers and criminal men. Indeed, as Scripture says, the redeemer of the world was condemned by a most shameful death. Fifth, because of the shameful mockery: for before the whole multitude he is hung naked on the cross, and, as if a fool and a madman, is blasphemed by all. Concerning this the psalmist says: “All the day my shame is before me.” Sixth, because of the shameful company: for, counted with criminals, he is hung in the midst of robbers. What are you doing now, O Christian soul? Behold, your savior hangs in the middle among robbers, and do you not fear to sit in the first place in council?

for seven reasons. First, because of the delicate vitality of his flesh, which in its natural constitution was most excellent, and therefore was more grievously afflicted in the endurance of pains. For delicate flesh suffers more than flesh coarse by natural condition. Therefore, since the flesh of Christ was subtle and delicate, he emitted a bloody sweat in the garden. Second, because of the character of those who killed him: for Christ was killed not by strangers to his own people, but by his own blood-relatives and neighbors, as he had earlier complained through the prophet: “My people, what have I done to you?” For one suffers more tolerably from strangers and enemies than from one’s own people, from whom at least friends, by reason of nearness of blood, show compassion. Third, because of the bitterness of the Passion: for he is afflicted more in those places where pain is felt more greatly because of the nerves. For, raised up on the cross, he is fastened through hands and feet; the Lord of all, thirsting, is given gall and hyssop to drink. For the psalmist says of him: “They have pierced my hands and my feet; they have counted all my bones.” Who could conceive in the mind the pain of your most holy heart, good Jesus, when the hardest nails began so cruelly to pierce your hands and feet? Would that my soul might always show itself grateful to you; would that the remembrance of your most holy Passion might not depart from my memory. I shall easily be able to obtain from you the effect of my prayers if it should befall me to delight without interruption in the exercise of your Passion. Let each person think what he can; I prefer the memory of your Passion to all exercises, through which I trust that in my prayers I shall receive whatever is fitting for salvation. Fourth, because of its duration, the Passion of Christ was most bitter: for from the hour of his capture until death he was always in most bitter torment. “All the day,” he says, “I went about sorrowful,” and “there is no health in my flesh.” Fifth, because of the generality of the Passion: for in whatever ways Christ could suffer, he suffered uniquely.
