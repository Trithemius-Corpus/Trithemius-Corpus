It would be a long matter if I wished to run through even the names alone of those who shone marvelously in our order both in learning and in holiness. Great devotion dwelt in them; an inestimable zeal for regular discipline, which never allowed them to be idle. In them the love of divine studies always flourished; conceived through holy reading, it called those keeping watch to prayer. Those who had exercised themselves more fully in the divine Scriptures, in the hours free from the service of God, devoted themselves to expounding them; they composed books and various treatises by which they stirred the zeal of their brothers toward the love of God. The other brothers were not free for idleness, but after prayers earnestly devoted themselves to manual work according to the Rule. Those fit for it wrote books, for the adornment of the divine service, and also brought together in one place works published by others. Some skillfully bound the written codices; others corrected them; others marked them out with red minium. Everyone desired to share in holy labor. The younger brothers, moreover, were trained in scholastic disciplines: some in grammar, some in rhetoric, some in philosophy, some in arithmetic, some in dialectic, and others in other disciplines, each according to the measure of his nature and talent. Once they had been grounded in the elementary sciences, they were called to the theological Scriptures. In the monasteries no place lay open to idleness; each one applied himself with the greatest fervor to his own labors and studies. They dedicated the little works they had composed to emperors, kings, princes, and prelates of cathedral churches, by whose favor and authority they might more securely come forth into the light for our instruction. Thus Alcuin dedicated countless little works to Charlemagne; thus Rabanus to Louis; thus Berno of Reichenau to Aribo of Mainz and Pilgrim, archbishops of this holy church of Cologne. zeal for the Scriptures, most excellent volumes. I pass over the rest, whose number is almost infinite, who published splendid little works for princes. But where now is such zeal among monks? Where is the abbots’ love for the divine Scriptures? Shame! Now a man is called great and useful in the order not because he cultivates and loves knowledge of the Scriptures, but because he makes himself filthy in gathering perishable riches.

O fathers, O brothers, our commonwealth did not once flourish so; our order did not grow so in ancient times. But abbots given over to the world are accustomed to answer, and monks who are friends of vanity excuse themselves from study: “We willingly wish to remain in our simplicity, since we see even learned men going headlong through the cliffs of vices into perdition. Profound knowledge will not be required of us at the Last Judgment, but a good life.”

Fools and senseless men, you do not hide your sloth; rather, to your greater confusion, you openly show yourselves enemies of the divine Scriptures. It is well said in the proverb that knowledge has no enemy except the ignorant man. You lovers of the world, you followers of vanity, who foolishly cast away from yourselves the study of the Scriptures: why do you not love it in others? Or do you not know that all Scripture, divinely inspired, is useful for teaching, for arguing, for rebuking? What will you teach your monks, you who are ignorant of the Scriptures? How will you be able usefully to correct those who sin, you who do not know how to apply the testimonies of the Scriptures?

Remain, then, in your simplicity, not upright; remain in asinine humility. Refuse to understand so that you may act well; refuse to heed the Rule of Saint Benedict; refuse to withdraw from your folly. Do you know, then, what our lawgiver says in the Rule? I know that you know. “The abbot,” he says, “must be learned in the divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth a sermon.” An unlearned prelate is a mute dog, an empty vessel; a soul that does not love the Scriptures of God. O you ignorant abbots and enemies of saving knowledge, who consume the day with obscene rumors, who drink wine to satiety, who give your attention to earthly profits, who foolishly play at the gaming table: what will you answer to almighty God and to your lawgiver Benedict?
