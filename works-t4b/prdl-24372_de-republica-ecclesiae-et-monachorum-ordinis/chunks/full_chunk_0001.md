An address on the commonwealth of the Church and of the monks of the order of the holy father Benedict, delivered at Cologne in the annual chapter by the reverend father Lord Johannes, abbot of Sponheim, on the first day of the month of September, in the year of the Lord one thousand four hundred ninety-three.

As I was preparing, reverend fathers in Christ, in merit of obedience, to deliver an address before your paternities according to laudable custom, after I had long and much deliberated silently with myself what subject I should bring forward that would more effectively stir your minds to devotion toward the order, I was at last persuaded by certain venerable men of your council to speak, by the gift of the Most High, about our commonwealth: for love of it, for its defense, and for its adornment, you have come together into this assembly from distant regions of the earth.

It is holy and wholesome, I confess, to speak of your commonwealth, O fathers, both holy and religious; but this belongs to a learned man, an educated man, a holy man, and one fit to persuade. Such a man I know myself not to be, since I am poor in talent, bare of merit, and uncultivated in speech. How, then, could I dare presume to speak of so excellent a commonwealth, when I am slow to discover, unpolished in speaking, and cold in persuading? Another speaker, therefore, fathers, another orator should have been sought for you: one to whom natural ability would supply strength for speaking, learning would supply understanding for teaching, and doctrine no less than merit would be fitting for persuasion.

But why do I struggle in vain, when I am compelled to obey? Therefore, although the burden imposed far exceeds the powers of my talent, nevertheless, trusting in the mercy of the Almighty and in the support of obedience, I shall undertake the work enjoined upon me not so much willingly as cheerfully. Nor shall I need Plato in his ten books, nor Aristotle in his eight, nor Tullius in his six volumes On the Commonwealth, since our Lord Jesus has provided abundant material for discussing this matter according to our intention in today’s Gospel, when he said: “A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers,” and so forth.

Although that parable is chiefly to be understood of the destruction of the whole human race, nevertheless nothing prevents us from interpreting it also, without injury to the faith, in reference to our commonwealth. For the condition of the commonwealth, not only ours but that of all religious, indeed of the whole Church militant, has declined from Jerusalem into Jericho.
