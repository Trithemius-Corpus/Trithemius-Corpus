Let those who wish read it, and let friends read this volume without irritation; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves be judges, without envy, whether they are natural or superstitious. For I know and am certain that all things are sincere, pure, natural, and removed from every pursuit of evil superstition. Whenever the discourse is more obscure, this is done for this reason: that a madman should not misuse the sword of defense for destruction, while a man sound in mind and understanding carries it for his own protection. Yet nothing evil is contained in it on that account, since perverse people misuse even good things for evil. But whoever presumes to condemn this edition of ours is himself conscious and witness that he has never truly understood it. The mysteries are concealed under enigmas, lest the secrets of good men should in any way be made manifest to fools.

If anyone delights to know these things, let him approach as a reader with envy set far aside. For I am a Christian, I am a priest, and also a monk under the rule of the divine Father Benedict; I love Christ and, devotedly, with whatever sincerity of mind I can, I always adore him. I have no dealings with demons, have had none, and, God being my protector, shall have none; no pursuits, no occupations, no teachings in magical, necromantic, or profane arts. Whoever thinks otherwise of me thinks wrongly, does me injury, and defends Bouillus’s open lie.

Likewise in chapter 32, or the last chapter, of the first book of the Steganography, toward the end, he says: “Indeed the great mysteries of this art can be penetrated only by the most studious men: namely, those whom love of secrets and nature prompt to investigate, and who, from desire to obtain knowledge, refuse no honorable and possible labor. But sluggish men, dull in intellect, and those whom neither love kindles to the study of the secrets of wisdom nor nature assists, when they have been unable to understand this most secret art of our invention,

will understand that this is not to be imputed to us, but rather ascribed to their own dullness, sloth, and malice, since they either, having their intellect immersed in the pleasures of the flesh and worldly cares, cannot devote themselves diligently to examining these things, or, already considering themselves wise or most learned, they despise our compositions as unworthy of their reading. There will also be some so accustomed to rely on old and foreign traditions that whatever they have not learned in them they judge either impossible or superstitious. Nothing is more unfit for the knowledge of this profound art than those who either do not wish or are unable to contemplate its deepest matters. But those who are both fit and willing to study this art, if any such there shall be, we have resolved to admonish briefly at the end of this book, so that they may be able to make progress. First of all, they must be admonished to keep the art itself always hidden, lest it penetrate into the hands and knowledge of the wicked, who would commit many execrable crimes

through it. For although the art itself is truly good in itself, nevertheless its practice would conduce no less to evil through the impious than to good through the good. Therefore let that remain a secret among the good which, if made public among the wicked, would do harm. Yet good people ought not on that account to despise this knowledge because it helps the wicked toward evil, just as one does not despise the sword because it strikes a man down.

And lest this great secret,” he says in the preface to the key of the Steganography, “come to the ears of the unskilled or of wicked men, whoever you are who will be the reader, by our Lord Jesus Christ, the Savior of faithful souls, I warn you, I ask you, and I adjure you not to disclose this knowledge of writing to anyone unworthy, immodest, carnal, or old in malice, because many evils could be done through it among men: adulteries, fornications, murders, and betrayals, if, God forbid, it should be revealed.
