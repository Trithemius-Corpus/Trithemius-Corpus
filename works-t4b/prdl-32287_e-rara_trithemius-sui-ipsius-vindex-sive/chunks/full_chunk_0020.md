which, together with that same universal Church of Christians, I hold and believe; and, so long as I shall live, with God's help, firmly preserving it always inviolate, I shall hold it in heart, mouth, and deed, nor do I ever intend to depart from it on any occasion. Far be it from me, therefore, either to learn or to teach anything that is contrary to the Christian faith and its purity, harmful to holy morals, or in any way opposed to my regular profession. I fear God, and in his worship I have sworn; from him neither while alive, nor, as I trust, when dead, shall I be separated. Not without cause have I set this protestation of mine before this whole work of ours, because I know there will be many who, when they cannot understand the things we have written, will turn to insults and attribute our good and holy studies to evil arts or superstitious inventions.

Since such men will exist, we earnestly beg both them and all who will read these synthemata of ours that, if they understand this secret tradition of ours, they should keep it hidden forever and not pour forth into public view mysteries so wonderful. But if they do not understand it,

which we know will happen to many, let them learn before they condemn. For truly he shows himself a rash judge who gives sentence on a matter before he has learned the truth of the case. Learn this art first, and then judge it. If you cannot understand it, condemn not the art itself, since it is good, but your own dull wits. For I know and am certain that no one of sound mind can censure this work of ours unless he happens to be wholly ignorant of it. As for those to whom it is more familiar to despise wisdom than to have learned it, I neither hope nor would wish that they should penetrate these mysteries of ours. Thus Trithemius in his own defense.

I believe you have heard, whoever you are who accuse Trithemius of magical superstition, whether tacitly or expressly: you have heard this solemn protestation of his, plainly worthy of a Catholic man; you have heard his defense; you have heard how much he asks, how much he insists, that you should not believe him to be a magician or necromancer, or to have made use of any superstitions. He certainly would not do this so often and so anxiously

if he had ever been devoted to such depraved arts. Therefore let no one do injury to a dead man who, while alive, did injury to no one. Let each person consider that everything and every individual point handed down rather obscurely by Trithemius was handed down for definite and often-named reasons; and let him beware lest, together with Bovillus and others who did not know this art and had already long ago rashly condemned it, he deserve Trithemius's just censure. And let it suffice to have set forth these things which Trithemius himself, as advocate, had to say in his own defense. From our cloistered dwelling, in the year 1615.
