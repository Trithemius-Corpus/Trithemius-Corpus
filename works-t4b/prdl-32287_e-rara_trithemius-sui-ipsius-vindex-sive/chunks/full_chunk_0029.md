But to create has been reserved to God alone, who is incorruptible and everlasting; every corruptible thing necessarily fails, yet it is preserved by the goodness of the creator so long as it does not lose the influence of its cause. Hence it neither could nor ought to have happened that man, by the condition of his own nature, should be created incapable of sinning. That belongs naturally to God alone, the maker of all things, who is unchangeable and everlasting. But whenever it is granted to a human being that he seems unable to sin, the freedom of choice is not taken away, nor is the possibility simply removed; rather, the will is confirmed in the good. And whenever this gift is conferred upon a creature, it is a gift of grace, not of nature. For Scripture says: God created man from the beginning, and left him in the hand of his own counsel. For, properly speaking, it cannot be that mortal man, conceived in original displeasure, should both have free choice and be unable to commit sin. For the most holy bishop Augustine says:

The first freedom of the will was to be able not to sin; the last, in glory, will be much greater: not to be able to sin. Therefore, if man could not sin, he would not have dominion over his own will. Hence, although almighty God does not will sin, nevertheless he justly permits human beings to sin and to do evil, so that, while freedom of choice remains, both the virtue of a good work may receive its merit and voluntary depravity may receive the evil it has done. For no one either does good or sins unwillingly. Therefore almighty God justly permits human beings to sin and to do evil, since he granted them freedom of choice from the beginning. Now there are many reasons, long ago assigned by the holy Doctors, why the Almighty permits such great acts of sorcery to be done through the cooperation of demons; of these we shall necessarily make brief mention in order.

The first is so that the excellence of the divine majesty may be made manifest, for he alone is unchangeable and far above every condition. But every mutable creature has no comparison with the unchangeable

creator. Hence it is read in Numbers: God is not like a man, that he should lie, nor like a son of man, that he should change. In Isaiah also it is said: God is faithful, without iniquity, just and upright. It is also written in the book of Tobias: You are just, Lord, and all your judgments are true, and all your ways are mercy, truth, and judgment. And in the book of holy Job we read: Far be impiety from God, and iniquity from the Almighty. For he will render to a man his work, and will repay each according to his ways. Therefore every man who rightly understands his own ignorance and weakness plainly recognizes how he stands in comparison with his creator. For God justly permits man to sin, so that freedom of choice may be continued and the excellence of the great God may become known to human beings.

The second reason is so that the unsearchable wisdom of God may become known to mortals, since it knows how to draw good from our evils, when it makes one person’s sin become another’s merit for the increase of virtue. For if evil

were not permitted to happen, which of us would understand the good? For God makes and ordains the good; but evils he does not make. Rather, when they have been done by the wicked, he himself disposes them so that they do not come about in a disorderly way. The malice of human beings does not hinder the will of God, nor can a person do anything that God does not permit.

The third cause is that the Almighty permits acts of sorcery to be done in order to manifest his most merciful goodness toward those who have sinned and done penance; he could not show this in any way if he did not permit the creature to sin. For if there were no sinner, God could not be merciful. But because in many things we all offend, everywhere God finds something to forgive; everywhere God meets something in which, as a loving creator, he may show his mercy. Hence the holy bishop Augustine says: If the disease were slight, the physician would be despised and not sought; the disease would not be brought to an end. Therefore, where sin abounded, grace also abounded, by which alone he distinguishes the redeemed from the lost, whom he gathers together into one.
