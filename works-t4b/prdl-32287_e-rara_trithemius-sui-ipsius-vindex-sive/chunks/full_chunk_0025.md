Therefore whatever witches do is done by God's permission; without his will they can do nothing, because, unless God grants that they can do what they wish, they will obtain it neither from themselves nor from demons. Thus every power is from the Lord God, in whom all things exist and are moved, as in the first cause of all things, since from him from whom we have received freedom of choice we also have the ability to do what we will.

But since the understanding of witches is very confused, they perpetrate the evils they do not by human art, but by the cooperation of demons. Nature has made demons subtle, and long experience has made them swift in producing marvels. For the wondrous effects of witches are not caused by such means as they use for witchcraft, such as heads of the dead, toads, serpents, characters, images, and things of this sort, but by the devil who instituted these signs; since he is most proud, he desires

Indeed, just as almighty God has instituted certain sacraments in his Church, which, when worthily administered and with his assistance, signify and produce a saving effect, he who instituted them bringing about salvation, so also the devil, who desires nothing more than to be regarded as equal to God, has instituted certain signs as if they were sacraments. When he is invoked by those with whom the malignant worker has been allied by pacts, he is present; and whenever God permits it, he effects the evil that is asked. If God does not permit it, he does absolutely nothing.

For the devil is the agent of all necromantic effects and of witches' effects; through the application of the means which he himself previously instituted, he produces the desired work in the matter subjected to him, sometimes in reality, sometimes only in appearance. For the exceedingly savage depravity of witches' will in a certain way turns them into frenzy; when this has taken possession of the mind, the demons immediately attach themselves to the raging women and, invited by pacts and signs, with God's permission, furnish to the madwomen the effect of their request. Plato related something similar in the book On Poetic Frenzy, saying: Just as Bacchant women, when not of sound mind, draw honey and milk from rivers, but when of sound mind cannot draw them. This is brought about by their spirit, carried outside itself into fellowship with an evil demon. We have it as an established fact that witches have drawn butter from stirred-up rivers at times, something that can in no way happen otherwise than by the ministry of a demon. But another person, who is not subject to demons by pact and surrender, even if he knows the things suitable for witchcraft and also the manner of working, and although he displays the signs, nevertheless rarely obtains the effect, because he is not counted among the demons' familiars. For the devil, swollen with pride, imitates the Church of Christ to such a degree that, just as one who is not ordained cannot consecrate the body of Christ, however much he may pronounce the words of consecration, so also the demon, however much someone invokes him and displays signs or characters, either never or rarely comes to the aid of one whom he does not know to be subject to him by pact.
