Thus far he. For my part, considering the matter more deeply, I cannot grieve enough that so great a man, one so well deserving of letters and learned men, and of the Catholic Church itself, has fallen into such suspicion, as though he had written and committed to public light something contrary to the holy and orthodox faith, harmful to the Christian commonwealth, and unworthy of a religious conscience. Yet from the very title prefixed to the Steganography, as from the end that the author set before himself as his sole aim, it is clear that nothing else is contained in that book than a definite art of disclosing the intention of one’s mind to absent persons through hidden writing. For the use of this, as is easy to see in the Key prefixed to that work, there is certainly no need of a magical and diabolical art, but only of a physical and natural one. Nor, says Trithemius, would the knowledge of this most secret art, if divulged among the wicked and reprobate, do less harm to the commonwealth than it would do good among the good. For just as good men, and those devoted to virtues, use all discoveries for good and for the common benefit, so the wicked and reprobate hunt for occasions not only from evil things but even from good and most holy institutions, by which they may become worse.
