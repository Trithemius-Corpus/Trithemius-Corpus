For just as a good man and lover of honor can, for a private or common good, make known and convey and express at any distance of places the secret of his will to another who knows this art, whenever and as often as he wishes, secretly, securely, and without any suspicion by any mortal, perfectly, copiously, and quite fully, through letters visible to all, open or closed, so that no one, however learned or curious, can suspect anything about the sender’s secret, nor, even if he suspects it, detect it; so also any perverse, wanton, or malicious person, even one previously wholly ignorant of the Latin language, as soon as he has acquired this art, which with a master teaching he can acquire in two days at the longest, will thereafter write letters in Latin, with proper composition, clear in any narrative, elegant and sufficiently ornate, to a friend skilled in this art. In these letters he will wrap the secret of his will, accessible to that friend alone,

in such great mysteries that they cannot be penetrated at all by anyone, however studious or learned, without the aid of this art. Thereafter the learned and the unlearned, man and woman, boy and old man, good and bad, chaste and lascivious, would write throughout all regions of the world, in Latin or in whatever language known to them, Latin, Greek, or barbarous letters free from all suspicion, by which they would show one thing openly to those ignorant of the art, and another secretly to those who knew it. Nor, once this science had been made public to the reprobate, would the fidelity between spouses, contracted by sacrament, remain safe thereafter, when a wife, though hitherto ignorant of Latin, now sufficiently instructed through chaste, honorable, and most holy words of any language or idiom, could most fully understand the evil and shameless mind and intention of an adulterous lover or fornicator, even while her husband carried the letters and praised them as excellent, and could send back her own desire to him in the same way, as broadly and copiously as she wished, most securely in the same or other letters, in a beautiful and quite ornate sequence.

Indeed, although this science is in itself excellent and quite useful to the commonwealth, nevertheless, if it came to the knowledge of the wicked, which God forbid, the whole order of the commonwealth could over time be considerably disturbed, public trust endangered, all letters, legal instruments, written documents, and finally the very speech of men turned into the gravest suspicion. No one would then trust letters, however holy and honorable, without fear, but only rarely would anyone give credence to epistles. For however honorable and chaste the words might be, deceit, fraud, and deception would always be thought to lie beneath them, and people would become fearful in everything, and for the most part suspicious no less of friends than of adversaries.

Therefore, to remove all these inconveniences that I have mentioned, and so that this science, in itself physical, natural, and lawful, should not create such great evils and inconveniences if it came to everyone’s knowledge, Trithemius not without reason used numbers, signs, and foreign names, as of spirits and others, by which,

as by mysteries, he wrapped that art, so that it would be readily accessible to no one except one who knew that art, that is, to an honorable and pious man, but would remain forever hidden from the perverse and malignant. And we have learned that the most ancient wise men, whom in the Greek language we call philosophers, once did this too; for it is the opinion of the most learned that, if they had discovered any secrets either of nature or of art, they concealed them in various ways and figures, lest they come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews also confirm that Moses, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in describing the creation of Heaven and Earth, covered ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words. Hence in many places of Sacred Scripture, besides the literal sense, the allegorical, tropological, and anagogical senses also have been received by theologians. Saint Jerome, the most learned among our own, affirms that almost as many mysteries lie hidden in the Apocalypse of John as there are words.
