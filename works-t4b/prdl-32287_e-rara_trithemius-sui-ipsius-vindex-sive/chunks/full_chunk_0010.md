Steganography of Trithemius. 27

Nor does it matter that Trithemius, in order to veil the secret, used conjurations in almost all the chapters. For in them there is nothing other than hidden steganographic secrets; and Trithemius himself warns, in the triple General Key which he himself wrote and communicated to lovers of this art who would show gratitude and keep it secret, that all of them must first be examined by means of each of the steganographic methods.

The same judgment must be made about the arithmetical numbers. He employed them not in order to attribute any magical power to them, but so that, however many of them you find, you would know that they signify so many meaningful letters, while the O denotes nothing; this is clear without difficulty to one who reads and understands those methods. Indeed, I freely and willingly admit that letters, whether Latin or Greek or written in any language whatever, can be read and understood in their own way, and even, what is more, can be written and composed according to rules and good syntax by someone who, provided he knows the alphabet, has never studied either grammar or

Yet Trithemius’s works, the Steganography and the Polygraphy, teach that this can be done easily and without any forbidden and demonic magic. For if prohibited magic is defined as a faculty or art by which, through the force of a pact entered into with demons, certain marvelous things surpassing the common understanding of human beings are accomplished, but the most religious man and most perfect master of natural magic, Johannes Trithemius, never entered into any pact with demons in his steganographic work, as will later be clear from his own declarations, but used lawful, natural, and physical means as a lover of the more secret philosophy, then it is readily evident that the Steganography must not be counted among magical books.

The third book alone causes difficulty. Because we cannot understand it for want of a Key, many criticize it as suspect under the mark of superstition, or at least as a supposititious offspring.

Steganography of Trithemius. 29

But I, precisely because we do not understand it, judge that that book should rather be approved than condemned, rather retained with all our strength than removed from the remaining books. For who is so unfair an assessor of things that he would dare to judge something evil, when nevertheless in his own understanding he finds it neither good nor evil, and therefore does not understand it? Certainly, in doubtful matters the safer and better side must be chosen; moral theologians as well as scholastics teach this. For, says Trithemius, he shows himself a rash judge who pronounces sentence on a case before he has learned its truth. But if they assign a reasonable cause for their criticism, if they certainly and evidently convict Trithemius of forbidden magic, if they bring forward from that book something impious and contrary to the faith of the Roman Church, then at last I would believe it to be a supposititious offspring and altogether unworthy of the name of Trithemius.

Note well. Considered externally and according to the literal sense of the verses, the Steganography can perhaps seem superstitious; but not so according to the author’s intention and according to the kernel lying hidden within.
