For Trithemius himself, writing to Rogerius Sicambrus, who had asked for the titles of Trithemian works, wrote among other things as follows: “After these things I set about writing a great and very difficult Steganographic work of marvelous invention, to be divided into eight books. The first and second books of it I brought all the way to completion; but the third I left defective, and all the rest I have left entirely buried down to the present day, persuaded by many considerations, since the labor is great and the reward perhaps small. Let it therefore remain imperfect as it lies, until usefulness persuades otherwise.” He says almost the same thing in the letter to Nicolaus Phorcensis, and elsewhere throughout his letters to friends. The first reason that holds me back from publishing it is consideration of the future and the very likely fear of evil, which could occur through the abuse of wicked men, since wicked men would use our inventions for evil no less than good men would use them for good. The second is the undertaking of very great labor and the small return of reward. For, as you saw, the work is difficult and of the keenest speculation, and cannot be completed without the greatest labor. And what return is there for such labor, except perhaps a little weight of gold, which is transitory and for that reason rather to be despised by a Christian monk? It has never been my intention to labor for temporal reward, since I see that I have been called into the vineyard of the everlasting King. I have as yet written nothing about which anyone could deservedly marvel; I have done nothing astonishing; and yet I suffer the opinion of the crowd, since many think me a MAGICIAN, asserting that I have raised the dead, summoned demons from the underworld, foretold the future, brought back thieves by incantations, and bound robbers. Although, with my conscience as witness, I do not regard these prattlings of the ignorant as important, nevertheless I do not intend to give occasion for evil suspicion, so far as possible.”

And see how conscious of right his mind was, and how much good Trithemius feared for himself, lest by this work of his he offend anyone, especially the unlearned and inexperienced, who dare to pass an unfavorable judgment on such good and holy studies.
