To the reader.

Not ignorant of the future, he set forth here and there, scattered through his books, statements on behalf of his Steganography; these I shall bring into the open. From all these things it will shine out clearer than daylight that Johannes Trithemius was not a magician, but a most obedient, most submissive, and most humble son of the Church; and that all the things he wrote in the Steganography rest upon physical and natural principles, and therefore are by no means to be condemned under the name of forbidden magic. Read, friendly reader; weigh, and judge.

Farewell.

JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
ABBOT OF SPONHEIM.

Johannes Trithemius, abbot, was born in the village of Trittenheim, in the diocese of Trier, on the banks of the river Moselle, on the first day of the month of February, at the eleventh hour and thirtieth minute after midday, in the year of the Lord’s Nativity 1462, in the tenth Roman indiction: his father was Johannes de Heidenberg, and his mother was named Elisabeth de Longo Vico, that is to say,

in the year when the city of Mainz, captured in the quarrel between two pontiffs, lost its liberty and found servitude, many of its citizens having been driven into exile; in the fourth current year of Pope Pius II of that name, and the twenty-second of Emperor Frederick III.

In the year of the Lord 1482, in the monastery of Sponheim in the diocese of Mainz, namely on the first day of February, on which he had once been born, he laid aside the secular habit and there took up the monastic habit under the rule of the blessed Father Benedict, according to the observance newly introduced. In the following year, which was 1483, after Abbot Johannes de Colenhufen had been transferred to Seligenstadt by canonical postulation, Johannes Trithemius, by the common election of the brothers, was advanced and adorned as abbot of the aforesaid monastery of Sponheim on the fourth day before the Kalends of July, and presided for twenty-three years, one month, and nineteen days. When these had passed, not without many labors,

provoked by the rashness of certain rivals, he accepted the abbey of St. James at Würzburg, which was being offered to him, and of his own free will resigned Sponheim, which was full of useless cares.

In both places he wrote many small works, and from among them he recently listed for me, in his own hand on a sheet, the following. Since you, as a friend, ask me for their titles, I gladly assent to your wishes, so that, from consideration of such great labors of his, you may hold in closer memory our common friend and teacher.

And first I shall record the titles of those works which he laboriously composed while serving as abbot at Sponheim.

He wrote first:

THE CONSTITUTIONS OF HIS ORDER, WHICH WERE MADE IN THE PROVINCIAL CHAPTERS BY ORDER OF THE PRESIDENTS, one book.

On the Visitation of Monks, one book.

On the Manner of Holding a Provincial Chapter, one book.

On the Rule of St. Benedict, two books.

Homilies and Exhortatory Sermons to His Monks, two books, which he dedicated to his predecessor.

On the Temptations of Cloistered Persons and Their Remedies, two books.

To Nicolaus, priest of Mernich, On the Priestly Life, one book.

On the Vanity and Misery of This Life, one book.

A Penthic Work, that is, a mournful work, On the Ruin of His Order, one book.
