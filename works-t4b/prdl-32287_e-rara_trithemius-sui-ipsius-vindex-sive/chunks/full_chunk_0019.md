But someone might object to me, Trithemius says in the same place a little before the end: “If you wanted this science of yours to be hidden, why did you wish to commit it to writing?” To him, if an answer must be given, I say that I neither ought nor was able to hide this science from the best prince, by which he may be able to free his clients, of whose number I am one, from many dangers, if the case should occur. Likewise, in the preface to the second book of the Steganographia, he says: “But since I shall again have to mention spirits, I think I must again state beforehand:

that in this art of ours there is nothing frivolous, nothing contrary to evangelical tradition or to the Catholic faith, nothing at all superstitious handed down. For all the things that we have either said in the preceding volume or shall say in those that follow rest upon natural, lawful, and honorable principles; and the mystery, veiled only by foreign instructions, and words wrapped in the names of spirits, require a learned reader. For we use the service of a spirit to veil a secret which, if made public, would harm the reprobate,” etc.

You speak excellently, Trithemius; excellently you plead your case; excellently you defend yourself not only from the biting Bovillus, but also from every malevolent tongue, whether present or future. But let us hear what you say finally in the proem to the first book of the Steganographia.

“And lest any future reader of this work, when in the course of it he often encounters the names, offices, orders, distinctions, properties, prayers, and any other
