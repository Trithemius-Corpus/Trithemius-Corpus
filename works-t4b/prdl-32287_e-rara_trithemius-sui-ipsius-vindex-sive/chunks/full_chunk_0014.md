The uses of the Polygraphic and Steganographic books.

Indeed, what uses and advantages can arise from this kind of writing, when rightly employed, Trithemius himself teaches most beautifully, though in part obscurely, in his preface to the Polygraphia addressed to the Emperor Maximilian. And although he refers most of those uses to the Polygraphia, they can nevertheless also be applied, not unsuitably, to the Steganographia.

The first use, therefore, says Trithemius, is that a complete understanding of this work gives to one who needs it an easy method of making known the secret of your mind safely, secretly, and without any suspicion on the part of anyone watching, to one who knows the practice of the art, as widely as there may be need, across any distance of places, by regulated speech. What lies hidden in these matters the work of the Steganographia brings out; it requires neither letters nor a messenger who knows the secrets he carries. Do not take the matter otherwise than it is. The second use is that

--- Page 054 ---

by the service of this work, in a short space of time, you will teach a man ignorant of the Latin language, provided only that he knows how to read and write letters in his mother tongue, to write in the Latin language, as you wish, elegantly or suitably, and to conceal and hide all the secret mysteries of his will, and to announce them to one who knows the art as broadly as he pleases, safely, secretly, and without suspicion. Again I warn you beforehand not to understand us as promising anything other than what we promise.

The third use is that, if you apply the duty of your office prudently, you will teach one unskilled in the Roman language, not only in a few hours to write and read what we promise, but also to understand it; and this we have decided, not without reasonable cause, to keep reserved. For the pupil of the new art will write a language he had not known, and he will understand Latin speech composed under regulated instruction, and, if you order him, he himself will interpret it sufficiently word for word. Finally, this instruction can be extended to all the affairs and business of the world. How these things will be able

--- Page 055 ---

to be done, if anyone wishes to learn from me, let him remember first of all to show himself such that he deserves to receive it. For not all things are owed to all people, but one person is to be preferred to another.

The fourth use is that, through the enlargement of the likeness of the thing received, all these things can be extended into any language, when the instituting binary has been reduced to unity. This, however, not just anyone at random can do, but only the man whom, with God going before by his special grace, art and nature have made worthy.

The fifth use is that, by imitation of likeness, we can communicate to one who knows the art of our invention any secrets of our mind whatever, as abundantly, manifoldly, and broadly as you wish, in various ways that can neither be known nor observed by anyone else. Here there is given a manifold speech without voice, through which, safely, securely, very broadly, with every suspicion removed, we shall be able to indicate most fully to those who know the art all the conceptions of our mind, however difficult they may be. And this wonderful invention has

innumerable progressions, capable of all mysteries.

The sixth use is that, through an understanding of this work, you will be able to discover any regulated metathesis or transposition of letters whatever, and nevertheless to form it in such a way that it remains subject only to those who know the art, and to absolutely no one else in the world. And the form and operation of metathesis together have great power; it both has many modes and is varied in many ways.

The seventh use is that, through an understanding of this volume, a very great capacity is given to those who wish to enlarge the memory without labor, whenever the seeds of minutiae are placed in their proper degrees. For the sake of goodness, all things lie hidden, wrapped in the secrets of words and figures; if anyone desires to understand them more fully, let him call to mind that relentless labor conquers all things. For it will have been enough for me to have discovered the principles of the art, and to have handed down a rough and deliberately unformed method of writing symbolically. For there was neither time nor reason for our discoveries to be formed in a more polished way.
