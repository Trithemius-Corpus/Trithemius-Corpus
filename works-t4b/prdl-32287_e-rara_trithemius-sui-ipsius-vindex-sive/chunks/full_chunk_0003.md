JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
first abbot of Sponheim, and afterward abbot of St. James in the suburb of Würzburg.
Most excellently learned in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew letters.
Poet, orator, philosopher, theologian.
Most accomplished master of natural magic,
as, among his other writings, his Steganographia and Polygraphia especially show.

He was born in the village of TRITENHEIM on the banks of the river Moselle, in the diocese of Trier, in the year 1462.
At last he died in the year 1516, in the not yet completed fifty-fifth year of his age, on the very day sacred to St. Lucy, not without the grief and lamentation of all great men, princes, and learned men.

ON TRITHEMIUS, THE VINDICATOR OF HIMSELF AFTER DEATH.

Is it so, then? Do we not allow buried spirits to find rest,
nor pious ashes to lie softly?
Trithemius is said to have practiced the ill-omened arts of Erebus,
and gods whom it is not lawful to worship.
Yet great Trithemius stands forth in noble arms,
and himself vindicates his own reputation.
Plainly, uprightness has no need of foreign arms;
for it holds strong enough weapons in itself.
Most beautiful virtue has no need of camps pitched high;
it is enough that it has not lain too deeply hidden.
Yet scarcely, Trithemius, do you clear away the arts charged against you,
nor by this art do you escape the mark of a grim name.
For he whose ashes, whose urn, we have known,
who now lies in his own tomb, an ancient shade,
will he go again, as though alive, against opposing enemies?
Will he wage posthumous wars with unconquered hand?
Will that bony Mars again stand in noble arms?
Who would not say that this man was a great Magus?

TO THE READER.

It will perhaps seem strange to someone that I, entangled in household affairs and having long since turned my mind away from the most beautiful studies of the liberal arts, should now suddenly, after setting aside the turmoil of business, undertake to defend the cause of Johannes Trithemius, a man long since dead and taken from human affairs more than ninety-nine years ago. But if that person considers more deeply the reason for my plan, he will not only say that I am doing nothing foreign to my purpose, but will affirm that I have entered into this apologetic writing for the most just causes.

In the year 1499 Johannes Trithemius composed, and then some years later dedicated to Philip, Count Palatine, prince-elector and duke of Bavaria, at whose persuasion and encouragement he had begun this work, or, as Johannes Duraclusius says, to Joachim, Margrave of Brandenburg, prince-elector,

To the Reader.

that wonderful and laborious work, the Steganographia. In that book he transmits the most certain art of safely and securely disclosing the will of one’s mind to absent persons through hidden writing.

And lest so great and important a secret come to the ears of the common crowd of the untrained or of wicked men, he assigns offices, orders, distinctions, properties, prayers, and various operations of spirits of every kind; through the understanding of these, all the secrets of this science are closed and opened.

Some persons, offended by such operations and names of spirits, among whom there have also been very serious, learned, and religious men, taking the words just as they sound, have drawn some bad and sinister suspicion about Trithemius. They silently thought that the said books had been written contrary to natural principles, were filled with superstition, were crammed with forbidden magic, and therefore should be reckoned among the books prohibited by the Church. As for them, just as, with charity preserved, I have always opposed them as best I could in familiar conversation and in disputation,
