used visible letters, writing with vermilion or common encaustic; but others formed invisible characters at their own discretion and command. The methods of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many and altogether innumerable; to mention them all is neither within our intention nor within our ability. But of invisible letters there are three kinds more commonly used among mortals today.

The first is called Dermatic, by which a certain artificial writing is made on the backs of messengers through the washing of a certain fluid, visible only in a particular way; no one can penetrate it who does not know the secret of the art. The second is called Hyphasmatic, by which something is written on cloth with certain substances; when dried it cannot be seen at all, until, when moistened in cold water, it is perceived. The third kind is called Aleotic, which is manifold and varied, both in form and in matter, and has almost innumerable methods of writing secretly. Its writing is made on paper or without paper, with nemilua and cala, with raphocam lapolce,

and with various other things. Letters are likewise written and hidden in various ways and places: under race tabulata, under rofiri lacubior, in leopi, in necoflas, in ceocali, locubat, in ratera, in lispilia, and in other similar things almost infinite, etc.

See, reader, in how many ways you can announce the conception of your mind to an absent friend, even without letters, and without the knowledge of the very person who performs the office of messenger. This is perhaps what Trithemius meant when, in this book, he maintains that letters can be sent without a messenger, as we also noted above. And hence Trithemius, in the said third book, on the varied motion of the planets and the interpretation of tables, almost at the end, wishes the operator to use encaustic, not the common kind, such as he mentioned before when speaking of visible letters, but one prepared, as he says, “in our manner” for this purpose. That incaustum, or encaustic, is a kind of painting made by the application of fire, namely with burnt colors and wax dissolved by fire, Calepinus reports from Apuleius and Pliny.
