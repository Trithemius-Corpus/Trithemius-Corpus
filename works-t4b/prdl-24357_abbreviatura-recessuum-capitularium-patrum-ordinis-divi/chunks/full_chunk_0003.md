Therefore let visitors strive first to visit themselves, and so proceed to the correction of others. For it is written in Job 5: “Visiting your appearance, you will not sin.” For he who neglects himself cannot amend another, as the Lord says in Luke 6: “If the blind lead the blind, both fall into the pit.”

*That visitors should have zeal for God and souls. Chapter 4.*

Finally, the visitors themselves ought to be zealous, according to knowledge, for the salvation of those to be visited, so that they do not carry out their office negligently and perfunctorily, lest they be judged useless under the sentence of condemnation with the lazy servant. And let them have zeal for regular discipline and for the constitutions of the fathers, knowing that without doubt they will be abundantly rewarded for their labors by the giver of all good things. Let them perform the office of visitation entrusted to them as if the care of all whom they visit belonged to them alone. Yet their zeal itself should be discreet, lest, which God forbid, if they correct offenders indiscreetly, they themselves incur a graver fault. 24, q. 3, Non impetu. And where they find anything worthy of correction, let them correct it not from love of vengeance nor from hatred, but from zeal for justice. 23, q. 4, § last. And the just and holy are indeed indignant at sinners not from hatred, but from zeal for discipline. 45, dist., Vera iusticia. Nor should they be lax where a fault has deserved discipline, because if they let the faults of delinquents go unpunished, they sin gravely. 11, q. 3, Si is; 23, q. 5, Qui vices. And a prelate who does not correct crimes is called not a prelate but a shameless dog. 83, dist., Nemo. He who neglects to amend when he is able to correct has the guilt of the doer. 86, dist., Culpam; 83, dist., Error. And not to correct the wicked but to foster them is to kill them. 23, q. 5, Qui vices; 45, dist., Sed illud. And it is not charity but languor if evil habits are not corrected. 23, q. 5, Non putes. And yet correction itself must not be indiscreet, but, as blessed Gregory says in the Moralia, one judging justly, holding a balance in his hand, should carry justice and mercy in each scale. 45, dist., Omnis. For excessive harshness in rebuking receives neither correction nor salvation. Ibid., Cum beatus; and c. Disciplina. Therefore let zeal not be judged indiscreet, but let it be tempered with mercy and gentleness.

*That visitors should be discreet in inquiries. Chapter 5.*

Those who are appointed as visitors should also be wisely discreet, imbued with the divine Scriptures, and experienced in this sort of business either in themselves or in others, so that they may not rashly believe things not to be believed, or shamelessly conceal things that ought to be believed. For both are evil. For he who believes quickly is light of heart. Ecclesiasticus 19. And we ought not quickly believe evil things about others. 86, dist., Si quid. And evil things about one’s neighbor must first be proved before they are believed. 2, q. 1, Deus; and 3, q. 8, Iudicet; 11, q. 3, In cunctis and c. Quamvis. For it is written in Ecclesiasticus 19: “Do not believe every word.” 11, q. 3, Non solum per quod. Yet to be willing to believe no one belongs to a shameless man. And indeed the innocent always believes well and judges well of others. Proverbs 14; 22, q. 4, Innocens. Therefore let visitors be discreet and prudent, so that, considering the zeal, life, and merit of those making depositions, they may be able to know whom they ought to believe. For witnesses are to be weighed according to trustworthiness, dignity, and seriousness of conduct. 4, q. 3, § Item in testibus, and throughout there. Therefore, as far as can be done, visitors ought diligently to examine the intentions, motives, and causes of those making depositions, lest perhaps they are moved by no just reason. Finally, our fathers established the following on this matter: Visitors should be especially zealous for religion, mature in morals and discreet, and also very solicitous and circumspect in carrying out the act of their visitation. And a little later it is added: But visitors should not readily believe every spirit, 1 John 4, nor condemn anyone on suspicion alone or on the deposition of one witness. Manifest matters, however, do not require accusation. 2, q. 1, Qualetarius, § In manifestis, and the following c. I judge him not a lover of charity but fraudulent who reveals the hidden crime of a brother for the sake of defamation or revenge. 5, q. 5, Qui ambulat; 6, q. 1, Summa. “Crime” can be understood not only as mortal offense, but also as every venial one. 25, dist., Unum orarium, § Nunc autem. In serious matters, however, and those concerning reputation, diligent inquiry should be made; but before just proof, except in manifest cases, let no one be judged or condemned. 30, q. 5, Nullum ante veram, and the following c. And in such matters two or three testimonies must be brought forward. 2, q. 5, c. 1 and c. Placuit. Therefore visitors should be cautious in judging such secret matters, to avoid scandal, unless the person has been convicted or has confessed freely. 2, q. 1, Nos vero quemquam, and the following c., Nomen presbyteri, and the following c. For if the accuser fails in proof, he ought to be punished with the penalty of retaliation. Extra, De calumniatoribus, c. 1; 2, q. 3, Calumniator, and the following c.; q. 8, Qui crimen; 3, q. 10, Eos and the following c. It must also be guarded against that matters which have been corrected and punished should be brought up again. Extra, De accusationibus, c. De his; and 2, q. 1, Prohibentur; 23, q. 4, Si illic. Thus also the holy fathers established: Let each one beware that he not disturb the minds of the brothers or of their fathers with beginnings. Moreover, if any secrets have been revealed to the visitors themselves, let them beware of bringing them into public. 2, q. 1, Si peccaverit; 6, q. 2, Episcopus si clerico, c. Placuit. And concerning hidden matters one must not inquire too curiously. Extra, De accusationibus, Qualiter iii. Yet concerning manifest matters, as I said above, the clamor of an accuser is not necessary.
