Nor is there to be an appeal from correction: De officio ordinarii, c. Licet; and De appellatione, Ad nostram; and c. Reprehensibilis, and c. Cum speciali. Understand this unless the one correcting has exceeded due measure. 23, q. 4, Qui peccat.

Visitors, therefore, according to the decree of Honorius III, in c. Ea quae, if they find monks delinquent, should command the abbots of the places to correct them, as is said in the aforesaid constitution in these words: But if abbots are found negligent in making corrections according to the visitors' command and the regular statutes, either in themselves or in their monks, let them be proclaimed and rebuked, and let them be punished publicly in the general chapter, so that their punishment may be an example to others. And note according to what is said there: visitation of this kind ought to be carried out plainly and without the noise of judicial proceedings, De accusationibus, Olim; because regulars are readily removed from their administrations, De simonia, c. Per tuas; and therefore judicial order is not always to be observed in inquisitions of this kind concerning regulars, De accusationibus, c. Qualiter, II, and there at the end. And visitors should diligently inquire into the condition of monasteries and regular observances, and should correct and reform, both in spiritual and in temporal matters, what they see should be corrected.

But it is asked whether the bishop of the place can visit the monasteries of his diocese. The answer is that he can, as in c. In singulis, § Porro. He can visit them, but he ought not bring secular persons in with him, as Hostiensis notes on the same title in the gloss. For the visitation which the visitors carry out does not remove the bishop's jurisdiction, as is noted in the same c. In singulis. Custom, however, holds otherwise, because bishops also quite often commit their own duties to abbots.

in agro, against disorderly monks or those forgetful of their profession, etc. Likewise c. 6, § Decretum, says thus: Where they do not find disciplines effective for reform, let them institute new ceremonies according to the canonical sanctions and the statutes of the provincial chapter, which those visited are bound to observe without any contradiction. If it seems necessary to the visitors, they can bind those to be visited by a bodily oath to tell the truth, as in the constitution of Lord Nicholas, Chuf. le., c. 3; and these matters must be discussed in their proper place. In c. 15 all those to be visited are commanded to receive their visitors with all reverence and humility and to treat them charitably, and diligently to heed and observe their admonitions and ordinances. The visitors themselves also have authority to impose penalties greater than those expressly set out in the constitutions, and to compel rebels, by ecclesiastical censure, to obey their commands.

But many impediments sometimes occur in connection with visitations of monasteries. For sometimes the monks themselves and abbots maliciously impede them by themselves; sometimes through the ordinary bishops; sometimes through princes; and frequently through their relatives, friends, and supporters. What should be done in these cases, if they occur, I shall try to show from the statutes of the fathers. In c. 15, which was celebrated second at Seligenstadt, we read the statute:
