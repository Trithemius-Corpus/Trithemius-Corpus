Concerning the household of the visitors.

The visitors, however, shall have with them an honorable household and retinue, and each of them shall be content with four mounts, unless a reasonable cause requires more.

Those who impede visitation incur the sentence of excommunication.

Lest anyone impede so wholesome an office of visitation by any contrivance or pretext, we will that all and every abbots, priors, provosts, and monks who refuse to receive their visitors, or who gainsay them, and who are rebellious and disobedient to them, and who impede the office of visitation by nod, deed, writing, or word, or who arrange, procure, cause, or attempt, by themselves or through another or others, directly or indirectly, that it be impeded by their friends, noble or ignoble, or by any subordinates whatsoever, so that their visitors cannot freely and quietly exercise an office of this kind, shall by that very fact incur the sentence of excommunication. The visitors themselves shall be bound to publish this sentence of excommunication against them and duly have it carried into execution.

Likewise we will that all and every abbots, provosts, priors, convents, and persons who have rejected from themselves this reformation of the lord cardinal legate and of the chapter, previously received and continued for a time, shall incur the sentence of excommunication, unless at least within the time to be fixed for them by their visitors they effectively take it up again and continue it.

Begging under pretext of need is forbidden.

It has come to our ears by a sure report that persons of both sexes of our order go about, not so much compelled by poverty as for the sake of begging, procuring that their begging of this kind be announced from the pulpits, and even begging door to door to the disparagement of the order. We forbid this to be done or to occur henceforth, and we exhort the people of God not to stretch out helping hands to such persons, but to send them back to their monasteries.

Or, if the resources of those monasteries do not suffice, the same brothers or persons of the order shall see to it that petitions of this kind are published and collections conveyed, not by themselves but through secular persons; and they shall seek the necessities of life from neighboring monasteries of the same order. If, after this prohibition of ours, they do not desist from the foregoing, any president may and must proceed against them as disobedient, according to the quality of the offenses.

That permission to wander should not be given to monks.

We have also learned that some abbots of monasteries and prelates do not fear to receive into obedience fugitive monks and others fleeing the reins of discipline, who go about after permission has sometimes even been extorted from their superiors and without proper proof; and that they give an occasion for wandering to those who wish it, to the peril of their souls and the scandal of many. We therefore, desiring to remedy a deadly disease, strictly forbid such things to be done henceforth, under penalty of infamy and incapacity; after warning has first been given, the visitors shall impose this penalty on the prelates who give, and on the monks who receive, permission to wander, with heavier penalties nevertheless to be added if the fault requires it.

Concerning procurators of absent persons.

Let there be a procurator for absent abbots, even three of them, under penalty of suspension from divine services, to be imposed by the presidents then in office, unless an evident cause stands in the way. Whether that cause is sufficient shall be proposed to the presidents.

Concerning ancient statutes here repeated anew.

Consequently, in this chapter very many statutes of past chapters are repeated in form, namely: that abbots should not henceforth withdraw themselves from the chapter, as above, chapter 13; and that all should publicly and everywhere wear the habit fitting to religious life, as above, chapter 1; and also that those visited should pay their visitors three florins for travel expenses, as chapter 4; and certain other things which are set out more fully above in their own places.
