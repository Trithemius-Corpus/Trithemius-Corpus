Yet he will again be free from blame if all the diligence of a shepherd has been applied to a restless and disobedient flock, and every care has been shown for their diseased actions. Their shepherd, absolved in the judgment of God, may say with the prophet to the Lord: “I have not hidden your justice in my heart. I have declared your truth and your salvation; but they, despising them, have rejected me.” And then at last death itself shall be the punishment for the disobedient sheep under his care.

Therefore, when someone receives the name of abbot, he must preside over his disciples with twofold teaching: that is, he should show all good and holy things by deeds more than by words. To receptive disciples he should set forth the Lord’s commands in words; but to those hard of heart and simpler, he should demonstrate the divine precepts by his own deeds. All things, however, that he teaches his disciples to be contrary to his own deeds, he should indicate are not to be done, lest, preaching to others, he himself be found reprobate; lest God someday say to him in his sin: “Why do you recount my justices and take up my covenant in your mouth? But you have hated discipline and have cast my words behind you.” And: “You saw the speck in your brother’s eye, but in your own eye you did not see the beam.”

Let him make no distinction of persons in the monastery; let not one be loved more than another, unless he has found one better in good deeds or obedience. Let not the freeborn be preferred to one converted from servitude, unless some other reasonable cause exists. But if justice so dictates and it seems good to the abbot, let him do this from any rank whatever. Otherwise let them keep their proper places, because whether free or slave, we are all one in Christ, and under one Lord we bear an equal service of military duty; for there is no acceptance of persons with God. We are distinguished before him in this respect alone: if we are found better than others in good works and humble. Therefore let equal charity be shown by him to all; let one discipline be applied by him to all according to their merits.

XXIII

In his teaching, therefore, the abbot must always observe that apostolic form in which it says: “Reprove, entreat, rebuke”; that is, mixing times with times, blandishments with terrors. Let him show the stern affection of a master and the loving affection of a father: that is, he should reprove the undisciplined and restless more harshly; the obedient, gentle, and patient he should entreat, so that they may advance toward what is better; but the negligent and contemptuous we admonish him to rebuke and correct. Nor should he dissemble the sins of offenders; rather, as soon as they begin to spring up, he should cut them out by the roots before they gain strength, mindful of the danger of Eli the priest of Shiloh. Those of better character and understanding he should correct with words at the first or second admonition; but the wicked, hard, proud, or disobedient he should restrain at the very beginning of sin with blows or bodily chastisement, knowing that it is written: “A fool is not corrected by words.” And again: “Strike your son with the rod, and you will deliver his soul from death.”

The abbot must always remember what he is, remember what he is called, and know that more is required from the one to whom more is entrusted. Let him know how difficult and arduous a thing he has undertaken: to rule souls and to serve the characters of many. One he must guide by blandishments, another by rebukes and persuasions; and according to each one’s character and understanding, he should so conform and adapt himself to all that he not only suffer no losses in the flock committed to him, but rejoice in the increase of a good flock.

Above all, let him not, by dissembling or making little of the salvation of the souls committed to him, show more concern for transitory, earthly, and perishable things; but let him always consider that he has undertaken to rule souls, concerning whom he will have to render an account.
