How prelates should correct their subjects.

Finally, in the bowels of Jesus Christ, and under the adjuration of his strict and final judgment, we exhort and beseech all and every abbot, provost, dean, conventual prior, and cloister prior of the said order, that each of them so correct, amend, and reform his subjects and monasteries, and bring their conduct into regular discipline, that when the visitors come to their monasteries for the purpose of visitation, they may find rather what edifies them than anything that stands in need of correction.

The third provincial chapter was celebrated at Fulda, in the diocese of Wurzburg, in the year 1420.

In the year of the Lord 1420, in the thirteenth indiction, in the third year of the pontificate of lord Martin, pope the fifth, on the fifth day of the month of May, the third provincial chapter was celebrated in the monastery of Saint Boniface at Fulda. At it presided the venerable fathers Lambert, abbot of Mons Monachorum near Bamberg, Frederick, abbot of Hirsau, and Henry, abbot of the monastery of Saint Godehard in Hildesheim.

On its statutes.

In this chapter also, many statutes that had been issued in the first chapter celebrated at Constance were repeated and inserted word for word, as they are given above. In it it was ordained that visitors must not have more than six mounts, unless necessity requires otherwise; and whatever they have beyond that must be assigned to the use of the chapter.

On the discipline of praying.

For the reform of the order. First, we ordain and decree that both the day and night office be celebrated devoutly, deliberately, and with pauses, without rushing through, without syncopating, and without one person anticipating another, and at the appropriate hours, as is contained in the Rule of our most blessed father Benedict. For this, as soon as the signal has been heard, all are to leave whatever is in their hands and come together with the greatest haste, yet with gravity, lest frivolity find occasion. And lest under the pretext of ignorance, etc., as is contained above in the first chapter. Thus nothing is to be preferred to the work of God, for cursed is the man who does the work of God negligently.

On the establishment of monks.

But lest anyone be able to excuse himself from celebrating divine offices of this kind because of a lack of persons, we wish that in each monastery as many monks be maintained and received as can conveniently be supported without want from its goods and revenues.

That monks are not to be received for a price or simoniacally.
