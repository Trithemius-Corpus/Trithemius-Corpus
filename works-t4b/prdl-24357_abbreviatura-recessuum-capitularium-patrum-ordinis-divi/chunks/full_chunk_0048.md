Let each monk sleep clothed in a separate bed, in the same place, if this can be done, or at least in groups of ten or twenty.

But they are not to sleep in private rooms, especially if these have not been suitably latticed, so that the bedding in them can be properly and clearly seen, according to the manner of conversion and according to the abbot’s disposition. And those who have feather mattresses are not to use them.

But if private rooms are found in the dormitory, we command that they be destroyed by the visitors, or by their order, or at least suitably latticed. In what places and at what times silence is to be kept, let it be commanded according to the Rule. We further will that silence be observed at all times in the oratory, dormitory, refectory, and cloister; nor after Compline may anyone speak at all, except as is contained in the Rule. But before the said Compline, let the Collations of the Fathers, or some other edifying work, be read concerning the conference.

Concerning the porter of the monastery. At the gate of each monastery one porter or doorkeeper, provident and discreet, is to be appointed, lest without the permission of the abbot or another presiding officer in the monastery there be granted freedom to leave it. But if anyone goes out without such permission, let him be subjected to the penalties of the constitution of Benedict XII concerning permission not to be given for going outside the monastery without reasonable cause.

The porter is also to be attentive that he permit women in no way to enter. But concerning a sufficient master of novices in the monastery: we ordain that abbots shall place in their monasteries sufficient masters, who shall introduce their novices into the elementary sciences; and, once introduced, they shall send them to the general studies, assigning them a pension according to the form of the statutes of the aforesaid Benedict, Pope XII.

Let an inventory be made at the assumption of each abbacy by each abbot, as is contained in the Benedictina. And so that the condition of the aforesaid monasteries may be known, we command, in virtue of holy obedience and under penalty of excommunication to be imposed by

the visitors, that every abbot be bound to hand over to the visitors in writing, distinctly, the ancient and modern condition of his monastery.

Concerning grants and leases to farm, and concerning sales and loans. Let the places of the aforesaid order not be leased to farm unless the Benedictina has first been consulted, concerning places not to be leased to farm; and we will that it be firmly observed. We also will that, when loans must be made under urgent necessity, the constitution concerning loans and sales shall likewise be considered, and likewise observed.

Let there be maintained in the monasteries as many monks as can be suitably supported. We will and ordain that the anciently established number of monks be maintained, if resources suffice for it; but if not, then as many as can suitably be maintained. Nor may anyone defend himself in any way by privilege or custom, which should rather be called corrupt usage, by which they say that they ought to receive no one unless he is noble, especially if they do not have nobles who may be received or who wish to enter the aforesaid monasteries; but if such are found, other things being equal, let them be preferred.

Necessaries are not to be supplied to monks in money. Food and other necessaries to be supplied to monks are in no way to be supplied in money or revenues, notwithstanding any constitution or ordinance whatsoever. And in this matter let the constitution of the aforesaid Benedict, beginning Ne vicarii in pecunia, be observed. But we will that the aforesaid religious be supplied with the said food and clothing in the three ways ordained in the Benedictina, De censibus et redditibus solvendis, chapter 21.

Concerning nuns. The things that have been said concerning monasteries of men we will likewise to be observed in monasteries of nuns, as to the articles pertaining to them; nevertheless willing and ordaining that they be held under perpetual enclosure, as is contained in the constitution of Boniface that begins Periculoso, De statu monachorum, book 6.
