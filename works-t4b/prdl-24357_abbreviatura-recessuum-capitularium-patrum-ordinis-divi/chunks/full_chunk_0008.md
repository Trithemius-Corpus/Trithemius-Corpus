Then, in the third chapter, which was celebrated at Fulda, it was enacted that no visitor should bring more than six mounts with him. But Nicholas of Cusa, cardinal legate, in his form of visitation, which he handed over to the visitors, orders that each visitor should be content with a chaplain and a servant, with three horses. Nevertheless, in the same constitutions of lord Nicholas, cardinal, he commands the visitors themselves to conduct themselves properly and to have respectable servants, so that they may not be burdensome to those visited. In chapter 15, celebrated second at Seligenstadt, it stands thus: Let the visitors themselves also have an honorable household and retinue, and let each of them be content with a number of four, unless a reasonable cause demands more.

How long visitors should remain in one monastery. Chapter 14.

Therefore, lest visitors become burdensome in expenses to those whom they visit, they should take care to dispatch their business as quickly as they can; and, as the fathers have enacted, they should not stay more than three or four days in one place visited by them, unless there is evident necessity. But if such matters should occur as demand delays of a longer time, they should take care to dispatch their business in such a way that they prove in all things that they seek not themselves, but God and the peace of those visited. Let the visitors also advise their servants to conduct themselves honorably and without murmuring in all things.

Those visited are bound diligently to guard the property and horses of the visitors. Chapter 15.

Since frauds and deceits are sometimes accustomed to occur against the visitors themselves among those especially who hate the one correcting them, with malicious subordinates or wicked persons plotting at the gate, lest the malicious should gain advantage from their own dishonesty, the fathers, in chapter 5, celebrated first at Wurzburg,

established as follows. This sacred chapter has decreed and enacted that when visitors come to some monastery for the sake of visitation, the abbot and convent of that monastery shall have the horses and property of the visitors guarded with the greatest diligence. But if anything from their goods or property should happen to be lost or taken away, before all else the abbot and convent of that monastery shall without any contradiction pay the value of the lost goods or property to those visitors without delay. And nevertheless such negligence shall be punished in chapter at the judgment of the presidents. This was also often enacted afterward in other chapters.

On the penalty for visitors negligent in visiting at the proper time. Chapter 16.

Since the increase of holy religion and regular observance arises in no small measure from frequent and diligently conducted visitations, lest so salutary an institution of the fathers be destroyed by the sluggishness of negligent visitors, since where the governance of discipline is despised, it remains that religion suffers shipwreck, as in *De statu monachorum*, *Attendentes*, in the Clementines, the fathers in nearly all chapters celebrated up to now have imposed penalties on visitors if they neglected their office. And first, in the constitution of Benedict XII, chapter 3, on visitors, it is said as follows: But if those visitors neglect to execute their office, we will that by the very fact they incur the penalties incurred by those who do not come to the provincial chapter without having a canonical impediment. And in chapters 4 and 5 and certain others it was enacted as follows: This sacred chapter has further decreed that those deputed to visit, if they neglect the time appointed for them, namely within the year, shall pay the penalty like those who neglect to come to chapter, namely thirty Rhenish florins, with moderation and just assessment reserved to the judgment of the presidents in the provincial chapter.
