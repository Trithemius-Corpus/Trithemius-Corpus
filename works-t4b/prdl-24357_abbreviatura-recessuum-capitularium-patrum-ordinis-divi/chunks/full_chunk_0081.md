Third, concerning nocturnal rest and silence: namely, whether all sleep in one dormitory, girded and clothed; whether each sleeps in a separate bed, or two in one bed, which ought not to be done; whether seniors are mixed with juniors; whether the dormitory is closed, and when; whether the prior has keys to all cells; whether they sleep in woolens; whether the cells are sufficiently latticed, as they ought to be, under penalty of excommunication latae sententiae; whether the beds are frequently inspected because of private property; whether silence is kept in the dormitory, oratory, cloister, and refectory after Compline, and in chapter and at other times and places, in the manner and form set down in the Rule.

Fourth, concerning subjects: whether they are obedient; whether they are proprietors, possessing anything whatever from offerings, Mass stipends, labors, offices, or any other sources whatsoever; whether there is a report of incontinence concerning anyone.

Fifth, concerning shaving and bathing: when, how, and with what discipline they have them; and, concerning the sick, how they are provided for; whether they have an infirmarian and cellarer who are diligent and fear God; whether the sisters and the others in the convent, and also they themselves, are provided with suitable food; whether they have appropriate comforts at the proper time; and whether the young are instructed by competent masters in morals and ceremonies, and also in the elementary sciences; and by whom, whether by monks or seculars; and whether any are at study.

Sixth, how they conduct themselves toward one another: whether humbly and charitably; and whether, after a disturbance has arisen, they seek reconciliation according to the Rule.

Seventh, how they conduct themselves toward outsiders and strangers: whether perhaps they are too familiar with them, or whether, because of them, or also because of relatives, friends, or parents, it often happens that they leave the monastery. Likewise, conversely, whether they receive guests who come to them with all kindness, to lodging and to table, with the prescribed washing of feet and reading of the divine law.

Eighth, how they conduct themselves when leaving the monastery: whether they leave with permission, and whose permission; whether with a companion, and what sort; with how many horses and servants, and what sort; whether with a blessing both when going out and when coming in; whether also with fitting habit and books of hours, and so forth concerning the other matters about which above, chapter 1, and below in the following rubric.

Ninth, concerning individual persons, a particular consideration and inquiry must be made. First, concerning the abbot: whether before election he had been reformed, or at least had remained in reform for a year; whether afterward he continued the reform; whether he attended chapter and paid the contribution; whether he gives an accounting each year; and whether he handed over in writing the state of his monastery to the former visitors; and whether he obeyed them in all their ordinances; whether he alienated, pawned, or handed over the goods of the monastery to a friend; how he confers benefices; how he orders offices, whether with counsel or according to his own opinion; whether he has priories, provostships, or churches annexed to the monastery; how he confers them, and on whom. Likewise, whether each year he receives an account from them and holds a chapter; whether he frequents choir or devotes himself to hunting; and so concerning the other matters that are noted above in various places about the abbot, and will still be touched on below in certain rubrics. And so also one must proceed in like manner with the prior, cellarer, custodian, vestiarian, and the other officials, with each according to the requirement of his office. Likewise with the porter: whether he is old and mature, joining the others for refectory, dormitory, etc. Likewise whether they permit women to enter or to live inside; whether the brothers frequently go out; and so concerning the other matters. All these things are set out more clearly in the recesses of the first and third chapters, and also of other chapters, and in the method of visiting, folio xiii, as well as in the bull of lord Nicholas, the legate often mentioned, whose points are given below in the final rubric.
