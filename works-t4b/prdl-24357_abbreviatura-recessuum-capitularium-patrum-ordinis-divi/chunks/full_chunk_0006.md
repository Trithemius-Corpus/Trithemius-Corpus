But if the visitors cannot carry out the foregoing, in chapter 21, celebrated fourth at Nuremberg, it was thus established: that the visitors themselves should denounce the disobedient and contumacious persons to one of the nearer presidents, who should proceed against them with appropriate legal remedies by apostolic authority. And in chapter 23 what follows was established: We also strictly forbid anyone henceforth, by himself or through others, in any way to arrange with diocesan bishops, princes, or any other temporal lords whatsoever, that the visitors should not be admitted; nor may he claim in any way that the visitors themselves are not to be admitted without the permission of the local ordinaries, since, being directed by apostolic authority, they do not need it, under penalty of thirty Rhenish florins, to be paid irrecoverably to the chapter as often as any abbot or any of the other rectors shall have acted to the contrary.

But this penalty seems to be one to be imposed on the rectors of monasteries, without removing the foregoing provision that they are to be compelled to obedience by ecclesiastical censure.

*If a bishop wishes to conduct visitation with us. Chapter 9.*

It is sometimes customary for it to happen that the ordinary bishops of places wish to visit monasteries with us; whether they are to be admitted in this way or not is often doubted. To resolve this scruple, one must consider that the impulse of bishops to visit monasteries with the visitors of the order has two reasons from which it arises. Either they do this from zeal for discipline, or from a contrivance of malice. If for the first reason, if their zeal is known and there is so great a necessity of visiting the monastery, they can be admitted; but only in their own person, because laymen should by no means be present at a visitation unless it is very necessary.
