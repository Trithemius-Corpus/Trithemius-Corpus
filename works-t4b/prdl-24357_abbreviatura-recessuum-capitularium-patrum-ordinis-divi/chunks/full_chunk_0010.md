After this, the visitor is to give an exhortation. Chapter 19.

Note that the visitation ought not to begin with examination or correction, but with exhortation, as in *De statu monachorum*, *Attendentes*, in the Clementines, and as Francis Zabarella also notes on the same chapter, 18, question 2, *Visitandi*, and chapter *Semel*. This is also contained in the statutes of the chapter in this form: Then one of the visitors shall give to those being visited an exhortatory address on discipline, on fraternal charity, on peace, on spiritual progress, on perseverance, and on the other virtues, according to the requirements of the persons or of the time.

How necessary depositions are to be made. Chapter 20.

When the exhortation has been completed, the visitor shall then instruct those being visited how and in what manner they ought to conduct themselves in their depositions; and he shall say in order as follows:

Beloved brothers, although you who have often been visited are not ignorant of the form of the visitation itself, nevertheless, for the sake of those who have more rarely been present at a visitation, I shall briefly set forth the method and form in which you ought to conduct yourselves in your depositions. First, we exhort you, all and singly, that, just as visitation was established for this purpose, that vices may be rooted out and virtues introduced, so each person should direct his intention in making proclamations to its proper end. Likewise, each person should make deposition out of charity, if he has seen anything that necessarily must be corrected, and not out of hatred, which God forbid, nor out of aversion, for this would be a grave sin. Likewise, each person should strive to observe due and honorable measure in depositions of this kind. Likewise, no one should bring up again matters that have worthily

On whether an oath should or should not be required from those being visited. Chapter 21.

But it is rightly asked whether those being visited should be bound by oath to speak and to answer the truth concerning the questions put to them. On this point it should be known that, as I have found in the old statutes, including those of other provinces of the order, where it seemed necessary an oath was required from those being visited. This still seems to me quite reasonable, especially if the monks have conspired, or made an agreement, or maliciously concealed the truth. And I judge that this statute, which I approve, should be strengthened.

Nicholas of Cusa, cardinal legate, writes among other things as follows to the visitors of the province of Mainz and the diocese of Bamberg: But because among many people words of exhortation have little effect unless a coercive penal censure is also added, therefore in the monasteries or places that you visit at that time, after the act of salutary exhortation, and after receiving from all a bodily oath to speak the truth in their examination, you shall proceed according to the tenor of the commission delivered to you in other letters.

Likewise, I have found the contrary in the statutes of the chapter. For in the sixth chapter, held at Erfurt, the statute is read as follows: It also pleased the fathers that, without requiring an oath, after the word of God has first been set forth, the visitors themselves should complete their act.
