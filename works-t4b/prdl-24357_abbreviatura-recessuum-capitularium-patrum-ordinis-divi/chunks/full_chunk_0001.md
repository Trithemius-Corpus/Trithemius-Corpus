Folio II

The Abridgement of the chapter recesses of the fathers of the Order of Saint Benedict throughout the province of Mainz and the diocese of Bamberg, held from the time of the Council of Constance down to the year of the Lord’s incarnation 1493, together with the manner of visiting monasteries of both sexes, and with a summary of the privileges of the aforesaid province, diocese, and order, begins happily.

The preface on the visitations of monks begins. Chapter 1.

“You shall visit your brothers, if they are acting rightly.” 1 Kings 17. In these words it is briefly indicated what sort of visitation of monasteries ought to be made. For the visitation itself ought to be charitable, not hateful: 10, question 3, Cauendum. And this is marked in the phrase “your brothers.” For the visitor, if he is to produce the worthy fruit of his visitation, must show himself brotherly and charitable toward those who are to be visited, and must know that he is the brother of those to be visited, not their lord; he should hate vices but love the brothers: distinction 86, Odio; 23, question 4, Duo; and in the Rule of the holy father Benedict, chapter 2, Qualis debeat esse abbas. For if the visitation is to be fruitful, it must be charitable, since charity is the proper gift of Catholic unity and peace: 1, question 1, Multae autem. And where there is no charity, there can be no justice: 24, question 1, Ubi. Therefore visitation must be made from charity, without hatred and cruelty: 10, question 3, Cauendum.

Second, visitation ought to be an examining visitation, namely concerning the life and morals of those to be visited: 10, question 1, Episcopum; Extra, De statu monachorum, Ea quae. And this is indicated in the word “you shall visit,” that is, “you shall examine.”

Third, visitation should be discreetly deliberative; and this is indicated when it says, “if they are acting rightly.” But these matters will be discussed in their proper places at a suitable time.

Therefore let visitation be such as was instituted by the holy fathers, and it will bring much fruit. For if it is properly carried out, it is the reform of the order,

the preservation of discipline, the restoration of peace and concord, and the completion of all honor and beauty. It raises up what has collapsed, gathers what has been scattered, reforms what has been dissolved, restores what has been broken, brings back what wanders, puts together what has been torn apart, and duly preserves the things that are holy and just. It reforms unity and peace between head and members, abbot and brothers, and reconciles inferiors to superiors. If visitation is carried out zealously, charitably, and discreetly, it compels subjects to show the obedience owed to chapters. Let it cease, and the discipline of religion will quickly expire. Finally, if it is neglected, the provincial chapter will soon suffer contempt from its own sons. For it is visitation that preserves the obedience of the good and humbles the insolence of the contumacious. It makes delinquents fear, and embraces those who are making progress in the bosom of charity. Few are those who obey out of love; servile fear restrains many from evils. And blessed is the necessity that compels one toward better things. For as a bristle introduces the thread, so initial fear introduces charity: De poenitentia, distinction 2, section Haec quae, before the end. And woe is there where fear has perished from the earth: Micah 7; 16, question 7, Et haec diximus, after the middle.

Since, therefore, visitation has been instituted for this purpose, that through it gains of souls may be sought: 10, question 3, Cauendum, all visitors must carefully consider what is required of them. And lest they depart from the statutes of the ancients, they should always strive to imitate their forms and examples. Therefore, for the sake of this matter, the present formula for visitation, drawn from the constitutions of the fathers, has been brought together in the following manner, so that new visitors, seeing before their eyes the proper order of their office, may avoid various and dangerous errors.

Who Has Authority to Appoint Visitors of Monks

The presidents of our provincial chapter are to appoint visitors of monks, as in Extra, De statu monachorum, chapter In singulis, where it says as follows:

In the same chapter, let religious and circumspect persons also be appointed, who, in our stead, should be diligent in visiting each abbey of that kingdom or province, not only of monks but also of nuns, according to the form prescribed to them, etc. And in the constitutions of Benedict XII, chapter 2, it says as follows: Let them also provide which abbots, and how many and how often, ought to visit cathedral churches, monastic monasteries, and other places of the same order or religion up to the provincial chapter itself. Long-standing custom also, since it is not contrary to the constitutions, is to be held as law: distinction 11, In his. From this it is clear that the authority of the presidents has the right to appoint visitors; yet if at any time it should seem necessary and useful, they may employ the counsel of the chapter in choosing visitors.

What Sort of Persons the Visitors Ought to Be. Chapter 3.

Moreover, in order that the visitors may be able to visit others fruitfully, let religious men of the same order be chosen, having zeal for God and for souls according to knowledge, as in Extra, De statu monachorum, In singulis. And in the constitution of Benedict XII, chapter 3, it says: Let the visitors be suitable, solicitous, and discreet in carrying out the office of visitation; and let them observe all the things that are known to have been established in the aforesaid constitutions of the same council and in the other constitutions of the said Honorius, our predecessor. Thus there.
