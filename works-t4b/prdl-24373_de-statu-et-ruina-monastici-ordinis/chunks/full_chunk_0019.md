Therefore, fathers, if we wish to keep our own houses unharmed by fire, let us meet the fire with water while it is still far away. For if the flame is allowed to pass as far as the neighbors, thought is taken for a remedy much too late. Hence the poet says: “Resist beginnings; medicine is prepared too late, when evils have grown strong through long delays.” Dangers, when dissembled, are strengthened by time; and what at the beginning easily admits a cure gradually becomes an incurable wound.

At the same time, take care that an error neglected now may not, with the passage of time, grow immeasurably, when no one is found who will stand firmly against it on behalf of the order. But if you say that opposition has always been made to the authors of dismemberment, and that they nevertheless have prevailed, I answer with that saying of Virgil: “because the ploughmen came too late.” After the confirmation of letters, a sentence once issued is not easily revoked. For if Pilate did not wish to change what he had once written, how will the supreme pontiff, the vicar of Christ, easily revoke what he has once decreed? A sentence is more easily prevented than revoked. Moreover, he who neglects his own right yields it to his adversary.

Everyone must keep watch in a cause that touches everyone, because what is snatched from one is judged to have been taken from all. Whenever a monastery is torn away from our order, our common inheritance is dissipated in part. If someone attempts to occupy your house or your field, you at once accuse him before the judge, at once seek an injunction, and until you win you do not cease from litigation against your adversary. Why, then, do we not all do in the public causes of the order what each person does in his private affairs? Surely what one man does will not be impossible for many, unless perhaps where there is a multitude there is also confusion, so that what is entrusted to several is preserved more negligently. For we gladly embrace what each person judges to be nearest to himself. But while, with the whole body drawn in from every side like a hedgehog, we turn our own concerns over within ourselves, we cast aside care for the order.

Therefore now, reverend fathers, with the goodwill that is fitting I admonish you, and with all the earnestness I can I ask you: come to the aid of your order, which is almost falling, and oppose these dismemberments as quickly as possible. Do not put money before such great necessities; do not love coins more than the order. Shameful is the keeping of riches by which the commonwealth is not helped in necessity. What will your insatiable desire for gold profit you if the desolation of your order, so holy, so venerable, so glorious, gains the upper hand? If our ancestors had not kept watch for the order with greater care than we do, the name of our religion would long since have failed in many monasteries. Let us therefore imitate the constancy of those whose inheritance we have received. If we love God, if we love the honor of the order, if we desire to preserve it, let us think of a remedy in time. Let us contribute a little to the preservation of the order, so that we may deserve to receive much; let us spend a part, so that we may be able to preserve the whole; let us offer cheap things, so that we may be able to acquire precious ones; let us give earthly things, so that we may possess heavenly ones. Let us spend money, and let us also spend prayer, since by the former man is appeased, by the latter God.

Whatever we have belongs to the right of our holy father Benedict; when, for the preservation of the order, we spend anything on him, we do not give him what is ours, but return to him what is his. Indeed, we are bound to him not only for the souls committed to us, but also obliged to render account for the property of the monastery. Moreover, a steward is never rendered safer concerning his account than when money is suitably spent in the causes of the Lord. But if money is spared in the Lord’s necessity, suspicion arises concerning the servant’s unfaithfulness.

At the beginning of this chapter we set down the title concerning the manner of resisting the dismemberments brought about by wicked men. Lest we seem to have passed over it untouched, let us explain it briefly. And although this treatment does not pertain to me, nevertheless, since I too am a fellow-nursling of your order and a lover of the chapter, the advice of a disciple will not, as I think, be burdensome to you. It is yours, fathers, to discern the true from the false; it is enough for me to have explained what I think.

The manner of opposing the presumptions of monks is easily learned if it is turned from example into practice. Look at the other orders, and from their diligence it will be recognized what you must do. They appoint procurators of their own in the city of Rome, who manfully resist the beginnings of evils and do not allow anything against their order to gain strength. And you, fathers, unless you do something similar, will in future suffer grave losses.
