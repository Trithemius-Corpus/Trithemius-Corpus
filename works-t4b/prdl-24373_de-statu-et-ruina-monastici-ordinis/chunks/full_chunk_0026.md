As witnesses to their private possession, contrary to statutes, contrary to laws, contrary to the Rule, they each inhabit separate houses; in them they practice not the studies of monks but those of clerics. They neither fear nor love God; they think nothing of the life to come, since they put the concerns of the soul after the pleasures of the flesh. They do not read the Rule of monks; they pay no attention to the statutes; they despise the decrees of the fathers. They spurn the vow of poverty; they do not know chastity; they make light of obedience. And would that, since they refuse to be chaste, they at least deigned to be continent. The dung of vices smokes all around, and what everyone knows, we alone, who are ordered to correct it, ignore.

Why should I use many words? So many, so great, so varied are the unlawful things they commit against the Rule of Saint Benedict that I would judge it easier to train a monk from the beginning than to be able to amend the life of these men. For nothing of monastic honor has remained inviolate among them; instead, everything lies neglected, everything spurned, everything despised and torn. Angers, quarrels, dissensions, envies, slanders, curses, oaths, insults, and things like these are not judged among them to be sins, because what is public has begun to be lawful. A place lies open to vices and crimes; virtue alone does not deserve admission. They laugh if anyone begins to speak before them about repentance, and, as Mantuanus says, they despise those above and Acheron alike: there is no fear of punishment, no fear of the Judge. You would see among them, as though in a spring of all vices, pride, greed, lust, gluttony, sloth, anger, hatred, and every pleasure of the flesh abounding; if they can seize it, they rejoice; if they cannot, nevertheless they eagerly desire it. They have rotted like beasts in their own dung, and, as if reason had been lost, they do not wish to be recalled to repentance.

O good God, to what great calamity has the order once so exalted been brought, an order which deservedly could be preferred to all orders. O holy father Benedict, you who once were so solicitous for your religion, why do you abandon us to the end? Why do you not look upon your inheritance? Why do you not aid your order, now already falling? Have you cast off care for it?

Or has your soul loathed your sons? Help, assist, bring aid, for never has your religion been surrounded by such grave evils. Behold, it is destroyed from without by lovers of the world; behold, it is torn apart within by perverse monks, so that no one can easily discern which is the greater crime: for monasteries to be transferred into the hands of secular clerics, or to remain longer under the hands of such wicked monks.

Alas, holy father, what shall I say? When you once established abbots, you commanded them to be solicitous about weak monks. Now shepherds have no care for the sheep, no diligence for the salvation of souls; all seek the things that are their own, not those of Jesus Christ. Behold, your sons, father, having zeal, desire to help, but they accomplish nothing, hindered by the rashness of the wicked.

Who, then, amid these things, is to help? The bishop? But he cares for his own affairs, seeks his own, despises ours. The prince? But he sells favor, rejects discipline, seeks temporal things. There is no one who grieves over the crushing of your order; no one who helps; no one who gives assistance. All who can restore it neglect it; others who seem to have zeal for discipline try to help, but cannot.

O abbots, abbots, who are the occasion of all evils in your monasteries, who, given over to vanities, do not at once correct monks who offend: how harsh a sentence of condemnation you will receive. To you, believe me, to you will be imputed the damnation of your monks; for, having been placed as their rulers, you neglected to form their morals. Woe to you. It would be better if you had never been abbots. You, occupied with greed and lust, neglect the work of the Lord; you despise the care of souls; you think nothing of the path of observance. You who ought to correct the vices of monks commit vices, and, lest your own be reproved, you dissemble those of others.

You see, most honored fathers, what the condition of your order is, what the abbots are, what the monks are: those who would correct the wicked are lacking, and among them only those who need correction are found. Why, then, do you delay? Why do you not think about the reform of such men? Why do you not restrain the wicked pursuits of monks? Their ill repute confounds us; their examples scandalize us, because the more numerous they are, the more they are believed by those who do not know them to be better in conduct.
