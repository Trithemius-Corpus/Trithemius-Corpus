Moreover, it is a moral principle for all that whoever lives by the support of justice must not dare to deny anyone what is just. The definition of justice is to render to each person what is owed to him by right. But we, established in the monastery, have nothing of our own; rather, in good faith we possess what belongs to another. What belongs to another, however, is judged liable to restitution. For the property that princes and faithful peoples have conferred on monasteries for the support of monks is remembered as having been handed over to Saint Benedict. If therefore you have dissipated his substance, if you have scorned to return to him what is his, you will be subject to a double condemnation, since you will be judged guilty of sacrilege for faithlessness, and criminal for faith denied.

Therefore render to each person what is his, and do not usurp for yourself what is not yours. Give to your lawgiver what you owe him, who conferred everything on you. Why do you deny a little to him from whom you have received much? Can you plead the poverty of your monastery, you who so shamefully consume so much? I am ashamed to say what many abbots are not at all ashamed to do: submerged in the pleasures of the flesh, not so much as mad with them, they preserve nothing of monastic honesty and omit nothing of worldly shamefulness. They think all things lawful for themselves, since they seek out not what is fitting, but what is pleasing.

Behold, one abhors monastic quiet and seeks comfort in the world through wandering excursions. Another, impatient of regular seclusion, proceeds pompously in public. A third, disparaging the discipline of honesty, sits all day drunk at feasts with frivolous persons. This one, scorning the meekness of the saints, entangles himself in lawsuits between disputants. Another sits at gaming tables; another, wholly absorbed, dances to the drum. What do ambitious abbots not presume? What pleasure do the intemperate leave untried? At the servants’ meal knees are bent. As he passes through churches, carpets are spread. As he goes forth in public, a huge crowd of servants follows him. You will find, I do not lie, even among those who boast in the name of reform an abbot whose ambition is so great that he does not wish to be called inferior to a bishop; who sits at table with such great display

that the abundance of vessels, the variety of food and drink, and the multitude of servants could suffice for any prince. Finally, if you should meet this man as he proceeds through a city, and should see him surrounded by so great a multitude of servants, you would think not that an abbot but that an archbishop was passing by. If they did not pretend to be poor but actually were poor, they would by no means overflow in so great a solemnity of vanity. For I am certain that this crowd follows plunder, not a man. Who would choose to find plunder among the poor?

O abbot, fugitive shepherd, ungrateful and faithless steward of the goods of your Lord, you who spend the substance of the monastery so uselessly, who live pleasurably and shamefully: who will help you in the final examination? Whom will you appoint as advocate when the account must be rendered? Behold, you consume the goods of Saint Benedict in pleasure; you support your relatives from them; you lavish gifts on shameful people; you distribute presents to your flatterers; you serve gluttony and drunkenness day and night. You eat the fattest things of the earth and drink the best wines; poor monks live in want; humble brothers eat the bread of sorrow; the sheep entrusted to you are fed on cheap things.

O vain shepherd, who neglects the Lord’s flock, who scorns the necessities of your brothers, remember to whom you are going to render account; recall how dreadful it is to fall into the hands of the living God, who does not regard a person’s status, who repays each one his reward according to his works. But hear my counsel and do penance, and redeem your sins with alms; rather, discharge them by paying what is owed, so that before the eternal judge you may deserve to find mercy. Put down your arrogance; cast off pomp; abandon vanity. Be abbot to your brothers according to the Rule; be a shepherd in solicitude, a father in charity. Feed them with the word; feed them by example; feed them with nourishment. If you omit one of these, you will be a useless shepherd. For without spiritual things, temporal things accomplish nothing for eternal salvation; without the aid of temporal things, spiritual things do not subsist long in this life.

If therefore you wish to be a good shepherd, do not deny that the care of both kinds of feeding, namely spiritual and bodily, has been laid upon you. I had said that your sins must be released by paying what is owed. What debt? That, plainly, by which you are judged bound to Saint Benedict. What is that? Everything that you have.
