For it pleases everyone, but it benefits only the humble when kept apart. Therefore the whole matter of celebrating a chapter consists in the appointment of those presiding, which requires men who are zealous, learned, and gentle. For if they are lax, if inexperienced, if cruel and impious, how then will they reform others to gentleness? If the man who sits as one about to punish crimes admits crimes himself, whom will he correct among those like himself? Therefore this fault must be referred to you, fathers, if you have chosen deformed and vicious abbots for the presidency. With the greatest solicitude, then, see to it that you appoint such men to preside over the chapter as an irreproachable life commends, the zeal of holy religion sharpens, and the training of saving knowledge tempers. For it is proper that the one who presides over others be a man upright in morals, mature in conduct, instructed in knowledge of the Scriptures, adorned with gentleness. Such a man, O fathers, is deservedly to be preferred by us, one whose life provides an example and whose teaching provides an increase of virtue. If the presidents of the chapter are such men, those subject to them will easily be brought back to obedience. For those will be suitable for presiding who can instruct the teachable by word, the untrained by example, and the contumacious by authority. In these ways, indeed, the chapter will be celebrated duly, and the souls of the subjects will also be edified.

The other things that make presidents suitable I am not permitted to describe on this occasion, since I believe none of you is ignorant what sort of men they ought to be. To you, therefore, O fathers, I turn again; you who have assembled in this place for the celebration of the chapter I address, I exhort, that you direct your intention to the restoration of the order, and carefully consider why the chapter was instituted. Do you wish to know why chapters were instituted? Seek out the decrees of the supreme pontiffs, and you will learn the cause of their institution. But lest either laziness or occupation impede the understanding of this matter, I shall briefly explain the cause of the establishment of the chapter. The principal cause of the chapter, then, is the preservation of the regular institution. For the Roman pontiffs and the presidents of general councils, seeing that our order could not long subsist in observance without a special remedy,

resolved to aid it through the provincial chapter, in which, by the diligence of the presidents, all would be instructed to live according to the Rule, and no one could deviate from the reform once begun. For in a chapter of abbots vices are to be corrected and exhortations to virtues are to be made, so that the bad may find what they should fear and the good may hear what will make them better. Therefore various things are read for edification, so that increases may always be present to good minds. There the abbot is instructed concerning his governance; there he is warned about the danger of pastoral care; there he is commanded what he should do, and, for the love of God, forbidden what he should avoid. In short, when we come together in this way for chapter, many useful things arise for us, which, if we seek the salvation of souls, are very necessary.

For, to pass over the rest, mutual acquaintance in charity is conceived there, and experience shows how much this contributes to love of regular observance. There, from acquaintance, fraternal charity is both preserved and increased; otherwise it would quickly grow lukewarm if it did not have the nourishment of mutual recognition. Here easy access to uniformity lies open, where, from the conference of the fathers, a mutual kindling is prepared in honorable conduct, and from their common life an example of holiness is provided. These are the camps of God, O fathers, and the bulwarks of our order, by which all the contrivances of our adversaries are reduced to nothing.

But one sincerity is not present in all, nor does equality of conduct appear among you. Whence could such great disparity of manners have arisen in one order, where the unity of a chapter is seen? Who has divided you from the Rule of Saint Benedict, O fathers with locks grown thick upon your heads? Why do you not look toward uniformity, you who celebrate a gathering of unity? If you love the holy order that you profess, why do you not love uniformity in it? What is the use to you of the shadow of a hairy head? What ornament do you think the crown of a curled scalp bears? Believe me, fathers: a face covered with hair is judged a disgrace in a monk, not an honor. O how beautiful this assembly would be if, as it ought, it came together in one discipline of regular observance. What shall I say? I praise the gathering, but I rebuke the deformity in its members.
