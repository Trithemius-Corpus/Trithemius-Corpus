Do you suppose that the supreme pontiff would wish to involve his conscience in your sins? Read the bull attentively. Consider its words. Note the intention of the one dispensing, and you will find that even after the dispensation you are still a monk before God and the Church. For I myself have both seen and read the bull concerning this transfer. In it the pope consents that the monks may have prebends and benefices, and nevertheless commands that they render to Saint Benedict what they have vowed. He grants them the habit of a canonicate, but by no means removes the monastic form. See how secure you are, you who overstep the limits of the indult, who invent falsehoods in place of truths, who lie to the vicar of Christ, who no less extort bulls than obtain them.

In matters that belong to positive law, dispensation makes lawful what otherwise was not lawful, if a reasonable cause requires it. But you are commanded by divine law to render a vow made to God; and from this the pope never intends to absolve you without necessity. But tell me, I ask, what is the cause of your transfer? Perhaps you will answer what all those so transferred are accustomed to answer: There are many causes that necessarily drove me to this. What, then, are they? The poverty of the monastery; the attacks of ravagers; the exactions of princes; the bad government of abbots. These are the causes of our transfer. But a net woven from spiders’ blood is easily broken if any weight is placed upon it.

You apostates, however, recount falsehoods as truths, and under the fiction of poverty you deceive the vicar of Christ, indeed, even yourselves. Would that your poverty were real, as you allege; would that you had less earthly substance, as you mendaciously pretend. Why do you allege poverty, you who possess very great riches? Who does not know that the condition of the monastery of Saint Alban in temporal goods was most abundant before the transfer? What shall I say of Ellwangen, once a distinguished monastery, whose riches could have sufficed for a prince? Surely the monastery of Saint Burkhard ought not to have been transferred on account of poverty, since even to the present day it surpasses all the monasteries of that province in riches? What shall I say of Seltz, what of Klingenmünster, monasteries very abundant in relation to a moderate standard? Could poverty truly have been alleged in their transfer? C iij

O betrayers of your order and transgressors of your vow, do you believe that lying words can remain hidden? Your deceits are not concealed from us, nor are we ignorant of the riches that you deny. If you were poor in the way you pretend, some of you could not spend two thousand florins, others four thousand, and some even more, for your transfer. Why, then, do you allege poverty, when you are not poor? Why do you say that you have nothing from which you can live as monks, when you can spend such great sums of money in order to become canons?

Finally, if the poverty of the monasteries oppresses you, why do you not undertake reform? Why do you not bring private possessions, contrary to the Rule, back to common use? For as long as each man loves his own little provision, the condition of the community falls. But if you lived without property according to the Rule you have professed, with the Lord providing, you would abound in all riches. But you, neglecting spiritual things, devote your labor to the pleasure of the flesh; for that reason you have come down to lack of temporal goods. I would say this against those who, through contempt of spiritual goods, also suffer want of temporal goods.

But those who transferred themselves from the order, if they had been poor, would have remained in it. For many expenses, costs, and perquisites are required, which the poor could never have paid. Therefore those who take poverty as the cause of their transfer are lying. And as for the fact that they allege wars, battles, and devastations by enemies in defense of their dismemberment, every prudent man knows that this is frivolous, empty, and insufficient. For all the things they recount can be hostile no less to canons than to monks.

And because they call the government of abbots a cause, this is rightly ascribed to the fault of those whose election sets a bad rector over the monastery. Nor unjustly: as the monks are, so is the monks’ abbot. For bad monks are judged unworthy of a good abbot. Finally, if the abbot is bad, how does a good provost come forth? Other remedies should have been sought by you from experienced men, by which you might have advanced in the order without complaint. But he seeks an occasion who wishes to depart from a friend; he invents countless causes who desires to dismember himself from the order.
