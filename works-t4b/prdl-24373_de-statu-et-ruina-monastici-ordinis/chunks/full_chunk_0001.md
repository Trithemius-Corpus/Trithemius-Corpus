To the venerable and most beloved father, lord Blasius, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau: brother Johannes, called abbot in Sponheim, sends greeting and charity.

Although your request and that of your brothers, dearest father, far exceeds the powers of my talent, nevertheless I have preferred to undertake the arduous burden rather than fail, as far as I can, to satisfy your charity. For the love of God, zeal for the order, and fervor for discipline make you worthy that I should deny you nothing when you ask for the honor of God. You asked me, when I was present, by words; when I was absent, by frequent letters; and in a certain way you compelled me by the authority of your presidency to write something to you about the calamity of our order, so that you might summon the fathers, to be assembled with you in chapter, to compassion for it.

The request is holy, but the difficulty of obeying is great. I know that I shall be torn by the teeth of the envious, who will judge me either presumptuous or excessive. But with my ears stopped I shall pass by them as if deaf, provided that this work, such as it is, may please holy and learned men. I do not recall having written anything in it that rivals can tear apart, but rather true things, manifest things, and things that lie open to the eyes of all. If they do not wish these things to be written, let them grieve that they were done as well. In this work only elegance of speech is lacking, and for this I do not fear being blamed among religious men. What then? Is truth to be despised because it is not clothed in the ornament of eloquence? Away with that. Plain and true speech is better than ornate and false speech. But these matters are nothing to the point. I therefore submit this little work to your examination, so that, if you judge it worthy, it may be read to the brothers; but if unworthy, it may flee the eyes of the lords. Farewell. In the year of the Lord 1493, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of May.

Here begins the deeply sorrowful book of lord Johannes, abbot of Sponheim, on the ruin of the order of our holy father Benedict.

When I call back to mind the former beauty of our order, I grieve vehemently over the deformity which it now suffers, so that I would rather shed tears than form a mournful discourse. For who would not weep over its ruin, if he weighs its ancient glory? O, if only we would at some time recall to memory the once golden ages of this most sacred order, when, like a river of paradise in doctrine and holiness, it watered the whole Church throughout the world, we would easily perceive that its present condition is nothing. Indeed our observance, which we call regular, when compared with the institutions of the ancients, is found not only lesser, but in many respects more lax. How the gold has grown dim; how the finest color has changed! The splendor of extraordinary holiness, once gleaming, has been darkened by desire for earthly things; the standard of honor has yielded to the enticements of pleasures. Therefore let my eyes bring forth tears by day and by night, and let them not be silent, because the religion of my people has been crushed with a great crushing, with a very grievous wound. If I go out to the fields, behold the idle and vain; if I enter the monasteries, behold those wasted by pleasures. For I find many professors of the rule of father Benedict; I discern few walking according to his tradition. “You have multiplied the nation,” says the prophet to the Lord, “you have not increased the joy,” because, since the holy exercises of monks fail in many, corrupt hearts do not admit the true joy that is born of virtue. If only we would awaken, even late at last, after the destruction of so many souls, which, despising the yoke of regular observance, have descended into the abyss of damnation. If only we feared the sentence of Benedict our lawgiver, who so often strikes the madness of a hard abbot with the account that must be rendered to God.

I complain of your spiritual ruin.

Now therefore my speech must be directed to you, most reverend fathers, who celebrate the chapters of the order by apostolic authority; who receive the power to compel the rebellious to discipline; who have zeal for our order according to knowledge. And so I address you; to you I shall strive, according to my strength, to urge that you aid the order, now nearly collapsing, and I exhort you with all earnestness to do this. Consider, fathers, the miserable state of our religion, and aid its calamity as much as you can, if indeed you desire to endure to the end. You are not unaware of the wickedness of many who, so that they may be able to escape the discipline of the order, labor to tear themselves away from it. Their rashness must be resisted firmly, lest the unpunished malice of a few provide many with boldness for sinning. And the wickedness of a few, when deferred, always grows. Although the public body ought not to be corrupted by the evil of one person, nevertheless the ignorant crowd blames everyone because of the offense of a few. Therefore superiors who do not punish the wicked, who by their deformity cloud the reputation of the good, strengthen them. And so it pertains to you, fathers, to correct deformed monks as far as you can; it is the duty of your office to preserve the order in its vigor. And because a fool is not corrected by speech, what our hierophant set down almost monastically against the incorrigible should be applied generally to these men. Rise up now, fathers, for it is time; arm yourselves with zeal for regular discipline; I beg you, defend the inheritance of holy father Benedict; drive out the proud; correct the rebellious; rebuke the hard. Fight bravely for the preservation of our order. Arm yourselves with the armor of God and love of the order, intending to fight not so much by your own labors as by the help of Christ. His grace will provide you aid, if with purity of heart you pour out devoted prayers to him. Why then do you dissemble? Why do you not defend the portion of Saint Benedict? How long will the efforts of the wicked prevail against the holy order? Truly, fathers, truly the great necessity of the order demands a swift remedy. Look closely at the conduct of irregular abbots and monks, and weigh how wickedly they kick against the discipline of the order.
