I love, venerate, and guard it with all the diligence I can. But I do not know whom you will have as patron, since you cast aside the order, offend all the saints, and publicly lie to God. For the Rule shows that anyone who, after profession of the order, lives otherwise than he promised is to be condemned as one who mocks almighty God. For it says: Therefore, if he should act otherwise, let him know that he will be condemned by him whom he mocks.

Hear this, sons of Belial; pay attention, deserters of the order, and if there is any sense in you, shudder. “Let the monk,” it says, “know that he will be condemned by him whom he mocks.” What greater mockery is there, what crime more wicked, than to deny the faith promised to God, to set aside the vow, to lie to him, and to withdraw service from him? I wonder, and indeed very greatly, whether such criminal apostates can ever, even for an hour, have true gladness amid the confusion of conscience and the remorse of synderesis. But, to confess simply what I think, I cannot be induced by anyone’s persuasions to believe that they will henceforth live at any time without the gravest torment of their conscience.

We have learned by probable experience that the monks of whom we are speaking, after the transfer of their order, become almost mad, prone to every shameful work, desperate, without devotion, without affection, without piety, without conscience, without honor, senseless, disturbed, foolish, and obstinate, so that they care nothing for God, for his kingdom, for purgatory, for hell, for death, for virtue, or for conversion; they attend to nothing and wish to hear nothing. And in them is fulfilled that saying of the wise man: “When the impious man has come into the depth of evils, he despises.” And, with a troubled conscience, he always presumes that saying upon himself.

Hence such apostates, as if freed from a long prison, boldly cast themselves into every vice, and compensate for the shortness of time by the magnitude of their crimes. They are offended if anyone wishes to speak in their presence about the love of God, the study of the Scriptures, or the honorableness of life. Nor is it surprising if they cannot hear God’s words, since after so unspeakable an apostasy they exist as unbelievers. They hate the one who rebukes at the gate; they do not admit words of salvation; they despise the admonition of penitence. Therefore the wise man says: “Consider the works of God, because no one can correct the one whom he has despised.” If God could ever despise any men,

these, or men like them, are foremost: those who, after their vow, become unfaithful to him. Therefore, being for this reason despised, they rarely flee to penitence. So let them know that they are condemned by him whom they mock. For they are guilty of a double sacrilege, because they unjustly take themselves away from God and their monasteries from Saint Benedict. And whoever has furnished occasion, favor, counsel, or help for these dismemberments has done irrecoverable harm to the order and to Saint Benedict. For the rule of law says: “He who gives occasion for damage is seen to have caused the damage.” Therefore such men are liable to the holy lawgiver of monks and bound to restitution. Let them see how, at the last judgment, they will answer God and his saints, whose temporal goods they unjustly usurp for themselves or for others.

Woe to those who unjustly take what belongs to others, things which they can never restore either in this life or in the life to come. Woe also to you, apostate and renegade monks, who confound right, despise equity, and violate faith and covenant. Woe also to you abbots, who do not correct the evil pursuits of your monks, who consent to their apostasy, and who yourselves apostatize from the order along with them.

O abbot, shepherd and idol, you who make wolves from sheep and secular men from monks, what answer will you give to the highest shepherd when you are compelled to render an account of your stewardship? What will you say to Saint Benedict when he demands his sheep back from you? He will require monks from you, not canons. What then will you answer him? Are canons monks?

When that day comes, to be dreaded by the guilty among mortals, on which the just Judge will repay each person according to his works, Benedict, the lawgiver of monks, do not doubt it, will accuse you gravely. Against you he will produce as witnesses all true monks of his order, who with one voice will cry out to the Judge: “Just Judge, Jesus Christ, who attend to all that is ours, behold the madness, crime, sacrilege, and cruelty of this apostate, who destroyed the holy order, called monks back to the world, broke faith, and with unheard-of presumption violated the vow made to you and to us.”

Wretched man, what will you do, so gravely accused? Where will you flee from the face of the Lord’s fury? To whom will you flee? There will be no place for flight, but you will stand before the tribunal of Christ to receive a harsh sentence, unless by penitence you first make amends for these things.
