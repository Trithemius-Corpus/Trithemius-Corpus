There is no share for the impious with the sons of God. See, you squander the inheritance of the Lord; you enrich strangers from the goods of the holy father Benedict; you deny your promise; and, so that you may sin without restraint, you heap falsehoods upon truths. If you are a good monk, do not abandon your purpose; do not turn away from the seclusion of the monastery. But if you are a bad monk, you will never be a good canon. For one who is not good is bad; and one who is bad is not as he ought to be, and therefore is unjust. The unjust have no share in the kingdom of God. And it matters little where you are, so long as, being bad everywhere, you are tormented everywhere in misery. Unless perhaps you wish to be liable to a double condemnation: that, while you are a bad monk and an unrighteous canon, guilty and false in both places, you should claim the reward of both.

Do not put before me the dispensation of the Roman pontiff; unless God approves it, it will by no means excuse you. Not everything that is done on earth through the supreme pontiff is pleasing to God. Now if you cry out and are angry with me, as though I were judging the deeds of the vicar of Christ, I answer: I truly embrace, honor, and venerate the supreme pontiff as the vicar of Christ, and I in no way censure his deeds; but I know that he is a man, and that as a man he can both be deceived and deceive. No wonder: amid so great a care for all the churches, amid so great an occupation with affairs, when false things are reported as true, a mind distracted in many directions is easily deceived. But you, apostate from the holy order, who think yourself safe because of the bull of dispensation, see that after death, as the common saying goes, you do not meet an illiterate demon, who does not know letters, who does not defer to an appeal, who despises the pleas for your immunity. Believe me: there is no cleverness that can deceive God.

O wretched, unhappy, senseless apostate, what will you answer to Saint Benedict concerning the profession that you spurn? What will you answer to almighty God concerning the vow that you despise? Whom will you be able to appoint as advocate for your case in that fearful judgment, where nothing can be hidden? There all things, all things, will be naked and open; there no place for saving repentance is any longer left; there the opportunity for excuse is denied to all. What will that dispensation, rashly presumed here, profit you there, when it will be permitted neither to prove it by testimony nor to defend it by law? Then you will see, O perverse monk; then you will understand what strength a license fraudulently obtained contains in itself,

when you have felt the vengeance of the eternal judge in punishment. Why do you allege dispensation to me, when you proclaim a license to sin? Do you think that by the authority of the supreme pontiff you can escape the wrath of God? I dare say it: the pope's dispensation will profit you nothing if it has been obtained by fraud, by deceit, by lying, or unjustly. It is not good, it is not just, what is acquired by evil arts. For a dispensation to be just, four things are required, without which a dispensation will by no means save you. What are they? Necessity, suitability, usefulness, and propriety. But you, perjured monk, who are led by will alone, who despise God and the order, invent necessity, display false suitability, lie about usefulness, and compose propriety by fraud. Let the necessity be evident and manifest, the suitability true, the usefulness not private but public, the propriety not fictive but proper; otherwise the dispensation will be unjust.

There is certainly nothing for which I am angry at the supreme pontiff in this dispensation; I censure nothing in his deeds, since I know well that he has been circumvented by the false reports of monks. You are the guilty ones, you the deceitful, you who must be marked with iniquity, O perverse monks, wicked apostates, who by lying, deceit, and fraud seek what you despair of obtaining by just prayers. What necessity drives you to desert the order and pass into the state of secular life? What suitability persuades you to break what, once promised to God, no one is permitted to revoke? What usefulness is there in laying aside the monastic habit, deserting the order, denying one's purpose, transferring the monastery, and not only breaking but utterly casting away the vow made to God before all the saints? Or what propriety is there for a monk dedicated to God to return to the world, break faith, commit perjury, and perpetrate sacrilege? O transgressors and impious men, how will you ever be able to excuse so unspeakable a presumption? None of these four accords with your transfer; none of them confirms your dispensation. For what necessity could have driven you to this rashness? What cause of this dismemberment could you allege before the vicar of Christ? The poverty and ruin of monasteries. But you could quickly have restored this, if you had lived according to the Rule of Saint Benedict.
