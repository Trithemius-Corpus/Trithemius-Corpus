There will be no defender there of a depraved action, no advocate of iniquity; for where all things will be laid bare, there each person’s own accusing conscience will be enough for condemnation. There the army of the saints will stand against those who have wickedly profaned the dwellings of monks, who have impiously destroyed the order of the holy fathers.

But the authors of these things, which I seem to myself justly to have called iniquitous, try to excuse their deed by saying: “The corruption of monks, driving monasteries to extreme poverty, cannot be corrected and amended unless it is transferred into another state,” on account of many reasons which they devise for themselves, and which, God granting, we shall show in what follows to be empty. Finally, they say: “To correct bad monks by transfer not only is free from sin, but is also a great part of justice and piety.” But where can these things be distinguished? How will you prove by what has been said that they are true? To convert bad monks to goodness is praiseworthy and holy; but to make them canons is not to amend monks, but to have corrupted them. A bad monk will never be a good canon. A good monk, moreover, will not cast off the order that is his, but will cherish, cultivate, and love it.

I do not now wish to contend with words; I do not wish to be too severe or vehement; I do not wish to depict the effect of this transfer, which I do not doubt will gradually come to light. If you wanted to make bad monks into canons, why did you not put good monks back in their place? A change that is presumed to be made for the worse is not without transgression of a vow. Woe to you, deserters, who, so that satisfaction may be given to your pleasures, despise the order, deny the vow, and confound the rule of equity. We shall all stand before the tribunal of Christ, as the Apostle says, so that each one may receive according to what he has done in his body. What will the destroyers of monasteries receive there, who do not defer to the honor of the Almighty, who violate an agreement, who nullify the wills of the saints, who despise the decrees of law? If it is good to found monasteries, it is evil, let no one doubt, to destroy them. A contrary action will receive only a contrary reward. Finally, if no one dares to infringe the last will of a dead man in legacies made to any human being whatever, why do lovers of the world presume to usurp for themselves what has been offered to God and the saints?

If one who violates a man’s testament is guilty of a crime, what punishment does he deserve who wickedly changes to alien uses the foundations of monasteries made by bishops and princes with their approval? For indeed kings, princes, prelates, and the rest of the faithful who founded monasteries for the honor of God are proved to have wished this above all: that the foundations of this institution should obtain an inviolable effect. If they had wished to have canons in the monasteries, they would not have placed monks there.

But the blind ambition of men has gained such strength that it thinks of nothing less than future things, and loves nothing more than perishable things. O wretched condition of human frailty, O damnable forgetfulness ever of God’s commandments. Laws have consented to sins, and what is public has begun to be permitted. Crimes blaze everywhere, and far and wide, in a manifold kind of sinning, wicked ingenuity works harmful deceits and frauds through corrupt minds. This man confounds an order; that one utterly destroys it. This man withdraws the will of the saints; that one writes false allegations of dismemberment. Here monks are barred from monasteries; there the goods of one order are given to another. The enemy incites the restless; the slanderer attacks; the false witness defames. Already to be innocent among the guilty is a crime. What love of honesty, what integrity could there be in minds where the purpose of the order is abandoned, where the vow made to God is revoked? What shame about things remains there, what virtue can possibly stand firm, where divine right is violated, where there is no fear of laws, no dread of the judge? What can be called more inhuman, what more cruel, than to wish to usurp divine right for oneself, to confound what is lawful, to break a vow, to withdraw the worship owed to God?

O that day, fearful and dreadful to all mortals, when the wrath of God, long tolerating these evils of monks, will be avenged; when apostates will feel their due punishment, because as adversaries of the holy order they will pay the vengeance for their rashness. What then will it profit you to have denied the vow made to God, to have extinguished the sacred order, to have enjoyed a brief and shameful pleasure in the flesh? When the Lord begins to count his worshipers, to reward true monks with the promised prizes, to separate the good from the evil, to divide the truthful from the false, what place will you receive, what seat will you have?
