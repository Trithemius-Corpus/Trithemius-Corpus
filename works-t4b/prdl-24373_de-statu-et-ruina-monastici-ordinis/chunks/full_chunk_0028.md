They willingly admit what, once they have admitted it, they denounce as a crime. This is a boldness fit for the abusive, and an impudence suited to the shameless. Often the greedy man rebukes the avaricious, the luxurious man the impure, the proud man the haughty, the criminal man the base. What do you think, fathers? Can such deceivers be found even among the visitors themselves? I do not dare say it; yet I have experience of it. Many say many things; few show in deeds what they say. They cannot reform themselves and their own, and yet they strive to force others into observance. They say great things and do very little. Foolish presumption, which neglects its own affairs and rushes off to those of others. He who is not sufficient for himself: for whom will he have abundance? He who cannot bring himself and his own to amendment of life: when will he be able to amend others? I have said this against those who are such men, who condemn in others what they scorn to amend in themselves.

As for zealous, good, and suitable visitors, of whom I have known several, I would not wish anything to have been said to their injury. But you, most excellent fathers, who must choose visitors, take care to appoint better men to that office, if you desire to bring monks to regular observance. For the sum of regular discipline lies with the visitors: when it is continued, it grows; when it is neglected, it fails. Indeed, if visitors diligently fulfill their office, they will preserve reformed monasteries in regular observance and will easily bring deformed ones back to a better state. But if they are negligent, even what others have well begun will quickly be destroyed. For nothing so confounds unity, nothing so disturbs peace, nothing so injures charity, as the ignorance of visitors, when it grows heated not by reason but by undiscerning will. Experience has very often taught us what great disturbances, dissensions, anger, quarrels, slanders, and hatreds unskilled visitors have stirred up in congregations of monks, men for whom everything consists in authority, and nothing in knowledge and charity. But I have said enough about this, lest I seem to accuse those whom, because of the dignity of their office, we are commanded to treat with honor.

How Dangerous the Governance of Souls Is. Chapter 11.

If we considered how dangerous a thing the governance of souls is, how heavy the burden of pastoral care, how subject it is to strict examination, it would be a wonder if our heart ever presumed upon security in this life. For Scripture says that the harshest judgment will be made against those who are in charge. For, as blessed Benedict declares in the Rule, the abbot will have to render an account to God for the souls of all the monks committed to him. Therefore he says: Let the abbot who has been ordained always consider what burden he has taken up, and to whom he will render an account of his stewardship. It is certainly a heavy and dangerous burden to render account for the souls of many, when each person can scarcely answer for himself.

O what will abbots devoted to earthly things answer to the supreme Shepherd, they who destroy the souls of their brothers by bad example and feed them neither by word nor by deed? What shall I say about certain abbots of our order, who neither read the Rule of Saint Benedict which they profess, nor, if they do read it, understand it? I would be lying if I said I did not know abbots established under our chapter who are wrapped in such darkness of ignorance that they do not understand even the least sentence from the Rule. They neither know nor love the divine Scriptures; submerged in the vanities of the world, they vanish away; they serve the desires of the flesh, not of the spirit. According to the command of our lawgiver, the abbot must be learned in divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth things new and old; chaste, sober, merciful, and the rest.

What will these men say? What will they teach? What exhortation will they give, when they understand nothing of the holy Scriptures and nothing of regular discipline? O good God, to what misery our order has come, which once was full of the most learned and most holy men. Where now is the love of regular observance? Where is the study of the Scriptures? Where do we find the traces of honorable conduct? Our abbots, with few exceptions, are either given over to avarice or drawn away by the vanities of the world; they think of nothing less than knowledge of the Scriptures, although [unclear].
