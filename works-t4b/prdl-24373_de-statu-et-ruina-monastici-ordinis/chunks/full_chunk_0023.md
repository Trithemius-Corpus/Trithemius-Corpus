These things must be brought against you, O fathers, who neglect to subdue rebels at the beginning. Consider what this dissimulation will at last bring forth; and if you love your order, think as quickly as possible about a remedy. A body is weak and very near dissolution when it is deprived of the support of its own members. Behold, out of one hundred twenty-seven abbots, how few you see in the present chapter, though by statute all ought to have been present. Where are the rest? Where are the proctors of all the absent? Few have contributed to the necessity of the order; fewer observe the statutes of the order; very few have come to the chapter. Where are the letters of excuse from those who did not send proctors? Where are the double expenses of the rebels? Which of the absent and rebellious has ever, in many years, been compelled to pay the penalty of double amount? What good are statutes to us? What do laws help? What do the privileges of the supreme pontiffs help, if in time of necessity they are not put into practice? Behold, Pope Benedict XII provided for us in all necessary things when he promulgated the restraints of censure to be imposed against the obstinate and rebellious. Why, then, do we delay? Why do we not put into practice against the disobedient the remedies granted to us?

Truly, fathers, this dissimulation of ours, which we practice, is not praiseworthy. For the rebellion of a few, if overlooked, will become an occasion for many to resist. But if a few are compelled to obedience by discipline, the boldness of the multitude is restrained by fear. For a kingdom divided against itself will be desolated, and every religious body at variance with itself is gradually dissipated. There is no fair hope for the order where confusion makes a tumult against unity. For in every commonwealth the first road to desolation is the disobedience of subjects. Therefore, venerable fathers, if you desire to continue the provincial chapter; if you wish to resist the dismemberments of the monks; if, finally, you desire to defend your order from the hands of enemies, take care first of all to recall the obstinate to obedience, lest what you acquire outwardly by effort you lose inwardly through division among subjects. This indeed must first be attempted with the gentlest exhortations; but if they scorn these with hardened neck, they must be pursued with harsher rebukes.

But if they care for neither gentle words nor severe ones, then ecclesiastical censure is promulgated against them. For fools are not amended by exhortations, but become proud. Yet in these matters measure and necessary discretion must be used, without which no rebellion is ever softened. For it is better to have all weak things than none at all. But who is so capable as to preserve discretion everywhere? It is difficult to apply measure to all deeds. Yet one who wishes to cure sick souls must be both severe and kind, and tempered as though by a certain mean between the two, so that severity is not excessive nor kindness slack. For the one breaks; the other loosens. The fairest manner of restraining rebels and the obstinate is that which the long experience of elders commends.

On Celebrating the Provincial Chapter. Chapter VIII.

When we come together as one, most excellent fathers, let us carefully investigate what the celebration of the provincial chapter requires. For the greatest strength of our stability is placed in it, if it is celebrated properly. It is the one stronghold of our order; without it our whole religious life would be thrown into confusion, as appears among those who for many years have held no chapters. For the chapter is the preservation of the order, the increase of regular discipline, the uniting of unity. In it, indeed, integrity is given to morals; purity is increased in minds; the quality of regular life is renewed. But what is required for it to be celebrated properly? Hear what its sincere celebration demands. It requires zeal; it requires knowledge; it requires piety. Without these three the celebration of the chapter has no value. For if zeal is lacking, who will rebuke offenders? If knowledge is lacking, what fruit will excessive zeal bear? Again, if piety is lacking, zeal does not sharpen, knowledge does not direct, and piety produces no fruit. Without zeal, knowledge remains cold; without piety, fruitless. Likewise, zeal without knowledge becomes excessive and unprofitable.
