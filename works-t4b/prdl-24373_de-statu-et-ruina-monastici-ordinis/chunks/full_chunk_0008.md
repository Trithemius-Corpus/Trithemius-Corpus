But I should like you to remember, fathers, two verses of Horace in which he says: “There is a measure in things; there are, finally, fixed limits, beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.” We have many adversaries, and of different kinds. We must not fight against all in one way, but in different ways, in varied ways, and with discernment. We are monks, professors of humility; our struggle must be begun with prayers, entreaties, and supplications. High Jupiter is not to be provoked with savagery; judgment is not to be brought rashly against the gods. Divine powers are appeased by prayers, not by threats.

But enough of this. For I believe, fathers, that you understand what I mean. Nor are these matters to be explained indiscriminately to everyone, but only to those who have zeal for the order and the power to carry it out.

Against those who transfer themselves from the order, or have already long since transferred themselves. Chapter iii.

Whether I am justly moved enough against those who try to tear themselves away from our order, I do not quite know; and even if I knew, I would scarcely dare to say, since the patience of the Almighty tolerates them so long and scourges them so slowly. But when I look upon the end of those who have already long since torn themselves away from the order, I consider this long-suffering of God’s patience to have been avenged with a sufficiently severe punishment. For, as we have learned from the report of those who knew many of them, few of those who cast off the monk died a good death. Rather, called suddenly from this life without signs of penitence and without the sacraments of the Church, they gave manifest proof of their unhappiness.

What then shall we say to these things? Shall we rejoice in the perdition of the dying? Far be it. The Lord is the one who tolerates sinners here for a long time so that they may be converted; he is also the one who condemns the unconverted more harshly. But we fear his sentence with the prophet, saying: You are just, Lord, and your judgment is right. And the just man, whatever death may overtake him, will be in refreshment. But concerning the impious the prophet says: The death of sinners is most evil. We recall the wicked men’s destruction for this reason: so that at least by fear we may call the rest back from the evil they have begun.

But how will they receive our words, they who mock both those above and those below? Unless deeds are added to words, exhortations accomplish nothing among malicious minds. Still the stiff-necked people murmurs in the desert; still the impious race blasphemes its lawgiver; still the shameless nation deliberates returning to Egypt. They fear dying in solitude, when they should have feared this alone: dying outside solitude.

O hard mind of men, O destructive liberty of monks, how far does your cruelty rage, which does not know how to spare even those whom it has as authors and friends? He who is worthless to himself, to whom will he be good? O unheard-of zeal of monks; O monstrous crime: to mock God, to deny what was promised to him, to break faith, to withdraw the service that is owed. What is more cruel than this impiety, what more wicked than this sacrilege?

O perverse monk, O wicked apostate, O most criminal perjurer, how long will you set yourself against the kindness of God? How long will you mock the mercy of the Almighty? You are a monk; you will also be judged as a monk, even if you have put off the skin along with the habit. Why do you depart from the holy order? Why do you lie to God? Believe me: the Almighty will not give his honor to another; he will not remit to you forever what he once solemnly accepted as offered. Before you promised God, you were free and of your own judgment; but after you promised, you began to belong to God. Now it is not permitted to you to give up what belongs by right to God. Your voice bound you to God: either you will be saved as a monk, or otherwise you will not be saved.

Behold, before your eyes are the way of life and the way of death; choose whichever you wish. If you keep faith with God, if you fulfill your promise, you will have a reward; if you deny it, you will receive punishment for your contempt. Hence Paul admonishes you, saying: Brothers, do not be deceived; God is not mocked. For whatever a man has sown, that also he will reap. What do you presume you will receive, you who sow in the flesh? For he says: Those who sow in the flesh will also reap from the flesh. But the fruit of the flesh has no share in the inheritance of the saints.

Why then do you deceive yourself; why do you trust vainly in vanity? Why do you transgress the ancient boundaries that the holy fathers set? You deny the vow that you solemnly made to God, and you expect a portion with God’s elect as though you were just.
