...they are considered by the ignorant to be the more important in action. Rise up, fathers, for the days are evil, short, and bad. Reform, as far as you are able, the deformed order; raise up the cast down; restore what has collapsed. For unless you apply a remedy quickly, you will soon suffer grave loss.

For dissolute monks and enemies of regular discipline always aspire to worse things, and, lest they be saved within the order, they labor to abandon it. For beginning the reform you have the aids of law; you have the Rule; you have statutes. This alone is required of you: that you put them into practice. Therefore, if you love your order, indeed because you love it, do not neglect this, do not delay, for necessity presses very greatly, since the impiety of wicked monks presumes everywhere. Behold, the rich of the world violently seize the goods of monasteries; behold, worshipers of greed invade the order’s possessions; behold, secular clerics receive the abbeys of Saint Benedict in commendam. None of these things would happen if abbots and monks had persevered in regular observance. Let what we suffer, then, be imputed to us, since we did not resist the rashness of the wicked from the beginning. To us, I say, this fault is justly ascribed, since, intent on our own advantages, we do not care for the harms of the order, as though they were someone else’s.

Let us rise up, I beg you, fathers; let us rise up at last, even if late, after so many losses, so many perils, so much ruin of souls, and let us do what lies in us, henceforth asking the mercy of God to supply what is lacking in us, to direct our zeal, and to bring holy efforts to completion. And if we cannot bring the whole order back to the unity of good conduct, let us strive at least to reform those monks who are in our own province. But if complete conformity cannot be had in all things, let them at least be reformed in the three substantial matters, without which the life of monks must be reckoned nothing other than the shameful brothel of an unfaithful soul.

Reform, however, must begin with the abbots and then be continued among the monks. For unless the shepherd has been brought back to sincerity, the health of the sheep will not last long. For it is commonly said: When the head is sick, the other members suffer. Therefore every adornment of the body must begin from the head. There are certain deformed abbots who wish to compel their monks to reform,

while they themselves are unwilling to submit to regular observance even in the least thing. They hate in themselves the good that they love in others, and they are not ashamed to do themselves what they forbid their subjects. Do not trust such men, O fathers; for they are the very ones who confound us. They spurn visitations; they contemn the decrees of chapters. Let experience make us cautious, whom zeal without knowledge has at times made penitent.

That visitors ought to be solicitous for the reform of our order. Chapter 10.

The greatest part of reform, however, is placed almost entirely in the diligence of those who are to carry it out. Therefore it is said in the constitutions of the fathers that suitable men should be appointed to the office of visitation, who can effectively instruct those to be visited in honorable conduct by word and example.

For a visitor must be learned in divine law; he must be holy and irreproachable in life, so that he may teach the teachable by word and instruct the obstinate by example. For one without the other will not suffice in a ruler of souls. If he is ignorant, how will he form others? If his conduct does not offer those visited an example of good action, how will he compel them to reform? He will surely hear the objection of the proverb that says: Physician, heal yourself. And not undeservedly. For by what presumption are you led, O talkative visitor, to attempt to reform others, when you still lie in your own deformity? You are polluted by the filth of vices, and will you be able to cleanse others? Believe me: the Holy Spirit will not give power to your words when you offend his grace by a contrary life. A discourse lacks efficacy in the heart of the hearer when the preacher’s conduct rebukes it. What shall I say? I would be lying if I said that the contumacious man does not often rebuke the contumacious, the shameful man defame the shameful, and believe that he has escaped his own conscience, as though conscience were not enough. There are, I say, vicious men among those who denounce vices, accusers in public; in secret, guilty parties.
