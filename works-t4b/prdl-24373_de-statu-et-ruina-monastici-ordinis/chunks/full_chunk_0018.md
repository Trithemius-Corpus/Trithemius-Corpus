of our order, you who have assembled in chapter, who labor for its restoration: bring back to mind its former condition, and consider into what great calamity it has now fallen. See, monasteries are being snatched from the hands of Saint Benedict; our order is being destroyed all around by the enemies of God; monks are returning to the world; impious men are tearing apart our religion; strangers are being endowed with our goods. Why should I speak of internal wars? What should I report about civil evils? It is no wonder, fathers, pay attention, if we are confounded from without, since we are torn apart within by our own people. Our abbots kick against discipline, tear apart the unity of the order, neglect the statutes of the chapters, spurn visitations, and despise your censures, O fathers. Unless you strive to bring them back to regular observance as quickly as possible, you will not be able to keep them long under the order.

Why, then, do you dissemble? How long will you sleep? When will you wake up? When will you fight for the inheritance of Saint Benedict? O fathers, arm yourselves with zeal for God, for the order, and for discipline, because it is time. Fight for your rightful possessions; defend the rights of holiness; stand manfully against transfers of the order. See, already in the province of Mainz alone six monasteries have been transferred from our order in our own times, and unless we keep watch in another manner, we shall suffer heavier losses day by day. While we dissemble in this way, not to say grow sluggish, the order is gradually diminished, until at last it will be wholly scattered. Why do we not meet such dangers? When the others are perishing, do we wisely trust in our own security? Did not the poet speak truly: it is your own affair when the nearest wall is burning? Yesterday danger like this threatened them; tomorrow it will threaten us. Each one of you, I do not doubt, loves his own monastery and would in no way consent for it to be transferred from the order, so that he desires not only its present but also its future happiness to be extended for eternal years. Why, then, do you not defend in common what each of you separately fears to lose?

I fear, fathers, that in this most wretched dissembling our stinginess is being exposed. We love money and lose a monastery. We fear small expenses and lose broad dwellings. We do not wish to spend a little money, and we allow the most abundant monasteries to pass from the order to outsiders. We put private advantage before the common good

and do not care to preserve the honor of the order. We say many things; we make many resolutions for the useful defense of the order, but we are afraid to spend all the coins. O weak zeal, which greed makes useless. If we will not give money for our brothers, when will we risk our life? If we do not contribute small expenses for the preservation of the order, when will we give our bodies? One who is tight-fisted in a small matter, when will he be generous in a great one?

I shall now speak to you, fathers, who dissemble while the order is placed in such great dangers. Blessed Benedict made you rich from poor men; now he asks alms from you, and, shameful to say, he does not receive them. See how many are found among us, if I may say so by your leave, who distribute their substance to frivolous persons, prepare banquets for birds and dogs, enrich their parents and relatives from the goods of the monastery, and squander the rest in vain pleasures. Few are those who grieve over the desolation of our order; fewer think about a remedy; very few supply assistance.

Is the sacred order of Benedict, once the most excellent among all, thus not defended from the attack of enemies? Wake up, fathers, while vigilance is useful, and do not repent too late that you have slept. Look at the superiors of other orders, how carefully, how zealously, how steadfastly they defend the rights, privileges, and monasteries of their religion, sparing neither expenses nor labors nor contributions, so that they may preserve their orders in vigor. Are you inferior to them? Are you poorer than the mendicants? If one should like to consider the number of monasteries in the order, we are more numerous; if the substance of temporal goods, richer; if power and authority, undoubtedly more distinguished. What do we lack, except zeal and constancy, that they possess?

Why, then, do we hesitate? Why are we torpidly entangled in idleness? Why do we not keep watch for the defense of the order? Do you not know, O fathers, how the laws assist those who are vigilant? That patience is not good by which the honor of God is neglected, the worship owed to him is diminished, and the integrity of the holy order is violated. What shall I say? You grieve over monasteries already transferred, and yet you do not undertake to preserve those about to be transferred.
