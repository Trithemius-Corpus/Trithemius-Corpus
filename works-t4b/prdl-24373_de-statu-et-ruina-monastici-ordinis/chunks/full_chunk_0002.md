On the Most Holy Primitive State of Our Order. Chapter 2.

I somewhat doubt whether I shall be doing a thing pleasing to everyone if I commend in a few words the earlier state of our order, since I judge that, by praising the ancients, I have shown too little regard for the monks of our own time. For there are some who think themselves either the best monks, or dare to compare themselves with those excellent fathers who preceded us. However they measure themselves, let them not be angry with me if I do not dare to make our observance equal to that of the ancients. For if we contemplate their life in a proper light, we shall scarcely be able to compare the best among us, as he is regarded, with a good monk of that time.

Indeed, their holy way of life, bright with virtues, approved by signs, and illustrious through knowledge of all the Scriptures, earned favor with God and with men. For, hearing the voice of the Lord saying, “If anyone wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me,” they flew to monasteries in contempt of the world; and, placing their necks beneath the yoke of the rule, they humbly accepted the norm of obedience. Loving God with their whole heart, they sought nothing worldly in this life; but, despising all perishable things and being tested in the furnace of poverty, they happily came to the heavenly kingdom.

They were mature men, zealous and most learned: firm in faith, robust in hope, shining with twofold charity; pure in mind, outstanding in devotion, and adorned with the honor of every virtue. They had no care for earthly things; no enticement of pleasure was within them. Rather, turned toward God with all their strength, they conquered the world with its vanities. Hence kings and princes of this age, devout men and dear to God, because of the monks’ most holy way of life, became lovers of the order and magnificently exalted it with gifts and largesses.

They rejoiced that, in their times, the holy studies of monks were spreading and extending with glory into the farthest regions of the world. For the more the holy monks loved God, the more they were also loved by men. And the less they sought earthly things, the more, by God’s providence, they abounded in the necessities of the body. For to those who love God, the whole world is riches.

O how becoming was the face of your youth, holy religion of Benedict; how beautiful your appearance; how adorned with the splendor of all virtues your majesty. Who could be capable of weighing your worthily? You show to the people of God, freed from the servitude of Pharaoh in the desert, the way of three days, when you persuade men, rescued from the darkness of the world, to come for the love of God to the desert of cenobitic life through the three substantial vows of religion.

Blessed are the professors who, departing from the Egypt of this age, yearn with all their desires through this way of three days toward the land of eternal promise. This is the threefold cord of holy religion, which is not easily broken, which is adorned with the splendor of obedience, chastity, and poverty; which calls its lovers back to the heavenly homeland; which presents its devotees rejoicing to the supreme emperor, Christ Jesus. For although many things are necessary for regular observance, nevertheless I would dare to say that whoever has kept these three inviolate stands secure in the order, like a threefold cord. For all the statutes of the order were made for the preservation of these substantial things.

The holy monks of antiquity, carefully considering this, observed this way of the three substantial things with the greatest zeal: poor in spirit through contempt of all worldly things; continent through chastity of mind and body; obedient through humble subjection; and rich in every virtue. They shone forth as most renowned in doctrine and holiness, and by word as much as by example they turned many from injustice. Hence, as their merits advanced, many of them were promoted to the governance of churches; and the higher they had been placed, the more renowned they became in virtues.
