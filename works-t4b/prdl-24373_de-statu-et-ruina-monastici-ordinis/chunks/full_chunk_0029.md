... many may consider them learned. But the learning of their mind is known from their works. Now the greedy man is believed to be learned; now the man who joins field to field is called religious: the one who keeps watch over earthly profits, who lets his appetite loose in gathering riches. But believe me, fathers, for I am about to say what is true: temporal riches do not make discipline stable in the order. O what a terrible sentence abbots will undergo: those who neglect the Rule, who do not go before their monks in word and example of holiness, who despise spiritual exercises, who devote themselves to earthly profits, who scorn the discipline of monastic honor. Would that they were wise and understood and looked ahead to their last things, and either governed the burden of so great a rule with care, or, if they could not, humbly laid it down. Happy is he who stands carefully upon his own watch, who always does the will of the Lord, who lives without pretense according to the purity of the Rule.

But for the present, the things we have said about the state and reformation of our order are, I believe, sufficient; and since their reading is at the end of the chapter, let it also receive an end. Moreover, each and every thing that I have written, or shall write, either in this treatise or in others, I submit to the examination of the sacrosanct Roman Church, whose faith, doctrine, and definition, as long as I live, I always and everywhere wish to preserve, teach, and defend. But if anything, God forbid, has been said by me otherwise than what she holds, teaches, and believes, by these presents I now simply revoke it, and wish it to be regarded as not having been said. I wish and judge the Supreme Pontiff, the vicar of Christ, to be in all things, by this my protestation, the judge and moderator of my faith and doctrine.

The present treatise was read at table in the provincial chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict of the province of Mainz, held at Hirsau, in the year of the Lord 1493, on Jubilate Sunday and on the following days until the end. And with all the abbots gathered there willing and consenting, it was decreed that it should be printed and in future provincial chapters always read publicly at table, as is clear in the statutes.
