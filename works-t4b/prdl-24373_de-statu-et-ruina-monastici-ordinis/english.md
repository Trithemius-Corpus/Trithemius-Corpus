To the venerable and most beloved father, lord Blasius, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau: brother Johannes, called abbot in Sponheim, sends greeting and charity.

Although your request and that of your brothers, dearest father, far exceeds the powers of my talent, nevertheless I have preferred to undertake the arduous burden rather than fail, as far as I can, to satisfy your charity. For the love of God, zeal for the order, and fervor for discipline make you worthy that I should deny you nothing when you ask for the honor of God. You asked me, when I was present, by words; when I was absent, by frequent letters; and in a certain way you compelled me by the authority of your presidency to write something to you about the calamity of our order, so that you might summon the fathers, to be assembled with you in chapter, to compassion for it.

The request is holy, but the difficulty of obeying is great. I know that I shall be torn by the teeth of the envious, who will judge me either presumptuous or excessive. But with my ears stopped I shall pass by them as if deaf, provided that this work, such as it is, may please holy and learned men. I do not recall having written anything in it that rivals can tear apart, but rather true things, manifest things, and things that lie open to the eyes of all. If they do not wish these things to be written, let them grieve that they were done as well. In this work only elegance of speech is lacking, and for this I do not fear being blamed among religious men. What then? Is truth to be despised because it is not clothed in the ornament of eloquence? Away with that. Plain and true speech is better than ornate and false speech. But these matters are nothing to the point. I therefore submit this little work to your examination, so that, if you judge it worthy, it may be read to the brothers; but if unworthy, it may flee the eyes of the lords. Farewell. In the year of the Lord 1493, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of May.

Here begins the deeply sorrowful book of lord Johannes, abbot of Sponheim, on the ruin of the order of our holy father Benedict.

When I call back to mind the former beauty of our order, I grieve vehemently over the deformity which it now suffers, so that I would rather shed tears than form a mournful discourse. For who would not weep over its ruin, if he weighs its ancient glory? O, if only we would at some time recall to memory the once golden ages of this most sacred order, when, like a river of paradise in doctrine and holiness, it watered the whole Church throughout the world, we would easily perceive that its present condition is nothing. Indeed our observance, which we call regular, when compared with the institutions of the ancients, is found not only lesser, but in many respects more lax. How the gold has grown dim; how the finest color has changed! The splendor of extraordinary holiness, once gleaming, has been darkened by desire for earthly things; the standard of honor has yielded to the enticements of pleasures. Therefore let my eyes bring forth tears by day and by night, and let them not be silent, because the religion of my people has been crushed with a great crushing, with a very grievous wound. If I go out to the fields, behold the idle and vain; if I enter the monasteries, behold those wasted by pleasures. For I find many professors of the rule of father Benedict; I discern few walking according to his tradition. “You have multiplied the nation,” says the prophet to the Lord, “you have not increased the joy,” because, since the holy exercises of monks fail in many, corrupt hearts do not admit the true joy that is born of virtue. If only we would awaken, even late at last, after the destruction of so many souls, which, despising the yoke of regular observance, have descended into the abyss of damnation. If only we feared the sentence of Benedict our lawgiver, who so often strikes the madness of a hard abbot with the account that must be rendered to God.

I complain of your spiritual ruin.

--- Page 005 ---

Now therefore my speech must be directed to you, most reverend fathers, who celebrate the chapters of the order by apostolic authority; who receive the power to compel the rebellious to discipline; who have zeal for our order according to knowledge. And so I address you; to you I shall strive, according to my strength, to urge that you aid the order, now nearly collapsing, and I exhort you with all earnestness to do this. Consider, fathers, the miserable state of our religion, and aid its calamity as much as you can, if indeed you desire to endure to the end. You are not unaware of the wickedness of many who, so that they may be able to escape the discipline of the order, labor to tear themselves away from it. Their rashness must be resisted firmly, lest the unpunished malice of a few provide many with boldness for sinning. And the wickedness of a few, when deferred, always grows. Although the public body ought not to be corrupted by the evil of one person, nevertheless the ignorant crowd blames everyone because of the offense of a few. Therefore superiors who do not punish the wicked, who by their deformity cloud the reputation of the good, strengthen them. And so it pertains to you, fathers, to correct deformed monks as far as you can; it is the duty of your office to preserve the order in its vigor. And because a fool is not corrected by speech, what our hierophant set down almost monastically against the incorrigible should be applied generally to these men. Rise up now, fathers, for it is time; arm yourselves with zeal for regular discipline; I beg you, defend the inheritance of holy father Benedict; drive out the proud; correct the rebellious; rebuke the hard. Fight bravely for the preservation of our order. Arm yourselves with the armor of God and love of the order, intending to fight not so much by your own labors as by the help of Christ. His grace will provide you aid, if with purity of heart you pour out devoted prayers to him. Why then do you dissemble? Why do you not defend the portion of Saint Benedict? How long will the efforts of the wicked prevail against the holy order? Truly, fathers, truly the great necessity of the order demands a swift remedy. Look closely at the conduct of irregular abbots and monks, and weigh how wickedly they kick against the discipline of the order. Apart from the habit, and this itself disordered in many, nothing honorable

On the Most Holy Primitive State of Our Order. Chapter 2.

I somewhat doubt whether I shall be doing a thing pleasing to everyone if I commend in a few words the earlier state of our order, since I judge that, by praising the ancients, I have shown too little regard for the monks of our own time. For there are some who think themselves either the best monks, or dare to compare themselves with those excellent fathers who preceded us. However they measure themselves, let them not be angry with me if I do not dare to make our observance equal to that of the ancients. For if we contemplate their life in a proper light, we shall scarcely be able to compare the best among us, as he is regarded, with a good monk of that time.

Indeed, their holy way of life, bright with virtues, approved by signs, and illustrious through knowledge of all the Scriptures, earned favor with God and with men. For, hearing the voice of the Lord saying, “If anyone wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me,” they flew to monasteries in contempt of the world; and, placing their necks beneath the yoke of the rule, they humbly accepted the norm of obedience. Loving God with their whole heart, they sought nothing worldly in this life; but, despising all perishable things and being tested in the furnace of poverty, they happily came to the heavenly kingdom.

They were mature men, zealous and most learned: firm in faith, robust in hope, shining with twofold charity; pure in mind, outstanding in devotion, and adorned with the honor of every virtue. They had no care for earthly things; no enticement of pleasure was within them. Rather, turned toward God with all their strength, they conquered the world with its vanities. Hence kings and princes of this age, devout men and dear to God, because of the monks’ most holy way of life, became lovers of the order and magnificently exalted it with gifts and largesses.

--- Page 007 ---
They rejoiced that, in their times, the holy studies of monks were spreading and extending with glory into the farthest regions of the world. For the more the holy monks loved God, the more they were also loved by men. And the less they sought earthly things, the more, by God’s providence, they abounded in the necessities of the body. For to those who love God, the whole world is riches.

O how becoming was the face of your youth, holy religion of Benedict; how beautiful your appearance; how adorned with the splendor of all virtues your majesty. Who could be capable of weighing your worthily? You show to the people of God, freed from the servitude of Pharaoh in the desert, the way of three days, when you persuade men, rescued from the darkness of the world, to come for the love of God to the desert of cenobitic life through the three substantial vows of religion.

Blessed are the professors who, departing from the Egypt of this age, yearn with all their desires through this way of three days toward the land of eternal promise. This is the threefold cord of holy religion, which is not easily broken, which is adorned with the splendor of obedience, chastity, and poverty; which calls its lovers back to the heavenly homeland; which presents its devotees rejoicing to the supreme emperor, Christ Jesus. For although many things are necessary for regular observance, nevertheless I would dare to say that whoever has kept these three inviolate stands secure in the order, like a threefold cord. For all the statutes of the order were made for the preservation of these substantial things.

The holy monks of antiquity, carefully considering this, observed this way of the three substantial things with the greatest zeal: poor in spirit through contempt of all worldly things; continent through chastity of mind and body; obedient through humble subjection; and rich in every virtue. They shone forth as most renowned in doctrine and holiness, and by word as much as by example they turned many from injustice. Hence, as their merits advanced, many of them were promoted to the governance of churches; and the higher they had been placed, the more renowned they became in virtues. What church would not seek a priest from the order of Benedict? What people devoted to the Christian name would not desire a teacher from the company of monks? For for many years

monks also in the order of the holy father Benedict, men most outstanding in every learning and holiness, have shone forth, as almost all who have written the old histories testify. And indeed, to speak without envy, the holy religion of Benedict easily surpasses all orders, if one considers its dignity, its widely extended dominion throughout the world, its men distinguished in learning and holiness, and finally their multitude. For this venerable plant has produced eighteen supreme pontiffs, more than two hundred cardinals, sixteen hundred archbishops, and three thousand five hundred bishops. But if anyone calls this total of illustrious prelates excessive and brings it into doubt, let him turn over the chronicles and old deeds, and he will find a few named out of many. Yet no one should think that the former beauty of the order is to be measured by the present, since ours bears no equal comparison to that, when in our times monks do not shine, as once they did, either in knowledge of the Scriptures or in holiness of conduct. Perhaps even now you will find devout monks, though few, but simple men. Yet for the governance of souls in this time devotion is not enough, if knowledge of the Scriptures is lacking. But if our monks are neither devout nor learned, they are rightly judged unworthy of the governance of churches. Many, indeed, walk well as subjects; but if they are raised to the government of others, they have never remained in the tranquility of their former life. And, to say briefly what I think, neither are monks worthy of churches, nor churches of monks, in this time. The reason, however, is not hidden from the prudent; it will have to be brought into the light hereafter, in its own time. Now, if you please, let us inquire briefly what the present condition of our order is.

On the lamentable present condition of our order. Chapter III.

My eyes have failed because of tears; my bowels are truly troubled; my liver is poured out upon the earth over the destruction

--- Page 009 ---
of my order, since the humble little child has fainted within it, since, cast down without honor, it lies open to the hands of the enemy. Let no one despise me as I grieve; let no one accuse my sorrow. I do not seek earthly honors; I do not covet ecclesiastical dignities, but I groan that learning and holiness have fallen away from my order. What have we to do with vain honors, we who seek our life in heaven? Alas, the crown has fallen from our head; monastic devotion has grown lukewarm; charity has grown cold; the honor of regular discipline has very greatly failed. Where now in our order is the devotion once so abundant in its most sacred men? Where is the venerable erudition in every variety of the Scriptures? Where is the solicitude of abbots for the care of the Lord’s flock? We see many monasteries founded according to the rule of Saint Benedict, in which, apart from the name and the habit, we see no trace of regular discipline. Over this, fathers, take up lamentation; deplore the ruin of our most holy order, if perhaps God may turn to mercy and leave a blessing behind him. And you transgressors, who are the cause of these tears, return at last, even late, to your heart; return, and fear the indignation of your blessed lawgiver. Attend to the desolation of the order that you bring on by your evil; fear the wrath of the Almighty, which you provoke by your crimes. Woe to us. The city of our religion is being destroyed by enemies all around us; the house of discipline is being reduced to nothing; and no one is found to resist, no one to set himself as a wall for the house of God. We all seek the things that are our own; we all conceal the common necessity of the order, until little by little we too fail. Our inheritance has been turned over to strangers, our houses to foreigners. Monasteries consecrated as dwellings for monks are now becoming stables for clerics and fortresses for fighting soldiers. What shall I say? I am ashamed to utter what men without conscience are not ashamed to perpetrate in the cloisters of monks, men who disturb the privacy of religious persons and turn houses of prayer into dwellings of merchants. I suffer with your calamities, O venerable religion of Benedict; I groan that you have been exposed to plunder by every passerby. There is no one to console you among all your dear ones, because those who could help you

are unwilling to succor you; those who wish to help you cannot. O with what beautiful grace you once flourished in the Church of God, when you brought forth men distinguished by every holiness and learning, and, as one younger, strong and steadfast, you used to drive away from yourself every dishonorable way of life. But now, as if wearied by old age and the oppression of many days, you have failed, and the strength of your youth has been taken away, so that your beauty is no longer in you. Where now is your zeal? Where is the comeliness of your face; where your honor, where the power once spread throughout the whole world? Where are your sons around your table, men most learned and most holy? Where is that study of letters, venerable to God and the angels, whose fervor once made you exalted above all orders? For they have failed, and you have been reduced almost to nothing; you have lost the beauty of your youth, and the strength of your adolescence has been scattered. To whom shall I compare you, or to whom shall I liken you, secluded daughter? To whom shall I make you equal and console you, mother of monks? Great as the sea is your crushing sorrow; who will heal you? O, if you had guarded against this ruin which you now experience, how blessed all nations would have called you. But you said in your abundance: I shall not be moved forever. Plenty has made you poor, and the abundance of temporal things has stripped you of all your adornment. You have brought forth perverse sons, disobedient, rebellious against holy institutions, who, hiding beneath the cloak of your honor, have stripped you of all your beauty. Alas, mother and virgin, you have grown old in a foreign land; you are counted with the dead; there is no appearance of your beauty. Rise now, blessed Benedict, most holy prince of monks, and see your order which you established, and your inheritance which you planted. Look upon the monks forgetful of your profession, immersed in earthly things, given over to pleasure, full of every outpouring of vices. You once gave them the best precepts for living; you commanded them to be free from the cares of secular affairs; you ordered their hearts to be stretched out toward the love of God alone. Rise, I say, most blessed father, and see the desolation of your order, which is confounded within by its own sons and scattered without by enemies. Monks despise the institutions of your piety; alien men seize the monasteries of your order. There is no one to defend the honor of your name, no

one to guard the beauty of your most holy order. Rise also, O great pope Gregory, who under anathema forbade places consecrated to God for monks to be transferred to the uses of secular men, and see monks expelled from the monasteries and worldly clerics thrust in. You, once the chief of all churches, used to devote the greatest care to monastic honor, taking anxious precaution that nothing be presumed in the sacred order beyond the rule of Benedict. Now behold the ways of monks, most reverend bishop, bare of all honor and full of every disgrace. See how monasteries dedicated to your order are handed into the hands of clerics, and those that once could be compared to angelic choirs now become brothels of prostitutes. Rise, most glorious emperor and unconquered defender of the Christian faith, Charles, glory of the Germans, and see the calamity of the monks, you who once made the order of Father Benedict glorious and, by your zeal, spread it through almost the whole world. See now that the monasteries which you once founded have all been exposed to the plundering of enemies. You who once venerated the monastic order as the camp of God, now have compassion, holy prince, on it as despised by all and stripped of its goods. You once used, with great care, to bring tepid abbots and monks back to the observance of regular discipline, and, once reformed, to defend them with great solicitude from every harassment by your own men or by anyone else. Rise also, Berno, abbot of the monastery of Cluny, with your successors, you who in ancient times magnificently restored the holy order as it was collapsing and brought it back everywhere to its former beauty. Rise now, I say, and see how the iniquity of monks abounds: fury rules, crime overflows, and pleasure makes almost all of them mad. The beauty of reform has been taken away; discipline has collapsed; zeal for holy conduct has perished; humility has breathed its last. Rise, William, most reverend father, abbot of the monastery of Hirsau, who renewed the Cluniac restoration in Germany after its lapse, who provided abundant statutes for monastic conduct, who by your precepts strengthened the limbs of the weak, who by teaching and holiness illumined it like a most brilliant star.

Rise also, O most excellent fathers, authors of the various reforms, and see that your labors have now perished: your constitutions neglected, your exhortations despised, the protests of your solicitude held in contempt. By life and teaching you made the monastic order glorious, beloved by God, venerable to angels, holy to yourselves, and reverend to men. Where now are those merits? Where now are those labors of the saints?

Alas, venerable mother Religion, to what great calamity you have come. Those who once glorified you now despise you; those who honored you now hold you in contempt. You have abandoned the commands of your lawgiver; you have cast away the study of the Scriptures; therefore you have become contemptible. The sons whom you bore have done this evil to you. For as soon as monks began to savor earthly things, to neglect sacred reading, and to put inward things after outward, your excellent color was changed into filth. But what then? One who falls often sets himself to rise again; but you, once fallen, have never risen up completely.

What could Cluny, what Hirsau, what Melk, what the very recent Bursfelde, and whatever other reforms there may have been besides these, do for the whole order spread throughout the world? For although Cluny poured out the light of its integrity through all the provinces of the world, still it left many monasteries in various kingdoms in their former deformity. Indeed, from the time when our religion first fell, there has never been any general reform. O how easy is the descent to Avernus; how ready is the will to sin; how great, after a fall, is the difficulty of rising again to uprightness. All, so to speak, have fallen; few have risen from their deformity; many rot away in their pleasures.

I grieve over you, O flower of monastic beauty. I grieve, and very deeply indeed; and if I cannot help you otherwise, I pour out tears, hot and most bitter, for your contrition. But you too, O most reverend fathers, who love the honor of your order, who grieve over the contrition of our mystical Joseph, who desire to come to the eternal inheritance: attend to the state of our religion, look upon its desolation, think upon a remedy. See, wealthy monasteries are being snatched away in turn from the hands of Saint Benedict; see,

cloisters of monks are becoming dwellings of clerics, and monks are being transferred from bad pursuits to the worst. You err, O prudence of the world; you are deceived, O piety of the age. Never will you be able to make a good canon out of a bad monk.

But you also, transgressors, pay attention; apostates from the order, listen. What madness drives you to destroy for Saint Benedict monasteries that you did not build? Do you think yourselves free from damnation if, after casting off the monastic habit, you sin as clerics? In the strict examination, will a presumed license to sin save you? Perhaps in another habit you will be unknown among monks? We shall see whether God approves your transfer, when you are in that dreadful judgment, where no order but everlasting horror dwells. For what other dwelling he is to receive, who has become a worse canon from being a bad monk, I do not sufficiently understand.

Senseless men, enemies to your very selves, who will teach you to flee from the wrath to come? Who will be able, or dare, to defend your wicked cause against Saint Benedict in that dreadful examination? Whom will you have as advocate for your rashness? Saint Benedict, prince of monks, will stand as witness against you; he will accuse you and condemn you. Who will excuse you? Do not flatter yourself, wicked despiser of the regular vow; do not soothe your conscience. If you have Benedict as your adversary, you will find none of the saints as your friend. For whoever offends one citizen of the heavenly homeland [offends all]; where there is highest and blessed tranquillity, there too will be the indivisible unity of souls.

Who, then, will be able to deliver you from the hand of the Lord, if all the saints stand against you? Call upon those who now deceive you, who take from you the habit of the order, who lead you away with flattering words, who become collaborators in your apostasy, so that they may defend you from the tribulation that is at hand. For if the righteous will scarcely be saved, where will the apostate and destroyer of the holy order appear? In that day the justice of a saint will not free the wicked man, nor will it bring help to itself or to another. Rather, those very men whom you now win over to yourself with gifts and presents as collaborators in such presumption will then be witnesses of your unhappiness, and companions as well.

of your unhappiness, and companions as well. There will be no defender there of a depraved action, no advocate of iniquity; for where all things will be laid bare, there each person’s own accusing conscience will be enough for condemnation. There the army of the saints will stand against those who have wickedly profaned the dwellings of monks, who have impiously destroyed the order of the holy fathers.

But the authors of these things, which I seem to myself justly to have called iniquitous, try to excuse their deed by saying: “The corruption of monks, driving monasteries to extreme poverty, cannot be corrected and amended unless it is transferred into another state,” on account of many reasons which they devise for themselves, and which, God granting, we shall show in what follows to be empty. Finally, they say: “To correct bad monks by transfer not only is free from sin, but is also a great part of justice and piety.” But where can these things be distinguished? How will you prove by what has been said that they are true? To convert bad monks to goodness is praiseworthy and holy; but to make them canons is not to amend monks, but to have corrupted them. A bad monk will never be a good canon. A good monk, moreover, will not cast off the order that is his, but will cherish, cultivate, and love it.

I do not now wish to contend with words; I do not wish to be too severe or vehement; I do not wish to depict the effect of this transfer, which I do not doubt will gradually come to light. If you wanted to make bad monks into canons, why did you not put good monks back in their place? A change that is presumed to be made for the worse is not without transgression of a vow. Woe to you, deserters, who, so that satisfaction may be given to your pleasures, despise the order, deny the vow, and confound the rule of equity. We shall all stand before the tribunal of Christ, as the Apostle says, so that each one may receive according to what he has done in his body. What will the destroyers of monasteries receive there, who do not defer to the honor of the Almighty, who violate an agreement, who nullify the wills of the saints, who despise the decrees of law? If it is good to found monasteries, it is evil, let no one doubt, to destroy them. A contrary action will receive only a contrary reward. Finally, if no one dares to infringe the last will of a dead man in legacies made to any human being whatever, why do lovers of the world presume to usurp for themselves what has been offered to God and the saints?

If one who violates a man’s testament is guilty of a crime, what punishment does he deserve who wickedly changes to alien uses the foundations of monasteries made by bishops and princes with their approval? For indeed kings, princes, prelates, and the rest of the faithful who founded monasteries for the honor of God are proved to have wished this above all: that the foundations of this institution should obtain an inviolable effect. If they had wished to have canons in the monasteries, they would not have placed monks there.

But the blind ambition of men has gained such strength that it thinks of nothing less than future things, and loves nothing more than perishable things. O wretched condition of human frailty, O damnable forgetfulness ever of God’s commandments. Laws have consented to sins, and what is public has begun to be permitted. Crimes blaze everywhere, and far and wide, in a manifold kind of sinning, wicked ingenuity works harmful deceits and frauds through corrupt minds. This man confounds an order; that one utterly destroys it. This man withdraws the will of the saints; that one writes false allegations of dismemberment. Here monks are barred from monasteries; there the goods of one order are given to another. The enemy incites the restless; the slanderer attacks; the false witness defames. Already to be innocent among the guilty is a crime. What love of honesty, what integrity could there be in minds where the purpose of the order is abandoned, where the vow made to God is revoked? What shame about things remains there, what virtue can possibly stand firm, where divine right is violated, where there is no fear of laws, no dread of the judge? What can be called more inhuman, what more cruel, than to wish to usurp divine right for oneself, to confound what is lawful, to break a vow, to withdraw the worship owed to God?

O that day, fearful and dreadful to all mortals, when the wrath of God, long tolerating these evils of monks, will be avenged; when apostates will feel their due punishment, because as adversaries of the holy order they will pay the vengeance for their rashness. What then will it profit you to have denied the vow made to God, to have extinguished the sacred order, to have enjoyed a brief and shameful pleasure in the flesh? When the Lord begins to count his worshipers, to reward true monks with the promised prizes, to separate the good from the evil, to divide the truthful from the false, what place will you receive, what seat will you have?

...the outcome, you who deny the holy purpose of religion, who break your vows, who despise the order, who violate your faith. Would that you weighed the brevity of this life; would that you feared the examination of future retribution; would that, pierced by saving teachings, you heeded the voice of the Lord calling you to repentance and saying: “I do not desire the death of the one who is dying, but that he turn back and live.” And through the prophet: “Return to the Lord your God, for he is merciful and loving, patient and of great mercy, and he revokes his sentence,” if only you would wash away your guilt with tears.

But you do not fear the judgment of God; you spurn his clemency; you despise the patience of his long-suffering. Therefore the Apostle says to each of you: “Do you not know that the patience of God leads you to repentance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart you store up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath and of the revelation of the just judgment of God, who will render to each one according to his works.” He said that the judgment of God is just because it is late, because he delays long and greatly, so that the long patience of God may take counsel for you unto life. But how will you do penance, you who do not acknowledge guilt? You will grieve, but too late, when there is no longer a place for repentance, but only the repayment of vengeance. Then punishment will pay back the impious man and the sinner, when repentance for sin can no longer be of use.

O how such men will burn then; how wretched, how unhappy those who, for the briefest pleasure of this age, have made themselves deserters of God and rebels against the command of God, and have done the will of the devil, so that they must be tormented with him in inextinguishable fire. Would that these things clung in their hearts; would that those who desert their order, who break their vow, who so boldly lie both to God and to his saints, thought on these things. But we strive in vain. They in no way receive our words, who despise the words of God and of the Church and of Saint Benedict. Therefore this cause of the order must be carried on not with words, but with deeds.

Let us therefore arm ourselves, fathers, with all our strength, and let us prepare for the contest with an uncorrupted mind, devoted virtue, and firm constancy, against those who desire to destroy our order, who strip off the monk, who take away monasteries, who desert the rule, who stand as rebels against their visitors. Let us arm ourselves, I beseech you, fathers, with zeal for regular discipline and for the honor of the order, sparing no labors and no expenses, lest this pernicious plague, through our silence, gradually infect the whole order.

Never has so great a necessity lain upon our order; never has it been entangled in so many evils. Behold, monks strip off the habit; monasteries are transferred into dwellings of clerics; and while we are absorbed in private advantages, while we flee labors, while we spare expenses, while we take counsel for our own avarice, the order perishes, religion passes away, the worship of God vanishes. Already many monasteries, by the malice of the times, not to say by our own sloth, have been transferred away from the order; and unless we keep watch in another way, the rashness of wicked men threatens that greater losses will be inflicted on us. What shall I say of the monks whom we still have in the order only by habit and tonsure? How disobedient and rebellious abbots are to be compelled to aid the order. Chapter vii.

If judges lack the authority to restrain rebels, legislation will be made in vain. For we always strive after what is forbidden, and unless compelled, we resist commands. For where there is no fear of power, the condition of law is quickly despised. The preservation of law, therefore, is fear of authority. On Celebrating the Provincial Chapter. Chapter VIII.

When we come together as one, most excellent fathers, let us carefully investigate what the celebration of the provincial chapter requires. For the greatest strength of our stability is placed in it, if it is celebrated properly. It is the one stronghold of our order; without it our whole religious life would be thrown into confusion, as appears among those who for many years have held no chapters. For the chapter is the preservation of the order, the increase of regular discipline, the uniting of unity. In it, indeed, integrity is given to morals; purity is increased in minds; the quality of regular life is renewed. But what is required for it to be celebrated properly? Hear what its sincere celebration demands. It requires zeal; it requires knowledge; it requires piety. Without these three the celebration of the chapter has no value. For if zeal is lacking, who will rebuke offenders? If knowledge is lacking, what fruit will excessive zeal bear? Again, if piety is lacking, zeal does not sharpen, knowledge does not direct, and piety produces no fruit. Without zeal, knowledge remains cold; without piety, fruitless. Likewise, zeal without knowledge becomes excessive and unprofitable.

How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our province would be. Chapter IX.

How necessary a diligent reform of the monasteries in our province may be, you yourselves know, fathers, since you are not ignorant of the condition of the monks of this time. For the sum of regular discipline lies with the visitors: when it is continued, it grows; when it is neglected, it fails. Indeed, if visitors diligently fulfill their office, they will preserve reformed monasteries in regular observance and will easily bring deformed ones back to a better state. But if they are negligent, even what others have well begun will quickly be destroyed. For nothing so confounds unity, nothing so disturbs peace, nothing so injures charity, as the ignorance of visitors, when it grows heated not by reason but by undiscerning will. Experience has very often taught us what great disturbances, dissensions, anger, quarrels, slanders, and hatreds unskilled visitors have stirred up in congregations of monks, men for whom everything consists in authority, and nothing in knowledge and charity. But I have said enough about this, lest I seem to accuse those whom, because of the dignity of their office, we are commanded to treat with honor.

How Dangerous the Governance of Souls Is. Chapter 11.

If we considered how dangerous a thing the governance of souls is, how heavy the burden of pastoral care, how subject it is to strict examination, it would be a wonder if our heart ever presumed upon security in this life. For Scripture says that the harshest judgment will be made against those who are in charge. For, as blessed Benedict declares in the Rule, the abbot will have to render an account to God for the souls of all the monks committed to him. Therefore he says: Let the abbot who has been ordained always consider what burden he has taken up, and to whom he will render an account of his stewardship. It is certainly a heavy and dangerous burden to render account for the souls of many, when each person can scarcely answer for himself.

O what will abbots devoted to earthly things answer to the supreme Shepherd, they who destroy the souls of their brothers by bad example and feed them neither by word nor by deed? What shall I say about certain abbots of our order, who neither read the Rule of Saint Benedict which they profess, nor, if they do read it, understand it? I would be lying if I said I did not know abbots established under our chapter who are wrapped in such darkness of ignorance that they do not understand even the least sentence from the Rule. They neither know nor love the divine Scriptures; submerged in the vanities of the world, they vanish away; they serve the desires of the flesh, not of the spirit. According to the command of our lawgiver, the abbot must be learned in divine law, so that he may know and have a source from which to bring forth things new and old; chaste, sober, merciful, and the rest.

What will these men say? What will they teach? What exhortation will they give, when they understand nothing of the holy Scriptures and nothing of regular discipline? O good God, to what misery our order has come, which once was full of the most learned and most holy men. Where now is the love of regular observance? Where is the study of the Scriptures? Where do we find the traces of honorable conduct? Our abbots, with few exceptions, are either given over to avarice or drawn away by the vanities of the world; they think of nothing less than knowledge of the Scriptures, although many may consider them learned.

[segment 29]
[Page 060]
... many may consider them learned. But the learning of their mind is known from their works. Now the greedy man is believed to be learned; now the man who joins field to field is called religious: the one who keeps watch over earthly profits, who lets his appetite loose in gathering riches. But believe me, fathers, for I am about to say what is true: temporal riches do not make discipline stable in the order. O what a terrible sentence abbots will undergo: those who neglect the Rule, who do not go before their monks in word and example of holiness, who despise spiritual exercises, who devote themselves to earthly profits, who scorn the discipline of monastic honor. Would that they were wise and understood and looked ahead to their last things, and either governed the burden of so great a rule with care, or, if they could not, humbly laid it down. Happy is he who stands carefully upon his own watch, who always does the will of the Lord, who lives without pretense according to the purity of the Rule.

But for the present, the things we have said about the state and reformation of our order are, I believe, sufficient; and since their reading is at the end of the chapter, let it also receive an end. Moreover, each and every thing that I have written, or shall write, either in this treatise or in others, I submit to the examination of the sacrosanct Roman Church, whose faith, doctrine, and definition, as long as I live, I always and everywhere wish to preserve, teach, and defend. But if anything, God forbid, has been said by me otherwise than what she holds, teaches, and believes, by these presents I now simply revoke it, and wish it to be regarded as not having been said. I wish and judge the Supreme Pontiff, the vicar of Christ, to be in all things, by this my protestation, the judge and moderator of my faith and doctrine.

The present treatise was read at table in the provincial chapter of the Order of Saint Benedict of the province of Mainz, held at Hirsau, in the year of the Lord 1493, on Jubilate Sunday and on the following days until the end. And with all the abbots gathered there willing and consenting, it was decreed that it should be printed and in future provincial chapters always read publicly at table, as is clear in the statutes.
