STEGANOGRAPHIA:
That is:
The art, by hidden writing, of safely disclosing the will of one's mind to those who are absent;
by the most reverend and most distinguished man, Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Spanheim and most perfect master of Natural Magic.

Prefixed to this work is its key, or true introduction, composed by the author himself; until now much desired by many, but seen by very few. Now, for the sake of students of the more secret philosophy, it has been made public.

With the privilege and consent of superiors.

Darmstadt.
From the printing office of Balthasar Aulæandti, at the expense of Johann Berner, bookseller of Frankfurt.
In the year 1621.

TO THE READER.
You should be advised, candid reader, that of the letters R. and S., in this work sometimes added as numbers, sometimes as characters, sometimes as words written above on the page or added in the margin, the former denotes rubric, or red color, and the latter ink, or black color. You will also observe this: that the individual titles of the individual chapters are likewise to be painted and adorned in rubric. You may seek the significance of this matter from the Key prefixed to the work. Farewell.

HERE BEGINS THE FIRST BOOK OF THE STEGANOGRAPHY OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS, ABBOT OF SPANHEIM, OF THE ORDER OF SAINT BENEDICT, OF THE DIOCESE OF MAINZ,
TO THE MOST SERENE PRINCE, LORD PHILIP, COUNT PALATINE OF THE RHINE, DUKE OF BAVARIA, AND PRINCE ELECTOR OF THE HOLY EMPIRE, ETC.

It is the opinion of the most learned that the most ancient sages, whom in Greek speech we call philosophers, if they had discovered any secrets either of nature or of art, concealed them by various methods and figures, lest they should come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews confirm that Moses also, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his description of the creation of heaven and earth covered the secrets of ineffable mysteries with simple words. Saint Jerome too, one of the most learned among our own, affirms that almost as many mysteries lie hidden in the Apocalypse of John as there are words. I pass over the Greek sages, held in no small esteem among their own people, and our own most learned philosophers and poets, who, applying themselves to the writing of fables, by the ingenious invention of a single continuous narrative conveyed one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men. Although I cannot perfectly imitate these most devoted lovers of wisdom because of the slowness of my intellect, still I do not neglect to admire them and to read them with whatever diligence I can. For when I consider that they, by their own study, devised many things beyond other men,

PREFACE.
I rub myself by a certain force, as it were, so that I may grow warm, and I drive myself toward some imitation, however slight, even with the last of those who have gone before. Nor, as I think, has this opinion wholly deceived me, since by the continual study of reading I have both learned many things that I had not known before, and by my own reflections have opened for myself, more than for others, an approach to investigating more secret and deeper mysteries.

For although my learning or industry is not so great that I would dare to profess that I have grasped in every respect that ancient method of concealing the mysteries of the sages, nevertheless I have found certain various methods, not wholly contemptible, as it seems to me, by which I can safely and without deception, suspicion, or detection by anyone disclose the most secret intention of my mind, as widely as I wish, to another who knows this art, through open messengers or open letters. And this most secret art, a new invention, at the request of the most serene prince, Lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and ever unconquered Prince Elector of the Holy Roman Empire, the wisest of all philosophers and my Maecenas, than whom no one seemed to me more worthy for this great secret to be revealed to him, I have committed to writing and, not without very great labors, gathered into the present volume.

But lest this great secret come to the ears of common, unlearned, or wicked men, I considered it no small part of my duty, since it teaches mysteries, to wrap it in mysteries unintelligible to one who does not know how to understand, so that there will be no one from the number of the unlearned, no one unless most devoted to study, who can perfectly and fully penetrate the secret of this science by its power and according to our intention, except by receiving it from a teacher, which the Hebrews call Kabbalah, set forth in most hidden mysteries. Nor would the knowledge of this most secret art, made known among the wicked and reprobate, harm the commonwealth less than it would benefit the good. For just as good men and lovers of virtues use all aids for good and for the common utility, so evil and reprobate men seek out occasions for themselves, from evil things and also from good and most holy institutions, by which they may become worse.
