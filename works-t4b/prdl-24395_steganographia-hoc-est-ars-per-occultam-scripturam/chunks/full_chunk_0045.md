In this table we have set down thirty-one principal spirits, who preside, each in his own office and order, over all the operations of our Steganography, about which we have spoken in this first book of ours. We have assigned to each the character appropriate to him, and have also added the number and order of princes, dukes, counts, servants, and subservient spirits in their proper ranks, so that no one who desires to study this art may fall into error because of the diversity of the operations.

The great mysteries of this art can be penetrated only by the most studious men: namely, those whom love of secrets and nature urge on to investigation, and who, in their desire to obtain knowledge, refuse no honest and possible labor. But as for men who are lazy and dull in intelligence, whom neither love inflames toward the study of the secrets of wisdom nor nature assists, when they have been unable to understand this most secret art of our invention, understand that this should not be imputed to us, but rather ascribed to their own sluggishness, idleness, and malice. For either, having their intellect immersed in the pleasures of the flesh and worldly cares, they cannot devote themselves diligently to examining these matters, or, already considering themselves wise or very learned, they despise our compositions as unworthy of their reading.

There will also be some who are so accustomed to stand by old traditions and those of others that whatever they have not learned in them they think either impossible or superstitious. Nothing is more unfit than these people for the knowledge of this profound art, since they either do not wish or are not able to contemplate its highest matters. But those who are both capable and willing to study this art, if any such shall exist, we have resolved to admonish briefly at the end of this book, so that they may be able to make progress.

First of all, they must be warned always to keep the art itself hidden, lest it penetrate into the hands and knowledge of the wicked, who would commit many execrable crimes through it. For although the art itself is truly good in itself, nevertheless its practice would conduce no less to evil through the impious than to good through the good. Therefore let that remain secret among the good which, if made public among the wicked, would do harm. Yet the good should not therefore despise this knowledge because it serves the wicked for evil, just as one does not despise the sword because it lays a man low.
