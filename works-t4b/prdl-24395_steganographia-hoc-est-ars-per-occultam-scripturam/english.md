TO THE MOST SERENE PRINCE, LORD PHILIP, COUNT PALATINE OF THE RHINE, DUKE OF BAVARIA, AND PRINCE ELECTOR OF THE HOLY EMPIRE, ETC.

It is the opinion of the most learned that the most ancient sages, whom in Greek speech we call philosophers, if they had discovered any secrets either of nature or of art, concealed them by various methods and figures, lest they should come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews confirm that Moses also, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his description of the creation of heaven and earth covered the secrets of ineffable mysteries with simple words. Saint Jerome too, one of the most learned among our own, affirms that almost as many mysteries lie hidden in the Apocalypse of John as there are words. I pass over the Greek sages, held in no small esteem among their own people, and our own most learned philosophers and poets, who, applying themselves to the writing of fables, by the ingenious invention of a single continuous narrative conveyed one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men. Although I cannot perfectly imitate these most devoted lovers of wisdom because of the slowness of my intellect, still I do not neglect to admire them and to read them with whatever diligence I can. For when I consider that they, by their own study, devised many things beyond other men,

And this most secret art, a new invention, at the request of the most serene prince, Lord Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, Duke of Bavaria, and ever unconquered Prince Elector of the Holy Roman Empire, the wisest of all philosophers and my Maecenas, than whom no one seemed to me more worthy for this great secret to be revealed to him, I have committed to writing and, not without very great labors, gathered into the present volume.

But lest this great secret come to the ears of common, unlearned, or wicked men, I considered it no small part of my duty, since it teaches mysteries, to wrap it in mysteries unintelligible to one who does not know how to understand, so that there will be no one from the number of the unlearned, no one unless most devoted to study, who can perfectly and fully penetrate the secret of this science by its power and according to our intention, except by receiving it from a teacher, which the Hebrews call Kabbalah, set forth in most hidden mysteries. by the principal spirit Pamersyel, Anoyr Madriel, through the ministry of Ebrasothean, Abrulges i Trasbiel. And Nadres Ormennitules Rablion Hamorphiel. To these let the commission of all things be made, with an exorcism.

The operation of this first chapter is very difficult and full of dangers, because of the pride and rebellion of its spirits, who obey no one unless he is highly experienced in this art. For they not only refuse to obey novices and those less tested in the art, but, if they are pressed too hard, they often injure them and afflict them with various illusions. They are malicious and faithless above all aerial spirits, and they obey absolutely no one unless compelled by the greatest sacraments; and the secret entrusted to them they often faithlessly show to others. For as soon as they have been sent out with letters, they fly away, and, bursting in disorderly fashion toward the person to whom they are sent, like a people fleeing battle without a leader, they hurry furiously and, filling the air with their shouting, often reveal the sender’s secrets to everyone in the vicinity.

We therefore advise that no one intending to work in this art compel them, nor anxiously seek their services. Chapter III.

Whose prince is Camuel, having under him the spirits presiding over daytime operations, 10, and likewise over nighttime operations, 10, with the same number of sub-observers; their office is to announce the sender's arrival, way, and journey. Roth.

The first and highest of all the spirits of the third mansion toward the southeast is called Camuel. On the Varying Motion of the Planets and the Interpretation of the Tables.

Although in the present chapter a special treatment is to be given of the motions of Saturn and of his spirits, and of the operations in Steganography through them, nevertheless, lest we be forced to repeat in each chapter things once said opportunely, the studious reader must be advised to take care to remember also in the following chapters the things that we say in this chapter itself.

Now the motions of the individual planets are various, and all are most subtly divided into diverse other motions. The pure and proper motion is manifold, and is varied by very subtle divisions. Direct, retrograde, mixed, and confused motions are varied by almost as many points as they are separated from the beginning of their motion in the circle. What shall I say about finding the conjunction of the planets themselves? For, as Ptolemy says in the Centiloquium, there are one hundred twenty conjunctions among the planets themselves, namely: binary, 21; ternary, 35; quaternary, 35; quinary, 21; senary, 7; and one septenary. And we shall speak of these in their own place.

Concerning the other motions, however, although we cannot give a fixed term because of the infinite diversity by which, as points daily increase or decrease, they are varied without our knowing it, still, as fully as we have been able to observe, we have found more than 300,000 divisions, in which all the operations of this art are often varied as to their effect. And unless someone is very experienced in all these things, and knows perfectly the mean motions of the planets themselves, and then the very smallest punctual divisions, which arise from fourths, thirds, seconds, minutes, and degrees most subtly divided into equal parts, he will easily fall into true errors and will scarcely escape grave danger.

Therefore, for the perfect instruction of this science, we have arranged three tables. In the first of these, we have reduced the motion of Saturn, according to the four parts of both day and night, namely its proper and pure motion, to its punctual root, and have arranged it according to the true point of the ascendant of each sign.
