TO THE MOST SERENE
AND MOST UNCONQUERED
lord of the world, Emperor Maximilian:
the preface of Johannes Trittenhemius, Abbot of Peapolis,
to the book of eight questions.

I have obeyed your commands, most unconquered Emperor Maximilian, and the eight questions which you proposed to me some time ago, when I had been summoned to your Highness at the castle of Boppard, I have resolved with what brevity I could and set down in this form of a little book. You wished, moreover, if I remember rightly, that, so far as could be done, I should follow the way of nature in their solution, for the sake of those who grant less to belief. Yet this did not seem possible to me in all cases, because the subject matter of the questions in most places rested on theological rather than natural principles. For even if the Christian religion, among the sects of all mortals, is held to be nearer to the laws of nature, more conformable to them, and closer to them, nevertheless the principal articles of our faith are not all provable according to the common way of nature.

This, we must necessarily believe, was done so that faith too might have its place, and firm belief not lack its own merit. And so, having entered as far as my strength allowed upon a middle path of disputation at your command, I have used the testimony partly of nature and partly also of the divine Scriptures. For truly, the things which it befits us to believe are not to be rashly examined by human arguments, since, as holy Gregory says, faith has no merit where human reason supplies the proof. Therefore, with fear of God and reverence for our undefiled Christian religion, I have determined to answer the proposed questions in such a way that I depart from the definitions of the Roman Church neither in jest nor in earnest. Hence whatever I have ever written, or shall write, I wish to be wholly subject to its determination and judgment.

On faith and understanding

The First Question.

The first question of your most serene Majesty was this: Why did almighty God wish rather to be believed by mortals than to be known and understood, namely in that mode of knowledge and understanding by which he is known and understood by the holy angels?

I remember that I answered this question, put before me in person by your most sacred Majesty, as follows: Almighty God, who is intellect without beginning, pure, everlasting, and unchangeable, existing above every position knowable to us, far more perfect than infinite perfections, by his most wise will created from nothing, at the beginning, two intellects, namely the angelic and the human. The angelic intellect, at the beginning of its creation, was made in act with respect to all knowable things, and knew all things; but the human intellect at first consists in potency to all things, yet is ignorant of all things. At the end, however, through many and continuous upward movements, it will become like the angelic intellect, knowing both God and all things that pertain to the cognition

of the promised knowledge and understanding. The angelic and human intellects differ as any two opposites do: as act and potency, light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance, possession and privation, beginning and end, the immovable and the movable, substance and accident, truth and likeness, nature and art, health and sickness, whole and part. For the angelic intellect is like act, light, knowledge, possession, beginning, the immovable, the immediate, simple substance, truth, nature, and the whole. But the human intellect is like potency, darkness, ignorance, privation, end, the movable, the mediated, the composite, accident, likeness, art, and part. Hence whatever belongs to the angelic intellect from the beginning, these same things are present in the human intellect at the end, after it has laid aside the burden of the flesh. For just as beginning and nature perfect the angelic intellect, so the end and art, after many exercises at last, raise the human intellect upward. Therefore between these two intellects there is great inequality at the beginning, though at the end they are finally made equal, when the human intellect will be as great by art as the angelic intellect was before it by nature. Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect, as soon as it was created, knew all things from the beginning, without any action or motion of its own, by immovable substantiality and by nature.
