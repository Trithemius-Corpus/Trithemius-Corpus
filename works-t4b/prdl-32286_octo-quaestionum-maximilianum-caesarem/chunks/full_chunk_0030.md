I remember that, when I was long ago placed before your Majesty, I answered this question in this way: Almighty God, just and upright, kind and merciful, permits justly whatever he permits, because he is just, and there is no injustice with him; but all things that happen, he either does or permits in true judgment. Therefore, when it is asked why God permits evils to be done, we answer: he permits them because he is just.

Let the answer I have given not seem absurd to your Majesty, O Caesar, since it in no way prejudices the divine goodness that he permits evils to be done for a most just reason; rather, it would prejudice it if he did not permit evils to be done in the present world. For indeed, if he did not permit evils to be done, then man could not sin, and would certainly be impeccable; but that does not belong to his nature, to whom the Creator of all things gave free will from the beginning. For since man is a creature and not the Creator, he is necessarily capable of turning both to good and to evil, since his being depends on the supreme Maker of all creatures, just as every caused thing depends on the cause of its being. But to create has been left to God alone, who is

incorruptible and everlasting. Every corruptible thing, however, necessarily falls away, yet is preserved by the goodness of the Creator so long as it does not lose the influence of its cause. Hence it neither could nor should have happened that man, by the condition of his nature, should be created impeccable, for this is enjoyed naturally only by God, the Maker of all things, who is unchangeable and everlasting.

But whenever it is granted to a man that he seems unable to sin, the freedom of the will is not taken away, nor is possibility simply removed, but the will is confirmed in the good. And whenever this gift is granted to a creature, it is a gift of grace, not of nature. For Scripture says: God created man from the beginning and left him in the hand of his own counsel.

For, properly speaking, it cannot be that mortal man, conceived in original displeasure, should both have free will and be unable to commit sin. For the most holy bishop Augustine says: The first freedom of the will was to be able to sin; the last, in glory, will be much more to be unable to sin. And so, if man could not sin, he would not have mastery over his own will. Therefore, although almighty God does not will sin, he nevertheless justly permits men to sin and to do evil, so that, while freedom of will remains,

QUESTION SEVEN.

both the virtue of a good work may receive its reward, and voluntary wickedness may receive the evil it has done. For no one either does good or sins unwillingly.

Therefore almighty God justly permits men to sin and to do evil, to whom he granted freedom of will from the beginning. Now many reasons have long since been assigned by the holy doctors why the Almighty permits such great acts of sorcery to be done through the cooperation of demons; of these we shall necessarily make brief mention in order.

The first is that the excellence of the divine power may be manifested, for he alone is unchangeable and far above every position. Every mutable creature has no comparison with the unchangeable Creator. Hence it is read in Numbers: God is not like a man, that he should lie, nor like a son of man, that he should change. In Isaiah also it is said: God is faithful, without iniquity, just and upright. In the book of Tobit too it is written: You are just, O Lord, and all your judgments are true, and all your ways are mercy, truth, and judgment. And in the book of holy Job we read: Far be impiety from God, and iniquity from the Almighty.

For he will repay a man according to his work, and according to the ways of each he will restore to them. Therefore every man who rightly understands his own ignorance and weakness openly recognizes that he is nothing in comparison with his Creator. For God justly permits man to sin, both so that freedom of will may be preserved and so that the excellence of the great God may become known to men.

The second reason is that the inscrutable wisdom of God may become known to mortals, by which he knows how to draw good out of our evils, when he makes the sin of one person become a merit for another, for the increase of virtue. For if he did not permit evil to be done, who would understand our good? For God makes and ordains the good; but evils he does not make, but, once they have been done by the impious, he himself disposes them so that they do not come about in disorder. Neither does the wickedness of men hinder the will of God, nor can man do anything that God does not permit.

The third cause, moreover, why the Almighty permits acts of sorcery to be done is that he may manifest his most merciful goodness toward those who have sinned and done penance, which he could in no way show if he did not permit the creature to sin. For if there were no sinner, neither could God be merciful. But since we all offend in many things, everywhere God finds something to forgive; everywhere a guilty person presents himself.
