For it was neither fitting, nor could it in any way have come to pass, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect. For if mortal man, by the guidance of human nature, comprehended the immortal God, then certainly he would have to be called no longer man, nor mortal, but an angel; and either man would become comparable to God, or God to man. For so long as the angelic intellect stands between, the human intellect cannot understand God. But it stands between until the death of the flesh, when the mind, having obtained its natural freedom, will become wholly equal to an angel.

You now see, I believe, most manifestly, Emperor, that it was neither fitting nor possible that the human intellect should comprehend the divine, the created the creator, the temporal the eternal, the changeable the unchangeable, the weak the omnipotent, since, as a certain philosopher says, there is no proportion of the finite to the infinite. And therefore it is necessary for mortals to believe in the immortal God, whom we cannot comprehend by any manifest reasoning.

Although the order of the whole universe providently declares the author of all things, and although we understand that nothing apart from him was produced without a beginning, nevertheless what God is and in what manner he is the human mind cannot grasp, since he is far above all things that we can understand concerning him, or anything mortal whatsoever, and is far outside and above every affirmation, as holy Dionysius says. Indeed, he is neither soul nor sense, nor imagination or opinion, nor reason, nor intelligence; nor does he have reason or intelligence.

Nor is he spoken of, nor understood. He is neither number nor multitude, nor order, nor magnitude, nor smallness, nor equality; neither likeness nor unlikeness. He neither stands nor is moved; neither does he produce rest, nor does he have power, nor is he power. He is neither light, nor does he live, nor is he life. He is neither substance, nor age, nor time; nor is there spiritual contact of him. He is neither form, nor truth, nor kingdom, nor wisdom; neither one nor unity, nor deity or goodness; nor is he spirit, so far as we can know. Nor is there for him the denomination of son or father, nor anything else among those things that are known to us or to anyone else in the world. Nor is he anything of those things that are not, nor of those things that are; nor do the things that are know him as he is. There is no speech of him, nor name, nor knowledge. He is neither darkness nor light, neither error nor truth; nor is there of him any affirmation at all, nor negation. For he is above every affirmation, the perfect and singular cause of all things. And his excellence is above every negation, namely the excellence of him who is simply absolved from all things and is above all things.

Therefore we say, as Dionysius says, that since as the cause of all things he is above all things, he is neither without substance nor without life, nor does he lack reason or mind. Nor is he a body, nor figure, nor species,

nor does he have quality or quantity or mass; nor is he in a place, nor is he seen. Nor does he have sensible touch, nor does he sense, nor is he sensible. Nor is he subject to rashness or disturbance, agitated by fleshly passions; nor is he weakened by being subject to sensible accidents. Nor does he need light; nor does he admit change or corruption or division or increase; nor is he, or does he have, anything else belonging to sensible things.

Hence, since he is far above every affirmation and excels it, he could not be comprehended by a creature placed beneath him, which necessarily uses the senses for understanding, as the Peripatetic truly said: “There is nothing in our intellect unless it has first been in sense.” Therefore God, who is not subject to the senses of mortals, far exceeds the human intellect. According to affirmative affirmation, he truly is altogether in all things; and according to negative removal, by which one approaches more closely, he is none of all the things that are subject to the cognition of mortals. Indeed, since he is above all things, he cannot be any of those things that are subject to him. For this reason Empedocles beautifully gave the similitude, saying: God is a sphere whose center is everywhere and circumference nowhere. Let the human intellect now raise itself as much as it can.
