For even if among us all our things are finite, nevertheless many things often occur which, because of the slowness of our understanding, seem infinite to us. Who will be able to count the drops of the sea? And who will penetrate the inscrutable heart of man? Just as finite and circumscribable number can be led onward by progression into infinity, so the arrangement of this art is enlarged immeasurably by varied transposition. Whatever is seen anywhere in the whole world conforms in every respect to our arrangement, nor is there anything known to us that is empty of mystery, if the method is understood from the teacher. But we, persuaded by reason, have settled upon the service of the binary, and briefly explain the smallest part of the whole. Nor is an occasion given us to raise our attempt to weightier matters; but if an acceptable occasion should be offered to us, we shall not refuse to make more excellent things clear to those who desire them. For it is written: Where there is no hearing, never pour out speech. Therefore, adding the second book of the Polygraphia to the first, we keep the same form and method, setting out in the light of day three hundred and eight paraphrases of simple progression for honorable remembrance of the most chaste Mother of God; if time had been favorably at hand, we could have given more graceful things, and, with sufficient broad unfolding, more extensive things. But studious posterity will easily provide both, so as to add brilliance to the material and sufficient progress to the form. The first inventor of any art transmits greater things to posterity, because, since the beginnings of an art are hard and unfamiliar, no subtle invention is found perfected from its origin. From Peapolis, which is also Herbipolis, a city of the eastern Franks, on the twelfth day of the month of February, in the year of the Lord 1508.
