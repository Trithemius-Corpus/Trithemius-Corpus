By these they themselves attempted in particular what we have promised universally in our Polygraphy. From their number, for the sake of the example proposed, we have first introduced Caesar Augustus, whose secret method of writing you may understand plainly in this way.

He changed the breathings of the conceived voice from clear signs into obscure ones, and by a certain beautiful invention, by a movement of metathesis, made their barbarousness, resistant to wishes, obedient to his command. That is, the great Caesar Augustus, so that he might write secretly and safely whatever he wished, changed the letters of the alphabet by various transpositions, so that he put B for A, C for B, and so on with each letter in turn. And so that the writing, being more twisted, might remain more secret, he transposed the letters at his own discretion, breadthwise and lengthwise, to any distance of order, number, and place.

Mystery. And this receptive order, as though perpetual, subject to the will and to the reckoning of time, although it keeps messengers safe on the road from the attack of spies, nevertheless, since it has no regulated limits, makes all who encounter it anxious with suspicion of a hidden matter.

Explanation. Although no one can easily grasp with the understanding the secret mystery lying hidden beneath the transposition of letters, nevertheless this writing makes those into whose hands it comes suspicious of the hiding place of the matter enclosed within.

Mystery. Archimedes, that Syracusan, easily the foremost of mathematicians, his white bird, suited to his principles, naked through head and feet, and so forth.

Explanation. Archimedes had two little rods of the same length and thickness fashioned, either round or square. He kept one for himself and delivered the other to his absent friend. And whenever one wished to write something more secret to the other, he wound the white bird, that is, a sheet of papyrus of small width but of twice the length of the rod, around the wood, and fastened it with wax above and below. He then wrote on the sheet, which had been continuously and artfully joined to itself at the sides in a heap, whatever he wished. Then he loosened the sheet from the rod, and at once so great a distance appeared between the letters that not even a single word cohered with another. He turned the sheet

Mystery. Marcus T. Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, using talent and art, changed the small elements of speech into signs, and the rest.

Explanation. He wrote a book of shorthand signs for his son, which the blessed martyr Cyprian later enlarged for the use of Christians: that is, in the manner of a dictionary, where first the characters or signs are placed in order, and afterward the words designated by those same characters, so that any given word is signified by the sign placed before it. We once saw, written in these signs or characters, a complete and beautifully copied Latin psalter in the library of the greater church of Strasbourg. Some man, ignorant of judgment in such matters, had placed on its outside the following title: Psalter in the Armenian language. But he was wrong, since it should rather have been written thus: Latin psalter written in Tullian characters. For each character represents one Latin word.

Nor am I mistaken in this judgment, since I possess the very dictionary of Cicero, corrected by Saint Cyprian, written complete and uncorrupted in an ancient volume, which I have seen nowhere else. So great is the abundance of signs or characters, with the words subject to them, that they are altogether sufficient for writing in the Latin language whatever you wish.

Mystery. Faramund, the forty-third from Marcomer king of the Franks, when in the year of the Lord’s nativity 424 he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, and the rest.

Explanation. From the time when the kings of the Franks, having gone out with their people from the Scythian borders in the year 430 before the nativity of Christ, settled at the mouths of the Rhine, where Guelders now is, they waged continual war with the Gauls for almost nine hundred years, and at last violently made them subject to themselves, as we have written in the first volume of our Annals. From these, the aforesaid King Faramund, so that he might send messengers safely through the kingdom, devised new letters of his own, which no one could know without his voluntary instruction. Nor did he use only one new alphabet, nor a few, but many.
