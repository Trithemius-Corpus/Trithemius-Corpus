After Orifiel of Saturn, as those who hold such opinions say, the intelligence Anael, the mover of Venus, received the governance of the world by influence and continued it for 354 years, and for four months, days, and hours. Under him human beings began to be more cultivated, to build houses and cities more frequently, and also to invent various manual arts, namely the work of weaving, wool-working, and many other things like these. They also began to sink more deeply into the pleasures of the flesh, to take beautiful wives, and, after many children had been begotten from them, they notably fell away from their natural simplicity. They invented games and songs, began to play the cithara, and more eagerly devised whatever they affirm belongs to the influence of Venus, following too much the movement of the flesh.

They named the third intelligence of Jupiter Zachariel. Taking up the dominion of the world after Anael, he made human beings restless with desire for rule, made them engage in hunting, make tents, and adorn themselves with various garments. While he was ruling, in accordance with the character of Jupiter, who deprived his father of the kingdom, a great division arose between the good and the wicked: the former calling upon God, the latter given over to the flesh and the world. Under this governance Adam, the first man, died, leaving to all posterity the testament of dying once.

The fourth mover, or angel, of Mercury, Raphael, after Zachariel, began to exert influence upon worldly affairs in the 1055th year of the world, at the end of the month of February, and lasted until the 1410th year of the world. Under him the use of letters is said to have been invented first. The letters then, as they report, were quite different from those that the world has today, devised according to the form and likeness of trees, plants, and animals; yet with time they were afterwards changed in many ways, and took on an easier mode of formation and a more cultivated adornment. For the fictions of the poets said that Mercury was the patron of all literature, promising that great hidden mysteries of things lay beneath their stories.

After this, when the human race had multiplied and spread everywhere over the whole earth, the unity of letters did not long remain. Rather, the separation of bodies and minds far from one another discovered diverse and various modes of writing, concerning which the account would be long for us. Plato the philosopher, in the Phaedrus, says that letters and the use of writing,

together with the theory of weights and numbers and the game of dice, were first invented by a certain philosopher named Theuto, and that he taught them to Thamus, king of the Egyptians. But concerning the rest of the ancient men who are said to have given special letters to nearly individual nations and peoples, which remain in their use down to the present day, it is not my purpose on this occasion to set down or mark out an account.

For all the more learned men of our time know that Cadmus, son of Agenor, king of the Phoenicians, is said first to have brought sixteen letters into Greece: α. β. γ. δ. ε. ζ. ι. λ. μ. ν. ο. π. ϱ. σ. τ. ψ. To these Palamedes, at the time of the Trojan War, is reported to have added four: ξ. θ. φ. χ. And Pythagoras afterwards added the letter θ, so that they would be twenty-one. χ. κ. ω. were subsequently invented by others. Jordanes, bishop of the Goths, gave letters proper to his people.

When the unity of language had been confused, the simplicity of characters too was varied in many ways. Hence it came about that the use of letters, in the course of time, claimed such great license for itself that not only individual peoples, but also individual men, fashioning new characters for letters at their own discretion, prepared hiding places for their mysteries. Yet every writing in an unusual character will not be free from suspicion among those who see it. Notwithstanding this, kings, tyrants, and many ancient princes once discovered for themselves a familiar mode of writing set apart from the common one, taking thought for the suspicion of others, provided that what they wished to remain secret was not perceived.

In imitation of these, I too, though very well known, and desiring to bring this work of Polygraphy, as far as possible, to a perfect conclusion, have resolved in this sixth and final book to recall from the underworld certain characters of the ancients which have now ceased from the use of mortals, lest they perish entirely.
