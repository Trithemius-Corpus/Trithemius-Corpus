Six Books of Polygraphy, by Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapolitani, formerly of Sponheim, addressed to Emperor Maximilian.

There has been added one book, the Key to Polygraphy, by the same author.

Contained in these books is the method by which one person can communicate to another whatever he wishes, not only secretly, but sometimes even without suspicion. They also present a method most useful both in teaching and in learning. In addition, they provide the capacity for understanding the deepest mysteries of the arts and disciplines.

Explanations of several passages have also been added, especially of those in which the principles of the admirable work Steganography lie hidden; these indeed offer ingenious persons an occasion for discovering things far greater and subtler. By the very learned man Adolph von Glauburg, patrician of Frankfurt.

Frankfurt,
1550.

Bavarian State Library

To the Divine Emperor Maximilian: the letter of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Saint James at Würzburg, concerning Polygraphy.

I was wavering in mind, most unconquered Emperor Maximilian, and was somewhat uncertain within myself whether I would be doing something worthwhile if I dedicated the manuscript of our Polygraphy to your imperial majesty. For among the great you are the greatest, among the more powerful the most powerful, and among any wise men by far the wisest; for that reason nothing should be inscribed to you unless it is great, nothing addressed to you unless it has been worked out with the highest learning. But I, having attained no literary learning, or very little, hesitated for quite some time, not without reason, over whether I ought to present my trifles to so great a majesty, since I am neither suited by talent to elaborate anything nor adorned with the necessary charm of speech. For I know, and cannot deny, how scanty my store is, how rough my talent, and how there is in me no vein of eloquence for speaking. Therefore, after due consideration, I stopped for a while, and as an inept writer I feared to appear before your glorious majesty. For since, after God, you are by right lord of the world,

it is no wonder if I, an unlearned man and the least of monks, fear the judgment of so great a majesty as yours and am afraid to offer those compositions of mine which I know are not worthy of your hands. And while I was turning these matters over in my mind, a messenger arrived with letters sent by your highness, in which you summoned me to Cologne, the expenses necessary for the journey having already been sent earlier through another. But when I had read your serenity’s letter, confidence at once overcame modesty, and consideration of your innate gentleness drove fear away. For I know your nature to be most mild: since you are the most modest of all, you are accustomed both to be gentle to the humble and to resist the proud. You do not know how to be vainly puffed up, nor, in the manner of tyrants, to despise the humble who are devoted to your highness, since just as you have no further height to which you may rise on earth, so no envy will penetrate your mind. You are greatest; you have also accomplished many great things, no less bravely than wisely, both in peace and in war. By these you have gained an everlasting name, and with reverence for the highest honor you are celebrated by the love and favor of all your subjects. These are the riches of princes: the love and favor of the people, and doing justice to each according to the commandment of God’s charity. Therefore, since all carry your name to the stars with praises, and everyone proclaims you most mild and most humane, why should I suppose that you in particular would be more severe toward me?
