We have shown a sufficient method of numbering by Latin letters, after the manner of the Hebrews and Greeks. We have reasonably placed it here by way of digression, although it is also found earlier in the sixth book. And by the fact that, as we proceed, we have here enclosed our number within thirty times one hundred thousand, we openly declare that it can be extended to infinity. If this method of numbers displeases anyone, he will easily be able to devise a better one.

Also, in imitation of the Northmen, from the numerical letters written above we have arranged a new alphabet, by which you will be able safely to write and report to an absent friend whatever secret you wish. This method of writing is so secret, as well as new and never before us seen or heard of, that it seems to me wholly impossible that it could be understood by anyone without the service of a teacher. But now let us set down its alphabet.

Behold, then: you now have here an alphabet made from the Roman numerical letters in imitation of the Northmen. With it you will be able most safely to write and report to an absent friend whatever you wish, and no hidden observer will be able to penetrate your secret, except the one who knows the secret of the art. For all who see the writing will think that the letters have been transposed, and they will labor in vain in reckoning them. Let us set down an example.

Iaaihid ifeib, iefiegeicehfib. oa' eihid. i et Fahb. gidibid. eigh. ietigigh ib ig. & if. iaaihifid, ihe icda'ia. eih. icie&'iaib.

The hidden meaning of this writing is as follows: Beware the bearer of the present letters, because he is a very wicked man, a thief, a robber, a liar, and worthless. By this demonstration of an example it is easily given to be understood how safe and secret this method of writing is universally, in every language known to the writer,

and in every cause or business, however arduous, secret, great, or lengthy. Nor do I think there is a man living in the world who, from his own ingenuity and without a teacher, could read this very writing. For all who see it will think it nothing other than that the letters have been transposed; and when they cannot obtain an understanding of them, at last, wearied by despair, they will cease from their investigation, and whatever you have written will remain hidden. For no one, as I think, will examine by his own powers this art of writing secretly as it is, unless he has either the Polygraphy, or this key to it, at hand, or has been instructed by another.

From these numerical letters many other alphabets can also be given according to the order of numbering, when the beginning for writing alphabetically is taken not from unity but from any other following number. But in that progression you should know that one must take care that no more than two numerable characters be taken to represent one letter. Therefore, when you have arrived at wowo, that is, ten times one hundred thousand, stop your course and do not wander further, lest the multitude of characters confuse the beauty of writing by corrupting it. For after unity is excluded, ten times one hundred thousand, with the characters included, are sufficient for almost thirty individual alphabets. Yet the method of the first alphabet, beginning with unity, is better for enveloping written secrets in proportion as it is shorter and simpler. Finally, it renders the writing less tortuous.
