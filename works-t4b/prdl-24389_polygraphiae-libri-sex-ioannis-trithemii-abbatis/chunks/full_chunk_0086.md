THE THIRD BOOK
of the Polygraphia of Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapol, of the Order of Saint Benedict, begins.

What has confused the unity of language from the beginning of human creation? Was it not the division of bodies and the variety of minds? For as Truth declares in the Gospel: “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.” For the tongue is the instrument for bringing forth those conceptions which the mind has inwardly thought within itself. Yet there are many ways of conveying the secrets of the mind to perception through this ministry, and their properties vary both by use and by art. Usage, introduced at pleasure, is common to many; art, however, to few. Finally, even the very unregulated practice of speaking, how much it has varied among the Germans alone in six hundred years, no one will readily estimate unless he has often reread Otfried, the monk of Weissenburg, in his poems in the German language. The sea flows and ebbs; human beings, once they die, do not return again to the present life. Customs succeed customs, and the vigor of mortal minds always declines toward softer things. Who today among the Germans, those ancient emulators of the Greeks, keeps knowledge of the old idioms? In the chronicles and ancient histories of the Franks, Hunibald, Doracus, and the other writers who preceded us by a thousand years mixed into their compositions many sayings, both in old verse and in prose, of the German speech; today these are understood by few. Why do I speak of ancient times, when our own idiom among the East Franks, Swabians, Bavarians, Rhinelanders, Mosellans, Cologne people, Saxons, and Thuringians undergoes notable variation every day? Human beings change in time, and customs vary;

minds change, and garments vary; the simplicity of languages is altered, and the ancient property of idioms varies, and nothing stable is found in human affairs. For there is a vicissitude of all things, and variety succeeds the simplicity of minds. Who will be able to reduce this perpetual flowing-away, so to speak, to the concord of unity? For times pass, as Scripture says, and knowledge will be manifold.

That we add a third book to the two preceding books of the Polygraphia has not lacked a special reason. For since the wills of human beings are varied, many methods are rightly given for honorable operations. In those former ones, day illuminates night; in these, however, day lacks light. There light leads shadow around in light; here, however, the blind illuminates the blind, and, existing and remaining without eyes, supplies in darkness the sight which he does not have. Here night perpetually darkens day with gloom, and one walking in darkness without light provides guidance for the blind. These paraphrases serve all human beings throughout the world, and in every language they transmit any secret safely, securely, and without danger to the cabalistic binary. Latin raises laughter among barbarians, and barbarism itself arouses suspicion among Latins. Yet it matters nothing, or little, since however great the suspicion may be, it will nevertheless contain every most secret mystery.

Let the diligent operator recall to mind the rules and precepts that we have handed down, lest he labor uselessly and in vain for himself and for others. For understanding is required, without which the usefulness of no art will stand. We have shown in a few things the path to the greatest things, and with finite things we have given a method for infinite things. From things given and received, greater things are easily prepared from crumbs. Reader, remember that the retrograde mark can be set in every place until you reach the mark of rest.

Farewell. From Peapol, on the first day of March, in the year of the Lord 1508.

Y y
