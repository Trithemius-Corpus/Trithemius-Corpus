For this reason we have set small divisions before the individual degrees by an artful invention, so that in these matters we might relieve the practitioner from the labor of numbering the periphrases. If you are industrious, diligent, and studious in those things that we have handed down as the principles of a new art, believe us: you will discover greater things lying hidden than we have promised. Indeed, since many natural secrets are contained in these matters, know this one thing, not the least: by the prudent operation of metathesis, whenever you wish, you will make even the most skilled person in this art ignorant, the seeing blind, and the learned unskilled. If the false opinion of the Hebrews were true, this invention of ours would give to each person among eighteen thousand worlds his own ways of writing and announcing, safely and secretly and most widely, whatever hidden things they wished in Latin.

THE BEGINNING OF THE SECOND BOOK, OR CHAPTER,
OF THE POLYGRAPHY
OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
ABBOT OF SPANHEIM,
OF THE ORDER OF SAINT BENEDICT.

The reckoning method of mortals should imitate nature by art, since the monad admits no calculation. Therefore, although the arrangement of the preceding book of the Polygraphy, through the extension and metathesis of particulars, may suffice, as it were, for the universals of this art in infinite progressions of number, nevertheless a definite consideration has moved us to join a second book to the first. The condition of light in each is different, but night preserves the order of its course inviolate in both. The prudent reader, from these small inventions of ours, not polished quite to the rule, will easily be able to discover greater and more polished ones, if he flourishes in excellent talent. Whatever visible thing is placed in the whole circuit of the world offers ready support for the service of this new art of ours. Air and fire, water and earth, springs and rivers, trees and fruits, flowers and grasses, words and names, accident and substance, stones and sand, dung and filth, things and words, honorable and shameful matters, woods and metals, hard and liquid things, things existing and not existing, history and fable, beasts and dumb animals, men and beasts of burden, each and all things that speech can express or the mind can think, furnish the broadest field for our Polygraphy, and promise that the art, so far as it can be established among mortals, will be expanded without limit.
