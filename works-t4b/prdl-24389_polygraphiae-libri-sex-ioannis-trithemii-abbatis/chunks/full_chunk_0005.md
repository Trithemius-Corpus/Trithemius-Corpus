Preface of Johannes Trithemius, abbot of Saint James at Wuerzburg, to Emperor Maximilian, on the Polygraphia.

We read that many of the wise men of old, philosophers, kings, and princes, once devised various and manifold methods by which they might safely entrust to their messengers, when sending them to more distant places, the secrets of counsel and whatever hidden mystery had to be confided, so that they might be fit to carry them and secure from every assault of wicked men, lest either the simplicity of the cabalists should lay bare the mystery, or the inborn curiosity of the bacutii should bring harm to apostolic matters.

Among these, the great Caesar Augustus of the Romans, wishing in difficult affairs to use the obscure service of messengers, changed the breathings of conceived speech from clear forms into dark ones; and by a fine invention, through the dance of metathesis, he made their barbarity, though resistant to his wishes, conform to his command. And this receptive and, as it were, permanent order, nevertheless subject to intention and to the calculation of time, although it keeps messengers safe on the road from the attack of the bacucei, yet, since it does not keep within its regulated bounds, will make all who look on anxious with suspicion of the matter.

Archimedes of Syracuse, easily the foremost of mathematicians, affixed all around a white bird suited to his arrangements, bare at head and feet, with a staff cut straight into the form of a tetragon; and, when the attendants had been arranged in due proportion, he entrusted the work to the winged instrument, until the image of the white bird appeared changed into a magpie. When this had been properly completed, he loosened with his own hand what had been fastened; and when he had allowed it to fly away, there was suddenly a great separation among the familiar faces of the inner chambers, and soon there arose the image of a tortuous

Marcus T. Cicero, the eloquent orator, using both talent and art, changed the small elements of expression into signs, and set the industrious device, or character, of his intention in place of the customary phrase. Indeed, in this very novelty he used such abundance that his invention could suffice for nearly all things in the world. To these heralds of mysteries the discipline of the art gave a wonderful agility, so that, contrary to the nature of all things, they may serve, solid and intact, the good will of their master. A king can always use their service very safely, though not without great labor and the manifest suspicion of the bucacei.

Cyprian, the most holy bishop and martyr of the church of Carthage, wishing to provide for the benefit of Christians, considerably enlarged the very lessons of the Ciceronian system, and added what had been lacking for our customs.

Pharamund also, the forty-third after Marcomer, most powerful king of the Franks in Germany, when in the year of the Lord's birth 424 he powerfully entered the borders of the Gauls, bound foreign minutiae to his secrets, so that everything might be done safely.

Clodius Crinitus, his elder son, succeeding his father in the kingdom of the Franks, after he had violently subjected all Gaul to his rule, ordered all the Gauls, as conquered men and slaves, to be shorn and their beards shaved; but he commanded the Franks, conquerors and masters of the Gauls, to grow their hair and beard. From this he was called Crinitus. In imitation of his father he wrapped the mysteries of counsel in signs both his own and foreign.

Charlemagne by surname, the sixty-third king of the Franks after Marcomer, who was the first of the Franks to rule the Romans as emperor, with Nanno the grammarian as his teacher, wished to embellish the German language, which was his native tongue, with grammars after the model of Latin. He began an arduous work, which, however, being entangled in many affairs and overtaken by death, he did not complete. He bound owls to various and many minutiae, by which [unclear] greater born Brabant.
