Alchemy has many household servants, who guard their mistress with continual vigilance and put themselves forward in her name, so that they may preserve her forever untouched by the intercourse of so many importunate lovers. Vanity, fraud, deceitfulness, deception, sophistic falsification, greed, falsehood, lying confidence, folly, want, poverty, despair, flight, outlawry, and beggary are alchemy’s handmaids. Pretending to be the beloved mistress, they keep her inviolate, and willingly prostitute themselves to her seekers: the moneyed, the avaricious, the greedy, and the silly.

ON THE SIGNS AND WONDERFUL METHOD, BUT ONE TOO LABORIOUS, OF WRITING OF M. T. CICERO, AND AFTER HIM OF SAINT CYPRIAN, BISHOP AND MARTYR.

Marcus Tullius Cicero, the eloquent orator of the Romans, wrote a book of signs of no small size, which Saint Cyprian, prelate of the Carthaginians and martyr, enlarged with many signs and words,

--- Page 546 ---

adding terms necessary for Christian uses, so that the work itself became useful not only to pagans but much more also to the faithful.

The codex is rare and was found by me only once, and bought at a low price. For when, in the year of the Lord’s nativity 1496, I was looking through many libraries out of love for books, I found this memorable codex in a certain monastery of our order, neglected because of its extreme age, cast aside under dust and despised. I asked the abbot, a doctor of law, how much he valued it. He replied that he would prefer to it the small works of Saint Anselm, lately printed. I went to the booksellers, since the matter occurred in a metropolitan city; I bought the requested small works of Anselm for one sixth part of a florin, gave them to the rejoicing abbot and monks, and rescued the codex, which was already almost brought to destruction. For they had determined, out of desire for the parchment, to destroy it.

Having been admitted by Johann Geiler of Kaysersberg, the distinguished preacher of that place, into the library of the greater church, I found an entire Psalter written in these same signs of Tullius and Cyprian, and most fittingly written with golden capitals. But an inscription of this sort had been placed on the outside by someone ignorant of the mystery: “Psalter in the Armenian language.” I summoned the doctor, showed the falsity, and warned that it should be rewritten: “Psalter written in Ciceronian signs.”

Whether he did so or not I am uncertain, since I did not afterwards return to that library. So that you may see, reader, the form of Cicero’s notation, we have decided to add a few signs below.
