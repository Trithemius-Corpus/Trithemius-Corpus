We rejoice that the end of the first book of our Polygraphy is at hand; having been made readier for what remains, we judge that the powers of our talent should be tested. For newly discovered beginnings sometimes cause disturbance, because the one whom all follow has no one going before him. The first beginning of any invention is the more difficult; after it has been made firm, it brings greater confidence to its author. Therefore, completing with God’s favor and help the first book of our invention, we have divided the whole into three hundred eighty-four small units, assigning to each in order, as is seen, its own grades of periphrases. And these form a sufficiently beautiful Christian harmony: sixteen way-stations, with more than two hundred nine thousand phrases duly joined together in every respect.

Nothing is lacking here for students of these inventions of ours, so that in mystical, secret, and hidden matters, even in matters to be announced at a greater distance with the utmost security, they may have the fullest knowledge and faculty. All things that are contained in the sphere of the art proceed in circle and point; their atoms constitute a circular line, and, by the multitude of light, a straight one. Whatever is outside the line serves the common reckoning of day, not of night. For night, always enclosed within the limits of the small units and paraphrases, never goes out beyond them.

Therefore, whenever you cross the straight line of day and do not fulfill the purpose of the nocturnal syntax, turn the head back to the tail; and, when the connection has been made, let a binding of both with one another be made. Arrange the shadow in such a way that it does not confuse the sun by similarity of voice. And do not fear embarrassment if, in the first calculation, the day has not comprehended the shadow; rather, carefully consider the small units of the lines of day, and count one together with the other until the necessary proportion follows according to your wish. You will also be able to make a leap from the first into the second, knowing that in the first creation of days there was made evening and morning, one day. But when evening has been completed, bring this day back into the following one; and, when the sign has been given by the point, fix the mystery known at once to the binary and to the monad.

But so that you may be able to avoid this difficulty, distinguish the great revolutions of our whole circle by arithmetical degrees; and where day is repeated for night, place the mark of Archimedes upon the flying one, lest the immature ternary [unclear] return to unity.
