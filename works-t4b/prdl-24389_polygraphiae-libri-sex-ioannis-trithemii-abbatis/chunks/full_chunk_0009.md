In the first book of this Polygraphy of ours, we arrange and distribute the words of the Latin language, as many as appear there, in the order of our ternary system. By their use and service, princes throughout the world will find safe assistance in any secret matters; without danger to the mystery, without any mark, and without suspicion from owls, it will remain perpetually hidden. By these means the way is known to be safe even for owls, and never to be violated by anyone’s investigation. Knowledge of the hidden matter is granted to two persons only, if the binary is skillfully brought back to the monad. Ignorance is the mother of error, and one who scorns learning the good that he does not know loves blindness.

The periphrases themselves are set forth in the manner either of one advising or of one entreating, and can be varied for every visible subject in this world. For they are suitable to individual mysteries, and are set before Greek secrets no less than before our own. When you have understood the things that you see, do not rashly despise the hidden things that you do not see. You see the accidents; the substance remains invisible. Thus the greater things lie hidden, the lesser lie open to those who look. Since this has been done with reason, let no good man take it for evil, since from these things any suitable person may advance to higher matters, provided that he wishes to do so.

For my part, it has not pleased me on this occasion to pour forth higher things into the light, since an unsuitable time seems to stand in the way. I have set the letters of the Latin alphabet in order before the individual words with this consideration: that the operator may have an easy method in the proposed task, and that the correspondent in the reply may be relieved of excessive labor. are not devoid of the mystery of the art, since they serve luminous propositions for the completion of the necessary perfection, not obscure ones. Therefore they are also taken up in the former and neglected in the latter. For they themselves are the sure means by which every suspicion of an opponent is removed. All things have been set down with reason, so that not even one proposition is introduced idly.

For example, the farther the sun advances from the equinoctial circle, the more the days are lengthened for those who dwell in those regions. Therefore, so that unity may advance in right order through the ternary into the binary, the pattern that we hand down must be observed with the greater care. First, then, after prior consideration, let the intelligent operator of his intention observe what he is to accomplish by the ternary through the binary; let him divide the day with the night into minutes, and distinguish the small divisions of the winds not by another’s assumption of phrasing, but by his own.

In every mystical operation let him carefully beware lest he overturn the established order of the art, substitute one paraphrase for another, or assign to one what is due to another; otherwise unity does not admit the binary, and the whole operation will be confused and useless. Let the night not wander with excessive length, nor advance unsteadily beyond the limits established for it by the art; let curiosity not change water into snow; and let not the man sleep by day who keeps watch by night. Let the mind, alert in intellect, also make use of art, lest the hand by its own levity commit an error which, slight and small in the monad, is accustomed always to appear great and very large in the binary.

One paraphrase from any given order is admitted only into the service of the secret. According to the example of the servant, whenever a small particle of the day’s light demands it, let one phrase likewise adhering to it be taken up, not several. These things must be entrusted to a vigilant mind, and one must beware lest the wind be animated. After this a rule is prescribed for the person approaching the practice of the art: as far as he can by the mystical art, let him imitate the single person of a human being, consisting firmly of two natures, namely the soul, which surpasses visible things, and the flesh, which remains visible. For just as the soul is not seen, although it is placed in a visible body, so the secret of the mind, in order that it may remain secret and nevertheless operate at will, requires the guidance of light while continuing in its workshop of darkness. Therefore, whenever the body serves the soul, the servant the master, the will reason,

Next, we likewise advise the person who wishes to proceed that, whenever a leaf is moved by the wind, he should discern the property of each nature, compare the night itself with the day by degrees and minutes, and conform the darkness to the light. But if Phoebus should prejudice the night, or the night the sun, by which a confusion of eclipse would arise, either let unity take back the binary which it dismissed in the light, or let the shadow of night be reduced to the first line of the sun. Keep fixed in memory whatever has been produced anywhere in number, weight, and measure.

Therefore, when you have encountered the knot of the difficulty just mentioned, number the minute portions of the night, note also the paraphrases of the daylight, and so blend one from the other that the necessary proportion may follow the action. Beware lest, while an eclipse of the sun endures, you interrupt the necessary labor. Pay no less attention to remembering the ancient fathers: Thuyścon, who was the grandson of Noah and father of the Teutons, and Marcomer, who is read to have been the first to lead the people of the Franks from the borders of Scythia into Germany, to the mouths of the Rhine. For the Franks are brothers of the Greeks and were mingled with the Germans, once making two languages from one. Hence, according to the oracle, those who have lost one g receive g.g.g.

Finally, diligent inquirer into Polygraphy, you are warned that after you, eager for these inventions like one thirsty for water, have attained the true method of operation, you should guard all these things with foresight and not pour them out indiscriminately into the knowledge of all people, especially filthy men. For this reason do not ask, since you know that there is nothing in the world so good and holy that the wicked do not very often abuse it for evil.
