A letter made from fresh milk is also safe, and deceives the eyes; touch it with powdered charcoal and you will read it. That too deceives which is made with the point of a moist flax-thread; a little tablet brings hidden marks to light.

There are also other devices: for instance, they paint letters on flint with the fat of a goat or ox; when it has been placed in vinegar for several hours and then taken out again, it displays to sight what had been written on it. Likewise, they write with egg white and dip it into ink; when this is scraped off, the letters become visible. Finally, in this passage he has wrapped many things in obscure words not at all accepted in common usage, lest he give depraved people an opportunity either to misuse these things or to devise worse ones. He wrote race, with the syllables reversed, for cera, just as Roma for amor, nemilua for alumine, raphocam for camphora, etc. But since such matters must be handled rather sparingly for the public good, and do not belong to our undertaking, let us omit explaining everything. It is enough if it is understood what he wished to signify by this third kind of letters.

“There were also, as Bede attests)” The sense is this: the ancients not only wrote or spoke by words, as we do, but also, by a certain deliberate practice, transferred to the signification of things whatever the world contains and whatever can be comprehended by us. Diodorus Siculus, in book 4, says: “The letters of the Egyptians are very like various living creatures, the extremities of men, and tools, especially those of craftsmen. For their words are not expressed by the composition of syllables or by letters, but by the form of images, their meaning having been handed down to human memory by usage. They write, for example, a hawk, a crocodile, a serpent, a human eye, hands, a face, and other things of this sort. The hawk denotes a thing done quickly, since this bird is almost the swiftest of all; this notation is transferred to domestic affairs that are done swiftly. The crocodile signifies evil. The eye is interpreted as the preserver of justice and guardian of the whole body. Among the remaining parts of the body, the right hand with the fingers spread out denotes freedom,

but the left hand with the fingers clenched denotes graspingness and avarice.” In the same way, by the figure of the other parts of the body and by tools they marked some definite thing; and when these had been preserved in human memory by long usage and reflection, people recognized at once what they expressed.

Petrus Crinitus also, book 5, chapter 2, writes that this kind of method of writing existed among the Egyptians: “Concerning the letters of the Egyptians, which are called hieroglyphic, mention is found among the ancient authors, and this can still be observed on obelisks. On them those figures and images of various animals, plants, rivers, and trees are carved, and they designate the philosophy of that nation, as Strabo and Pliny, very weighty authors, testify. For on the obelisks of the kings Sesostris and Nuncoreus, which were brought from Egypt into the city, an interpretation of the nature of things was recorded by symbols of this kind and by hieroglyphic signs. Thus the philosophy of the Egyptians is believed first to have been made manifest, as is reported by the same Pliny and by others. Among other images they were accustomed to mark scarabs, bees, rivers, oxen, vultures, and other things of that sort. Moreover, from the very image of the bee the symbol of the king was signified, because he, in carrying out the duties of life, ought to have no less the sting of justice than the sweet clemency of honey. In the vulture they indicated the genius and majesty of nature, because among these birds females alone are found,” etc. They also painted many other signs in place of words; on these see Horapollo On Hieroglyphic Letters, Pliny book 36, chapter 8, and likewise chapter 12, where, according to the more correct reading, Sesostris is found for Sesotidis and Phaeron for Nuncoreus. Strabo, book 17, Cornelius Tacitus, book 13, Coelius Rhodiginus, Antiquarum lectionum book 29, chapter 26, and Volaterranus, book 33, also made mention of letters of this kind, although he writes that they have not yet been discovered by anyone.

“There were those who by accidents)” The meaning is this: whatever things exist in the whole nature of things, whether they are perceived by the intellect or by the senses, can be turned to this use, so that they signify in the same way as words.
