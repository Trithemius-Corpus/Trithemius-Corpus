V. Fifth, we advise that whenever you wish to write by this art about a difficult matter, you prepare for yourself a small booklet, in which you write your secret mystery in open discourse. Whoever sees or reads this booklet will not think that any secret lies hidden there; and even if he has a suspicion, he will not be able to discover anything of what you are writing.

VI. Sixth, it should be noted that if you transpose the order of the words throughout the whole Polygraphia, changing it by metathesis in each alphabet, and have two volumes written according to it, one for yourself and the other for any friend, you will write between yourselves, with the greatest security, whatever you wish; nor does anyone live on earth who could penetrate your secrets. This transposition, moreover, can be made in infinitely many ways.

VII. Seventh, we forewarn you that one must write polygraphically in one way to a person ignorant of the Latin language, and in another way to one skilled in it. For the former, all the words must be written out in full, without abbreviation, distinctly, completely, beautifully, and in every way legibly, without separation, without division, and without confusion, lest, deceived by an error, he think that one word is two, or judge that two are one. The things that are set down must be heeded.

VIII. Eighth, care must be taken that the person to whom you write mystically by this art possess a book like yours in every respect; otherwise you would write in vain and fruitlessly to someone who does not understand.

IX. Ninth, if you wish this technique to remain forever secret and hidden, rewrite the Polygraphia anew, but do not place the letters of the alphabet before the words, and no one will easily suspect the method of the secret. But so that no difficulty may arise for the writer and reader in the mystery, let the letters of the alphabet be written on a small slip in the order corresponding to the words, and let it be fitted, movable, to the headings of those words. The letters may also be engraved on a copper plate, so that they can be moved from one order to another.

X. Tenth, it should be known that although we do not use the Pythagorean Y in the words of German and Latin, we have nevertheless admitted it on account of the Greek language, so that we might have the twelvefold number doubled. Likewise we have taken the doubled W as the twenty-fourth letter, because it is wholly necessary for us in the German language.

XI. Eleventh, the person who receives letters written from the Polygraphia to be read should know that understanding is needed in reading. And therefore, whenever he receives a sheet or booklet written polygraphically, he should go to a secret place and, after opening the book of the Polygraphia which he must have with him, examine each word through all the alphabets, and very carefully note with chalk on a table, or with ink on paper, what letter each one represents. And when he has gone through the whole letter in this way, let him compose the letters into words, and he will easily understand what lay hidden in secret.

XII. Twelfth, let kings and princes take note, who have many deputies in different places, to all of whom it is not prudent to write with one single kind of hidden narrative. Therefore, so that they may have a proper secret method of writing to each one individually, let them change the order of the words as many times as they wish to have separate methods. Thus they themselves will possess the methods of writing of all of them, and no subject will understand the method of another. For the variable methods of writing more secretly by this art are infinite.

XIII. Thirteenth, it should be noted that, in order to stop the mouths of those speaking wicked things, we have shown the truth of this new invention of ours to be possible by placing the words in the alphabets of the first book so that they cohere with one another in such a way that, in the manner of an exhortation and supplication offered to God, they produce a fitting and Latin discourse. Under this narrative we can conceal whatever secrets occur to us, and write in such a way to a friend skilled in the art that if all the people in the whole world came together as one, they could never discover the secret lying hidden beneath the open discourse, provided it has been arranged correctly.

XIIII. Fourteenth, it should be known that in this very invention of our Polygraphia there still lie hidden certain things far better than those written above: namely, how, in a few days, not to say hours, you will be able to instruct someone ignorant of the Latin language so that he knows how to write, read, and understand Latin, not indeed absolutely everything, but, in a fixed manner, whatever necessary matter may occur to him in his affairs on any subject whatever. Likewise, how you may safely, securely, and most secretly make known to your friend far away, who knows the technique of the art, all the conceptions of your mind without writings and with the messenger unaware, and sometimes even naturally without a messenger and without [unclear].
