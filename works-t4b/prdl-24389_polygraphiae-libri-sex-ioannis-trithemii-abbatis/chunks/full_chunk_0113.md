All the letters of the Greeks and Palestinians are numerical, as was said earlier, whereas among the Latin letters, which we Germans also use, only seven are counted as numbers, namely c, d, i, l, m, v, and x.

The Northmen, together with the Asiatic Dacians, once came out from the Scandinavian islands, which are now called Gothic, and for more than forty years, roaming through Gaul, devastated it very severely. In order to look after their secret affairs, as Bede the English monk testifies, they devised the following new and elegant mode of writing from Greek letters. For they adapted the Greek numbers, comprised in ten letters up to twenty-four, to Latin characters, and prudently concealed our letters under a foreign form, that is, in the way in which we use numbers in place of letters, when, for example, we put 1 for a, 2 for b, 3 for c, 4 for d, and so on in sequence to the end of the alphabet.

It was indeed an elegant and sufficiently safe invention, which neither a Greek nor a Latin would easily understand. For the whole alphabet is not found in it, but only nine letters and one numerical sign, namely g, which among them signifies six and in this mode of writing represents f, the sixth letter of the Latins. Moreover, yielding to our German language in which we were born, we have added double w at the very end in place of the twenty-fourth letter of our nation, which we have formed according to the Greek number in this way: kΛ. By the aid of this alphabet the Northmen wrote with secure transmission whatever they wished to keep hidden among themselves. No Greek could understand this method of writing, since, although all these characters are Greek, nevertheless, because they represent Latin letters according to numerical order, they could be read neither by a Greek nor by a Latin who had not previously acquired knowledge of this technique.

This method of writing can also be varied in many ways, when the numbers and letters are changed by metathesis. This can also be done with complete Greek letters, according to the wish and judgment of any industrious writer. Nor will the manner of forming these characters be very difficult, especially for someone who has had a little practice. The characters written above are also quite suited to the German language, since with them we can mark down everything that occurs to us, in whatever speech we are skilled. Whenever iota is placed by itself, so that it may be known to denote the letter l, the tenth of the alphabet, it is marked above in this way: ī.

It is the opinion of very many that the Germans once had letters from the Greeks, and they steadfastly affirm that they also used the Greek language, especially in their sacred rites. For they say that a great kinship between the Greek language and ours has now been utterly extinguished. That most learned man, my former teacher Johannes Camerarius Dalburgius, most reverend bishop of the church of Worms, had collected several thousand Greek words which mean the same thing in both languages, Greek and German. And Caesar testifies in the Commentaries that the Druids, the ancient priests of sacred rites in Gaul, used the Greek language, especially in sacred matters. But when the Romans were ravaging the world, they at last violently imposed the use of their own letters, together with their dominion, upon many nations.
