Thereafter, for nine hundred and fifty years, waging almost continual war with the Romans and the Gauls, as Hunibald is the authority, they finally brought nearly all Europe under their empire or kingdom. Yet, once they had accepted the letters of the Romans, they did not abolish them; rather, being themselves mingled among Germans and Gauls, they learned their language and preserved their letters and the form of their writing. Vuasthaldus, a writer of the histories of this people, wrote in his native language books of the deeds of kings, dukes, and princes over almost seven hundred and fifty-eight years, adding, as Hunibald says, the characters which he mentions in his volume, which he published in eighteen books on the origin of the Franks.

Unless a scribe’s error has deceived me, I have represented Vuasthaldus’s alphabet suitably, as I think. In it some characters differ altogether from the Greek ones, while some seem partly to resemble them. Yet I do not know what to judge: whoever their inventor was, although he did not preserve the form of the Greek letters, he did observe the order of the letters. The codex, perishing from excessive age, could scarcely be read; hence I fear that I have rather been deceived by some interpolator than discovered Hunibald’s true and certain meaning.

Whoever wishes to use the characters written above should know that they must be written below the lines, in the manner of the Greeks, not above, as the Latins do. Likewise it should be observed that, among doubled letters, the first forms are written at the beginning and in the middle of words, but the second forms always at the end.

FROM THE GRAMMATICAL WORKS OF OTFRID,
monk of Weissenburg.

I have read that Charles, king of the Franks, surnamed the Great, taking pity on the barbarism of his nation, with Nanno, Theobald, Albinus, and Berenger as helpers, attempted to regulate the German language. And when, as Turpin testifies, he had begun so difficult a work, he was held back by frequent incursions of wars and did not complete it, but, forestalled by death, left it unfinished; nor did another arise after him who would call the care of this matter to mind. But Otfrid,

the monk already mentioned, once the hearer and disciple of Rabanus of Fulda, under Louis the Pious, the son of that Charles, having obtained that grammar, though imperfectly, wrote many things in our German language which seem to sound with a remarkable dignity of regular instruction. For he composed many poems according to that same norm, in both heroic and elegiac meter; furthermore, he composed some things in prose for Louis, king of Germany, son of Louis the Pious and brother of the emperor Lothar; also for Otgar, archbishop of Mainz, for the monks of St. Gall, and for others. Having also obtained fragments of this grammar, I have extracted the present characters of the alphabet from many others, lest they should utterly perish. It is established, on Otfrid’s testimony, that Charles devised several alphabets of his own, which he used securely throughout his very broad kingdom in secret matters with individual prefects. From these, with Otfrid serving as our guide, we commit a few things to these letters.

King Charles the Great, the most Christian emperor, fought for thirty years with the Saxons, whom at last, overcoming them by the sword, he converted to the Christian faith. But fearing that they might again, as they had done many times, apostatize from the faith, he appointed certain secret investigators, to whom he granted judicial authority.
