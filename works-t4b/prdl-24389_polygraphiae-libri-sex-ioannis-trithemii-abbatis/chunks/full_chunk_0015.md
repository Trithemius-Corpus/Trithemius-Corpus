Charles, called the Great, the sixty-third king of the Franks, who was the first of the Franks to wear the diadem of the Roman empire, and the rest. Explanation. This man, the elder son of King Pippin, is read to have been born as duke of Brabant in the county of Namur, bordering on the people of Liège, although some assert that he was born in the village of Ingelheim, not far from Mainz. He too invented various methods of writing, and, with Nanno the grammarian lending his assistance, devised different alphabets, by which he communicated secrets to the prefects of the kingdom in a special manner. Mystery. The Northmen, who once came out from the borders of Gothia, when they were laying waste Gaul in a cruel invasion, called owls from Greece and prepared a safe hiding-place for their secrets, and the rest. Explanation. The Northmen, while ranging through Gaul, as the monk Bede in England bears witness, in order to write their secrets safely among themselves, substituted Greek numerals for Latin letters, from A as the first as far as the twenty-fourth point, W, or gu, according to the custom of the Gauls. This manner of writing is altogether very beautiful: in it all the letters are Greek, and according to the order of Pelasgian numbering they designate our Latin letters, as A, 1; B, 2; C, 3; D, 4; E, 5; F, 6; G, 7; and so consequently as far as W, which is 24. Neither a Greek nor a Latin will understand this writing unless he has first learned the method and art of the device. Mystery. Our Bede, a monk in England, not ignorant of the deed of the Northmen, described many ancient inventions similar to these, by which they made a path bright in mysteries into one that was dark. He himself set down artrology, and the rest. Explanation. This monk Bede, in his little book On the Inventions of the Ancients, reviewed many and various methods of writing and marking secretly, by which they safely announced their secrets to friends and spoke without voice to those present. He was the first of all to commend artrology and dactylogy to letters; by their use one man most securely discloses to another whatever secret he wishes, which no one else among the bystanders will notice, much less understand.

He taught that this same thing can be done in many other ways as well; since we have written nothing about them in the Polygraphy, we rightly decline to say more here. We long ago assigned the place proper to these difficult matters in the Steganography, which we do not yet intend to publish. And who could set out all the ancient methods of writing secretly? For some had as familiar servants in the practice of the art certain owls called Philophoti; others had Mysophoti; others called both into practice from the art. Again, some transposed the known letters of the common alphabet; others fashioned characters of their own for themselves under the guise of barbarian letters. Again, likewise, some used the service of visible letters, writing with cinnabar or common ink; others, however, formed invisible characters at will according to their purpose. The methods of visible characters for secret writing are exceedingly many and altogether innumerable; to mention all of them is neither our wish nor within our power. Of invisible characters there are three kinds more commonly used among mortals today. The first is called dermatic, by which a certain artificial writing is made on the backs of messengers, visible only through washing with a certain liquid. [unclear] The third kind is called alleotic, which is multiple and varied, both in form and in matter, and has almost innumerable methods of writing secretly. Its writing is made on paper or without paper, with nemilua and cala, with raphocam, lapolce, and various other things. Letters are likewise written in various ways and hidden in places: under race tabulata, under rosi rilacubior, in leopi, in necostas, in ceocali, locubat, in ratera, in lispilia, and in other nearly infinite things of the same sort. But since none of these things serves our present purpose on this occasion, we should therefore hasten to the things that the plan requires. There is also another kind, dactiletron, not indeed of writing, but of signs, by which one person can communicate the conception of his mind to another in very subtle ways.
