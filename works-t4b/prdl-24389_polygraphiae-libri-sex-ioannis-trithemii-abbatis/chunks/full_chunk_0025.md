The ancient Lacedaemonians, when they wished to disguise and conceal letters sent officially to their commanders, lest, if they were intercepted by enemies, their plans should become known, used to send letters made in the following kind. There were two round sticks, somewhat oblong, of equal thickness and the same length, shaved and finished in like fashion. One was given to the commander setting out for war; the other the magistrates kept at home under oath and seal. When there was need of more secret letters, they wound around the stick a long strip of leather of moderate thinness, or as much as the matter required, in a round and simple coil, so that the edges of the joined and adhering strip that was being folded met on every side. Then they wrote letters on that strip across the transverse edges of the joins, in lines running from top to bottom. When the strip had been written on in this way, they unrolled it from the stick and sent it to the commander, who knew the device. The unrolling of the strip made the letters broken and mutilated, and scattered their parts and strokes into widely separated places. Therefore, if that strip fell into the hands of enemies, they could conjecture nothing at all from it. But when the man to whom it had been sent received it, he took the matching stick that he had, wrapped the strip around it to the end just as he knew it had to be done, and so the letters, coming together again around the same circumference of the stick, once more joined up and presented a complete and uncorrupted letter, easy to read. The Lacedaemonians call this kind of letter a scytale. See also Plutarch in the Life of Lysander.

There is also another no less ingenious method of concealing letters, described by Jerome Cardano in his books On Subtleties. Take two equal sheets of parchment ruled for writing, and in the very lines make small holes scattered here and there in corresponding positions on both sheets, yet according to the size or height of the letters. Some will contain seven letters, some three, some eight or ten, so that all the holes together contain one hundred and twenty letters, when joined with all the letters that can be written in them.
