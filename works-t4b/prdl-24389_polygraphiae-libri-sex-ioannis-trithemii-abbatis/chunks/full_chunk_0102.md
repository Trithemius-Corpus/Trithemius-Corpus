Now that the demonstration of the possibility of our new invention has been shown rather briefly and yet sufficiently, I do not think that I myself should be wearied any longer over the same matter at this time, since the method provides a road onward to infinity, if anyone should need a model for further things. Nor do I think it at all necessary to recall to memory the matters recited in the preceding pages, since a discerning reader, if he is diligent, will also be able, with the Lord’s help, to discover the greater things that lie hidden in these. We have given the material, and at the same time the occasion, to the wise and diligent man; if he wishes to use them prudently, he will surpass our ingenuity with his own. But whoever wants this road to be more beautiful in its shade should join hand and mind to ingenuity, and make for himself a path more beautifully adorned by paraphrase. If these things delight anyone, let him keep it fixed in memory that he must always remain within the appointed circle; if he transgresses it in any way, the whole operation vanishes as useless. There are many names of men; indeed, there are almost infinite terms for things, villages, cities, provinces, trees, stones, and words, all of which serve our purposes. From these it is clear that it is not impossible for a book and a method to be made, provided that intelligence is at hand with diligence, which would surpass our inventions without limit. But the man who is of such dullness that he cannot understand these childish things of ours, when will he penetrate the subtler things that remain? We place an end upon the fourth book of the Polygraphia, to which, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, we join the fifth.

The Fifth Book of the Polygraphia
The Beginning of the Chapter.

Those matters which, with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ, we have set out in some fashion in the four preceding books for the completion of our Polygraphia reasonably require that, in the fifth book, we briefly treat the remaining precepts and methods of this same instruction that still remain. Accordingly, in it we shall give a sufficiently concise method by metathesis, that is, by transposition of letters, by which a friend may write safely, securely, and most secretly to a friend whatever he wishes. We read that Caesar Augustus used this method of writing in secret matters. Although this method of communicating is indeed secure in many ways, it is nevertheless always regarded with suspicion by those who look at it, because as soon as the transposition of the letters is perceived, anyone who is inquisitive at once suspects that something lies hidden under them. Now the methods of this invention are almost infinite; from them, for a necessary introduction, we have marked out the following for the reader, while reserving the rest to be searched out by his own ingenuity. First, therefore, we have set before him the canonical table of this art, and below it we have written another, reversed. In these two tables the root and foundation of the aforesaid transposition is altogether placed, and upon it the structure of the whole edifice is set. For once these two tables have been properly known and understood, no transposition of letters will be able to escape the diligent practitioner. To these we have joined twenty-four alphabets in order, so that we might provide the reader with an abundance of ways to transpose letters. The use of these lies, as one chooses, either in individual alphabets or, if you prefer, in all of them. If you have secret dealings with many people, distribute the individual alphabets to your individual associates, but commit to memory what you have given to each one.
