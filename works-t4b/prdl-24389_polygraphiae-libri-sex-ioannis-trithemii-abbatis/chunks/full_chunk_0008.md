I treated him with such kindness as I could, and, as long as he was with me, showed him all the benefits of hospitality with a cheerful face. And since, as often happens among friends, especially those devoted to letters, I had shown all my things to my most welcome guest for him to see, among the rest the aforesaid work of Steganography also came forward, not yet at that time completed. Bovelles saw it and read it cursorily, his mind occupied elsewhere; he said that he admired the invention, praised it, and did not trouble himself about the manner in which it was to be understood. Therefore, since he did not ask for the key of understanding, he deserved to hear or perceive nothing of what was contained in that work of ours.

After this, having returned to Gaul, he repaid me evil for good and shamefully violated the covenant of Christian friendship. For when Lord Germain de Ganay, our common friend and later bishop of Orléans, asked him what he had found and seen at my house in Spanheim, he wrote back falsehoods instead of truths and lies instead of benefits, condemning and rashly judging things that he was in no way able to comprehend with his understanding. For since he believed that the palm of learning would be conferred upon him by envy and falsehood, he wrote back to that most learned man a letter filled with falsehood, lies, injuries, and many insults, in which, making mention of a Steganography that he had not understood, he falsely, mendaciously, and with excessively injurious rash presumption proclaims me devoted to evil arts, a magician and necromancer.

By God’s mercy, I shall shortly answer his most mendacious injuries and blasphemies in a brief letter, so that all posterity may understand both that I am innocent and that Bovelles is, in this matter, an impious, cruel, rash liar. I affirm steadfastly, I speak truthfully, and I swear confidently upon my soul that I have never had any commerce with demons or with evil or pernicious magical or necromantic arts; but that all and each of the things that I have either written or promised that I would write are pure, sound, natural, and in no respect at all contrary to the Christian faith. Therefore I do not fear to submit my promises for examination before good and learned men, whenever and as often as there is need, and I do not in any way intend to resist their determination. As to why I have inserted this story of Bovelles’s rashness into this preface of mine, there was a reasonable cause:

so that everyone may understand that it was done by mature counsel that the work of Steganography has remained until now buried in darkness, not because I fear the rash judgments of Bovelles, but lest I should seem to give many others like him cause to suspect similar things about me. Indeed, as far as I am concerned, I would rather appease my rivals by silence than irritate them with letters or provoke them to madness with writings. For I judged it better to consign all wondrous inventions to perpetual silence than, even by the false estimation of men, to incur the mark of magical or necromantic and pernicious superstition. Let Steganography, then, lie hidden in darkness, and let it not become common property for the lying company of Bovelles, which is accustomed to judge matters unknown to it and to corrupt the reputation of a good man from the mere lust of depravity.

Nevertheless, at the urging of friends, so that our rivals, together with Bovelles, may understand how rash and lying an injury he has done us, while Steganography remains within its own limits, we have agreed to bring into the light the work set forth below, which we call Polygraphy, divided into six books. Let those who wish read, and let friends read this volume without bitterness; when they have understood all its hidden mysteries, let them themselves be judges, without envy, whether they are natural or superstitious. For I know, and am certain, that all things are sincere, pure, natural, and far removed from every pursuit of evil superstition.

Whenever, however, the discourse becomes rather obscure, this is done for the reason that a madman should not misuse, to destruction, the sword of defense which a man sound in soul and mind carries for his own protection. Yet nothing evil is contained in it on that account, since perverse men also abuse good things for evil. Whoever presumes to condemn this edition of ours is himself conscious and witness that he has never truly understood it. Mysteries are concealed under enigmas, lest the secrets of owls be made in any way manifest to blockheads. If anyone takes pleasure in knowing these things, let him approach as a reader with envy put far away.

For I am a Christian; I am a priest under the rule of the divine father Benedict and a monk. I love Christ, and with such sincerity of mind as I can, I always adore him devoutly.
