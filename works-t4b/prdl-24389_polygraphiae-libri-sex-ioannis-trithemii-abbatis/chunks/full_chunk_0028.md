Pliny, if I am not mistaken, gives another method in book 26. “Our people,” he says, “call tithymalus the milk-herb; others call it goat-lettuce.” They report also that when the body has been written on with its milk and it has dried, if ash is sprinkled on it, the letters appear; and in this way adulterers indeed preferred to communicate with one another rather than by little notes.

*The second kind, Hyphasmaticon.* The most learned man Theodorus Bibliander has *hyphasmicon*, for this reason, I think: in concealment of this sort no letters appear before the paper has been immersed in water. Thus, when we write letters with alum, water poured over them will make the paper darker than the letters, which otherwise could not have been read.

*Letters are also written in various other ways.* Herodotus, near the end of book 7: after Xerxes resolved to undertake an expedition against Greece, Demaratus, who was at Susa, learned of it and judged that it should be reported to the Greeks. But since he could not do it otherwise, for there was danger that he would be detected, he devised this plan: taking a pair of writing tablets, he scraped off the wax from them. Then he engraved the king’s plan on their wood. When the writing had been engraved, he covered it again with wax, so that the tablets being carried would cause no trouble with the guards on the roads. When they had been brought to Lacedaemon, the Lacedaemonians could not interpret them until, as I understand it, Gorgo, daughter of Cleomenes and wife of Leonidas, warned them and explained the matter itself, ordering the wax to be broken off, for then they would find the letters in the wood. Listening to her, the Lacedaemonians found the letters and read them, and then sent them on to the Greeks.

Gellius, book 17, chapter 9: “There is also,” he says, “in the records of Greek history another deep and unexpected hiding-place, devised by a barbarian stratagem.” There was a man named Histiaeus, born in a not undistinguished place in the land of Asia. King Darius then held Asia under his rule. When this Histiaeus was in Persia with Darius, he wished to announce certain secret matters to a certain Aristagoras by a furtive writing. He devised this hidden device for letters: from his slave, who had long had weak eyes, he shaved all the hair from the head

as though for the sake of healing him, and marked the smooth head into the forms of letters. In these letters he wrote out what he wished. Afterward he kept the man at home until the hair grew back. When this had happened, he ordered him to go to Aristagoras. “And when you have come to him,” he said, “tell him that I instructed him to shave your head, just as I myself recently did.” The slave came to Aristagoras as ordered and delivered his master’s message; and Aristagoras, thinking that the instruction was not pointless, did what had been commanded. In this way the letters were delivered.

Herodotus writes almost the same thing. In the same author, also, it is read in book 1 that when Astyages was harsh toward the Medes, Harpagus, insinuating himself with each of them, persuaded the leading men that Astyages ought to be removed from the kingship and Cyrus chosen. After these matters had been arranged and prepared, Harpagus, wishing at last to disclose his intention to Cyrus, who was among the Persians, but unable to do so otherwise, since the roads were guarded, devised a plan of this kind. He gutted a hare which he had obtained by skill, without cutting it open at all, and placed inside it a little book in which he had written what he wished. Sewing up the belly again, he gave the hare, together with nets, to one of his own household hunters, a most trustworthy man, and, instructing him orally that when he gave the hare to Cyrus he should tell him to open it with his own hands and to do this without witnesses, he sent the man away into Persia. When the messenger had carried this out, Cyrus received the hare, opened it, and read the little book found inside it. See Justin, book 1.

Historians also report that doves served as messengers in difficult affairs: letters tied to their necks or feet were sent during the siege of Mutina by Hirtius the consul to Decimus Brutus, and by Decimus Brutus to Hirtius. Food had been set out in certain very high places, and doves, first shut up in darkness and afflicted with great hunger, had been trained to fly back and forth from there.

Some, to avoid suspicion, write on paper with ink, as though they wished to tell a friend something about household affairs; afterward they write the secret matter in the spaces with urine. Others use the juice of lemons or onions; others use crushed sal ammoniac with water poured over it.
