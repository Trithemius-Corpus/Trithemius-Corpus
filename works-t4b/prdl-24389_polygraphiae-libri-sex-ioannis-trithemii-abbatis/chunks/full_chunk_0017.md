A very suitable method for writing with the utmost secrecy whatever we wish, in which each word represents the letter set before it at its head. Nor should it trouble you, reader, that a multitude of words in the open text contains a brief meaning in the hidden text, since within this childish arrangement of ours there lies another method of writing whatever you wish much more broadly and much more secretly, which public reason has forbidden us to make available to all. But anyone endowed with intelligence, or who applies the necessary things to the investigation, will find subtler teachings hidden in these matters and will clearly understand things wonderfully unknown before.

Nevertheless, this open method of writing will suffice for everything, since you can repeat it as often as you wish, and the repeated narrative will always be varied. For if, as we have said, you take one word from the first alphabet, one also from the second, and so consequently one from each, and one from those inserted outside the ordered line, up to a period or the end of the book, a Latin and sufficiently coherent discourse will arise for you. But care must be taken that at one time, and all at once, no more than one word is taken from one alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive falsification, does not pass over any alphabet and confuse the order of the narrative.

By day understand the open narrative; by night, however, the hidden and concealed one, which should not be too long but brief. Those things that follow in the paradigmatic text concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen pertain to both narratives, the hidden and the manifest. As often as a leaf is moved by the wind, and so on, we advise you to count the secret narrative in relation to the open one, so that both may end together at the foot of each period. But if anything of the secret matter remains over, let it be enlarged by such moderation that both narratives end at one point, lest confusion or an omission be produced from them. The other things that follow in the first Polygraphy are referred implicitly both to the explanatory instruction and to everything we have said.

It should be noted, however, that no word under the order of the alphabets is vacant; rather, each one represents the letter set before it at its head. So that the reader may not lack perfect knowledge of this matter, we shall clarify the sentence by an example. Let us suppose the case that a wicked man asks through you to be promoted with some friend of his. But you, so as to cut off the importunate requests and yet not promote an unworthy man, may write more secretly to the friend through the very man who is making the request, so that he does not understand: Beware of that man, because he is a thief and a very worthless scoundrel.
