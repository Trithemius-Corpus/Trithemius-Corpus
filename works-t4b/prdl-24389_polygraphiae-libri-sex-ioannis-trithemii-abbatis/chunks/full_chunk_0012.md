For the pupil of the new art will write a language that he did not know, and will understand and interpret Latin speech composed under regulated instruction, if you yourself have sufficiently directed him word for word. Finally, this instruction can be extended to all the things and affairs of the world. If anyone wishes to learn from me how these things can be done, let him remember first of all to show himself such that he deserves to receive it. For not everything is owed to everyone, but one person is to be preferred to another.

The fourth usefulness is that, through an enlargement of the likeness of the thing received, all these things can be extended into any language whatever, once the instructing binary has been reduced to unity. Yet not just anyone at all can do this everywhere, but only the man whom art and nature have dignified, with God going before him by his special grace.

The fifth usefulness is that, by imitation of likeness, we can communicate to one who knows the art of our invention whatever secrets of our mind we wish, abundantly, in many ways, and most widely, by various methods that cannot be known or noticed by anyone else. Here there is given a manifold speech without voice, through which, safely, securely, most widely, and with all suspicion removed, we shall be able to indicate most fully to those who know the art all the conceptions of our mind, however difficult they may be. And this marvelous invention has innumerable progressions, capable of all mysteries.

The sixth usefulness is that, through understanding this work, you will be able to discover any regulated metathesis or transposition of letters, and nevertheless to form them in such a way that they serve only those who know the art, and absolutely no one else in the world. And great is the force of metathesis in form and also in operation; it has many modes and is varied in many ways.

The seventh usefulness is that, through understanding this volume, a very great faculty is given to those who desire to enlarge the memory without labor, whenever the seeds of minutiae are set in their proper degrees. All things, for the sake of goodness, lie hidden wrapped in the secrets of words and figures. If anyone wishes to understand them more fully, let him call to mind that relentless labor conquers all things. For it will be enough for me to have discovered the principles of the art,

and to have handed down a rough method of writing, and one deliberately unformed. For there was neither time nor reason for our inventions to be shaped in more polished form, lest honey produce vomiting in those who are nauseated, or truth produce hatred in those who are indignant. Every age must be valued according to its own merits, and swine are not to be adorned with pearls, nor burdened with precious gems. All things are untimely that do not agree with the customs and times of the mortals upon whom they are imposed.

There have been many very noble human talents in unsuitable times, and many new things are found almost daily in times not their own. What benefit did the marvelous invention of nautical art bring to the distinguished versifier Bartholus under Pope Innocent VIII? In a certain little book he offered it for sale for one hundred thousand ducats, when neither the supreme pontiff himself, nor the Venetians, nor the Genoese, to whom he proposed that his matter be bought, deigned even to come to terms with him for a modest price. For he promised that by his art he could keep a ship unharmed in the middle of the sea for thirty months, repair one that was broken, and bring back without danger whatever had been thrown into the depths. But no one cared. Nor does it escape me that many, with Naso, have perished by their own talent. It is therefore better to set a limit to knowledge than to expose oneself fruitlessly to the hatred and detraction of many, for whom it is more familiar to despise any knowledge than to have learned it.
