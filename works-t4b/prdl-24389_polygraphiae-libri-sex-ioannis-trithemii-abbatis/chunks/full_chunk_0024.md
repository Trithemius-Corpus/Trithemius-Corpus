APOLOGY
OF JOHANNES TRITHEMIUS,
prefixed to the Steganography.

It is the opinion of many that the ancient philosophers and sages of art and nature, if they had discovered any secrets, concealed them by various methods and figures, lest they come to the knowledge of wicked men. The more learned among the Jews also confirm that Moses, the most famous leader of the Israelite people, in his description of the creation of heaven and earth, covered the ineffable secrets of mysteries with simple words. Likewise Saint Jerome, most learned among our own, says that in the Apocalypse almost as many mysteries lie hidden as there are words. I pass over the sages of the Greeks, men of no small reputation and glory among their own people, who, devoting their labor to the writing of fables, handed down one thing to the unlearned and another to learned men in the course of a single narrative, by a prudent invention. These most devoted lovers of wisdom, even if I cannot perfectly imitate them because of the slowness of my intellect, I nevertheless judge it a beautiful and glorious thing to admire, and, with whatever diligence I can, to read and venerate. Thus, when I consider that they discovered great things beyond other men by their own study, I drive myself, doing a kind of violence to myself, toward some imitation of them, even if among the latest. Nor, as I think, has my opinion wholly deceived me, since by continuous zeal for reading I have both learned many things that I did not know before, and have reserved my thoughts for investigating more secret matters. For even if I am not of such great learning or industry that I would dare to profess that I have grasped that method of the ancient sages for concealing mysteries, nevertheless I have devised certain methods, not altogether to be despised, as it seems to me, of writing secretly and of concealing, entirely without any suspicion, whatever you wish. ria, Prince Elector of the Roman Empire, most wise patron of philosophers, than whom no one has seemed to me more worthy for this great secret to be revealed to him, I have committed them to writing and reduced them into the following volume, not without great labor, lest this great secret come to the ears of common, ignorant, or wicked men. I ask you, reader, whoever you may be, to consider how many evils could be committed in the commonwealth by wicked and slippery people through this science, if it came to the knowledge of many: adulteries, fornications, conspiracies, betrayals, murders, robberies, and countless evils. For just as a good and honorable man can safely and without any suspicion from anyone disclose and express the secret of his will to another who knows this art, as often as he wishes, for private or common benefit, so any perverse, slippery, and malicious man will be able to communicate his evil desire without any suspicion, however shamefully, to persons agreeable to his pleasure, with every obstacle wholly removed. Nor could trust between married people remain safe thereafter, since a woman, though until now ignorant of the Latin language, made most learned through holy and modest words of any language or idiom, could understand very fully the evil and immodest will of her lover, even while her husband presents and praises the letters, and in the same way could most safely send back to him her own desire, at whatever length she wished, in sufficiently eloquent speech. Indeed, although this science may seem childish to some, nevertheless, if it were made public, the whole order of the commonwealth would be disturbed, public trust would perish, letters, instruments of writing, and finally all the very conversations of men would be turned into perpetual suspicion. No one would any longer trust letters, however good, honorable, and holy; no one would put faith in epistles, because, however holy and modest the words might be, deceit, fraud, and evil deception would nevertheless always be thought to lie beneath them. Men would become fearful in all things, and no less suspicious of mutual conversations than of any letters whatever. Nor will anyone ever be able to become so learned and expert in this science itself that there would not remain infinite methods, sufficient for all purposes, of writing secretly and most hiddenly, which he himself has not yet grasped.
