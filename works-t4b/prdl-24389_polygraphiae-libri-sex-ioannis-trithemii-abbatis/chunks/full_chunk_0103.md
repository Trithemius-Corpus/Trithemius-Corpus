...you have handed it on. First, indeed, let the first alphabet be entrusted to the first person, the second to the second, the third to the third, and so on likewise with the rest. But if, because of the multitude of business or of one's household, the alphabets which we have indicated should not suffice for everything, or if some of them seem too open and obvious, we shall be able to introduce various new transpositions, of which the number may be very great, while the method of the secret always remains hidden. Let us set down an example of this: let B be a, f be b, l be c, p be d, t be e, z be f, c be g, m be h, and so on through the whole usual alphabet, so that between any adopted letter three letters lie idle and transposed. Afterward let there be four, then five, more or fewer, yet in such a way that the whole alphabet, as though revolved into a circle, comes together, and no letter is omitted at all, but each is placed in the same sequence for another in its own order. In whatever way you write it, you must know that it is to be entrusted firmly either to writing or to memory, lest, through a lapse of forgetfulness, you yourself can no longer read what you have written.

For the transposition of letters can be varied in as many ways as, according to the estimation of the human intellect, which, enclosed in temporal operation in a mortal body, does not properly grasp infinities, there will arise a mortal number that is innumerable. Therefore whoever wishes to have more methods of writing by metathesis, and methods remote from our instruction, if he is even a little ingenious and diligent, will easily attain the desired effect in his operation. For the ingenuity of a person can do very great things if it is aided by study and art. But he who despises the smallest things will never reach the greater. I know that many will come who will deride these studies of ours as vain and childish; to them we give as our answer that we shall be wholly content, provided they please those at whose request they have been composed by us.

In this canonical or straight table of letters, from our one usual alphabet of Latin letters, through change or transposition of the letters themselves, you have as many alphabets as there are monograms throughout it, namely twenty-four times twenty-four, which make 576 in number; and when multiplied by the same number, they amount to a little less than fourteen thousand.
