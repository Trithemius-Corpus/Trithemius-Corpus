Finally, that you may disclose the secret of your mind without letters to someone absent, even when the very person who performs the office of messenger does not know it, remains written among the inventions of steganography.

Indeed, since kings and princes encounter many affairs of which they sometimes do not trust themselves to give knowledge safely enough to their vicars far away by ordinary letters, it would be worthwhile for each of them to have some special methods of writing and announcing, by which they might most safely make known their secrets to friends, especially in these times, when, with license to sin prevailing, trustworthy faith is found nowhere in the world. The messengers of princes are everywhere captured by savage robbers, stripped, beaten, and very often killed; nor do the impious plunderers spare even the letters of kings sealed and closed with rings. Hence it would be necessary for your commonwealth, O most glorious Caesar Maximilian, that you put on the skin of Hercules, brandish the club in your hands, and purge the world committed to you by God of these savage monsters. There will never be peace in the kingdom unless the bacucei are removed from the midst. Whoever disturbs the peace of the kingdom shows himself to be the king’s enemy. These are the robbers who beset the roads of travelers and seize what belongs to others; with them the earth is full today, and nowhere is the road safe for those who walk.

A BRIEF EXPLANATION OF THOSE THINGS WHICH SEEM RATHER OBSCURE IN THE FIRST BOOK OF THE POLYGRAPHIA.

Although nothing obscure is contained in the first book of the Polygraphia, nevertheless, lest its composition perhaps seem deep to simpler readers, we briefly explain the things that are thought more difficult. We have arranged in sequence more than nine thousand words of the Latin language under almost four hundred alphabets, in such a way that if from each alphabet only one word after another is taken from beginning to end, they cohere with one another and form a suitable Latin discourse.

For it will be a very convenient method of writing most secretly whatever we wish, since each word represents the letter set before it at its head. Nor should it trouble you, reader, that the multitude of words in the open text contains a brief meaning in the hidden one, since within this childish arrangement of ours there lies concealed a higher method of writing, more broadly and far more secretly, whatever you wish; public reason has forbidden us to make it plain to everyone. But whoever is endowed with talent and applies the necessary powers to investigation will find subtler things among the teachings hidden here, and, to his wonder, will clearly understand things previously unknown. Nevertheless, this open method of writing will suffice for everything, since you can repeat it as many times as you wish, and the repeated narration will always vary.

For if, as we have said, you take one word from the first alphabet, one also from the second, and so consequently one from each, and one from those interposed outside the ordered line, up to the period or the end of the book, a Latin and sufficiently suitable discourse will arise for you. But care must be taken that at one time, all at once, no more than one word be taken from a single alphabet, and that the writer, through negligence or excessive haste, not skip over some alphabet and confuse the order of the narration.

By “day” understand the open narration; by “night,” however, the latent and hidden one, which should not be too long but brief. The examples that follow in the text concerning the invisible soul and the body that is seen pertain to both narrations, the hidden and the manifest. “As often as the leaf is moved by the wind,” and the rest: we advise that you count the secret narration in relation to the open one in such a way that both end together at the close of any period. But if anything remains over from the secret matter, let it be lengthened with such moderation that both narrations end at one point, lest confusion from an eclipse be produced.

The other things that follow in the first Polygraphic explanation are also implicitly referred to all the things we have said.
