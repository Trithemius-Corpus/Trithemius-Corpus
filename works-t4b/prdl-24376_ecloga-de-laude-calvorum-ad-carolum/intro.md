The *Ecloga de Laude Calvorum ad Carolum* — "An Eclogue in Praise of Bald Men, to Charles" — is one of the legendary curiosities of medieval Latin literature: a 133-line poem in which **every single word begins with the letter C**. It is a lipogrammatic tour de force, an entire mock-encomium conducted under that one constraint — the bald-headed are like the sun, like the moon, like polished bronze, like a clear and unclouded sky — and the constraint is the joke as much as the praise. Composed in the Carolingian ninth century and addressed to the emperor Charles the Bald, it earned its author a small immortality outside his more serious musical and hagiographic writings.

That author is **Hucbald of Saint-Amand** (c. 840–930), not Trithemius. The poem reached this corpus through Trithemius's entry on Hucbald in the *De Scriptoribus Ecclesiasticis* (also in this corpus, `prdl-70280`), which quotes it in full; the early-modern Trithemius compilations reprinted it alongside that entry, and that is the chain by which a ninth-century poem ends up among the works of a fifteenth-century abbot. It is retained here for full coverage of the printed-Trithemius witness, and because Hucbald's *ecloga* has **no readily available modern English translation** — this appears to be the first freely accessible continuous rendering.

The Latin is metric (mostly hexameters with elegiac interludes) and word-play-dense; the English here flattens the metrical play to readable prose while keeping the chapter structure (Prooemium, then Cap. I–XII) and the prose argument-headings Hucbald gave each verse block. This is the **Mainz edition, ca. 1496** (BSB scan); the translation sits in the top tier (S).
