Epigram of Richard Sbrulius of Friuli.

Who would deny that God is the parent of heaven and earth?
No one is so savage as to deny that there is a God.
God himself is worshipped everywhere throughout the whole world;
he is sought by a different path, yet he is the same.
The barbarian worships him as one who orders all things by fixed law;
he denies incense to the holy Son and to the Spirit.
The Hebrew boasts that he holds the true doctrines,
and, with Moses as his leader, thinks himself the people of God.
But the people of Christ, who took their name from his name,
steadfastly sings that God is triune.
Maximilian, greatest, who presides over the empire,
continually aids the cause and honor of Christ.
He asks, as it is fitting for an august king to ask such things,
why the highest God permits there to be so many sects;
why he does not present himself equally to all mortals;
why he wished many things to lie hidden under hard signs.
Father Trithemius sets forth everything to the king in order,
that leader and ornament of the hooded flock.
Whoever you are who used to doubt concerning the holy faith,
read these things; soon you will be a sure pupil of Christ.

End.
Royal Library of Munich.

To the most serene and most unconquered lord of the world, Emperor Maximilian: Johannes Trithemius, Abbot of Peapolitanus, The Book of Eight Questions. In the name of Christ begins the book of the monastery of Benedictbeuern.

I have obeyed your commands, most unconquered Emperor Maximilian, and those eight questions which you formerly proposed to me, when I had been summoned to Your Highness in the castle of Boppard, I have resolved with what brevity I could and set down in this form of a little book.

You wished, however, if I remember correctly, that, so far as possible in their solution, I should follow the path of nature, on account of those who yield less readily to belief. Yet this did not seem possible to me in all matters, because the subject matter of the questions in many places rested upon theological rather than natural principles. For although the Christian religion, among all the sects of mortals, is held to be nearer and more conformable to the laws of nature, nevertheless the principal articles of our faith are not all demonstrable according to the common path of nature. We believe that this was necessarily done so that faith might have a place and firm belief might not lack its own merit.

Therefore, entering upon a middle path of disputation, as far as my powers allowed and by command, I have used partly nature and partly also the testimony of the divine Scriptures. For truly, the things which it is fitting for us to believe are not to be rashly examined by human arguments, since, as Saint Gregory says, faith has no merit when human reason supplies proof for it.
