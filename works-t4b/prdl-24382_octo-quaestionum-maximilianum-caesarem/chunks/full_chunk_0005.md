But if the angelic intellect is compared with the human, it is as nature is to art, and as intellect simply speaking is to reason, which is closely joined to the principles of the disciplines, while reason proceeds discursively from principles to conclusions. Hence it is clear that the angelic intellect apprehends its proper object immovably; toward that object the human intellect, through the angelic medium, after many exercises of itself, as has been said, at last prepares an ascent for itself by reasoning. But the human intellect is separated, by the medium of angelic cognition, from the perfection of its natural object, and therefore is necessarily mobile, returning through that medium to its own principle. For every motion is made from one extreme through a middle to an extreme; but where there is no middle, the extremes are already immediate and proximate, and for this reason immobile. But the human intellect, placed as it were in a dark prison so long as it is held in the body, is moved by sense and reason toward understanding, although very confusedly and imperfectly; and for that reason it admits between itself and God the medium that we have called angelic, while it bears the body as the very great impediment to its destined perfection. As a certain lover of wisdom said: The corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation depresses the sense that thinks many things. Therefore it was altogether necessary for us to believe in God rather than to know or understand him, because it is utterly impossible for that super-divine and uncircumscribable majesty to be comprehended by us, who understand nothing without the service of the senses and the discourse of reason. But God, existing far above every position, remains subject neither to the senses of mortals nor to reason, as he is read to have said to Moses: No man shall see me and live. Hence also the divine evangelist John says: No one has ever seen God. Nor is this any wonder. For since God is the first and everlasting intellect, most pure, most simple, most perfect, immobile, the creator, incomprehensible, highest,

¶ But concerning that question which seemed to arise from the first, whether God, since he is omnipotent, could have made himself knowable to the human intellect just as to the angelic, I say that no one of sound mind has ever denied that God can do all things. But those things that imply contradiction we say God cannot do, because they are contrary to the order of wisdom that immutably disposes all things, just as they are not doubted to be contrary to the destruction of the whole, and, if I may say so, utterly opposed to it. For it neither was fitting, nor could in any way have happened, that God should be comprehended by the human intellect; since if mortal man, by the dictate of nature, comprehended immortal God, certainly he would no longer seem to have to be called man or mortal, but an angel, and either man would become comparable to God or God to man. For as long as the angelic intellect stands between, the human cannot understand God. And it stands between until the death of the flesh, when the mind, having obtained its natural liberty, escapes wholly equal to the angel. You see now, I believe, emperor, that it is clear that it neither was fitting nor was possible that the human intellect should comprehend the divine: the created comprehend the creator, the temporal the eternal, the mutable the immutable, the weak the omnipotent, since, as a certain philosopher says, there is no proportion of the finite to the infinite. Therefore it is necessary for mortals to believe in immortal God, whom we can comprehend by no manifest reasoning.
