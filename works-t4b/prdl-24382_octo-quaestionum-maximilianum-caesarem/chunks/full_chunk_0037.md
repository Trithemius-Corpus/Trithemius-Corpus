Since, therefore, that supreme intellect, the creator, preserver, and ruler of all things, almighty God, rules and governs the world by his unchangeable providence, holding all things under his command, surely no place anywhere is left for rash chance, which might impede the will of so great a majesty.

Therefore the universe, whatever exists anywhere or can exist, is comprehended under the rule of one divine mind, whose infallible judgment nothing can escape. But unexpected events happen to us because we are changeable and are changed almost daily, having no determinate knowledge of future things. But that best maker of all things, God, since he is altogether unchangeable, knows all things, beholds all things, and understands all things at once and simultaneously. His providence is not deceived; his will is never changed; his power is not broken; his ordering is not violated; his counsel is never interrupted, even if his decision sometimes seems to be changed. Hence, if we attend carefully, we shall plainly understand, as the ends of things and motions will show, that all these lower things are made, preserved, disposed, and moved by divine providence, to whose command nothing at all is alien. For God himself, the lord of the universe, understands both all things and each thing: not stretched out, after our fashion, among many things, but abiding in himself as an indivisible unity. By contemplating himself as the pattern of the whole universe, and the cause of causes, and through causes the cause of all things, he beholds all things; and in beholding, he cares for and provides for each thing, directing it to its end and good by an everlasting order.

Come now, most wise Caesar, so that we may understand divine providence governing all things, let us use arguments as briefly as we can for our purpose. Therefore, if God's action is his own understanding, which no wise man fails to admit as at once most eminent and most inward, then as much as he acts, so much surely

he understands, and conversely. But he acts in individual things, not only in great things but even in the smallest; therefore he understands individual things. For the first Being has, through each and every being that follows in fixed orders, a most ample influence; and the things that act after him are everywhere moved and act by his power.

Either, then, God understands or does nothing outside himself, which is false; or, in acting upon all things and each thing, he also understands those same things, which is true. For they would never be made unless first, to speak in our fashion, they were understood by God himself, who makes them, just as things are not made by the sun unless they are looked upon by it. But because the omnipotent one understands all things and each thing that he makes, there is no doubt that he disposes, rules, and orders all things that he has made; and from him things have not only that they exist, but also that they are certain causes of other things. Thus, if things have from God that they are causes, from him also they have received the proper office of a cause, that is, to preserve according to their powers and to care for what they have made. Therefore, if all causes have this property from the first cause, insofar as they are certain causes, rightly does that first cause naturally have this goodness and providence, so that he cares for all things and each thing that he has made, insofar as he is most of all the cause of them all and of each of them.

Indeed, unless these things are guarded individually by him who makes them, the common things that are preserved in individual things will never be guarded. For the whole, which consists of parts, will not be preserved if the parts themselves are neglected. Therefore from the intention of the first and greatest common intelligence proceeds the universal distinction of things. This intelligence, however, is distinguished of itself from others, and by the standard of its own knowledge distinguishes the rest among themselves. Hence, just as God knows all things and each thing most distinctly, so also he most distinctly rules, moderates, and cares for them.

Therefore I think it is manifest, most clement Caesar Maximilian, and sufficiently shown, that the providence of God, the supreme ruler, embraces, disposes, rules, and moderates all lower things and each thing, which otherwise, without the provident disposition of one who observes them, would not continue this order which we perceive.
